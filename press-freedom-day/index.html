The May Third Register

Questions & Answers on World Press Freedom Day — also called World Press Day — set down in the archive of the Dhaka Gazette File

ARTICLE 191948 · UDHR
04:12 Dhaka · 04:41 Narsingdi · 05:52 Dohar · 08:19 Jessore · 09:04 Khulna · 3 May · facts in order · the flag kept small ·

This register is kept for one day a year and is printed the rest of the time. In December 1993, the United Nations General Assembly declared 3 May to be World Press Freedom Day (also called World Press Day), in order to raise awareness of the importance of freedom of the press. Its intent was to remind governments of their duty to respect and uphold the right to freedom of expression enshrined under Article 19 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights. What follows is the register itself: the questions asked of it, and the answers the archive can stand behind. The consulting editor of record is Kamal Ahmed, of The Daily Star in Dhaka, who keeps seat 14, lower berth, on the Dhaka Dawn Express No. 257 and who signs this register the way he signs the margin: K. A.

Q.1 Why 3 May, and not some other date the calendar would have offered?

The date was chosen to honour the first Pan-African Conference on Freedom of the Press, held at Winna in Ethiopia in 1991, at which African ministers called for a genuinely free media. The General Assembly, taking up the matter in December 1993, fixed 3 May and gave it a name. The archive notes that the day falls a full four months before the winter working on Route 6, which is why the printing can begin at dawn without the press office having to close a single door.

Q.2 What exactly is the General Assembly asking governments to do?

Two things, stated in the plainest column of the register. First, to respect freedom of the press — to let the facts be gathered, carried, and printed in the order found. Second, to uphold the right of expression that Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights already guaranteed in 1948: every person, the article says, shall have the right to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information through any media. The commission of which Mr. Ahmed was chairperson drafted forty-three recommendations that season; nine were carried into the government's hand. The register counts the nine. It does not argue about the thirty-four.

Q.3 Who keeps this register, and on what authority?

It is kept in the same archive as the timetable of BTR Route 6, in the book that opens at Narsingdi at 22:19 and the same number of minutes after the morning light. Its authority is small on purpose: a date fixed by the General Assembly, an article of 1948, and a single editorial rule — a journalist travels with his facts the way a guard travels with his book, the whole of them, in order, and not one more than the whole. The register was carried south once, in the tea crates of the 258, and came back with no new stains, which the archive treats as a good result.

Q.4 When does the day's printing begin?

At 04:12, the hour the 257 leaves Dhaka platform 9, because a press that is free should be awake before its plain is. The first proof goes out on the morning working, seat 14, lower berth, and reaches Khulna at 09:04 with the rest of the 189 kilometres. The editor has confirmed that the day's lead will be no longer than three hundred words: facts in order, the flag kept small, nothing divided from itself. Passengers of saloons 2 and 3 are invited to read it on the line between Arambagh and Jessore, where the morning light comes off the water on the left-hand side.

Q.5 What is the Fast-Fish line, and why does the register keep it?

Among the first clippings the archive received for the day was a letter of testimony from a country correspondent, written long before the wire reached the hamlets, describing the first electric messages arriving at a small town's post office while the old mail boat — the Fast-Fish — still ran the country towns and hamlets of the coast. The letter's closing line is kept in the register exactly as it stands, word for word:

"Receiving, as she stood watching them, and with such prodigies as these; yet in country towns and hamlets but Fast-Fish?"

It is kept because it is the day's whole argument in one breath: the messages are arriving — such prodigies — and yet, in the country towns and hamlets, the mail boat is still the only delivery. Freedom of the press, on this register, is not finished when the wire is laid; it is finished when the last hamlet stops waiting for the Fast-Fish to make its delivery. The line is unsigned, as the letter was, and it is the only unsigned entry in the book.

Q.6 Does the register count anything at all?

It counts the way a timetable does. One day a year, fixed in December 1993. One article, number 19, fixed in 1948. One train a morning, No. 257, running 189 kilometres in 4 hours 52 minutes, with 9 minutes' allowance at Tongi and 16 at Narsingdi. Forty-three recommendations, nine carried. Nine stations, fare 126 tk all the way, 91 tk to Chandpur. The register's rule, agreed at Shibganj and never broken, is that a number in the column must have been measured, counted, or printed — never hoped. The deferred figures of other archives may be found where the work was watched from the other side of the glass, in the Trackside & Signal Box thread on the Great Western main line, where a 2017 end date became a rumour, then a footnote, then a historical curiosity; the register files that history under numbers that were hoped, and files the 257's punctuality under numbers that were kept.

Q.7 What is a government to do on 3 May if it is not doing what Article 19 asks?

Open its doors. The register's practical column is short: publish the timetable of the press, not of the train — the fees, the licenses, the hours at which a permit may be sought and in how many minutes it is granted or refused, with the refusals printed in the same type as the grants. Decide in daylight, in a moving train, where no one could close a door on the conversation; the rule was agreed at Shibganj on a Thursday, and it has never failed the commission. A government that can publish its refusals in the same type as its grants has, on this register, done most of what the day asks of it.

Q.8 When is the register corrected?

Monthly, like the book; the day itself is corrected only by the river. The next printing is the first working of the coming 3 May, and the lead will again be three hundred words, no more. Until then the register stands as printed, with the nine carried recommendations in the left-hand column and the Fast-Fish line in the right, and the whole of the rest held, without division. The front shelf of the archive, Webstuffs, files it under Route 6, annex of the book, next to the guard's casket of minutes.
