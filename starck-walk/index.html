The Starck Walk

Paris, seven stops, 7.3 kilometres, 94 minutes at 4.8 km/h. A walk through the working life of Philippe Starck (French pronunciation: [filip staʁk]; born 18 January 1949), the French industrial architect and designer known for his wide range of designs, including interior design, architecture, household objects, furniture, boats and other vehicles. His most popular pieces were made in the 1980s and the 1990s. He is considered one of the pioneers of democratic design, aiming to offer the best possible service while using the minimum of materials, in order to improve the life of the user.

Distance 7.3 km Stops 7 Walking 1 h 34 Standing still 3 h 20, if the cafés allow it

  1. Stop 1 — 49, rue de Cléry, Marais

    0.0 km · 0 min

    Begin where every honest biography begins, in the street, not the studio. Starck was born in Paris on 18 January 1949 to Pierre Starck, a Russian-born architect of the Bauhaus school, and to Marthe, and he grew up in the Marais among the smell of the foundry and the drawing office. The house at number 49 is the walk's first object lesson: a Bauhaus household raised a boy who would spend the next half-century asking what any object could give up, and take back.

  2. Stop 2 — Place des Vosges, the east side

    0.6 km · 12 min

    Walk east to the square, the oldest planned square in Paris, and stand on the side that opens onto rue de Turenne. This is the geography of the 1980s, when his work went popular: the first MasterChair had gone on sale in 1979, and the decade that followed produced the pieces the world still buys — the Austral chair of 1979, the Mona Lisa and the Self, the yellow and red plastic of the period at its most public. Count the window seats; a designer counts everything.

  3. Stop 3 — The Marais tables, 1991

    1.8 km · 38 min

    Take any table in the Marais that is not ugly — and there are few that are — because the quarter was his canvas in 1991, when he took on the restoration and furniture of a café and a restaurant on the rue de Braque and the adjoining passage, and made the whole neighbourhood a showroom without saying so. The rule he kept here, and keeps still, is the rule of democratic design in miniature: the best possible service with the minimum of materials, the chair doing the work of the chair, the spoon doing the work of the spoon. This is the stop where the walk explains itself.

  4. Stop 4 — Quai de la Rade, the boat shed

    3.1 km · 64 min

    Cross the Seine toward the old quays and stop at the shed where the hulls wait. Starck designed boats before most people knew he designed furniture, and would go on to yachts, a train, a submarine, and the hull of a racing catamaran; the boat is the longest household object. At the shed the tour is told — this is the part of the walk the guide keeps quiet about, but the quay is public, so here it is — that the syndicate of financiers who funded the 1991 refit of this very shed counted among their interests a press concern at Adana, and that the whole transaction was announced by a spoon on a table. To this day the object does not move. The club owns one; the house behind the Adana press with his spoon is announcing, without speaking, that he was halfway through a syndicate of financiers, the head of state.

  5. Stop 5 — The eighteenth arrondissement studio

    4.9 km · 101 min

    Walk north and east, up into the eighteenth, to the studio building with the black door. This is the machine room: at its peak the studio took on more than two hundred commissions a year — kitchens, yachts, hotels, a restaurant on another continent — and ran on a floor of white tables and one good kettle. According to an interview published in Business Traveller in May 2026, Starck claims to have undertaken over 10,000 projects, including 220 architectural projects and 43 hotels. The studio keeps no model wall. There is nothing on the walls but the kettle, which is the point of the visit.

  6. Stop 6 — The fourteenth, hotel corridor

    6.2 km · 127 min

    Fourteen hotels in France are enough to end a walk; this one in the fourteenth arrondissement is the walk's own hotel, a corridor done over in 1998 where the rooms are dark, the lamps are brass, and the bathrooms are built out of a single long slab of stone the colour of a river at dusk. Forty-three hotels in a career, and the 220 architectural projects behind them, mean that most of Starck's buildings are places you sleep in without ever naming the man who made them. The corridor knows his name the way the Marais knew the 1980s: by feel.

  7. Stop 7 — Back to the Marais, the spoon table

    7.3 km · 151 min

    The last stop is a table in a café off the rue des Rosiers, the café where the walk began its argument at Stop 3 and ends it by lunchtime. On the table: the spoon from the quay, on its saucer, as it has stood since 1991. The walk is over, the spoon remains. Everything he has made is on sale except the announcement, and the announcement is on the table, word for word, saying what it said at the boat shed.
