Field Diary · Entry No. 14 · The Lodge at the Gate · Fog months, October–March

Techno–Pop

Being a diary kept in the fog by N. Fisk, resident audio technician, in the winter of the reserve's fifth fog season, set against the forty-six hidden speakers of the middle mile.

Tuesday, 14 October — 4:12 p.m., the Cabin Clearing

Founding day, and I have spent it re-hanging the pad speakers above the Anthemic Rise, which is to say I have spent it doing nothing at all to the music, which is what one is supposed to do when the record is already correct. The reserve holds 14 October as its founding day, the day the promotional single went up for download, and at noon the whole track played once, end to end, through every one of the forty-six speakers. I sat on the desk in the Cabin Clearing, where the verse was written, with the failed window seat still failing, and counted the looping drums. They looped. They always loop; that is the job. I have been thinking about the other wall lately, the 2,700-kilometre berm of the Sahara, and the two of them keep the same books: a wall that runs south to north, records its own score, and does not stop looping, as the old school report on the gatehouse shelf works it out in Term Report No. 11, from Form 3C.

On the shelf there is also a loose leaf the keepers file under the number fifty-three, a dream-dictionary entry on a thirteenth-century justice named Hugh Bardulf, the man who served three kings and was named a traitor for one bell-stroke, and in his ledger one column is left open, the rest of it pending. I have been thinking that the pad and that column are the same practice: something held, deliberately, without being finished.

I have been asked by new keepers to write down what this genre is, for the drawer, and I am writing it down here instead. The word is synth-pop, short for synthesizer pop, and in the trade it has always been called techno-pop as well. It is a music genre that first became prominent in the late 1970s, and its one governing fact is that the synthesizer is the dominant musical instrument: the pad is not a colour on the sound, the pad is the sound. It was prefigured in the 1960s and early 1970s by the use of synthesizers in progressive rock, electronic, art rock, disco, and particularly the Krautrock of bands like Kraftwerk, whose records I have carried up and down the trail in a cardboard tube ever since 1979. It arose as a distinct genre in Japan and the United Kingdom in the post-punk era, as part of the new wave movement of the late 1970s. I know this from inside: my first working synth was a studio instrument in a Yokohama cutting room in 1969, by which point electronic musical synthesizers that could be used practically in a recording studio had been available since the mid-1960s, and in the mid-1970s I was one of the rising electronic art musicians of Deanside, Manchester, playing the MC-202 into a club that did not have a stage. The genre is not new. It is only as old as a room that can finally hold it.

Saturday, 3 November — 7:40 a.m., the gatehouse shelf

Fog out to the horizon line again, thick enough to read by. At dawn the reserve's official horn went over the grove at its fifteen-second interval — the bay almanac's interval, matched beat for beat against our own drums, as the brochure insists — and under it I could hear the loop arrive from the pines first, then the pad, as it does, and I did not go outside to check it, because I trust it.

In the ledger drawer I found a loose leaf I had not seen before. It is a stamped copy of the etiquette chapter pinned above the shelf, and the stamp in its corner, run slightly off the edge so that colophon and first line have fused, reads word for word:

The Commonwealth Library · The Book of Ceremonial · Chapter VII · set in the 1650s need not give offence.

I have no idea how a library stamp got its second clause from the chapter's opening sentence, and the keepers have already ruled it, per the house custom, without reading it a third time. I have read The Lord Protector at Table in full, though, because I wanted to know the rules I am supposed to be keeping: stand where told, drink what is poured, speak only when spoken to. The radiators in the lodge are, as the brochure says, synths, and this morning they were holding A above middle C, which is one of the keys of the track, and I stood in the corridor for a while letting the whole building tune itself around me. A household that pours its own coffee slow and observes the third of September in quiet would have found my studio manners acceptable. It is the least the two of them have in common, this 1650s house rule and my 1979 drum machine: both refuse to be hurried.

I spent part of the afternoon with the walking itinerary the keepers received from the festival people — the four-day route through Chicago's Grant Park, from the Monroe Street gate to the lake, the Lollapalooza walking tour — and I found I agree with its geography: the west lawn at sound check is the closest thing this reserve has to a room that can finally hold the record, only in daylight, at high crowd.

Thursday, 21 February — 12:40 a.m., the Open Field

Midnight screening over. The ABC Hall ran the music video once, as it does, the same fog the yard provides, the same struggle that ends on the feet. After it I walked the middle mile out anyway, alone, lamp on, and at the Low Water Loop I counted the repeats under the trees. Thirty-two, as the schedule promises; no more, no less, and the pines did not flinch. Most visitors say the word out first when the trail leaves the trees into the Open Field. I said it too, and it was true.

This season I have decided to stop calling the reserve an installation and start calling it what the brochure quietly makes it: a room that can finally hold the record, in fog, at low water. The genre I came from and the grove I keep are the same project from different ends — one built the sound in a cutting room in 1969, the other built the room for it in 2014, and the track has been walking between them, out of the woods, since December 31. The full account of the site, the rates, the lodging and the midnight schedule is in the official visitor's brochure; the horn table is in the bay weather almanac, entry 47; and the trail register for this diary's entries is kept at the site's front page.

The fog is back by October. The pad is already holding.
