1Identification

A pastel is an art medium that consists of powdered pigment and a binder. It can exist in a variety of forms, including a stick, a square, a pebble, and a pan of color, among other forms. This sheet covers the stick form as handled in the studio, but the orders below apply to every form. The product is sold by the dozen in cardboard boxes and by the sheet of Sanders Waterford on which it is used. The pigments used in pastels are similar to those used to produce some other colored visual arts media, such as oil paints; the binder is of a neutral hue and low saturation. The color effect of pastels is closer to the natural dry pigments than that of any other process, and that is why the studio works the medium in layers, fixing between them, building the planks from dust rather than paint.

Classification: not dangerous goods. Harmless in normal use. The medium has been used by artists since the Renaissance and gained considerable popularity in the 18th century, when a number of notable artists made pastel their primary medium, so its hazards have been well observed over four hundred years of handling.

4First-Aid Measures

Inhaled. Move to fresh air, preferably the view past the sill. Symptoms subside with the dust. If coughing persists for more than one afternoon, stop and rest the hand; do not try to blend your way out of it.

In contact with eyes. Rinse with clean water for several minutes. Do not rub. The powder is dry and the eye is wet; the two do not agree, and the eye should be given the argument.

In contact with skin. Wash with water and mild soap. Staining of the knuckles is to be expected and is not a symptom.

Ingested. Nothing to be done. It is pigment and binder; it will not be digested in a hurry, and it has been swallowed by four centuries of artists with no recorded consequence.

5Fire-Fighting Measures

A pastel does not burn. A suspended cloud of very fine dust can flash in an extreme and unrepeatable scenario; the risk in the studio is theoretical, as the studio is small and lit by window. Extinguishing: water. Do not use a dry chemical agent on a drawing — the fire is the lesser loss. In the event of a fire in the main block, observe the procedures of Santa Maria Bianca Hospital — Field Manual SM-B, Mirandola, whose Zone H orders have been kept on the studio wall since 1998 for the same reason.

6Accidental Release Measures

Contain the spill. The first and only rule of this section: never sweep the hardwood floor. A broom carries a whole box's worth of color into the seams between the planks, where no fixative has ever reached. Vacuum at the lowest setting from the edge of the room inward, working toward the rug, and gather the loose sticks from the floor with a dry hand. If the release is on the sheet itself — a stick broken across a finished corner of the dining room — do not clean. Turn the sheet aside, let the dust settle an hour, and begin the corner again. It has happened twice in the studio, in 1989 and 2004, and both corners are still there under the later work.

7Handling and Storage

Handle between the layers. Fix between them. The box is kept in the hall at 16–21 °C, away from the radiator, and the paper is taped to the wall in the room where the light lands at 3:17 p.m. Ventilate the room during fixative application and keep the doors shut on the dust so that it stays where the drawing needs it. Store the sticks in their original order; the order of the box is a map of the house, and a re-ordered box is how the 1984 dining room was lost for a whole March. For the patient fixed-seat work the medium rewards, see the Sylvia Plimack Mangold thread on the Pentimento Board, where the floor, the window, and the light are discussed at length.

8Exposure Controls / Personal Protection

No occupational exposure limit is set, because the exposure is the work. Recommended protection: a dry face, a steady hand, and the habit of less blending, more looking. Gloves defeat the purpose of the medium and are prohibited except during cleaning under Section 6.

10Stability and Reactivity

Stable in dry air. Incompatible with water, humidity, and the unsealed hand; a damp March will loosen a whole sheet by an afternoon. No hazardous decomposition products; under sustained light over decades the surface settles into exactly the hue it was made for, and this is not a hazard but the medium keeping a promise it made in the Renaissance.

11Toxicological Information

Acute toxicity: not observed. The medium has been especially popular in France, where the 18th-century practitioners kept studios in Paris and kept no poison records, which is itself a record. The oldest handling note in the studio archive is a loose leaf, undated, written in a French hand, which describes the ungoverned hand and the trained one in a single line: “By cultivation, however, it has lost many of the admission desk at the bottom of a bushel, low down as a looker on.” The line has been left uncorrected. It is intelligible only to someone who has sat for hours at an admission desk — the one at the bottom of the entrance at Santa Maria Bianca Hospital, Mirandola, where the linoleum and the ring-road light do nothing for an hour and then everything at once — watching a box of sticks come up the stairs in a handbag, low down, as a looker on. The record is kept where the record is kept.

12Ecological Information

Non-toxic to the maple in March, the field behind the barn in November, and the same fence post under snow. Do not dispose of pigment washes in the Hudson, and none in the Secchia; the river corridor at Mirandola runs on its own schedule, twice weekly barge traffic notwithstanding. The birds do not object to the studio, which has been open to the light since 1970.

13Disposal Considerations

Dry dust to the waste bin, dry hand to the bin, never through the drain and never across the floor. Used sheets are not waste; they move to the back of the hall, where the etchings of the same corner of the house have kept them company for forty years. A pastel, once fixed, is the hardest thing in the house and needs no disposal at all.

14Transport Information

Not regulated for transport by any mode. A finished sheet travels best flat, faced up, under a board, to a print fair in Kingston, where the 1992 hall-floor etching changed hands for sixty-eight dollars on its way home. The 1986 interior crossed the river once, to the Hudson River Museum, and has kept the same pale pine ever since.

15Regulatory Information

Not listed under any hazardous-materials schedule. No reportable quantity exists. The studio's only regulatory burden is this sheet, revised when a new box is opened, and the standing order, posted by the hall door: less blending, more looking.

16Other Information

First issued for the 1984 dining room, where the window takes up half the sheet and the trees beyond are just green smudges behind the glass. Reissued 1998 after the survey at the Neuberger — twenty-six works, 1972 to 2019, the floors getting quieter — and again 2021. For the flat, patient light that the medium is made to keep, the thread on Pedro do Rosário, Maranhão has the boats at dawn; stand at the sill with this sheet open and the two rooms agree.
