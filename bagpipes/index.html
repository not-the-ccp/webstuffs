Field Notes · Aerophone Series · Plate 4

A Stand of Pipes

Observations on the bagpipe, made chiefly in a cold room at Fenn Court, Old Greenwich, between 17 January and 9 February

AEROPHONE · GROUP A · BAG FURNISHED

Entry 1 · 17 January · 9:05 p.m. · cold room, Fenn Court

First observation

The subject of these notes arrived at the bench at Fenn Court on a Thursday, in a padded case with brass catches, and set itself up beside the walnut music-box organ that Build Log No. 41 describes, crank and red cloth and all. The room was at three degrees, as the cold room is always at three, and the subject inflated its own bag with about six short bellows strokes and held. I had expected a noise. What I got was a note — a steady, round, continuous note, exactly as continuous as a note can be in a room that does not move — and I understood at once that this is not an instrument you play in bursts. It is an instrument that keeps a reservoir. The piper was a man of some fifty winters who answered to no name I could get from him except what the other men called him, which was, to my ear, simply Pipes.

Entry 2 · 24 January · anatomy, sketched from the bench lamp

What the bird is made of

Sketched against the bench leg with the piper’s permission: a bag, which is the reservoir — the whole idea of the instrument; a chanter, a small wooden pipe with finger holes, into which the melody is sounded, its tongue a single reed enclosed and sealed inside the stock, so that the reed can never dry out or be touched by the air outside; and drones, three of them in the variety I observed, which are simply long tubes that hold one note each while the piper walks, talks, or thinks. The bag is fed by a blowpipe and, in this variety, by two short bellows under the arm, so the piper never has to choose between breathing and sounding. This last point is the whole of the subject and worth a second sentence: the air is held at a constant pressure, and therefore the note does not swell and fade the way a note does on any other wind, because there is no breath behind it, only a bag. I have filed it accordingly: a woodwind instrument, in the strict sense, using enclosed reeds fed from a constant reservoir of air, and so a member of the aerophone group, because air must be blown into it to produce a sound.

Entry 3 · 31 January · the names

What it is called by those who keep it

The common name in the books is bagpipe, and I have used it above because the books use it. The keepers of the subject do not. In three evenings I heard the pipes, and a set of pipes, and a stand of pipes, and I did not hear the long name once, not once, and the piper looked at me a moment when I used it, the way a keeper looks at a man who has called a dog by its catalogue number. A field naturalist notes names as spoken, so I note it: the term bagpipes is used, though pipers usually refer to the bagpipes as “the pipes,” “a set of pipes” or “a stand of pipes.” These notes follow the keepers. I will write the pipes where the piper would, and I will write bagpipe only in the first entry, where the books insisted.

Entry 4 · 7 February · range and stations

Where the species turns up

The variety I observed is the Scottish Great Highland bagpipe, the one the whole of the rest of the world files everything under, and it is the one the cold room at Fenn Court knew. But the piper, who has made the rounds, put the station list on one finger each: the pipes are not a local. People have played them for centuries throughout large parts of Europe, North Africa, West Asia, around the Persian Gulf and northern parts of South Asia, and the Great Highland set is only the loudest survivor of a family that is, and was, all of those places. One of those stations has since been written up in its own entry, from the gate log at the Umayyad gate — Damascus, Travel Guide Entry No. 37, which carries the evening the stand of pipes rang at the great gate, with the gatekeeper’s line kept word for word in the margin. He said it the way a piper says these things, without emphasis, as though noting the weather, and he put a drone in the note — the great drone of the set sits at about ninety-eight cycles, a G two octaves below middle C, and in a room at three degrees it is felt before it is heard. I took the reading on the tuner’s app, ninety-eight and a quarter, and I put the number in the margin because numbers keep a room honest.

Entry 5 · 9 February · shelf I, and the line

What the shelf made of it

On the last evening the builder carded the visit in his grandfather’s system, and I was allowed to watch the carding, which is a ceremony. The organ is filed Newton, J. / New Britain / Greenwich, and the pipes went down beside it on Shelf I under Pipes, a stand of / G, two octaves low / Greenwich, because the first mayor, by the shelf’s rule, is the town where the sound rang. The builder wrote a line under the card, in his hand, and I copied it word for word, because a record in the system is not a record until it is exact, and the system only works if no one tidies it:

John Newton published in 1779, in a cool place to treat the line as the meeting point rather than the destination.

My reading, entered for what it is worth: the cool place is this cold room at three degrees, where the organ plays first and the pipes kept company with it for three evenings; the line is the tune’s line, “New Britain,” held open by the bag the way the hymn is held open by the pins — kept warm, kept continuous, kept arriving. A line so kept is a meeting point: it is where the organ and the pipes met, where the two shelves of the bench meet, where the front of the collection meets the reader, and it is not a destination, because a continuous note has no ending to walk to, and a filing shelf is only a place people pass through. I have kept the builder’s line exactly as written, and I will not tidy it either. The curators of the Webstuffs placard for Mark of the Succubus collect precisely this kind of line, and they will want it, and it is carded for them already, with the drone reading of ninety-eight and a quarter in the margin. The bag is down, the drones are in their case, the bell at the foot of the log is up, and the note — if a room at three degrees can be said to hold a note — is still in it, the way it has been all winter.
