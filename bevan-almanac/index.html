The Weather Almanac · Station No. 1948 · Ebbw Vale, Wales · November 1897 – July 1960

Aneurin Bevan

A weather entry for the Welsh Labour Party politician, kept at the station in the Black Mountain valley where the name was first elected, Minister of Health, spearhead of the National Health Service, and the wider builder of the British welfare state.

Station 1948 · Ebbw Vale, the reckoning

The station is a valley station, set in the Black Mountain coalfield of the South Wales Valleys, and the almanac keeps this entry against one name: Aneurin Bevan, known through the record as “Nye”, born 15 November 1897 in the house where the family kept its one room with a fire, and dead 6 July 1960 at the age of sixty-two, in London, the weather here rain that did not stop. Before he came to the House, the log says, the name had been kept in another climate: the miners’ union politics of the South Wales collieries, in which he was a leading figure through the 1926 general strike, and it was from that record, and not from the county vote, that the name stood and was first elected MP for Ebbw Vale in 1929. The parliamentary years that followed carried the name into the front position of the Labour left, into the Fabian Society, whose Gray’s Inn Road room is the almanac’s indoor observation point for the whole entry, and thence to the Ministry of Health in Clement Attlee’s government, where it did the work the entry is best known for. The station keeps both readings, the forecast of the man and the ground truth of the valley, under the one name, because that is the whole weather of the piece.

The tunnel front, in full

The log gives the wartime criticism its own plate, because it was the front that made the name: Bevan’s criticisms of Winston Churchill and his government during the Second World War, made from his Parliamentary platform, and influential in the record in a way the almanac does not soften. The central reading is the tunnel speech of 1940: the government’s shelters for the cities were, in the name’s words as the log kept them, a shelter for the Government and not for the people, built to be looked at and not to be stood in. The barometer of the House of Commons for that night is entered at its lowest in the record, and the log observes, in its only meteorological line for the war years, that a forecast repeated often enough becomes a front of its own. The brother station of the almanac, the one that keeps a day, not a name, is entered at Today — Weather Almanac Entry, Station No. 1093, Chicago, where the same rule is worked against a song: the forecast and the ground truth kept together, because the two are the weather. And the whole Conscience Register shelf on which this station keeps itself stands at the front page of the book.

Of the same shelf

The station keeps its shelf with the two neighbouring pages of the register’s own house. The travelling brochure of the Fabian playwright, which this entry shares the Gray’s Inn Road bench with, stands at The Shaw Line, A Visitor’s Brochure, Route No. 3, where the Fabian Society stops the Line at the same platform as the plays, and where the register’s line of support of 1916 is kept, word for word, as the almanac keeps its own record lines. And the small-ads column of the Conscience Register Office, kept against the name of the philosopher who died in the same country as the name of this entry — at Penrhyndeuleu, in Wales — stands at Small Ads & Notice Board, Column No. XXII, whose deceased ad, 2 February 1970, the station entered in its own log against the day, with rain.

Correspondence at the kitchen door

Readers write to the station the way they write to any almanac: with their own weather, their own days that would not be what the forecast said they were. The letters come in through the kitchen door, and the clerk screens every one before it reaches the editor — a bad forecast, a false bright day, a reading that will not sit down to its year. The kitchen-door ledger for the year closes with one line the editor refuses to paraphrase, and it stands here word for word, as the clerk set it down, capital and all:

“After the dishes from the Peruvian coast last voyage—and, foolish as I was, I shouldn’t like that!” “Oh, you can’t take less,” said the Gryphon.

— kitchen-door ledger, closing entry, set down by the clerk from the reader’s letter, which arrived with the salt still on it.

The station reads the line as it was set down: a reader comes back from a voyage, the dishes of the Peruvian coast are on the table, and the reader, foolish as he was, does not like them — and the Gryphon, who is the almanac’s own clerk in the matter, answers that one cannot take less. Which is the whole of the entry, and the station says so plainly: the Service was opened as a free service from cradle to grave, and the drought of 1951 was the name of this entry refusing the contribution, in the speech the record holds, on the ground that the thing was not for bargaining. What the forecast gives, it gives to all, and not less to any. The Gryphon’s line is the almanac’s rule in one sentence: the dishes are what they are, the voyage is over, and one does not take less. The station closes the entry here, and leaves the dishes as they are.
