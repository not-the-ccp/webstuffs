Midtown Madness

A racing game of 1999, raised in Chicago, who never once stayed on the track — 1999 to the day the arcade lights went out.

FREE-ROAM FOREVER■ CHICAGO, ILLINOIS — AN OPEN WORLD■ DEMO RELEASED AUGUST 1999■ WON CARS: ALL OF THEM, ONE RACE AT A TIME■ EIGHT OUT■

Chicago. — Midtown Madness, also known as Midtown Madness: Chicago Edition, a racing game developed by Angel Studios and published by Microsoft for Microsoft Windows, has died. It was born in 1999; its demo version was released in August 1999, and the full game followed in November of that year, and by its death it had held, in the memory of its players, the streets of a whole city.

The deceased was unlike its kind. Where the racing games of its generation held their players to an oval, a circuit, a closed ribbon of tarmac, Midtown Madness opened the gates and gave them Chicago — a free-roaming, open world recreation of the city, with its avenues and its alleyways and its long wind down Michigan Avenue. The setting was said to provide “an unprecedented degree of freedom to drive around in a virtual city,” and the deceased lived up to the reputation. The object of its life was simple: win the street races, take the money, buy the next car, and win again with it. It did not need a reason to drive. It drove because the city was there.

Survivors & Lineage

It is survived by two children. The elder, Midtown Madness 2, was released in September 2000 and inherited the habit of leaving the track — the freer, stranger, better child, the one who set up shop in a city of its own and never looked back. The younger, Midtown Madness 3, was released in June 2003 for the Xbox and took the family name into a new console, a new decade, and a new kind of city at dusk. Both outlived the first, which is how it happens. It is also survived by the studio floor at Angel Studios, by the Microsoft publishing office that backed the 1999 run, and by a long line of players who first met it on the demo in August 1999 and never quite let the car come to rest.

Character

In its nature the deceased was generous in the way of good streets: it asked only that you press the accelerator. Players could explore the city using every road the map remembered — shortcuts through the industrial flats, the long straightaways, the roundabout that swallowed a whole morning — and each race won added a new car to a growing garage. The obituary committee has been told of one player, name withheld, who owned by his last session every vehicle the game offered, and who still drove the first one, the one won first, out of habit and affection. The game's police, famously, were not a wall but a weather: a level of heat that rose with your speed and fell with your patience, a pressure that made the whole city feel like it was watching.

The City It Gave Us

What the deceased leaves behind is not a track but a city — an address. Chicago, in it, was a place you could get lost in on purpose, and the getting lost was the point. The committee has preserved the map in the archive: the lakefront, the loops, the elevated rail that runs on schedule, the intersections that ring in your memory like a bell you once lived next to. It will be kept open.

Final Dispatch

At the arcade room where the last cabinet stood, the office manager copied down the last line to appear on the screen before the lights were killed, and it was not a score and not a name. He has filed the key, and filed the sentence with it, word for word:

“Latin-9 Outro 3:33 Eight out, eight characters in Norwegian film productions between.”

Read the way the office manager read it, it is a last lap: an outro, a timecode at 3:33, and a count of the passengers getting out at the terminal — eight out, eight in — before the doors close. The committee has chosen to believe it meant exactly that.
