The Elsewhere Almanac · Guidebook Series · No. 12 · Revised for the year 2000

Elsewhere

Twelve places, twelve months, one departure each — a traveller’s guide to Nikolaus Geyrhalter’s twelve-episode film

In the year 2000 Nikolaus Geyrhalter and his crews travelled to one place each month, twelve places in all, looking for ground the millennium hysteria had not yet reached. The result was Elsewhere (2001), an Austrian documentary in twelve episodes, and this guidebook is written for the traveller who wants to follow the route — Ekeschi in the Aïr of Niger, Karlgasniemi in Sápmi, Finland, Ombivango in Kunene, Namibia, Dumbol Territory in Western New Guinea, Indonesia, Siorapaluk at Thule, Greenland, Manmoyi in Arnhem Land, Australia, Umla in Ladakh, India, Kantek ko jawun in Siberia, Russia, Zhongshi [忠实] in Yunnan, China, Tharros in Sardinia, Italy, Gitlaxt’aamiks in British Columbia, Canada, and Falalop in Woleai, Micronesia — twelve episodes, twelve departures, and twelve different versions of what it means to arrive somewhere late.

The calendar was the timetable: one month, one location, one film crew. Each episode runs about as long as a slow train between towns, and each one leaves at a different season — January in the Finnish fells, March in the Namibian dry, June at the edge of the Greenland ice. This guide keeps the same order, stop by stop, with what to see, when to go, and how much time to allow. It also keeps the sentence that turned up in the margin of the field notebook, written in a hand none of the crews claimed:

“The walk closes where it makes room for latecomers in the thread where a carpenter’s pencil marks and its members take their tea downstairs in the station logger.”

The logger is the logbook of the last halt on the line, where the walk closes and a place is still kept for latecomers. Its members take tea downstairs in the station room while a carpenter’s pencil keeps the thread of the route marked on the wall — a traveller’s instruction, and as good a reason as any to follow it.

The route, stop by stop

01

Ekeschi — Aïr, Niger

Go between November and February, when the desert wind drops and the date palms cast long shadows over the Aïr massif. Ekeschi is a valley stop: wells, tuareg camps, and the Ténéré at the edge of the walk. Allow two days, and carry water for three.

02

Karlgasniemi — Sápmi, Finland

January is the month here: reindeer on the frozen lakes, aurora above the trees, and days that last four hours of blue twilight. The crew arrived by snowmobile; you may arrive by ski or not at all. Pack for −20°C and leave space in your pack for silence.

03

Ombivango — Kunene, Namibia

March brings the end of the rains to Kunene: desert elephants along the dry riverbeds, kopjes the colour of burnt ochre, and night skies without a streetlamp within four hundred kilometres. Visit the waterhole at dawn; the walk makes room for latecomers only if they are quiet.

04

Dumbol Territory — Western New Guinea, Indonesia

April in the highlands: rainforest, river crossings, and villages where the only timetable is the one the river keeps. Dumbol Territory rewards the slow traveller — five days minimum, a local guide required, and a notebook for names that do not appear on any map.

05

Siorapaluk — Thule, Greenland

June at the top of the map: sea ice breaking under the midnight sun, dog teams on the floes, and one of the northernmost communities on Earth. The film crew came south from the ice edge; most travellers arrive by boat from Qaanaaq. Do not schedule anything after three p.m. — the light will keep you out.

06

Manmoyi — Arnhem Land, Australia

July is monsoon country: sandstone gorges, rock art under overhangs, and the smell of wet earth after the afternoon storm. Manmoyi asks for permission before entry, a week of your time, and the willingness to walk where the carpet snakes do. The carpenter’s pencil marks this one in red.

07

Umla — Ladakh, India

August at nearly four thousand metres: barley turning gold, high passes still open, and monasteries that ring their bells before the sun clears the ridge. Umla is thin air and clear light; allow two days to acclimatise, and drink more tea than you think you need.

08

Kantek ko jawun — Siberia, Russia

September turns the taiga amber: larch forests, river bends, and winters that will eventually reach forty below. The September crew found the first frost already waiting. Bring wool, a thermos, and patience for the slow train east.

09

Zhongshi [忠实] — Yunnan, China

October in the tea country: mist over terraced hills, pu’er cakes in the market, and villages strung along the old horse roads. Zhongshi means faithfulness, and the place keeps it — pick your season carefully, because the mist arrives on schedule whether you are ready or not.

10

Tharros — Sardinia, Italy

November on the Sinis peninsula: Phoenician ruins beside the sea, columns standing where the harbour once was, and the Mediterranean changing colour with every hour. Tharros is the walk’s Mediterranean close — latecomers are still welcome, so long as they come on foot.

11

Gitlaxt’aamiks — British Columbia, Canada

December on the Nass River: totem poles in the village square, cedar canoes, and the winter salmon run. Gitlaxt’aamiks keeps its own time, counted in tides and feasts rather than hours. Arrive by road from Terrace, and stay long enough to learn which way the river runs. The province around it deserves a guidebook of its own: rainforest, inland desert, the Inside Passage, and a margin full of loose ends — kept at British Columbia — Traveller's Guide, where the Nass River is the first halt.

12

Falalop — Woleai, Micronesia

The year closes on an atoll: reef, lagoon, and outrigger canoes sailing where the charts run out. Falalop is twelve thousand kilometres from where the walk began, and the station logger’s last page is written there — members take their tea downstairs, the walk closes, and there is always room for one more latecomer on the mat.

Practical

When: follow the film’s calendar — one location per month, January through December 2000 — or compress the route into a single year of your own. How: the same way the crews did: one departure at a time, slowly, with notebooks rather than itineraries. Numbers: twelve episodes, twelve locations, one camera team, and exactly zero millennium celebrations en route.

Keep company on the way: The Book — Report, Class 7B marks its pages in red with Mr. Delgado, bound like these twelve episodes into a single volume; The Marble Limited keeps departures from Mott Haven Yard in marble time, the right frame of mind for a journey scheduled by the calendar; and Knight Island — Field Notes watches cordgrass, rails, and the marsh edge from three feet above the water at its highest. And The Vermilion Seal — Newsletter No. 17 of the Coopers’ Lane Han Society sends its eleven members out from the shed behind the bakery to the October stop at Zhongshi, arriving on schedule, which is to say late, and taking their tea downstairs in the station room.
