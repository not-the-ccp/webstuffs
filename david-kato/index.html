David Kato

Also Kisule · the teacher · the advocate · the man named in the magazine — David Kato Kisule, of Kampala, c. 1964 – 26 January 2011

To dream of David Kato is to dream of a classroom that has not finished a lesson.

The sleeper finds himself in a low building in Kampala, near Ntinda, where the door is painted a yellow gone silver with the weather and the windows are open on a red-earth lane. In the classroom there is a blackboard of chalk dust, a stack of exercise books, and at the head of the room a man of forty-six who writes in two columns, one in English and one in a careful, smaller hand, and who corrects the exercise books after dark when the other teachers have gone. The sleeper knows him. That is the first rule of this entry: in the dreams filed under K, the sleeper always knows him, and the knowing comes before the name, the way one knows a voice in a hall before one knows it is one’s own.

David Kato Kisule was a teacher and an advocate, and in the world that is waked the two offices were the same office: he was the advocacy officer of Sexual Minorities Uganda, SMUG, the office of the small table in the Ntinda house, and he is called the father of Uganda’s gay rights movement, and he is called, in the papers of the woken world, “Uganda’s first openly gay man.” In the dream the man does not say either of these things. He says only that the lesson is not finished, and that names are a matter of record.

The magazine of names

In the dream there is a magazine lying open on a desk, and on its page a photograph and a name set in print, and beneath the name a sentence in the voice of the crowd: execute him. The sleeper recognises the magazine at once, though he has never seen it awake, which is the second rule of this entry: the magazine of names is known in sleep the way the chalk is known, by the sound it makes. The sleeper understands that the printed name is a door taken off its hinges; that a name in a magazine is a name that has left the register and gone to stand in the lane by itself, and that this is how a name gets hurt — not in the register, where the ink is slow and the clerk is careful, but in the lane, where the print is loud.

In the waked world the magazine was called Rolling Stone, a Ugandan magazine, and it did the thing the dream describes: it printed his name and his photograph, identified him as gay, and called for his execution. David Kato took the magazine to court, and the court gave him the decision in his favour. The decision came in March of 2011, on the docket, in the ordinary slow way of records; the sleeper of this entry should be told what the dream keeps in the margin and the docket does not, which is that the man to whom the decision was given had not been standing at his own table for a full month before the ink was set. On the night of the dream, which is always the night of 26 January 2011, the magazine lies open on the desk, and the man at the head of the room has gone, and the chalk is lying in the tray in the position he left it in.

The ledger line, and its reading

The Keeper of the East Africa Station enters every dream under K with a closing line, in his own hand, and the line for Entry No. 47 reads, word for word, from the ledger:

“His kicking from hand this season: seventy-four, of which the train waits.”

The line is explained in the margin, in the manner of this dictionary. Kicking from hand is the dreamer’s name for the throw of the chalk — the small flick a teacher gives the chalk over the shoulder so that it strikes the blackboard and leaves a mark; in the school ledger it is not called kicking at all, but in sleep every office has its slang, and the chalk is kicked. This season is the term that ended on the night of 26 January, and the season’s count in the dream is seventy-four: the number of names on the class list, though there are only sixty desks in the room, and the extra fourteen names are the ones he kept in a paper folder in the small table of SMUG, the ones he carried, that is, from the lane back into the register. And the train waits is the reading of the dream itself, which the Keeper writes last: of the seventy-four names on the list, the train — the morning line out of Kampala, the one that runs along the lake at the hour the class is dismissed — is waiting for the sleeper’s own name among them. The sleeper who reports this dream is asked, and the answer is always the same: he does not know if his name is on the list, but he knows the train, and he knows it has not left the platform, and a train that waits is a record that is still open. That is all the entry rules. The chalk in the tray is in the position the man left it in, and the lesson is not finished.

Crossings

To dream of David Kato with a bell in the room is to dream of the hour of dismissal; the bell of this station has kept its quarter past the usual strike ever since the night of 26 January, and the sleeper who hears it four times is to be understood as a sleeper who has finished a term and is waiting for the term book to be signed.

To dream of the man named in the magazine in the company of the line from Clio is to dream of a service that runs on a fixed day and asks before it opens its door: the Clio & Lakes Line, which the station at Kampala corresponded with in its first years and whose rule of the road — asking first, and thanking the refusal — the sleeper of this entry will find set down service by service in the Official Timetable & Conductor’s Commentary of the Clio & Lakes Line, No. 1, published annually on the twenty-first of January, five days before the night this entry keeps. The two lines are not the same line; the one carries passengers toward one another, and the one that waits at the Kampala platform carries names toward the register, but the Keeper has ruled that a timetable and a class list are both forms of the same promise: that the departure is fixed, and that no one is put on the service who has not asked.

To dream of the name itself, of Kato, and to find that the name is not a person’s in the dream but a thing bestowed at the door, is to dream of the given name, and the dream belongs to the hall of nine doors, which keeps its bearers and its spellings and its last pencilled lines; the hall’s full entry stands at Given Name — A Dream Dictionary Entry, and it will admit the sleeper of this entry through whichever door stands ajar, which for one named Kato has, the Keeper notes, stood ajar ever since the ink of the docket was set.

Where the sleeper would read the entries of this shelf together, the whole miscellany gathers at the front page.
