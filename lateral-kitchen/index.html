the hall’s dining column · no. 9 14 march 2006 · seoul

The Lateral

A seven-seat counter on the brick street, near the hall · one seating at 19:30 · the tasting runs 71,800 won, or one correct spelling

Seven of nine doors the tenth is admitted, once, on insistence, at the second introduction

To dine at The Lateral is to eat with the air going around. A lateral is a consonant in which the airstream proceeds along one or both of the sides of the tongue, but is blocked by the tongue from going through the middle of the mouth, and the kitchen has made a floor plan of the fact. The seven seats face a low stone wall, and the pass sits at the far end of a corridor that bends twice, so that every dish reaches the table having gone around the room, never straight through it. The house keeps no median room, and is proud of the fact: a median consonant is one in which the airstream flows through the centre of the mouth, and the proprietor is reported to have told a guest who asked for a straight corridor that the air was already paid for by the side. It is, the guest later allowed, the only honest answer in the building.

The head of the kitchen is a Mrs. Park of the American south who answers to Larry, and the first course is named for the sound she makes in every language she speaks — the English L, the L in Larry. The course itself is a small, soft, round bread baked on stone and served in halves, which is right, because for the most common laterals the tip of the tongue makes contact with the upper teeth, as in a dental consonant, or with the upper gum, as in an alveolar consonant, and the house wants the guest to know the difference on the tongue before the menu does.

Of the Menu, and the Places of Making

The menu is arranged by place of articulation, and it is long. There are many other possible places for laterals to be made, and the house makes them: the dental course, a thin crisp with a cold cream, for the tip against the upper teeth; the alveolar, the main, for the tip against the upper gum; the retroflex and the palatal, both quiet and both correct; and the velar and the uvular, the latter in a lidded pot, served on the third visit, when the guest has stopped being surprised and started being expected. The most common laterals are approximants, and they belong to the class of liquids, and the house does not let you forget the liquidity — the Larry bread, the cold cream, a broth so round it is poured at a slant. But the menu admits the fricatives and the affricates too. The fricative is a thin hot soup that hisses as it is ladled and hushes the table, so completely that once, at the second seating of a full house, the two boxers of the same name who sat at one end could be told apart, per the book, by the hands. The affricate is a small cold course that stops at the fork and then releases, which is exactly what it is supposed to do, and exactly why it is the course the hall writes home about.

The Double, Returned 1999

The dish to order, for any reader of the proclamation, is the double. The sound ɭ, the raised lateral, was missing from the register for the whole drafting period of 1995, and was last heard at a table of the Academy, refusing to be spelled; it was restored, in its own double form, in the revised tables, and here it is served as a single dish made two — a pale, slow course in two parts, the second part set down at a distance of exactly one arm’s length, and no closer. It is the whole difference between an rr and a plain r in the alphabet the hall sets its names in, and the Registry keeps the receipt. The ledger of the evening closes, in the Registry’s own hand, with the line: “The Registry thanks the two consonants it confuses with.” The kitchen was told. The kitchen, it is said, took the double from the menu for an hour, and then, quietly, put it back.

Of the House, and Its Rules

The regulars are the nine of the hall, and the house runs the hall’s rules, which is why this is not a restaurant so much as a door. The skater Han Dong-hoon comes on the brick street in his skates and takes them off at the threshold, the same rule as indoors at the hall; the wine is offered at a distance of one arm’s length, because Rule three is Rule three anywhere the palm is visible; and the director Hoon Lee has been photographed correcting this house’s own signage, where the doubled L was once set single — the house laughed, the correction was the right one, and the board was re-set the next Saturday in the same handwriting as every other sign in the quarter. The quails arrive from the provisioner’s column, trussed like Snipe, with the vinegar; the marrows are boiled, not bowed. The eldest leaves first at 22:30, and the guest holds the door with the left hand, and does not mention that boxers do not open doors.

A house that makes every sound go around the room, and still gets every sound to the table, is worth the round. Seven of nine doors; the tenth is admitted once, on insistence, at the second introduction, and only in the tenth form. The whole shelf of the column stands on the Webstuffs front page; the house’s notices, its missing lateral, and its quails are posted weekly in The Roman Post, the hall’s classified sheet under Proclamation No. 2000-8; the rules of the house, bow by bow, stand in Chapter XI of the Book of Etiquette, of the Bearing Dong-hoon; and the sheet for the neighbouring sound, the nasal sealed in mouth, nose, and bring, keeps its own file, SDS-NC-01, at Nasal Consonant — Safety Data Sheet, issued from the same Registry.
