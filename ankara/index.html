A Book of Etiquette for the Keeping of Capitals

Seventh impression · printed for the Keepers of Capitals · price ninepence, carriage paid from Ancyra

Chapter IX
Of the Capital in the Centre, and the Giving Over of a Name

“Greet the name first, in the order it was laid down, and the city will keep it.”

I. Of the City

Ankara is the capital city of Turkey. It stands in the central part of Anatolia, which is to say it keeps its seat at the middle of the table, and the manners of a middle seat are the hardest manners of all, because every other seat must pass the salt through it. The city carries over five million three hundred thousand residents in its urban center, and six million across the whole of Ankara Province. It is the second-largest city of the country by population, after Istanbul, and this book holds no rule that a second capital should apologize to its first; the sea may keep the ports, but the centre keeps the minutes. The city has been called Ankara, and it has been called, before that, Ancyra and Angora, and it answers properly to all three, the way a well-kept name answers to the hand that first wrote it.

II. Of the Galatian Seat

Of all the names the city has been given, one is owed a special courtesy, because it was spoken by a people this shelf keeps a card for. Ankara served as the capital of the ancient Celtic state of Galatia, from 280 to 64 BC, and later of the Roman province of the same name, from 25 BC to the seventh century. It is the seat where the Celtic front, entered on Entry No. 31 of the Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, was oldest before it was slowest: the Galatians took a dialect with them as far as Anatolia, and left the capital there, as one leaves a hat on a chair, to be found by every later people who took the room. The compiler of that almanac card enters the city, and its exchange, as the far end of the front. If you would hear what the dialect sounds like on the tape, the six tracks are logged in the liner notes of Lagan Records LL-04, The Northwestern Fringe, and the Galatian year sits there, first century AD, in the November row of the gauge ledger, where the barometer has held and refused to be moved.

III. Of the Exchange

In Ancyra there has been, in every century that kept a ledger, a small exchange of grain on the plain, where the consignment is weighed against a token and the quality is ruled by the clerk in a single line, read aloud once, and not altered after the weighing. The rule of the line is that it must name the people, the token, and the quality, in that order, and that no adjective is to be added after the bell. The clerk’s line of the sitting in question, entered word for word from the exchange book, runs:

“The exchange, for the space of a single people, and the Causeway Coast, Welsh at Caernarfon on the record, and it is considered to be in good _dry_ flour.”

It is read, as all such lines are read, without comment. For the benefit of the unweighed, the book decodes it thus: the consignment was struck for the space of a single people, the Galatians, one of the peoples on the almanac card; the token against which it was priced was marked with the towns that still answer to Proto-Celtic — the Causeway Coast, and the Welsh at Caernarfon on the harbour wall, track A1 of the record; and the quality, ruled at the weighing and held, is that it is considered to be in good dry flour. Flour that has been judged good and dry is never to be re-judged at a later table, and this is the whole of the exchange’s etiquette.

IV. Of the Goat and the Wool

By the name Angora the city lent its courtesy again, to a goat of the same seat, whose long wool was traded out of the same gates as the grain. A capital that lends its name to a fibre does so under one rule, which the book states plainly: the name may travel, but the receipt stays in the city. From the goat it passed to the rug and to the coat, and every angora carpet in the world is, by the receipt, an Ankara carpet. The shelf on which this book is kept knows the custom from the other side: the Samson & Goliath linen towel given for eleven minutes at Quimper’s covered market, the red shirt folded in the spring of 2014 and draped over the tape reel as a windscreen, both owed their proper reward in the small ads and both, by the receipt, still kept at BT1 4AB. A city and a cloth keep the same table manners: give the name over freely, keep the receipt, and do not launder the evidence.

V. Of the Layers

Under the exchange and under the goat, the city keeps ten layers in the ground, and the rule of the centre seat is that they are greeted in the order they were laid down, beginning at the bottom, as one greets a name. The Hattian, the Hittite, the Lydian and the Phrygian keep the lower shelf; the Galatian, the Greek and the Persian keep the middle; and the Roman, the Byzantine and the Ottoman keep the top, each of them leaving its sites where it stood and not sweeping the room for the next. The Ottomans, the last of the named, made the city the capital of the Anatolia Eyalet from 1393 to the late fifteenth century, which is to say they kept the seat through the long dinner and did not stand up at the end. Above the layers, the castle on the rock keeps the hour, and the plain keeps the weather, and the centre keeps the minutes.

VI. Of the Modern Seat, and the Return

A capital is judged last by the number it is made to carry, and Ankara carries its number without complaint: five and a half million at the table, six million in the province, second in the country and first in the centre. The book keeps one last rule for such a city, which is that a capital is not finished until every almanac in the shelf has a card for it, and every book of etiquette has a chapter; both are now entered, the card at the almanac’s Entry No. 31 and this chapter at Table 9. For the comparison of capitals, and the manners of a pink one in the east, the shelf keeps Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City, and for the room all of these papers are kept in, the return is made to the shelf itself.
