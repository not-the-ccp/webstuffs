Hadith ḥadīth حديث

The Arabic word for a report or an account of an event; the Islamic oral tradition of anecdotes containing the purported words, actions, and silent approvals of the Islamic prophet Muhammad or his immediate circle — the companions in Sunni Islam, the Ahl al-Bayt in the Shia. Plural: aḥādīth or aḥādīth as the collectors wrote it; the grammar is the collectors' problem, not the record's.

§1 The Word and the Report

The report, as the collectors separated it, has a body and a chain. The matn is the body — the saying, the action, the silence read as approval. The isnād is the chain of names that carried it, one throat to the next, from the first hearer back to the source. A hadith is therefore not a sentence so much as a sentence with a genealogy stapled to its spine. The word itself belongs to the larger Arabic of the Comparative Lexicon Manual, whose Chapter 12 treats ḥadīth among the common nouns of the Central Semitic languages; the manual's ledger of cognates enters it beside its neighbours without comment, the way a harbour ledger enters a boat.

At first the reports circulated in the early post-prophetic discussions without formal attribution — told in the caravanserais and the congregational gatherings, the way a kitchen's rule is told at a table where everyone already knows it. That unattributed stage is the stage the record keeps best, because no one at the table would write anything down. The writing came later, and the writing changed the object. A report told by a chain of names can be checked against the chain; a report told by the room cannot. Both stages survive in the collections, and both stages survive in a courtroom minute, which is the reader's concern in this entry, because the reader has just come from one.

§2 The Isnād: A Chain of Reporters

The isnād reads forward in time and is verified backward. The first link is a companion who claims to have heard or seen; each following link is a man or woman who heard the previous link and can be dated, located, and found trustworthy or not. The collectors grew to treat a single broken link — a narrator who died before the next one was born — as a cut cable, and they marked such reports accordingly rather than quietly stitching them back together.

Consider the chain as this entry's minute-keeper would keep it, one name per frame, at the ledger's usual speed of sixteen frames per second — the speed at which the Naga Listeners' Club minutes the Yerevan Municipal Court, and the speed at which a report can still be heard whole:

  1. the Sourcewas heard by
  2. a Companion of the first gatheringwho told
  3. a reporter of the second generation, of the city of the brick kilnswho was heard by
  4. a teacher of the law, who wrote nothing and remembered everythingand whose students set down
  5. the collector, who travelled four directions with a notebook and a rule: two trustworthy reporters, or the report is not entered

Five links, one per line, and each line answerable for the one below it. It is the same discipline a verbatim record keeps with its speakers and its witnesses, only the court's chain ends at the bench and the collector's chain ends at a book that will outlast the bench.

§3 Compilation, 7th–10th Centuries

Between the 7th and the 10th centuries the reports were systematically compiled by the Islamic scholars who took the name muhaddith — a man who deals in reports, and in the trade's discipline. They organized the material first according to the primary authority to whom it was attributed, one opening book per companion, and subsequently by subject matter: the books of faith, of prayer, of alms, of fasting, of the pilgrimage, of sales. In the Kufa and Basra schools of the early centuries, the reporters worked in open gatherings where a chain would be recited and the room would correct it, link by link, the way a chorus corrects itself mid-verse.

The work culminated in the canonical collections: the Muwaṭṭaʿ of Mālik ibn Anas, gathered in the 8th century and the oldest of the great books; the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī (d. 870), who is counted to have sifted, it is said, some 600,000 reports down to the 7,275 he printed in his book, repetitions and all; and the Ṣaḥīḥ of Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj, whose book holds 7,563 in his own numbering. Six collections came to stand as the received core, and the trade kept grading everything else around them, the way a mill keeps grading fleece against a single record fleece weighed in the open.

§4 Grading: Ṣaḥīḥ, Ḥasan, Ḍaʿīf

Ṣaḥīḥ
The sound report: an unbroken chain, each link upright in memory and conduct, the matn free of hidden defect and of known contradiction. The collectors' highest grade, and the one a court would call the witness under oath who still agrees with the other witnesses.
Ḥasan
The good report: the chain holds, but a link is a shade softer in memory or conduct. Admissible, noted, and used with the grade written in the margin.
Ḍaʿīf
The weak report: a broken chain, a suspicious link, or a body at odds with its genealogy. Not thrown out — entered, graded, and shelved with the weakness stated — the way a record enters an unparseable notice rather than pretending it is not pinned to the door.

The method is the point, not the shelf. A hadith is never left floating: it is either carried by a named chain or marked as something the chain could not carry. The silence is documented. Even a silent approval of the prophet counts, in the tradition, as an event with a body and a chain — the man who saw him not stopping the practice, and who said so.

§5 Note from the Verbatim Record: Table 9, One Unparseable Line

The entry would be incomplete without the reader's actual point of departure. In the Yerevan Municipal Court, in Case No. 1726 of 2024, Aghajan v. Petrosyan, heard on a Friday morning before the Hon. Siranush Davtyan, the whole law before the bench was a rule of transmission: treat others as you would be treated, and do not treat them as you would not be treated. The plaintiff's house rule was pinned to the menu board at Table 9 of "The Pink Table" for eleven years, and the witness Anoush Grigoryan read it into the record word for word — Eat khash on Friday mornings — cow's feet simmered all night, eaten with garlic and dried lavash while the city is still cool — which is to say the court treated a kitchen card as a matn, the board as a chain, and the eleven years of the pin as the isnād.

The same record keeps one line it could not parse, entered in the margin of the Friday session and read in, uncorrected, by the clerk. It stands in the docket as follows, and this entry carries it over as it stands: "Was the khash as the side-ladder was being rigged from aloft, and while hot, break it into the record word for word." No one at the bench has reconstructed the first half — no side-ladder is on file, and the pot does not rig itself from aloft — and the court, in the manner of the graders, did not throw the line out for being unparseable; it marked it, and it is in the docket with its weakness stated. The second half, for its part, is a rule the record actually kept: while the khash was hot, break it into the record word for word. That is the collectors' rule in the kitchen's own language — enter it while it is hot, with the names attached, however strange the hour. The defendant was fined in the amount of one khash and ordered back to Table 9 on Friday 21 March, and the docket notes he returned at 6:35 and asked for the lavash by the kilo; the ledger closed, as ledgers do, at sixteen frames per second.

A later bench borrowed the first half of this entry’s own sentence — The work culminated in the canonical collections — and changed only the ending. In the examiner’s margin of Patent Application No. GY–66/07, the amide-linked thread of the Goygol yard, the line stands as "The work culminated in the oak-bark vat, and ends on the most violent knockings?": culminated borrowed from the collectors, the vat from the district, the knockings unexplained — entered, graded, and shelved in this entry’s usual manner, with the weakness stated.

The sister court across the border kept the same discipline at a different gate. In Case No. 44-41 of 2025 at the Goygol District Court, a notice pinned to the shed door since the winter of 2021 carried a second half — and whenever the shed gives frost and snow are seldom remarkable as faithful essays — that no one has been able to parse, and both courts behaved identically: the unparseable half was entered, not enforced, and the hours were kept. The Goygol bench has since set its fine down in a table game, The Goygol Tannery, Player’s Manual, Third Edition, where the ledger’s fixed form of a fine is carried over word for word, entered in ink and in triplicate, never paraphrased — the same rule as the unparseable half of the notice: the matn stands as entered, and the chain of ledgers stands as written. The city geography the first case walks — Republic Square to the brandy house, the Vernissage yard, the card on the board — is mapped in its own place in Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City, and the marshrutka drivers who received the garlic plate that 21 March are in that guidebook's morning routes.

Entered, graded, and shelved at the usual speed. The chain stands as given; the first link, as in all such records, was heard in a room and is known to no one's notebook except by what the next link said. The same discipline is kept on the shelf beside the court's in the liner notes of The Crown on the Forehead (ARL-1037), Atlantic Reel's western-Atlantic album, where one unparseable line from the field log — the names secretary's habit of carrying a name in its two spellings — is entered word for word, with its weakness stated, and the tape holds.
