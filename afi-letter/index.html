Letter to the Editor

The American Film Institute

Los Angeles · 14 October 1979

S·W
Los Angeles

Dear Editor,

I write to defend an institution that does its best work in the dark. The American Film Institute was established in 1967, in the wake of the National Endowment for the Arts, with George Stevens Jr. as its founding director, and it has spent more than half a century rescuing the motion picture heritage of the United States from nitrate decay, studio indifference, and simple forgetfulness. Its campus at 2021 North Western Avenue in Los Angeles houses the AFI Conservatory, and its Catalog of Feature Films is the closest thing this art form has to a census.

I was reminded of this the other evening while watching a 1914 Keystone short: Charlie Chaplin, twenty-four years old, reducing a bookstore to kindling with a hatchet in Mabel's Married Life. Chaplin went on to co-found United Artists in 1919 with Mary Pickford, Douglas Fairbanks and D. W. Griffith, so that artists could keep control of their own pictures. The AFI Catalog preserves those early shorts frame by frame, and it records that Chaplin's first speaking picture, The Great Dictator, opened in 1940. The Institute's Life Achievement Award began with John Ford in 1973; its festival has run since the 1980s; and the whole enterprise is supported by private funding and public membership fees, which is to say by people who still believe that films are worth keeping.

I drafted this letter at a booth in the Arcadia on Western Avenue, where the coffee tastes of chicory and the check arrives on a cracked saucer. The restaurant shares its name, its creator's hometown, and a vintage NCR cash drawer that chimes when it is liable to become soft. While I waited for the bill, a couple at the next table argued whether City Lights or The Gold Rush was the finer picture — a debate the Institute has archived in spirit, if not in its ledgers.

My plea is modest: become a member, attend a screening, nominate a film for the Catalog, take a child to a silent picture. The heritage the Institute honors is made of celluloid and membership cards alike, and both are perishable.

Yours sincerely,
Silas Wren
Los Angeles, California
