16 March — 09:12, platform 42nd, under the colonnade of the building

Platform 42nd Street — booking, and the slip

The service begins before it begins, at the platform. What the line requires of a passenger is a completed slip of consent, signed by both parties to the ride and renewed every time the doors open; a service without a slip does not leave the platform, and has never, in the running of it, left the platform. The ticket clerk at 42nd Street keeps his tickets on the old form — the Form 3B enrolment slip of the Ochs Avenue Day School two doors over, No. 4 189 6, the very number on the term report that the Form keeps in its manila envelope. He came to the hands of his stock of the enrolment slip. The last of the spring run, it came the way a harbour comes to its ships: slowly, then all at once, and he passed the season's supply to the counter, where it is torn off, one per boarding, and the remainder is filed in the envelope with the rest. I do not know what the school will say about its forms doing fare duty on the avenue; I have asked, and the reply, in red ink, was: the ledger will keep you; you need only keep the ledger.

The slip is not a formality. A signature that cannot be taken away is not a signature, and the clerk will not stamp a slip she has not read. Consent, on this line, is a through-ticket: it runs the whole route, and the passenger may alight at any station for any reason, with no penalty, no explanation, and no slow service.

And the age at which the hand that signs a slip is a hand the register counts is itself a scheduled service, a companion line running Ochs Avenue to Platform 18 on the same 41 minutes: the Eighteen Local, H.M. No. 14, where the close-in-age branch keeps its own small table and the inspector's red ink explains, station by station, why the platform is fixed by the register and not by the timetable.

17 March — 11:48, 34th Street, the carriage before the platform

Platform 34th Street — preparation, and what the line does not require

Passengers are asked not to arrive scrubbed. The line does not require internal washing, and the clerk will refuse a slip that smells of the enema cabinet; the rectum is self-cleaning in the ordinary course, and what the service actually requires is patience, time, and a body that has eaten a normal lunch. What the line does not provide, and here the timetable part ends and the anatomy part begins, is its own lubrication: the passage is dry by design, as a station is by design not painted on the inside. Carriage two therefore carries a trolley of lubricant, water-based, in quantity, and the conductor's standing order — Note 2, red ink — is that the service shall not move until the carriage has been oiled. Latex is issued at 34th Street 3 because the passage is not what the rest of the body is: the lining is thinner than the skin of the hand, it tears the way wet paper tears, and an infection that the body shrugs off elsewhere does not shrink, here, at all. The barrier is not an optional carriage. It is the service.

18 March — 16:04, between 23rd and Houston, the slow stretch

The slow stretch — entry, and the running of the service

The slow stretch of the route is 23rd to Houston, and the service is slow there by design, not by fault. Entry on this line is the whole art of the line. The two rings of muscle at the platform do not open for the service; they relax for it, gradually, and in the time they ask for — which is longer than a first-time passenger expects and shorter than a nervous conductor fears. The conductor Mr. Delgado, who walks the carriage in red ink, marks this stretch on the timetable with four added minutes 5 and the note: the stop signal is a hand, raised, and the hand is always obeyed. The thrusting that follows — the service proper, the run between platforms, at a pace the receiving passenger sets and may change at will — is no more than a timetable: it is kept by the couple, checked at each station, and the terminus is never anywhere fixed in advance but wherever the slip is still signed.

The toys of the branch services are inspected at the terminus 4 and the inspection is a single question the inspector asks with his thumb: is the base flared? A toy without a flared base is a carriage without a caboose, and the line will not run one. Nothing else about the carriage is the inspector's business; the manila envelope, which the school keeps for its finished reports and the line borrows for its tickets, is the only container either institution has ever trusted to carry the record.

19 March — 00:09, 14th Street, after the last service

Terminus, 14th Street — aftercare, and the census

The terminus is an open platform: water, a seat, a while. Note 6, red ink, is the whole of the aftercare code: the last door is the first door; no passenger leaves the platform without being asked once, plainly, how the ride was, and the asking is not a question that requires an answer. Then the ledger closes, the slip is filed in the envelope, and the avenue runs to the next 41 minutes.
