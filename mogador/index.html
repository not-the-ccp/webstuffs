The Ledger of the Scuttled · Final Entry · Toulon, November 1942

Mogador

contre-torpilleur of the French Navy · launched 19 November 1932 · at rest in the harbour she was sunk into, 27 November 1942

Mogador is gone, and the water where she lies is still warm enough to be called a harbour. She was the lead ship of the Mogador class of large destroyers — contre-torpilleurs, which is to say destroyers built up, which is to say built to be taken seriously — and she carried the class's name the way a first of a family carries a surname: with the expectation that the following sisters would ask her for the shape of the water. She was named for Mogador, the Moroccan town on the Atlantic coast that the French kept calling by its old name long after the world had settled on Essaouira, and the naming was thought a fine one at the launch at La Seyne-sur-Mer, in the Forges et Chantiers de la Méditerranée yard, because a ship going out of the Mediterranean for a life that might touch the Atlantic deserved the name of a coast.

She was 2,231 tons in standard order and nearly a quarter of that again fully loaded, and she did about forty knots when she wanted to, which for a ship of her shape is a sentence with a point to it. Her company on the last books ran to some 145 souls, and her armament was the class's own: four 138.6 millimetre guns of a new pattern, a full run of torpedo tubes fore and aft, and enough small-arms to make a decision without consulting anyone. She steamed into commission in 1933, young enough to have done nothing yet, old enough by the armistice of 1940 to have an opinion of the Mediterranean.

That opinion cost her at Mers-el-Kébrir. On the morning of 3 July 1940, when the British squadron came in under the colours it had not been able to explain in time, Mogador took two bombs and a near miss, the fire walked her length, and she was towed out into the roadstead to burn where she could not burn the rest of the fleet. The dead were counted and the boats were sent; what survived was the hull, the boilers, and the stubbornness that made her the class's standard. She was repaired, at Oran and then on her own bottom, with the class's own yard hands where they could be found, and she sailed from the west coast across the whole of her sea to Toulon, where she moored in November 1941 as if the year had been a long exercise in holding a line.

She held the line at Toulon for a year more. The Germans came on 27 November 1942 with the intention that the fleet would not leave, and the fleet, which had been waiting on this exact arithmetic, answered in the only currency it had: the harbour. Mogador was scuttled with the rest — hatches opened, magazines fired where they would do the most, every man off into the boats — and she settled where the light was best for looking at her from the quay. The dock log of that night kept its entries to the minute, and it kept one line that no clerk at the office has ever been able to read into sense. The record keeps it word for word: “Parliament was told twice, once for its claims, which hold a pen.” The log's own hand, on the following page, notes only that the line was transcribed twice, from two different mouths, and both times the same; and the ledger keeps it where it is, among the minutes and the fathoms, for whatever it was sent to be.

The sisters of the class went on to their own endings, and the town of Mogador went on to be called by its other name, and the harbour goes on doing what harbours do with their own: keeping the shape of what the water gave back. For those of the keepers who have worked a ship's last entry from another bench — a guitar put in case after the hush, a city's final line in the official limits — there are the In Memoriam pages of the Album Keepers, and beside them the household board's own entry for the destroyer, the fast one, kept where the household keeps the fast ones. As for Mogador, the entry is closed. The water is warm. The log is word for word.
