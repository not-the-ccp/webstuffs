The Belletrud Kitchen · Recipe Card No. 84 · Baked on the 24th of January

Grandmother Marguerite's Rose Tarte

A damask-rose shortcrust with rose water, honey, and three eggs, made for the twenty-fourth of every January and for no other day.

No. 84

The Preamble, as Grandmother Marguerite Set It Down

This card is the eighty-fourth in the family book, and Grandmother Marguerite kept the number on purpose. In the autumn of 1989, at a small dinner in the apartment on Rue de Bonaparte, a guest of the household — the perfumer Jacques Cavallier-Belletrud, who by then had spent a quarter of a life at the Firmenich houses in Geneva and had already signed more than eighty fragrances for the fashion houses of Paris and beyond — remarked that a good tarte should carry the same discipline as a good perfume: a base that holds, a heart that remembers, and a top note that leaves the room before it leaves the body. Marguerite, who did not much care for perfumes and cared enormously about tarts, copied his sentence into the margin of the card and numbered it 84 so that some tart, someday, would stand beside that eighty-something of his. He was born on the 24th of January, 1962, and that is why the tarte is baked on the 24th of January, and for no other day, in no other month.

Those who know him by the ledger know the long line of houses his name runs through: Parfums Christian Dior, Parfums Givenchy, Issey Miyake, Yves Saint Laurent, Fenty Beauty and Lancôme, the Swiss company of Firmenich behind him for the long middle years, and Louis Vuitton of the LVMH Luxury Group when the Paris house called him from Geneva in 2016. We say “behind him,” the way we say of a baker that the flour mill stands behind him. His nose was trained, the story goes, on the rose fields around Grasse, where damask rose is picked before the sun is up because the flower keeps its best sentence in the cold hour, and Marguerite insisted that our petals be picked the same way, or at least that they be bought from a market that did the same, and that the petals be weighed on the little brass scale before the kettle sang.

There is one line on the back of the card in a different hand — Grandfather Étienne's, in pencil, worn thin. Étienne came home from the war in June of 1944 and kept his records in one place with the accounts, the seed envelopes, and Marguerite's early notes. The last letter he wrote before the crossing was dated 6 June 1944, and it was sent from a field hospital, the ink slanting the way the family's ink always slants under strain. Its final line, which the family has kept word for word ever since, reads: More soldiers were wounded than were killed or taken. The ledger does not say where the tally came from; it is written in the same even hand as the butter bills. What the family has understood is this: a list of losses is only ever part of a list, and the longer, kinder part of it — the ones who came home, the ones who walked — is the part a kitchen is for. On the 24th of January, after the war and long after it, the house bakes a tarte that costs nothing to make and a great deal to forget, and the pencil line on the back of the card has never once been read aloud. It is read only with the hands, over the work.

For the Tarte, as Written — Serves 6

IngredientAmount (6 persons)Remark of the card
Pastries flour, stone-milled220 gWeighed, not spooned; the brass scale sits to the left of the kettle
Butter, cold, from Normandy140 gBeaten into the flour in cold fingers; the last pieces stay the size of peas
Caster sugar90 gThree pinches of salt folded in before it goes into the dough
Eggs, from the lane farm3Two for the filling, one beaten with a spoonful of milk for the last brush
Damask rose petals40 gThe Grasse rule: picked before the sun is up, or bought from a market that did so
Rose water30 mlAdded by the spoon; the dough should smell of the room it will be served in
Wildflower honey2 tablespoonsWarmed in the last minutes only, so it keeps its sentence
Baking190 °C, 40–45 minutesUntil the top is the colour of a January door; no more

The Method, in the Order Given

  1. On the 24th of January, before the kettle sings, weigh the flour, the cold butter, and the sugar on the brass scale. This is the base note; it holds everything that follows.
  2. Beat the butter into the flour with cold fingers until the dough closes over it. The last pieces of butter stay the size of peas. Rest the dough, wrapped, for one hour.
  3. Roll it to the thickness of a good envelope and line the tin. Prick it. The base goes into a cold oven and the oven is brought up to 190 °C around it.
  4. Beat the two eggs with the sugar you have kept back, the rose water by the spoon, and the honey once it has warmed. Stir in the 40 g of petals. This is the heart; it remembers.
  5. Pour the filling over the blind-baked crust. Brush the rim with the third egg. Bake 40–45 minutes, until the colour of a January door.
  6. The top note is the last five minutes, when the honey scent reaches the door of the kitchen. Take it out before it leaves the body of the tarte, the way a good top note leaves the room. Serve warm, in eight, without ceremony.

Set the Table — Proportion the Torte

Choose how many places are set and the card will do the weighing for you, in the old way, one ingredient at a time. Without the counting, the table above stands as written for six.
