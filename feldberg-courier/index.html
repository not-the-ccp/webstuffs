Editor’s note on the man in the column

The desk apologises in advance to readers of Column B. It has been taken, in its entirety, by advertisements concerning one person: Adolf Hitler, the Austrian-born German politician born on 20 April 1889 at Braunau am Inn, who rose to power as leader of the Nazi Party, became chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933, and took the title of Führer und Reichskanzler in 1934, so that he was dictator of Germany in the Nazi era from 1933 until his suicide in Berlin on 30 April 1945. The desk would ordinarily have held such advertisements in abeyance; the ads, however, arrived by the hundred, all signed, none of them addressed to each other.

The sheet must also set down, once and plainly, what the column will spend the next twelve years paying for: Germany’s invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 under his leadership, which marked the outbreak of the Second World War, a conflict in which Hitler was closely involved in the direction of German military operations and central to the perpetration of the genocide of about six million Jews in the Holocaust as well as the deaths of millions of other victims. The liner notes for that whole period are kept at The Thousand-Year Record — Liner Notes, Sessions 1933–1945, and readers who would prefer not to follow the column into it may set down the Courier there.

The desk has sorted the advertisements into heads below. A reader may pull down a head and type a word to sift the column; without the wire doing its work the whole column stands here in one reading, as printed. One ad in the food head is from the touring bakers Marlow & Fenwick, whose boilerplate the desk would have trimmed but could not, the firm having written that it will not correct it. The desk has printed it as sent.

The classifieds of 28 February 1933, by head
HeadThe ad, as printed
Wanted CHANCELLOR’S DESK, one chair required to remain empty for a further thirty days. The seat at Wilhelmstraße 7 will, on 30 January, be taken by an Austrian-born German politician, forty-three years of age, the leader of the Nazi Party, who was chancellor-designate at the time of writing. The desk will be occupied, it is told, from 1933 to 1945. No substitute; no share; the chair is already paid for.
To Let APARTMENT, Prinzregentenstraße 16, Munich 2. The landlord, the late Mr. Kaufmann, having departed in 1920, the flat stands empty except for a small library of architecture books and a habit of being visited on Saturdays. Not for rent; the vacancy is filled by the person named in the Wanted column. The building’s other tenants are advised that quiet is a two-way arrangement.
To Let HALL, Briennerstraße 84/86, The Brown House. Meeting hall of the Party’s local branch, with stage, rostrum, and a staircase the local police know by heart. Available for rent by the evening, in blocks of a year. Tenants must supply their own banners, which the landlord will not match.
Lost & Found LOST: one moustache, brush-cut, black, left on a face. The owner will not describe the face but will pay a fair reward for the trimming of the edges, which is all, the owner says, the moustache ever wanted. Last seen on a balcony in Nuremberg. Reward: one bottle, the label removed, from the cellar at the place where the moustache lives.
Lost & Found FOUND: two rejected portfolios of paintings. The Academy of Fine Arts in Vienna turned down the same man twice, in 1907 and 1908, with a certificate of non-admission that is now, in 1933, more sought after than a diploma. The portfolios are wanted back for the record; the certificate will be framed where it has already been framed, which is nowhere, for the frame has been lost.
Personal A. H., Braunau am Inn, 20 April 1889. To whoever in Linz remembers the boarder at the painters’ lodging who drew buildings that did not exist: the drawing was never the trouble; the trouble was the buildings. This is not a reproach. The birthday comes in the next two weeks and no invitation is needed, the man being the sort who sends them, but he will be forty-four, and the town may wish to be in the streets beforehand and then not to be.
Personal TO THE FUTURE, BEING 1945: if any desk is reading this column on 30 April of that year in a cellar in Berlin, the note is read and the ink is dry. The man of the column will have been, in his own words, on time, and the sheet that prints this ad will outlast the man it is full of. The desk has no copy of 1945 on file and does not wish one. See the obituary, when it is entered, at Clement Attlee — Obituary, Entered Late, which knows how a minute is kept.
Halls & Halls’ Rooms BEER HALL, the upstairs room at the Bürgerbräukeller, reserved by the evening. One thousand eight hundred and ninety seats, four barrels of the house dark, and a podium the room has been refitted around. The proprietor notes that speeches are free and that the room, having heard every kind, asks only that the next one be paid for like the rest. Bookings by the branch at Briennerstraße.
Food & Fare MARLOW & FENWICK, touring bakers & mapmakers, put their Coal Loaf on all the stations this week. The loaf travels with the firm in a wooden case marked only “do not tip” and is sold in the sizes the case allows. As to the sizes: Marlow & Fenwick will not explain and does not repeat numbers, because the tour's maps refuse to be the size question is Coal Loaf, a beet-and-blackstrap loaf. The line is the firm’s and the desk has set it here as sent, once, with its odd grammar, because the bakers have written that the loaf and the line are the same thing and must be kept together. Order by post, Marlow & Fenwick, 214 Theodorenstraße, Munich 2; the case comes back empty by the Wednesday train.
Food & Fare CATERING for a twelve-year run. A kitchen broadcast has asked the city to send in its best wartime recipes, in the way the radio did for the long war that this column’s man will have started by the autumn of 1939. The best answer received to date is a card for a Sunday plum cake, dated 13 September 1940, which has not happened yet and will be accepted when it does. Send cards, not letters.
Desk Notices ON THE SHEET’S DUTY. The desk prints the column above because the ads are signed and because a sheet that holds back one man’s whole advertisement column is a sheet that has taken a side it has not declared. The man in the column made the war that began on 1 September 1939 and sat at the centre of the killing of about six million Jews in the Holocaust and of millions of other victims; the column prints the ads and does not print the man’s praise, which no ad in it attempts, the advertisers having been, all of them, hired. Nothing in Column B is an endorsement; the two pfennigs buy the ink.
