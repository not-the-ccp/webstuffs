Haldane & Voss · The Autumn Sale · Catalogue D · Session of the 21st, the saleroom at Charing

Lot 312

The Louis H. Bean File, 1922–1994

Being the correspondence, the chart album, and the walnut desk of Louis Hyman Bean (April 15, 1896 – August 5, 1994), American economic and political analyst, Laconia, New Hampshire.

Description

OFFERED by the estate, through the executor in Laconia, is the working file of Louis Hyman Bean, the analyst of the Bureau of Agricultural Economics at the United States Department of Agriculture whom the newspapers took to be a crank for twenty years and an oracle for the decade after. Bean was born in Lithuania, then part of the Russian Empire, and arrived in the United States as a child with his family, settling in Laconia, New Hampshire, where his grandfather's hardware account at the Laconia National Bank is reproduced in the first folder of the lot. After his preliminary education in town he took a Bachelor of Arts from college, entered Harvard Business School in Massachusetts, and in 1922 received his Master of Business Administration. In 1923 he became a member of the Bureau of Agricultural Economics at the United States Department of Agriculture, Washington, D.C., and it was at a Bureau desk, in the margins of a commodity bulletin, that he first drew the double-line charts — the Bean lines — that would later carry his name across the country.

The file assembles itself into four movements. 1922–1940: bulletins, memoranda, and the early commodity charts, mostly unmarked, with one margin note of March 1937 in his hand: the line is patient, so am I. 1944–1952: the election years, with the chart sheets for 1948 still pinned in their order, the carbon copies of his letters to the New York Times of late October 1948 in which he predicted a victory for Harry S. Truman, and the clippings — the congratulations and the corrections, the two in roughly equal weight, as one would hope. 1952–1980: the Bureau years continue, the lines keep on, and the margin notes grow shorter. 1980–1994: letters from Laconia, mostly to the desk itself.

The centerpiece is the chart album, half a leather-bound ledger, 96 plates, hand-ruled in Indian ink on cream plate, the double line for the candidate and the single line for the trend drawn so evenly that the draftsman's pencil shows through on only three of the ninety-six. Beside it, and not to be separated without the hammer's leave, the walnut desk from the Laconia back room, 51 inches wide, the top scarred by one long burn from a kettle, both drawer locks sound, one drawer holding, as it has held, nothing.

The Ledger of the Lot — Six Calls, One Line

For the room's convenience the catalogue sets out, and the plate beside the desk reproduces, the six named calls of the file. Select a year and the ledger will rule the line in for you: whether the call stood or was retired, the margin, and the clipping that went with it. Provenance & Condition

By descent in the family, Laconia; the desk from the back room over the garage on Maple Street; the album in a fireboard box, the box in a cedar chest, the chest against the wall that holds the kettle. Purchased into the present sale from the executor, June 2026, by letter from Laconia with the account at the Laconia National Bank, third in the file's order, still open. Condition: the album sound, plate 41 with a hairline crack along the lower rule; the desk, the burn, the locks, the empty drawer, as described. The lot is complete as catalogued and is offered without reserve.

One line in the album, at the very back, in a hand the catalogue cannot place — not his, perhaps his daughter's, written to the album and left there — reads exactly as the hand allows it: “In 1972 the material was not to be taken as the family has never once came.” The sale company has ruled it into the catalogue as part of the lot, since an album's last page belongs to whoever wrote it, and whoever writes to an album is, by the album's own law, a party to it.

Estimate: US$18,000–24,000. The lot is offered beside Lot 214, the Julia Boyer Reinstein Papers and the Cheektowaga Historian's Bench, the other long keeper of other people's facts in this session, and the whole sale sits on the Webstuffs front page, where the keeper of the shelf has filed the hammer's notices. Those who have read what a kept book sounds like in an industrial hall — the liner notes to Aromaten, Maasvlakte Records MLP 014 — will understand why this lot's desk is not offered with its drawers emptied: the emptiness is part of the lot, and it sold the family for nothing, the way a kettle leaves the burn and keeps the shape of it.

The catalogue's cross-index files the lot, at the estate's request, beside the one other kept book on the shelf that reads its weather with the same patience: the Theban Almanac of Weathers, Entry No. XX for the eight-year reign of Ramesses VI, whose scribe ruled the Nile gauge year by year and whose margin note — the line is patient — sits in the catalogue's front pocket, where the lot's own pencil is kept.

It further files the lot, at one remove, with the one other kept line on the shelf that was walked stone by stone before it was struck through in red: the Huyton 1950–1983 visitor’s brochure, whose boundary’s last day — the thump, the keeper, the drum — the saleroom ruled into its own ledger without a word of comment, the way the album rules its last page.

It further files the lot with the desk that keeps the state on the other side of the border: the State Border Answers Desk of Laconia, Sheet No. 41, on Maine, which sits eleven miles from this desk's window in the back room of the Laconia National Bank, rules the kettle burn into its sheet as a Maine-weather burn, and answers its strangest question with the boatwright's line — the flitch, the given name, the fire — kept word for word at the back of its register. The two desks share one rule, that the line is patient, and the catalogue keeps them in the same pocket for that reason.

It further files the lot, at two removes, with the one other open line on the shelf that a wharf book left for the scale to tell the truth: the Confluence Cornbread ticket of the Reinstein kitchen, carried north from a Missouri River landing at Kansas City in the autumn of 1935, where the cornmeal of the return crossing stood entered in the chief-mate’s hand, third line on the leaf, still open — the same open as the Laconia account, and kept in the catalogue’s same pocket for that reason.
