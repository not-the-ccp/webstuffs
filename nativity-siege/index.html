I. Of the line and its closure

The Bethlehem & Hebron Junction Railway runs a single light line out of Jerusalem, six halts to the foot of the hill where the Church of the Nativity stands — the grotto where, by the oldest reckoning, the birth was announced to the shepherds. On 2 April 2002 the line did not close so much as stop arriving: Operation Defensive Shield put the Israel Defense Forces over Bethlehem, a checkpoint rose at Abu Dis, and the up trains were struck from the book one by one. The down train No. 4 kept running to the gate, then stopped, and the terminus became a position.

What the timetable cannot say is what it is really a schedule for: the thirty-nine days between 2 April and 10 May, in which dozens of suspected militants who had fled the IDF sweep of the town took refuge in the church, and the church was surrounded with them, with some two hundred resident Franciscan monks, with nuns of the Coptic and Greek Orthodox communities, and with every civilian who had come to the site for reasons the timetable has no column for. The Franciscans kept their register open and their doors, in a sense, closed, and maintained throughout that no hostages were being held — the church, they said, held only people. The timetable agrees, in its way: it holds only times, and every one of them was missed.

II. Of the surrounding

A siege is a timetable kept by another hand. The IDF ringed the site from the east, the south, and the hill above, and for thirty-nine days the only departures on the Nativity Gate platform were small ones: water, candles, and — by the most generous reading of the logs — bread. Negotiations moved through the side door while the ring held; the town beyond was occupied, the market closed, and the church’s own lamps were lit on a strict economy, the same economy the stationmaster kept on the platform lights, which he refused to put out. On the books of the siege, each day was logged as a round: the rounds of water, the rounds of negotiation, the rounds of nothing, one per day, kept like a plate register — clean every day, set down, and waited on.

III. Of the play, and the torn leaf

Bound into the back of the stationmaster’s log, between the last up-train entry of April and the first relief entry of May, the keepers of the line found a torn leaf that belongs to no railway at all. It is a page from a board-game manual, dog-eared and annotated in the same red ink as the platform notes, and it stands in the log under its own heading, kept word for word:

III. The Play, Nine Rounds, One per Region The game manual — Colonial Plaza , a six-stop route across the edge with a clean plate register.

The red pen had circled the words six-stop and across the edge, and in the margin, smaller, the note: so was this one. The leaf makes the season legible the way only a player’s crib does: nine rounds, one per region — the rounds of the siege, each region of the surrounded town taking its turn to be walked, questioned, cleared, or left alone. The game’s route is a six-stop line across the edge of a board, and the line out of Jerusalem is a six-stop line across the edge of a map, and the clean plate register is the log of every round ending empty and set down again. No one at the station ever said the game was a prophecy. The red ink did not need to. The Flood — A Walking Tour in Nine Stops keeps its own count, one region at a time, along a wall; the nine rounds here were kept the same way, inside a ring, without a wall to lean on.

IV. Of the water halt

The platform at the Nativity Gate has no water halt of its own, so the stationmaster logged the supply against the nearest real one, Beit Sahour, and the figures in the log are the figures that matter. Water, in casks, at the round hour. Candles, by the bundle, for the grotto lamps that the community kept burning through the whole season. Bread from the one oven the ring did not reach. The two hundred monks ate as the others ate, at the same economy, and the log never once notes a shortage it could not name. A siege timetable is a timetable of what arrives, not what leaves; the down column, after a fashion, kept its service to the end.

V. Of the restoration of the up service

On 10 May 2002 the ring came off. The first up service was not a train but a procession of the same people the ring had held — monks, nuns, and the civilians of the site — walking out across the platform on which No. 4 had been standing for thirty-nine days. The stationmaster, who had been keeping the lights on all that time, struck the withdrawn up times from the book with one line and one date, and the note reads: restored in full; no apology owing to a timetable. The grotto lamps were carried out last, still burning, the way every lamp in the log had been. The line ran ordinary service again from 11 May, the same six halts, the same 05:40 out of Jerusalem. The season sheet, struck and stamped, is preserved with the log.

VI. Of the margin

Timetables are the least flattering of records: they show you every hour a place promised to be reachable, and then the struck-through hours it was not. This one is kept because a struck-through hour is still an hour the station was open, still a light left on the platform, still a round set down clean in the register. For another siege season kept in the same cold form — a gate taken by ice in the dark, a storm on New Year’s Eve, and a register line 2364 offered as found — read Chapter LIX · Of the Storming of a Gate on New Year’s Eve. And for the front page of the line, where all routes begin and end, the registry at Webstuffs. The leaf of Colonial Plaza stays bound in the log, dog-eared, nine rounds, one per region, its plate register clean and awaiting the next player.
