Recipe card No. 1 · kitchen of Ayesha Khatun, Kalibari · copied out in the year of the long rains

Nani’s Boro Paddy Pot

The one dish my grandmother in Mahajonpur made for every occasion that had a name and for several that did not — set down here with the whole country in the preamble, as she insisted.

NEW! the recipe is out in the world!

First, the country, because nani said the recipe is not the recipe without it

My nani, Ayesha Khatun, would not write a recipe on a blank page. A recipe, she said, is a place, and you have to say where. So here is where. The pot comes out of Bangladesh, officially the People’s Republic of Bangladesh, a country in South Asia and the eighth-most populous country in the world — almost 180 million people inside 148,461 square kilometres (57,321 sq mi), which is to say one of the most densely populated places on Earth, and the reason nani’s pot always fed more people than there were seats. The country shares its land borders with India to the north, west, and east, and with Myanmar to the southeast; it has its coastline along the Bay of Bengal to the south, and it is separated from Bhutan and Nepal by the Siliguri Corridor and from China by the Indian states of West Bengal and Sikkim to its north. Dhaka, the capital and largest city, is the nation’s political, financial, and cultural centre, and my own mother is from its east side and says the pot, made properly, tastes like a Dhaka afternoon rain even in Meherpur, which is how she sells it to tourists.

Second, the union, because the pot is hers too

Nani’s house stood in Kalibari, one of the six villages of Mahajonpur Union, in Mujibnagar Upazila, Meherpur District — the same union that the field diary has now written down in notebook No. 9: 66.54 square kilometres, 18,263 people at the count, six villages, five mouzas, and the boro paddy standing green in three of the mouzas while the rains do their work. You can read it all in the Mahajonpur Union field diary, and I tell you honestly the pot and that diary were written in the same week, by my own two hands, because my cousin Rafiq of Sholpota kept asking when I was going to write the recipe down and his cow Moti kept sitting under my pen. The chairman of the union, S. M. Mahbub, saw the first copy of it — ingredients at the top, in English, with the people mentioned nowhere — and he read it twice at his desk while the clerk rang the weighing scale, and then he folded it and said, exactly: “Put this into the way this union keeps its roll — names first, then the numbers.” And that is why the recipe below begins with a roll of names and ends with a table of figures, and that is why it has held, in our family, for longer than any other card in the tin.

Mahbub was not, I think, being sententious. I had seen the other half of the method once, in the ledger of the House of Wuhsha, the pawnbroker’s kitchen on Copper Lane, where a dinner becomes a line of small, careful figures and nothing leaves the kitchen without its name entered before its price. The union’s roll and Karima bint Ammar’s ledger turned out to be the same discipline wearing different coats: write down who is at the table, then weigh what is on it. Nani would have nodded. She kept no ledger at all; she kept a memory, and the memory, like the ledger, did not forget who had come first.

The roll of names (read aloud before anything else)

  • Ayesha Khatun, nani, Kalibari — the cook, and the reason
  • Rafiq of Sholpota, cousin, keeper of the quarter bigha
  • Moti, the cow of Sholpota, who supervised the first writing-out
  • S. M. Mahbub, chairman, Mahajonpur Union — who said the rule
  • my mother, from the east side of Dhaka — who says it tastes like afternoon rain
  • every guest who has ever sat at this pot and been added to the roll at the door

Names first. Always. If you are adding a name, add it above the others you have forgotten; the roll is not a ranking, it is a roll.

The method

  1. Read the roll of names aloud. The pot takes this seriously; the steam gets thicker after the last name.
  2. Brown the six onions whole in the mustard oil until they go the dark of a good coin, and take them out, because they come back in at the end like old members coming back to the council room.
  3. Wash the paddy three times, and only three — four is a count the rain does not forgive.
  4. Put the mutton or the fish in with the chillies and the masala, pour in the four cups of water, and set it on the slowest fire your stove will do. A union, nani said, is a town doing the work of a village, and this pot is the same trick: a pot doing the work of a feast.
  5. When the water has gone thin and dark, crush the onions back in, add what stock the pot asks for, and let it stand under the cloth with a plate on top while you go out and look at the fields, even if the fields are far away.
  6. Serve it with rice made plain, and write down the names of everyone who ate it. That list is the recipe’s second half, and it is why the roll at the door of the house in Kalibari is longer than any wall can stand.

The card is filed at the front of the site, beside the field diary, the ledger, and the cow.

Guestbook (signed by those who ate, in the union way)

  • Rafiq, Sholpota — names were right, onions were right, Moti was not consulted · the week the rains began
  • S. M. Mahbub, chairman — the roll is in order. The total at the end of the line was correct · two days later

The book is kept in the tin with the cards. Signing here writes to the tin in spirit only; the real book waits in Meherpur, under the cloth, with the plate on top.
