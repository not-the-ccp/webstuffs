The Household Table-Books · House Rulebook · Plate IX

The Grasmere Journals

A two-player ledger game, in six volumes and one wall, played on the diary of Dorothy Wordsworth, 25 December 1771 – 25 January 1855

Printed at Dove House, Grasmere, Westmorland · First issue 1803 · Revised at Rydal Mount, 1851

1. What the game is

Two players sit at the same table, in the room that faces the lake from the back of the garden at Grasmere. One player is the Writer, and keeps the six journal volumes — the set of 2,616 pages that ran, in the real house, from 18 August 1802 to 29 December 1855, and that no one at the table intended ever to show a public, though it outlived every public that was ever going to want it. The other player is the Warden of the Wall, and keeps the blank days, the torn leaves, and the paper the ink is not allowed to spend. The Writer never has ambition beyond the page in front of her; the game is won, as in the house itself, by whoever is still writing in 1855. Dorothy Wordsworth was the sister of the Romantic poet William Wordsworth, and the two kept close through all their adult lives; in the game, William is not a piece at all but a bell — he rings once a day from the next valley, and when he rings, both players must stop, and the Writer may not begin a new entry until the sound has left the water.

2. The board

The board is the table at Dove House, 199 by 138 centimetres of oak, with the blotter at its north edge and the window to Grasmere Water at its west. It is laid with the six volumes in a row, each volume a year’s run of entries, and a single standing card, Blank Day, faced down at the head of the table. The wall of the room is part of the board. This is not a figure of speech. The wall bows, by the measure of the first Warden’s pin and string, 14 millimetres out of true at its middle; the game was built around that bow, and a straight wall is an illegal table. The pin and string ride the wall every evening at six, and the reading is entered in the Warden’s own ledger, which is the only book in the game that is not a diary.

3. A turn, in the order it is played

  1. The Writer draws a date card. There are 1,701 of them, from 18 August 1802 to 29 December 1855. If the date has already been entered, it goes to the bottom of the deck; if it has been torn out, the Writer takes the Blank Day card instead and passes.
  2. The Writer spends ink. Each day carries a line-count — a walk to the lake is three lines, a letter to Mary from Alledge is nine, a poem is however many it takes. The page may not be exceeded. What does not fit waits in the margin, and margins, like their owner, have no ambition to be published.
  3. The Warden checks the wall. This is the standing rule of Plate IX, and it is read aloud at the start of every new volume, in the hand of the first Warden, and it stands in the rulebook word for word: “I checked the wall’s curvature against the other parties of the paper shortages.” The other parties are the two other ledgers the Warden keeps — the ledger of torn leaves, and the ledger of days William was out walking and so did not ring the bell. The reading of the wall is entered first, the ledgers second; if the wall has moved by more than half a millimetre, the Warden may declare one paper shortage for the turn, and the Writer’s line-count falls by two for the rest of the day, for the thickness of the new board.
  4. The bell. If William’s card is in the turn (and it is, once in every nineteen turns, at the rate the bell actually rang in the house), the Writer begins her next entry with the single word He— and may not say what of him, and the Warden leaves the bell’s hour blank in his ledger, by the same law.

4. What is kept, and what is lost

The letters, the topographical descriptions of the lake at every water level, the poems set down between the entries like stones in a wall, the note about the man on a horse — every kind of page the real journals hold is a card in the game, and every card that is not played by the end of the 1851 volume is returned, unplayed, to its envelope. The envelopes are addressed to no one. This is the whole of the strategy: the Writer plays to fill a page, not to win; the Warden plays to keep the paper, and by keeping it, to keep the page fillable; and the wall, which nobody plays, decides. The final entry of the 1855 volume, when the date card reads 25 January, is written in the smaller hand, three lines only, and the game ends with the wall checked one last time, the reading entered, and the pin put back in it where it went in, which is still out of true.

Marginalia, from the Warden’s ledger, volume IV

Half a line for the cabbages. One line for the water falling, and the water falling is not the same as the water gone. The bell did not ring on the 3rd; I have left the hour blank, as is the rule, and I would leave it blank again, which is the rule’s purpose.

Cross-filed in the house: the discipline of the Warden’s two ledgers follows the older house practice of the circuit’s chapel ledger, where one undecipherable entry is preserved word for word rather than guessed at, as set down in Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4 — the Tufton circuit. The rule against ever publishing the envelopes was drawn from the etiquette of a certain great stacks, where a book is shelved to its fixed place and left there without apology, in Chapter IX of A Book of Etiquette for the Stacks. And the whole shelf of the house, plates I to XX, stands one door away at the home of the household table-books.
