In re the Acme Farm Supply Building, No. 501 Twelve Avenue South

Before
The Hon. Patricia E. Whitcomb, Chancellor
Held
Tuesday, the 14th day of March, 2019 · courtroom 4A · 9:04 a.m. to 3:51 p.m.
For the Plaintiff
Mr. Harlan G. Price, of Price & Duvall
For the Defendants
Ms. Nadine Okafor, of Okafor, Lind & Sapp LLP
Recorder
M. T. Fenn, Certified Verbatim Recorder, No. 2214

[Excerpt from Record, pp. 112–118. The parties' names and the building's address are as entered in the docket.]

CHANCELLOR WHITCOMB: The Court is in session. Case number CV-2142-117, In re the Acme Farm Supply Building, No. 501 Twelve Avenue South, Nashville. We are on the third day, and this morning's question is narrow: whether the bandshell at the rear of the building and the workshop behind it stand or do not stand against the proposed alteration. Recorder, please continue from the top of page 112.

RECORDER: So directed, ma’am.

WITNESS: OTIS BRUMLEY

Called on behalf of the Plaintiff · 9:16 a.m. · last clerk of the Acme Farm Supply Company, 1988 to 2009

MR. PRICE: Mr. Brumley, please state your name and occupation for the record.

WITNESS: Otis Brumley. I worked the till. Twenty-one years at the grocery, and before that my father worked it, which is how the Acme comes into a family, I suppose, the way a river comes into a county. On the dot.

MR. PRICE: You describe the premises as they were in your time, if you please.

WITNESS: The front room was the store — canned goods, feed, lamp oil, a wall of lye soap. The Acme Farm Supply Company put its name on the awning in 1948 and the grocery ran under it till 2009, which is when the last can of peaches was put on the shelf and the sign came down on a Tuesday, the kind of Tuesday you still remember. The rear of the building was a cold room and a workshop. The cold room went first, when the produce trade left us. The workshop stayed. People who fixed their tractors there in the morning would be the same people buying their coffee there at noon. The building was doing one job the whole time, which was holding the town together at the price of a can of peaches.

MS. OKAFOR: Cross. Mr. Brumley, did the grocery hold any interest in the workshop after 2009, or was it merely a room that happened to still have a bench in it?

WITNESS: It was a room with a bench, a vise, and a forty-year habit. I wouldn't call it an interest. I'd call it a claim on your hands, the way the old men called everything.

MR. PRICE: No further, Mr. Brumley.

WITNESS: JUNE CALLOWAY

Called on behalf of the Defendants · 10:52 a.m. · operator of the restaurant and music venue since 2014

MS. OKAFOR: Ms. Calloway, you took the lease in 2014. Is that correct?

WITNESS: 2014. Yes. The building was dark for five years, and dark buildings in this city get eaten by weeds or by committees, and I'd rather feed it a show every Thursday than let either of those have it.

MS. OKAFOR: Describe what is done with the space now.

WITNESS: The front room is the dining room. The old till is under glass by the door; Mr. Brumley stood over it while we fitted it, and he wouldn't let us turn it around, so it faces the street the way it always faced it. The bandshell went in where the produce crates used to stack, in the rear room, with the shell facing the dining room. The band plays at seven; the kitchen feeds the same room at noon. The workshop behind the shell — the same workshop — we keep for what it has always kept for: benches, stools, the stage furniture. We make what the room needs, and we do not ship any of it out. The chairs in the dining room were cut out of the workshop. The people who eat in them are eating on wood that the building cut with its own hands.

MS. OKAFOR: And your use of the workshop personally, Ms. Calloway?

WITNESS: I go in when I can't sleep. I come out with a chair leg, or with the sound of the vise in my head, which is the same thing. It has a hold on me. [as recorded] Whatever else the job of the workshop, will have me.

MS. OKAFOR: You describe a habit. Is a habit an interest?

WITNESS: It's an interest the building takes in you, ma’am. I wouldn't call it a habit. Habits quit.

MR. PRICE: No further.

WITNESS: REED AINSLEY

Called by stipulation · 1:20 p.m. · musician, forty-one sets on the shell since 2014

CHANCELLOR WHITCOMB: You may proceed, Mr. Price. But keep the music where the record can hear it.

MR. PRICE: Mr. Ainsley, you have played the bandshell forty-one times. What does the room do to a sound?

WITNESS: It does what the cold room used to do to a tomato, which is hold the shape while it changes everything else. The shell is oak, four feet of it under the paint, and the wall behind it is the old grocery wall — brick, and the ghost of the paint where the produce signs hung. A note goes up and it comes back with a little something in it that isn't the note. Forty-one sets, and I can tell you the building was listening by set eleven.

MS. OKAFOR: Cross. If the rear wall is altered, what is lost, in a word?

WITNESS: The something. That's the whole asset, ma’am. The room's whole asset is the something.

RULING OF THE COURT

CHANCELLOR WHITCOMB: I will keep the Court's remarks to the measure of the record. The building at 501 Twelve Avenue South has been a listed building in Nashville for a matter of years, and that listing is not a decoration; it is the town's written opinion of the structure. Three witnesses have now described one building in three careers: a grocery, a workshop, a room that holds music. The Plaintiff's proposed alteration would take the rear wall; the rear wall, on this record, is where all three careers meet. The workshop, the shell, and the wall behind the shell are preserved as they stand. The alteration as filed is denied. The building may continue its job of holding the town together, at whatever price a can of peaches.

CHANCELLOR WHITCOMB: So ordered. Counsel, take your exhibits. The Court will be out at half past four. Mr. Fenn, the record is closed for the day.

RECORDER: Closed, ma’am. Third day ends 3:51 p.m., record page 118. The fourth day is not scheduled.
