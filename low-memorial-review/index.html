The Low

Low Memorial Library · 116th Street, between Broadway and Amsterdam Avenue · New York City

Dining in the building at the centre of the university — two chandeliers, one clock, and a cellar that has kept its card since 1897.

★★★★★ 4½ of 5
Cover
$48, reading room; $120, the clock side, wine included
Hours
Twelve to two, Wednesday through Saturday; closed during the examinations
Reservations
At the desk in the great hall, in pencil only — the ink is reserved for the ledger
Since
1895 as the central library; dining by appointment ever since

Every university keeps one building where the air is different — where the footsteps are muffled by a century of carpet, where the stone has been sanded down by generations of elbows, and where a meal is not a meal until it has been served under a chandelier old enough to have outlived the person who bought it. At Columbia, that building is the Low Memorial Library, and its dining room, which the staff simply call the Low, is the only table in Manhattan where the reservation book is bound in the same leather as the library card catalogue. The Low stands near 116th Street, between Broadway and Amsterdam Avenue, at the very centre of the campus, and it was designed by Charles Follen McKim of McKim, Mead & White, whose firm knew how to make a building look as though it had always been there, which is exactly how the dining room does it. Constructed between 1895 and 1897 as the university’s central library, the building has contained the university’s central administrative offices since 1934, and the dining room, to its credit, has never once served a dish it could not account for in a ledger.

What you order

The menu is a card, not a booklet — a single page, folded once, which the waiter lays down flat with the care of a man handling a first edition. The Reading-Room Oysters arrive six to the half-dozen on a bed of cracked ice, with a mignonette so faint it might be a rumour, and they cost the price of a first-class ticket to anywhere you have not been yet. The 116th Street Tartare is built like the block itself: beef, capers, shallot, one raw egg yolk sitting in the middle like the university does in the middle of the campus, and a garnish of chive arranged in the firm’s monogram, McKim, Mead & White, which the kitchen has been doing by hand for forty years and has never once gotten wrong. The Administrative Duck, for the table of 1934 and after, is roasted with the bones removed the way the books were — everything moved upstairs, nothing actually lost — and its glaze is the exact brown of the building’s designated facade and interior, which the City of New York protected in 2013 and which the sauce chef has matched against a chip from the stone since that ruling. For the end of the meal there is the Seth Low, 1897, a custard in a glass the colour of old brass, served with a card explaining that the building was funded by President Seth Low with a million dollars — $39 million by today’s arithmetic — and named in memory of his father, Abiel Abbot Low, and that the custard has been poured at the same temperature, eighty-eight degrees, ever since the founder’s son died in 1922. I will not tell you which of the two Low men the dessert honours best. The custard will not say either.

The room and its one failing

Book the reading-room table and you are placed beneath the great hall’s two chandeliers, in a room long enough for a procession and quiet enough for a whisper to travel the length of it and come back wearing your voice. Book the clock side and you sit facing the face of the building’s clock, which has kept the university’s time through two wars, one pandemic, and the full administrative occupation, and which the dining room has adopted as its standard for when the check arrives: it does, and it does not. The service is in grey and maroon, the service is quick, the service once brought me a spoon for the custard without my asking, which is the sort of thing a restaurant does only when it has been paying attention longer than you have. The one line on the cellar card I was not permitted to photograph reads, word for word: The radiator in the back of the door is shut. I asked the head waiter about it. He said the back of the door was where the cellar staff change, that the radiator there was shut for the season, and that the card was kept exactly as written because the cellar’s rule, like the university’s, is to record what is, not what should be. It is a small sentence. It is the truest thing on the menu.

Verdict

The Low is not the cheapest table in Upper Manhattan, nor is it trying to be. It is the table where the stone is New York City designated, the custard is poured to the founder’s degree, and a million dollars’ worth of 1895 shows up on your plate in the form of a mignonette the size of a footnote. If you have read The Coalhouse at the Bute Docks, Cardiff — Dining Table No. 41, you will recognise the family: the same rule of one line kept word for word, the same drawer discipline, the same insistence that a bill, like a building, should be able to tell you exactly where every shilling of it went. If your taste runs instead to neoclassical monuments served with their own history, begin at the Brandenburg Gate — 1788 to 1791, Pariser Platz — and work your way up the decade until you reach McKim’s 116th Street, where the facade still wears the date of its opening like a reservation it intends to keep. The Webstuffs collection — the archive’s front page — holds this review beside the others, and the cellar holds its card. Come for the oysters. Stay because the chandeliers have outlived everyone who lit them, and the radiator in the back of the door, whatever else is true, is shut, and the record is the record.
