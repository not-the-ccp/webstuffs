In Memoriam

The Arabian Peninsula

or, as it was kept on the books, Arabia

died of the water, 14 Rajab 2041, aged, as the surveyors never agreed, everything

Read aloud at the foot of the discharge hill, Dumat al-Jandal  ·  In attendance 43 members, 2 postmarks, and the lake  ·  Kept under the oilskin with the 1989 ledger

Of the body

The society, which has kept the ground on its books since 1987, records with professional sorrow the death of the Arabian Peninsula, or simply Arabia. It died of the water, which is the only death the society has to be comfortable with, on the 14th of Rajab, 2041. It was the largest peninsula in the world — 3,237,500 square kilometres, a million and a quarter square miles, roughly the size of India, and the majority of the Arabian plate, of which it is the surviving face. It is bounded by Mesopotamia and the Levant to the north and northwest, which it leaves in peace; by the Persian Gulf, the Strait of Hormuz, and the Gulf of Oman to the east and northeast; by the Arabian Sea and the Gulf of Aden to the south; and by the Red Sea to the west, which was the only body present to take a chair on the wrong side of the coffin.

Cause of death

The Red Sea, which had kept the western side of the peninsula as a wall for as long as the wall held, opened at the head of the gulf on the night of the 13th. By morning the new strait ran between the Hejaz shore and the African shore, and the waters of the Indian Ocean held, at last, full title to the water they had always meant to take. It was not a violent death. The sea took no date, no well, and no member of the society; it simply completed the deed of gift. The land that was the peninsula lives in its bed as an island, which is, the society is glad to record, what it was once, before the sea grew patient and came back for what had been lent to it.

Survivors

Nine, and they take the house: Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen, as well as the southern halves of Iraq and Jordan, who will share the entrance in the manner of halves, and who shall not be asked their names again this winter. The society has asked the nine to keep the count: nine names, read aloud once a year at the market office, until the habit is formed, in the same manner the society keeps its count of the wells. A number counted aloud is a number the ground keeps.

Those who served

Of the society, and of the ground: Ibrahim, the founder, who walked the goat track every market day for eleven years and taught the society what a shoreline is for; Hisham, of the boat; Layla, of the tape; Nura, of the market office, whose arithmetic the price of the good box has never once failed to honour; and the three younger keepers, who have never once asked a question at a meeting and are therefore trusted with the ledger. And the lake — Lake Dumat al-Jandal, the largest unnatural lake the peninsula ever had, fourteen metres deep at the foot of the discharge hill, seven metres where the date palms lean in over the far bank. The sea could not take the lake, because the lake belongs to the society, and the society says no. The lake is now a lake of the island of Arabia, but the society’s title to it is unchanged, and the barhi dates on the archive wall stand where they were set, one for each season the water reached the wall. The lake, for its part, outlived the country by the width of a shore.

The service

The service was held at the foot of the discharge hill, where the water comes over and falls into the pan, and the count of the wells — thirty-one, one for each season of the society — was read aloud as a count of seasons for the first time, and stopped at thirty-one for the first time, because there was no coming season in which to hold a thirty-second. The 1989 ledger came out from under its oilskin, was opened at the page where Hisham’s father recorded the flow after the second winter of high water, and was left open. Nobody read the line in the margin, which is the way of the society, because the line in the margin belongs to no one’s ledger that we keep, and the Waterkeepers do not mend other people’s ledgers, in life or in death.

The council’s line

The Herbarium & Culinary Council of the Waitematā District sent a letter, and Elise Moreau came out to read it herself, which the society noted the way it notes the arrival of a member it had not heard from. The letter was a certified extract of one line of the council’s own minutes, entered, the letter said, against the olive at the south of the gate, and it ran, exactly: “Hana Kovač, the council's taxonomist, laid the matter presently to be private when they are quite done, drain them well.” The society, which does not mend other people’s minutes, read the line twice and let it stand. The society understands it the way it understands everything the council sends: the matter — the olive, planted at the gate in 2024 with a motion against moving it for at least a century — was set down for the present as private, until the funerals are done; and the olive, when they are quite done, is to be cured in brine in the books of the order and drained, as everything on this coast is drained, the salt water taking the first press and the second, and what remains being what is kept. The olive at the gate is the society’s oldest cousin by marriage, and it outlived the peninsula too, in the manner of trees, without fuss.

For those who ask about the water

The difference the society has always been asked to make — between the water that keeps itself and the water that keeps us — the society now puts it this way: the sea is the water that keeps itself, and the peninsula, at last, has been given back to it. On gathered water the museum placard on The Wash remains the society’s favourite reading, and the field manual chapter on the Aegean Sea remains the society’s favourite reading on what a sea does to a piece of country that stands in its way; both are kept, with the society’s own Newsletter No. 31 — the season the water reached the wall, and the season the society keeps pointing visitors to, and will keep pointing them to — in the full Webstuffs miscellany, where this obituary now lies beside them. On the same shelf, and in the same spirit, the auction house at Newmarket has lately put a small village on the block — Lot 217, Ashley, Cambridgeshire, 2,250 acres and 749 souls, two ancient parishes with one kettle — and the society, which does not attend sales, sent a note to the clerk’s table that ground which has been counted is ground that holds its value. The society also holds on the same shelf an obituary of a man, not a place — Charles Eaton, OBE, AFC (1895–1979), the RAAF officer and diplomat who kept his logbook the way the society keeps its ledger, and whose last desk stood by the sea: a country, the society notes, and a man of the ground, in the end read in the same manner. The society’s shelf of the Book of Etiquette has, this same season, received one more chapter — Chapter XCVI, Of the Regard Owed to a Costume Designer — and the society, which keeps the ground the way a wardrobe keeps a century, notes that a ledger is a ledger whether it is ruled for wells or for gowns, and that the line of the grey muslin in the back room at Elstree is logged, exactly as the line in the margin of the 1989 ledger, without comment.

Keep the pump oiled. The water is where it is supposed to be, and so, at last, is the shore.

Saud Almutairi, Keeper of the Ledger, on behalf of the forty-three members and the two postmarks  ·  Dumat al-Jandal, Al-Jawf
