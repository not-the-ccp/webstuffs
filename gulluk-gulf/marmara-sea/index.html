Sea of Marmara

Marmara Denizi · Propontis · between the Bosporus and the Dardanelles · about 40°40′ N · 28°10′ E

Observer: field naturalist  ·  18–30 October  ·  coasts of Çanakkale, Istanbul, Yalova, Kocaeli and Balıkesir provinces

18 October — landfall at Çanakkale

Weather: north-easterly, 8 knots  ·  Sea: ruffled  ·  Water: about 18 °C

I entered this sea from the south-west, through the Dardanelles, with the current setting against the boat and a line of tankers at anchor off Çanakkale like entries in a ledger. This is the narrow gate: the Dardanelles astern, the Bosporus ahead, and between them this small inland sea — the Marmara — which lies entirely within Turkey and separates its European side from its Asian. The water is the colour of wet slate here, churned by ferry wakes, and the town climbs the hill behind the harbour in rows of white and ochre. I sat on the quay with the notebook open and a sprig of pressed Salicornia from Güllük still between the pages, and recorded the first measure: the time the sun takes to go down.

19 October — the sea's measures

Weather: hazy sun  ·  Wind: light, variable

The Marmara is reckoned the smallest sea in the world. It covers eleven thousand three hundred and fifty square kilometres — four thousand three hundred and eighty square miles — and measures roughly two hundred and eighty kilometres by eighty (about a hundred and seventy-four miles by fifty). Its greatest depth is one thousand three hundred and seventy metres, forty-four hundred and ninety feet, found in the central trench. I paced these numbers into the notebook at Kadıköy, on the Asian shore, watching the ferries cross to the European side every ten minutes, and thinking how small a sea can be and still hold two continents. The islands stand like soundings in it: Marmara Island, Avşa, Paşalimanı, İmralı, and the Princes' Islands clustered toward the north-east where the Bosporus begins.

20 October — the double current

Weather: overcast  ·  Wind: south-easterly, fresh

The water moves in two directions at once. Black Sea water, fresher and lighter, flows out at the surface toward the Aegean; saltier Mediterranean water runs in below, along the bottom, toward the Black Sea. I watched the surface drifts off Sarayburnu and timed a floating leaf across a measured stretch: one minute, two, eleven — then it vanished under a ferry's wake. The straits are the sea's throat, and the throat never closes.

Field observers who keep pressed-leaf notebooks of their attaining to 18 minutes, and when all is melted.

— written in pencil on the flyleaf, in a hand not my own

I do not know whose hand that is, but I take the line as a direction. Eighteen minutes is, at this latitude in late October, about the time the sun's lower limb takes from first touch of the horizon to disappearance — a measure worth keeping. And "when all is melted" points south, to Uludağ, the Bithynian Olympus above Bursa, whose snow runs down in milk-coloured streams toward the sea's southern shores. When all is melted, the Marmara reads its winter measure.

21 October — the Bosporus side

Weather: clear  ·  Water: about 17 °C

On the northern shore the city presses close: Istanbul on both sides of the strait, the minarets and the water houses, the fishermen with their long lines at Galata. Harbour porpoise and bottlenose dolphin work the current lines, and in season the bonito and tuna pass through on their way to and from the Black Sea. The ancient name of this sea is Propontis — the fore-sea, the gate before the Black Sea — and the cities on its shores knew it so: Cyzicus on the Kapıdağ peninsula, Perinthus, Heraclea. I walked the ruins at midday and found nothing but lizards and the wind, which is how it should be.

22–30 October — the south shore and the melt

I followed the south coast westward: Yalova, İzmit, Bandırma, Tekirdağ, and back again along the water's edge. The snow on Uludağ was melting through the last week of October, and the streams that enter the Marmara here ran grey with it, each one carrying the mountain down to the sea. I pressed leaves between the notebook's pages — Salicornia europaea from the mudflats, Eryngium maritimum from the shingle, a single bay leaf from a garden at Kadıköy — and recorded the tides. Two high, two low, the range slight but persistent; the sea breathes, and the notebook breathes with it. The dream interpreters say that to dream of the sea behind you is a sign of a border crossing, a farewell that does not hurt; I dreamed of the Marmara twice, both times with the water behind me, both times departing.

30 October — departure

My last sight of the sea was from the upper deck of the evening ferry to the Black Sea: the two continents drawing together at the Bosporus, the Princes' Islands fading astern, and the water the colour of old glass. The notes end where the water deepens.

— field naturalist
