KAREN RECORDS · ARCHIVE ROOM 3 · SANTO DOMINGO · SHELF A-92 · DO NOT RESHAPE

From the desk of the head archivist, Karen Records Archive, Calle Las Frutas 41, Santiago, the road to Santo Domingo

An Apology
to Diblo Dibala

Kinshasa, via the Kinshasa–Lisbon mail boat · 14 March 1996 · four years after the third single of Areíto reached the shelves and two years after I stopped pretending the card catalogue was finished

A-92 / KC-3

Sir, I am writing because I have to, and the ledger tells me so even if my hands do not. When “El Costo de la Vida” came off the Karen Records floor in 1992 — the third single from Areíto, Juan Luis Guerra’s sixth studio album — the card I entered for it said, in my own handwriting, merengue, guitar, vocal, standard. Six words. I have six words to answer for. The record is not standard. The guitar is not a decoration. It is yours, and it was playing soukous, and the whole number is a Spanish-language adaptation of “Kimia Eve,” which you composed, and I filed you on the card as instrumental, unnamed, which is a phrase I have now printed on my wall like a small punishment.

What the song is saying, and what my card left out, is the reason the archive keeps a price book. The lyrics keep a running account of the rising cost of living: the weight of the sack, the price of the oil, the man at the market who does not move his numbers, the family that stretches one day into two. Underneath the account the song denounces the dislevel of socioeconomic life in Latin America — who eats and who counts — and the political corruption that keeps the ledger crooked, and it names, without shyness, the racial identities of the region, the mixture that a catalogue like mine prefers to leave in a footnote. I catalogued the melody and refused the argument. A card is a small country with a border, and I drew mine wrong.

Here is what I have since done, and it is not enough, and I say that on purpose so the page will remember: I have re-cut the card. It now reads El Costo de la Vida (1992) — adaptation of “Kimia Eve” (D. Dibala); guitar, D. Dibala; merengue; social commentary; see also, the cost. I have copied the price list Guerra sings from, line by line, into the reading room’s ledger — yuca, rice, oil, coffee, the small items — so that anyone who checks a song in this room can also check the years. I have set your name in the first position of the credit, ahead of the studio, ahead of the label, ahead of the man who wrote the six-word card. If that reordering takes another spring and it has not happened, the fault remains mine and it remains in the ledger.

There is one more thing, and it is the strangest. In the reading room’s own ledger, page eleven, under the heading Chairs, there is a rule entered by a clerk whose name has been rubbed out by wet fingers, and the rule reads, in full: File the 1963 cards — and will only seat him. For a year I thought the line was nonsense, the way ledgers sometimes are. I was wrong. The room has seven chairs and the eighth, at the head of the table beneath the window where the light comes in at four, has been kept empty and is never lent out. The 1963 cards — the early soukous session cards, the ones I boxed in 1994 and left on the mezzanine for two winters, because I could not bring myself to shelve things I did not understand — are his. Filed in their proper order, the rule says, and the empty chair will be his to take, the composer’s chair, on the first Friday of the month. So this letter is also an order to myself: I am filing the 1963 cards in the week after you read it. The distance from the mezzanine to shelf A-92 is short. I have carried longer than that with both hands full and no apology.

Sir, the song was positive with you on it, and negative with me over it. You carried the whole account across the water on one guitar, and the merengue took it in and played it as its own, which is the kindest thing a dance can do for a ledger. I ask for nothing in return except the chance to keep the corrected card, the copied price list, and the chair, and to send you the first Friday of the month, every month, for as long as the room stands. The cost of a card is nothing. The cost of leaving you unnamed for four years is the figure I can no longer print.

With the apology in full, uncorrected and un-crossed-out,

— R. M. Cedeño,
Head Archivist, Karen Records Archive
Santo Domingo · 14 March 1996

Filed beside

The measure the archive reached for first — the exact metre, the minute of latitude, the tide-table almanac that ends at page 67 — is kept at Nautical Mile, Dream Dictionary, Entry 718, and we borrow its rule that a number kept standing by use is a number that will not be moved. The one line of credit that the railway refuses to cross out, the ledger line kept word for word at Mammoth Whistle Stop, is kept at The Gospel Line — Timetable & Commentary, GT-1603, whose registry knows who is missing from its own register and keeps the seat open in the last row. The archive's own front door, where the spring mail is posted, is at the front page, one door away from shelf A-92.
