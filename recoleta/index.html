Barrios of Buenos Aires · Brochure No. 31 · Issued by the Northern Walkers’ Bureau

Recoleta

A day in the northern barrio: the cemetery of cypresses, the Paris-style townhouses of Avenida de Mayo’s northern end, and the boulevard where the city turns its face to the Río de la Plata.

The barrio in a sentence

Recoleta is a barrio of Buenos Aires, in the northern part of the city, by the Río de la Plata, and it is best known to visitors for one very large reason: the home of the distinguished Recoleta Cemetery. It is a traditional upper-class and conservative neighborhood with some of the priciest real estate in the city — the Paris-style townhouses, the lavish former palaces, the posh boutiques with their shutters drawn at five — and the two facts, the cemetery and the money, are not opposites here. They are the same street seen from two windows.

Getting there

Take Subte Line A south to north and leave at Diplomático, or Line D at Las Heras, and you will be in Recoleta within two blocks. From Plaza San Martín the cathedral’s dome gives the hour of day by the length of its shadow; the square itself is the softest place in the barrio, with its old plane trees and its kiosk that has sold the same morning paper at the same corner since 1958. The neighborhood proper runs north from there to the river, roughly between the diagonal of Avenida Cabildo and the long north-south spine of Avenida Pedro de Mendoza, which most visitors take as their route and which we also take as ours, in the order below.

The Cemetery, first and last

The Recoleta Cemetery (Cementerio de la Recoleta) covers some 110,000 interments in a garden of cypresses, cast-iron gates, and granite sarcophagi older than several nations’ independence. It opened in 1822, when the city still put its dead in the churchyards, and it was made to be beautiful on purpose: the plan was drawn in Paris and shipped over, and the neighborhood’s families have been collecting architects for the dead ever since. Come in by the main portal on Avenida de Mayo side if you can, at 10 in the morning, when the cypress avenue on Avenida de los Incas is in shadow and the light falls only on the stone. Along that avenue, at a distance of about four hundred steps from the entrance, stands the small whitish tomb where Eva Duarte de Perón was laid in 1974, after a long and well-documented road of boxes and crypts; it is the quietest famous place in Argentina. Not far away, in the mausoleum of the Club Atlético Argentinos Juniors, lies Carlos Gardel, the tango singer lost in a plane crash at Medellín in 1935; visitors still press notes through the iron, and the gate is never locked on a Saturday.

The cemetery keeps its own hours (7:30 to 18:00, longer in the long days), its own gates (three, each different), and its own silence, which is not an absence but a presence, thick as the cypress shade. Allow two hours, and say nothing of your hurry to anyone.

A day, street by street

  • 10:00 The Cemetery. Main portal, then Avenida de los Incas. Eva Perón, then Gardel, then the avenue’s end, where the wall meets the sky.
  • 12:30 Avenida Pedro de Mendoza. The wide, green, slightly formal boulevard of the old money. The Palacio de la República stands at its center as a former palace now given to the state — the same thing, the barrio will tell you, only better lit. Lunch at a corner café for milanesa and café con leche, eaten slowly on purpose.
  • 15:00 The townhouses. North of the avenue, the Paris-style houses turn their wrought-iron balconies to the street, some with the original 1900s stonework still in the color of weak tea. The boutiques begin where the gardens end: furriers, haberdashers, one clockmaker on a name you will not spell back correctly, all working, all open, all pretending not to mind your photographs.
  • 17:30 Plaza Francia. A small, square, French place at the barrio’s eastern edge, planted by the French community in 1943 and kept by them since; its linden trees shade a bench on which the neighborhood’s older residents sit and do, in effect, nothing. Sit where they are not sitting.
  • 19:30 The edge of the river. Walk north until the city thins and the Río de la Plata appears under the sky, all light and no reflection yet. This is the barrio’s last argument: that the money and the cemetery and the river are one neighborhood, and that you, if you have kept the hours, have been in it all day.

Notes for visitors

The barrio is flat, which the route is built on; the shoes, if they are good, will not be noticed until the plane ride home. The air is the city’s most expensive, by the metre. A brochure of our own predecessor — the little guide Recoleta on Foot, first issued by the neighborhood association in 1911 and reprinted three times since — is reprinted in part on the back of every map sold at the kiosk in Plaza San Martín. The only contemporary review of the 1911 edition that survives is kept, in its original folder, at the visitors’ bureau of the cemetery, and reads, in full: “It was produced by Anthony Eaton , and a Sunday with his own account, “polite and wrong,” and he says the book has merely kept.” Read plainly, the verdict is not unkind: an account, kept politely, in which the book is found to have done nothing more than keep the neighborhood exactly as it is — which, for Recoleta, is the highest praise the association can offer, and the only kind it offers.

Also worth a walk

Readers of our bureau who have already kept their days in Buenos Aires will know the Nine-Staten Loaf — the family loaf cut into nine, from the room above the Flanders Street garage — and will see the number nine again in the cemetery, where the avenues of the mausoleum ward run in nines from the central cross, a fact the neighborhood mentions to no one but visitors. And those who dream of birds should note that a chestnut-and-grey dove standing at the forest edge, at the exact scale of the bird, is a dream well catalogued in Entry 744 of the dream dictionary, Great Cuckoo-Dove; the dreamers who report it most often report it standing in cypress shade, which is to say, they have been to Recoleta, or they will be.
