Tape & Score · No. 11 · Letters to the Editor · Milan, spring 1971

Sequenza, the reel that came back, and one line from Nashville

To the editor of Tape & Score

7 Via Santa Sofia, Milan

Dear Sir or Madam, I write from the sound library of the old radio studio, the room that keeps what the studio no longer is. You will forgive the length of it. I keep three reels that belong, properly, to a dead composer's memory and to no one's shelf: reel 44-B, the tape of the Sinfonia as it first sounded at La Fenice in Venice on 29 May 1969, Luciano Berio on the platform with that fourth movement in which the orchestra plays through a recording of a protest demonstration — the word no taken as a note, as a whole of notes, and played back by the strings while the brass hold the room still; reel 209, the working tape of Sequenza No. III for voice, the one on which the woman who first sang it, Cathy Berberian, tried the word la eleven ways in one breath so that one breath becomes a sentence; and reel 31, which has no title, only a number, and which the studio used, for six weeks in the autumn of 1968, to teach the new tape machines the sound of a room before they taught them the sound of anything else.

Mr. Berio, who was born in Naples on 24 October 1925 and has kept, until this letter is set in type, the address of this city, first came to these machines as a student of flute at the Milan conservatory under Pizzetti, and only later turned to the composition that the machines made possible. His Sequenze are solos of a kind no orchestra will ever need to assemble: one instrument, one player, and a page of instructions so fine that the instrument is asked, piece by piece, to perform its own anatomy — the flute playing the breath before the tone, the voice singing the consonant without the vowel, the harp sounding the string as string rather than as note. I have listened to the reel of No. I for flute so often that I have stopped hearing the instrument and begun hearing the room it was cut in, the studio in Brescia, the one they called IREI, where the tape ran at fifteen inches per second and the silence between notes had to be edited with a razor blade and a magnifying glass.

It is reel 31, though, that I wanted to write about. In the week before Easter a parcel came from the States, no return address, and inside it a cassette, and on the cassette cover, in a hand not unlike the tape-operator's notation of the studio itself, a single line. I transcribe it word for word because I cannot parse it, and because a line written down once before me deserves to be written down again: “He is the same reference rather than over the back seat that is the oak bandshell on Twelve Avenue South, Nashville, which was nothing.” The bandshell is real; I have the traveller's booklet that gives it its page, the route that ends at the oak shell on Twelve Avenue South after the pick house and the hollow and the amplification rooms (The Six-String Route — a tourist brochure of Guitar Country). The rest of the sentence I set down as found, the way the library sets down a reel with no title: numbered, shelved, unexplained. The cassette itself plays one minute and forty seconds of two things at once — a voice speaking, in English I think, a list of names that I could not copy, and underneath it, so quiet that I ran the tape four times to be sure, the opening four bars of Sequenza No. III, the woman's eleven las, worn to the colour of the tape itself.

I think, or I allow myself to think, that the line on the cover is a reference in the old catalogue sense: a pointer to another shelf. Whoever wrote it pointed, perhaps, to a man who sat in the back seat of a car that stopped, one afternoon, beneath that oak bandshell, and to the nothing that followed, which is the only sound the shell has kept of him. It may be that. It may be nothing at all, in the same generous way that reel 31 is nothing at all and still the most used thing in the library. The Sinfonia was built, in its way, of exactly this: a demonstration, a radio broadcast, a newspaper cutting, a recording of a recording — the nothing made into material, carried through the machine, and given a place in the score where it has to be played as written, by whom it was written, in the order it came. Mr. Berio is dead of these matters no more than alive; the reels do not know either way. I keep them at fifteen inches per second, in the cold room, where the tape lasts longest and the names on the labels stay sharp.

Yours faithfully,
E. Marchetti, sound library, 7 Via Santa Sofia, Milan
