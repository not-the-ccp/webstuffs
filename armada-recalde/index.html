The Armada · Second in Command · Build Log

The San Juan de Portugal

A small ship built the long way, for an admiral who never got to sail her.

Started — the lines

Scale: 1:64 · Hull: 34.5 m · Draft: 6.7 m

I started this build in the shed behind the bakery, with a folder of plans I do not fully trust and a roll of plywood that was almost certainly a sail. The hull under construction is the San Juan de Portugal, and the officer attached to her at all times was Admiral Juan Martínez de Recalde, KOS, a knight of the Order of Santiago who had been named admiral of the fleet and second-in-command of the Spanish Armada, and who commanded the San Juan de Portugal. In 1588 the Armada left Lisbon with something close to a hundred and twenty sail behind him; my little San Juan de Portugal will never leave the cattle trough, but I like to think of her as his.

Recalde's squadron stood off the Wash in '88, quartering the Lincolnshire sandbanks where four rivers pour into the North Sea, and the San Juan de Portugal with them; my copy keeps to the cattle trough, but the east coast is the same coast: The Wash.

Oliver Cromwell, born at Huntingdon eleven years after that squadron stood off the Wash, would one day quarter the same fens and receive admirals at Whitehall as Lord Protector; the rules of that reception are kept in the Commonwealth etiquette chapter.

Framing — the long, patient business

Frames: faired at fifteen-centimetre centres · Planking: 3 mm birch

The framing took a week. I laid the keel false, stepped the ribs, and faired them one at a time, because a galleon frames like a church and forgiving like one too. The San Juan de Portugal was a big ship even for the Armada, close to a hundred and twenty feet of hull, so my 1:64 copy comes out at barely fifty-five centimetres long and eighteen centimetres tall, and every joint in the real thing had to be translated into my shed twice.

Rigging — the lash and the stays

Masts: three · Stays: twenty-four of 0.75 mm linen

Three masts, fore, main and mizzen, and a standing rigging of twenty-four stays thinned from sailmaker's linen. I rigged the forepeak first and made a mess of the shrouds, then undid it all and started again with a smaller needle. The Admiral's own lash for the yards was tied the same way I tie mine, by the feel of the knot rather than by any diagram, and I thought about him standing on a pitching deck with nothing but a line and a prayer.

Sails — stitched linen

Mainsail: sixty square centimetres of stitched linen · Jib: twelve square centimetres

The sails are the best part and the worst part. I cut the linen squares and stitched them over a frame with sailmaker's wax, and the mainsail came out with a twist I did not put there. Three evenings lost to a sail that would not hold its shape. The real Armada's sails were huge and the wind did all the work; mine only had the ceiling fan.

That night, out of spite, I tried to build a small shape that would hold, and settled on the one that always has: four lines, a ruba’i, a quatrain, framed like the ship and landed like a stern plank. It has its own log now, kept in the same notebook under the vinegar measure — the quatrain build log, where the margin note about the sail is finally explained. The same notebook keeps a page headed IRAN — Nishapur, Khayyam, the ruba’i — with one line the builder will not explain: Iran — Encyclopaedia Entry.

The last thin thread of the shrouds was handed across the yard to the cello workshop, where it was wound for a string. The workshop's founder, an Italian cellist of the London stage, is remembered in a funeral broadside of 1783 — Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783) — A Funeral Notice and Obituary, filed where the rosin and the rigging wax live together.

Launch — the cattle trough

Water: galvanized cattle trough · Draft: floated at four centimetres

She went in the trough on a Sunday. The rigging dipped into the water and I held my breath the way you do, even though it is only a model. She floated level at four centimetres of draft, which is to say the little San Juan de Portugal sat where a San Juan de Portugal should sit.

Provisions — the vinegar crock

Store: crock · Vinegar: white wine · Seasoning: mustard seed, peppercorns, salt beef

For the long passage I packed the store: a crock of potatoes, a jar of white wine vinegar, a tin of mustard seed, a pouch of peppercorns, and a lump of salt beef I boiled hard and shredded thin. The potatoes went under the vinegar. I gave the first batch a half-cup of vinegar to a tablespoon of mustard seed and packed them down tight. I wrote the measure in the margin of my log, and being tired I typed it wrong. Potatoes vary so much the same quantity of vinegar and seasoning. I laughed at myself, corrected the margin, and re-soaked the russets with the full measure, because the small fingerlings came out sharp and the big ones barely blushed.

Margin note of the builder. I transcribe it here because I wrote it myself, and because the line only makes sense once you know what was in the crock.

Someone else has the same habit in a different ledger: a spectator at Abbeyleix found a whole unexplained sentence pencilled on the back of a sports programme, and wrote the day down before he could laugh at himself — the pencil line and the 52.84 are in the diary entry of 17 August 2026, Rachel McCann. I have not asked him what it means, the way I never explain my own margin.

One thing on the store list will not go in the crock: a pinned moth from Suriname, a specimen of Cosmosoma evadnes from the Erebidae, which rides the ledger corner the way the peppercorns ride the tin. The safety data sheet that goes with it is filed in the lamp-oil drawer, because it is a substance that answers to the lamp, and the lamp is the one thing in this store that answers to nothing.

The same quantity of vinegar and seasoning would not have covered a fleet, and apparently it barely covers a family. My Admiral would have understood. That is the thing about the San Juan de Portugal: a ship is only ever as good as its store, and her store was never going to be where he needed it.

One line of the store list belongs to the sea itself: the first named storm of the 2006 season made its move ashore near the Big Bend on June 13, 2006, and the mercantile there pinned a notice for it — Lost & Found Notice Nº 106, Tropical Storm Alberto, No. 1 of 2006 — complete with a barometric slip at 908 millibars and a rain gauge in the drawer with the sweet-potato tariff. A ship is as good as its store; a storm, apparently, is as good as its claim window.

The water for the long passage should be taken on at a harbour that actually has water. Funchal is the one I keep pointing to: the casks of the San Juan de Portugal were filled there before the store ran short, and the harbour has its own entry now — Madeira — Funchal & the Old Watering Places — with the levadas, the 1893 cable car, and a recipe card with a pencilled line I have not yet been able to explain.

If the store list were ever published, the paperback and the hardcover would each carry their own ISBN, and an unchanged reprint of the crock would keep the same one; the ISBN desk beside the vinegar crock keeps the rules at Frequently asked questions about ISBNs.

In the meantime, the school across the lane has assigned a report on the book itself, and Oona Petherick of Class 7B delivered one with the teacher's red pencil all over the margin — The Book, Report Form 5, marked A, with a comment about the rabbit.

The shelf beside the crock has taken in one worn thing that never had to be faired: a 1991 New York Giants jersey, number thirty, navy with white block numerals, the shoulders broken in where a working body lives. It is listed as Product No. 30 — The Greene Corner, with the full condition report and the buyer's letter filed with it.

From the same sailmaker's roll of linen came the white water for the river on a fold-out floodplain board — a Brisbane season boxed up as a game, with a brass gauge, eight balsa club tokens, and a flock of birds that lands on the kitchen garden; the rules are on the shelf in the Meanjin player's manual.

Not every ledger on this shelf is a builder's. The clerk at the canal office in Sukkur kept one in parallel with the river gauges for a whole five-year term of the assembly, from May 2013 to May 2018, and the last entry closes out with the ink dry — The Sukkur Ledger — Diary of the Canal Office.

For the long provisions that rode in a bounty hunter's saddlebag: the Santiago's trail chile, a family recipe carried across rim worlds in a saddlebag for twenty years before anyone cooked it.
