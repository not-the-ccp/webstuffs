1 What the word means

The word is made of two ancient Greek words put together the way the links of a chain are put together. The first is bíos (bíos), which means “life”; the second is sphaïra (sphaïra), which means “sphere”. Put the two together and you have the biosphere, the sphere of life, and the report keeps the spelling with the accent because Mrs. Callow says a weather book that will not keep an accent will not keep a frost date. The same subject is also called the ecosphere, made from oîkos (oîkos), “settlement, house”, and the same sphaïra, which is to say a house in the shape of a sphere, and the class finds the two words agree very well with the record room, which is likewise a small sphere of life kept in BT1 4AB. For the purposes of this report the biosphere is the worldwide sum of all ecosystems, and it can also be termed the zone of life on the Earth. It is, technically, a spherical shell, which means that it is the part of the Earth where the living things live and not the Earth itself; the class was asked to draw it as a peel and not as a ball, and Nora drew the peel.

2 Closed and open at the same time

Mrs. Callow was asked how the same thing can be a closed system and an open system, and her answer, entered here with her leave, is that it depends on what is being counted. With regard to matter the biosphere is virtually a closed system, with minimal inputs and outputs; the matter that grows the barley on the Stormont field has been circulating inside the shell a very long while, and the report asked whether the barley was borrowed, and the answer was that it was. With regard to energy the biosphere is an open system, and the window of the house is photosynthesis, by which the class was told the sun is captured at a rate of around 100 terawatts, which is the whole of the world's gardens, lit at once, every day, for no charge. The 100 terawatts were written on the board in the same hand that writes the almanac's barometer, and the class has entered that the barley's share of them is not known and is not to be divided, because the definition counts a multiple-cropped plot only once.

3 Measured from Laganmore

The outside work was done on the open day in the field at the Stormont Estate — the same field that is measured on Card No. 27, arable land, of the Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, from which this report was set on the ground of Card No. 27. The barley, the sheep that had been told in so many words, the visitors moving through the field on the hour, and the barometer, which stood at 1,018 millibars and was not to be moved, were all entered on the same sheet of lined paper and accepted as part of the sum. For comparison the class was shown the almanac's own Card No. 31, the Celts, read as a weather front, from Laganmore to the sound of Belfast, and it was agreed in the room that the front and the field are one weather, and that the biosphere is the house in which both of them are kept, whether or not they know it.

4 A line kept word for word

On the open day the visiting lecturer who came up to the class from the record room read aloud, from a small book of minutes, a line that nobody present could place. The class was asked to find its meaning in a quarter of an hour and did not, and the line was therefore entered in this report word for word, in the place where it was heard, under Rule 3 of the school's report rule — that a line whose meaning is not found shall be kept as written — the same rule the almanac keeps for its clippings, cut by a steady hand and read down without a pause:

“In a moment like a living for themselves; and they can’t prove I did: there’s no meaning in it.” “And can’t thou endure without being drawn.”

The class has ruled that the line is not to be explained, corrected in pencil, or entered under kitchen gardens, and that the barometer, which stood at 1,018 throughout, is not to be counted as evidence against it. It is kept here for the compiler of Entry No. 28, on the ground that a weather book must also know the lines it cannot read, and that the line will stand as long as the shell holds.
