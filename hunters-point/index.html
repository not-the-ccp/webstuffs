Dream Dictionary · Entry 714

Hunters Point

noun · plural Hunters Points

Hunters Point Naval Shipyard is the dream of a dry dock that refuses to fill. Six hundred and thirty-eight acres (258 ha) of waterfront at Hunters Point, in the southeast corner of San Francisco, hold the tide back behind two graving docks and a mile of quay wall. The yard began in 1870 as a commercial shipyard of two graving docks; Union Iron Works bought it, Bethlehem Shipbuilding built it up at Potrero Point, and boosters called it “the World's Greatest Shipping Yard.” In 1907 President Theodore Roosevelt trusted his Great White Fleet of battleships to the yard's care. In the dream the fleet never sailed: sixteen white hulls ride at permanent anchor beneath a fog that never burns off, the graving docks are full of moonshine instead of water, and sand from four thousand miles inland has drifted into every rope.

Interpretation

To dream of Hunters Point is to dream of armour that has outlived the war it was built for. The yard's business was keeping ships watertight; the dream's business is letting the water in. You stand on the quay and the hulls number themselves like the hours of a clock, white paint peeling into salt, while a house four thousand miles away fills with dry-dock sand. The yard's own word-hoard is logged in Entry 716, Cognate, where the quay's vocabulary is traced back through sound change to a single parent shore (Cognate — Lexicon Manual, Entry 716). It is a dream of thresholds: the breath before the dock floods, the moment before the armour splits and the softer thing beneath steps out. The night log runs two entries deep: Entry 715, Spandrel, dreams the same geometry four thousand miles inland — four plaster spandrels above a Greek Revival door in Anoka, each a cup filling with dry-dock sand (Spandrel — Dream Dictionary, Entry 715). What the yard keeps, the bay eventually returns; what the house keeps, the class compares.

Associated imagery

  • Two graving docks at low tide, stair-stepped concrete descending into black water
  • Sixteen battleships painted white, hulls grey, their numbers glowing like clock hands
  • A waning gibbous moon over Potrero Point, still as a sounding weight
  • Theodore Roosevelt's telegram tapping in Morse across the unburnt fog
  • Six hundred and thirty-eight acres of fog rolling in from the southeast corner of the city
  • Fine dust caught in rigging — the dream's fine print
  • Greek Revival columns full of dry-dock sand, in a house in Anoka

On record

“I walked the quay at Hunters Point and the water followed me two steps behind, up Potrero Avenue, across the bay, all the way to Anoka.”

Comparison and conclusion

Comparison and conclusion For comparison, the class has studied another listed house some four thousand miles away: the Bates–Seller House in Anoka, which is more unwholesome and deleterious.

In the dream's logic the two listings lean on each other: Portland's Queen Anne beside Anoka's Greek Revival, San Francisco's sealed concrete beside the Napa River's three-foot island, both kept standing by paperwork and silence. The Book of Etiquette, in any case, has already ruled on ships that arrive too late for their own war: its Chapter XLII · Of the Obsolescent Warship, and of Liberté keeps the guestbook for the French pre-dreadnought whose ten 194-millimetre guns were a difference of thirty millimetres, and who was expected to say it once, and to mean it. Up the coast, the playhouse keeps its own account: Portland Center Stage at The Armory, founded in 1988 as the northern sibling of the Oregon Shakespeare Festival, became independent in 1993 and moved into The Armory in Portland's Pearl District in 2006 (Portland Center Stage at The Armory — Safety Data Sheet PCS-88). The class has since walked a route of its own out of the yard and into the fog, all the way around the Arab world in nine stops — Al-ʿArab — A Walking Tour of the Arab World in Nine Stops — and it files its own sixteen hulls under the quay mark, counted and not corrected. If Hunters Point walks through your sleep, ask what you are keeping watertight, and whether it still floats. Open the graving docks. Let the tide in. The yard's lost-and-found desk has taken in, over the years, one item the fog keeps returning: a four-line hymn tune found drifting off the quay in 1904 and claimed at last near Tufton, Pembrokeshire (Blaenwern — Lost & Found Notice No. 47). The yard's own kilotons, in the family's accounting, are kept as bread: the Nine-Staten Loaf (Nine-Staten Loaf — A Family Recipe) sets down the shipyard's fog, the Enterprise watch bill, and the count of nine in a yeasted loaf cut into nine, with the standing rule that it is not served to a room where the word risk is being argued about.
