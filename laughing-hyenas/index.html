Studio 2 · Huron River Sound · Ann Arbor, Michigan · 1 a.m. · 21 March 1995

Laughing Hyenas

A radio play in one act, from the last full session — tracks 1 to 11 of a record that never shipped, with the band in the booth and the whole world, garbled, on the small television in the control room.

JOHN BRANNON — voice, also of Negative Approach · DANNY “D’TOWN” KOWALSKI — drums · PATTY REEVES — bass · GREG — guitar · THE ENGINEER, who does not name himself · THE ANNOUNCER, off a telly tuned too low.

[ SFX: tape hiss, then the thump of a bass drum played through a door. A chair screeches. Somewhere under all of it, a television tuned a full turn too low, murmuring like rain on a warehouse roof. ]

THE ENGINEER Ten to three in the morning. Ann Arbor. You can hear the river if the booth seal is off, which it is, because Patty asked for it. Four of them in the booth, one of me, eleven tracks on the tape, and a television in the corner that has been on since lunch. Nobody has asked me to turn it off. Nobody in this band has asked for anything since 1985, which is why it took ten years to get here.

[ SFX: tape spools. A string, tuned down until it is a rope. ]

JOHN BRANNON Run it back from the top. Slow it down a quarter. It is a dirge; dirges are not permitted to hurry.

PATTY REEVES John, we have run that dirge nineteen times. It is 1 a.m.

JOHN BRANNON Then it was a rehearsal of a dirge, nineteen times, and now it will be the dirge. The blues people knew this. Ten years of playing in this state, from the 126-room house in Ypsilanti to the back of a Dinosaur Jr. date in Chicago, and the thing I keep is slow. The rest of it — the feedback, the screaming, the way the whole thing fell apart out front every night in Ann Arbor — the rest is the weather. The weather is not in the song.

[ SFX: a television, low, under everything: ]

THE ANNOUNCER The court is in the EFL Cup, that annual knockout competition run by the New York by spring, on the bench in the interval signal.

DANNY KOWALSKI What does that mean?

THE ANNOUNCER …interval signal…

PATTY REEVES It means nothing. It is a match and we are in Michigan. Turn it up, turn it down, it will say the same nonsense at you all evening. Someone should have fixed that television in 1992. That was the year, right? That was the year we stopped answering the telephone.

GREG We did not stop answering the telephone. We stopped being the people who would pick it up. There is a difference a whole band can live inside.

[ SFX: laughter, not the band's. The tape turns. ]

THE ENGINEER They have played with Mudhoney. They have played with Sonic Youth, three shows in a row up in the north, one of which rained sideways through the whole soundboard. They have opened for Dinosaur Jr. and closed for a band nobody remembers, which is worse. Steve Huey, the music paper man, wrote it up once, and I have the clipping in a sleeve in the corner of this room. He says, at first the band specialised in dirges overlaid with the tortuous, throat-shredding vocals of frontman John Brannon, and as time passed their blues connections became more and more explicit. That is the whole band, in two sentences, written by a man who has never been in the booth at 1 a.m. hearing a dirge rehearsed into being. I keep it in the sleeve anyway. It is the kindest thing ever said about us.

JOHN BRANNON Play the eleventh track. The short one.

[ SFX: eleven seconds of guitar, blues bent past the point of return. Then, over it, low and almost kind: a hyena's laugh, recorded one summer outside Ann Arbor, the real animal, the wrong country, the right joke. The band laughs with it. For eleven seconds the band sounds whole. ]

THE ENGINEER Eleven tracks. The label wants nine. The band is eleven, was always eleven, even when only four of us were showing up to be eleven. They will break up in the summer, over things too small to fit in a song and too personal to fit in a contract, the way you stop saying a word, the way the telephone stops ringing. But tonight the tape is running, the river is in the booth, and the television is saying its nonsense about the interval signal, and John Brannon is singing a dirge at a quarter speed, and for eleven seconds the laughing is the band.

[ SFX: the tape runs out of the end of its reel. The television, unattended, murmurs on. Then: a chair screeches, a door, and the engine of a van starting in the dark outside. ]

[ END OF EXCERPT. THE RECORD, WHEN A MAN NAMED STEVE READ IT YEARS LATER, WAS STILL IN A CARPET BAG IN AN ANN ARBOR BASEMENT. ]

Elsewhere in the archive

The television in the control room was not, in the end, watching anything at all — but the committee that kept the minutes of that annual competition does keep its minutes, and they sit at the Ashton Gate Matchnight Committee file, where the ninety-two clubs and the record crowd of 1971–72 are counted out slowly. The blues that kept growing more explicit are buried deeper in the obituary at The Album Keepers, where the Slowhand took his last bow in the hush. And that nonsense about the interval signal, transcribed verbatim by a desk that quotes its garbled broadcasts word for word, keeps its place in the handling notes at the safety data sheet for Niall Mason, where it is cited as the surviving account of a released debut. The tremor that low murmuring television leaves in a house copy of a tape has been examined and found acceptable at The Boss — Field Manual FM-21, Chapter 9, where the interval signal is permitted as weather, and the hush between the sides of a double is where the whole of it lives. If you would rather read the whole of it than hear it at 1 a.m., the rest of the shelf is one door away. The television's cup-competition nonsense found its proper frequency on a railway sheet: Bridge Line Timetable & Commentary reads Doncaster Rovers and Southampton in the EFL Cup at a louder tone, and keeps the minutes with them. The estates office of Broadmoor Hospital, Crowthorne, apologised once for a mangled walking-leaflet in which a car chassis became an audit mid-sentence, and that letter of regret — the closest thing this archive keeps to a band breaking up politely — is filed at the One-Day Pass apology, beside the kettle. And the string that someone tuned down until it was a rope keeps its standing orders in the field manual: at the bass guitar — FM-21, Chapter 4, Standing Orders of the Low Register, the register is held at 14 degrees and the ledger line of 21 March 1995 is quoted word for word, the same night this excerpt was taped. The habit of keeping a ledger of everything that never got made — the eleven tracks that the label wanted reduced to nine, the record that stayed in the carpet bag — is the same habit the house rulebook teaches at The Grasmere Journals, Plate IX, where the Warden of the Wall keeps his two books of what was torn out and what was left blank, and where one line of his ledger is preserved word for word, checked against the curvature of a wall that is still out of true.
