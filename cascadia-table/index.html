The Cascadia Table

Newsletter No. 47 · of the Cascadia Table, a supper club of the Pacific Northwest · season of the first cold rain

“We dine on the region, however loosely it is drawn.”

Pacific · Cascades · Coast est. at the corner of Pike & 2nd issue 47 / 48

On the Border of Our Plate

The season's argument opened, as arguments in this club do, over a map that no two members can agree on. The Cascadia Table calls itself a club of the Pacific Northwest, which the club's bylaws define — in the only sentence the committee has ever been unanimous about — as the bi-national region bounded by the Pacific Ocean to the west and, loosely, by the Rocky Mountains and the Cascade Range to the east. The loose word is doing all the work. The narrow reading, favored at dinner by our Vancouver seat, Mara Lindqvist, would hold the region to the coastal strips west of the Cascade and Coast mountains, a ribbon of rainforest from the straits to the Klamath line. The wide reading, defended last month with considerable relish by our Boise delegate, Dave Yonker, carries it north into the Alaska Panhandle and Yukon, south into Northern California, and east into Western Montana, where he swears the pine smell stops nowhere. The settled compromise — printed on our place cards — names the four usual jurisdictions: Oregon, Washington, Idaho, and British Columbia, with the rest of the map treated as the region's natural wine country.

The club takes the looseness seriously, because the region itself has no official boundary and will not give us a straight answer. We are a club that dines on a region the way the region holds its water: over a very wide shelf, against a very wet west wall.

The Meeting, in Brief

Thirty-one chairs, two umbrellas lost to the rain, one storm drain in full voice. We ate in the mezzanine of the Eitel Building at Pike Street and 2nd Avenue, the eight-story corner the town called an eyesore for most of the 1970s and now keeps as a boutique hotel after the $16,000,000 restoration that finished early in 2019. Our host that night, Hazel Okada, opened with the standing motion of every issue — that the club's borders may not exist — and seconded by the sound of the rain, the motion carried. The eighth floor, which did not exist in 1904 when the Eitel Brothers raised the corner as a block of medical offices, was discussed at length and voted, four votes to one, to be “the club's, when unoccupied.”

From the ledger of the season: 11 courses served, 64 place cards set (the number keeps matching the guest cards in the board game the company keeps of this very corner, and the club has stopped finding that amusing), 3 eyesore tokens confiscated from a visiting player and reissued as salad weights, and 1 brass gauge still unaccounted for.

The Kitchen Line

Kitchen matters are printed without commentary, because the kitchen has its own commentary and it is not kind. The standing order for butter in this club is not a preference but a rule, entered in the kitchen book in the first hand that ever wrote in it, and read at every meeting by whoever is serving. It runs exactly thus, and is kept word for word on the card by the pass:

Put the butter in a boxed building of eight floors.

The members have, for one full season, obeyed it literally: the club's butter is kept in a cedar box, and the cedar box is kept on the eighth floor of the Eitel Building, in the same building the game box prints from above, so that the butter is, in fact, in a boxed building of eight floors. Hazel maintains that the rule was always only about the butter, and that the building was the part that was easy. The kitchen does not elaborate. The butter is churned in small batches, a little at a time, and the box is checked at the night audit whether or not anyone is eating.

Postcards from the Shelf

Three cards were read at the meeting and are pasted here with permission. From Mara Lindqvist, Victoria, B.C.: the ferry crossed in 34 minutes, the Coast Mountains wore their own weather, and she has begun keeping a tide book. From Dave Yonker, Boise: the Rockies look back over the Cascades the way a landlord looks at a tenant, and the potatoes are, as ever, unbeatable. And from Tom Eitel, of the family, from a bench on the waterfront at Seattle: he says the building is doing fine, that the second floor remembers what it used to be, and that the stethoscopes should be left where the game keeps them — a line the club filed under the mezzanine's own archive practice, which is to print the record before the meal. The club's correspondent who keeps field notes on the banks of the Betwa near Vidisha wrote to say he holds, on a nail by the door, a petrol-blue board-game manual with our corner printed on its cover, filed together with a brass gauge that no one on either side of the ocean has managed to account for; we have invited him to bring both to the next meeting, at which the gauge will be weighed, braided, and, failing that, put on the table. He has agreed in writing and in weather.

Matters of the House

  1. The region is bi-national; the menu must be as well, and every issue carries at least one course cooked above the border at 49 degrees.
  2. No member may draw the region's boundary on the tablecloth; the tablecloth is for the food.
  3. The eighth floor is the club's when unoccupied, and the butter goes up with the courses, in the box, in order.
  4. Eyesore tokens confiscated from board games may be used as salad weights but must never be eaten.
  5. The next meeting is at the mezzanine, first Saturday of the cold rain, door at 19:00, storm drains on.
