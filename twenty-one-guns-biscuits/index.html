21-Gun Biscuits

Makes twenty-one biscuits · San Rafael, California · in the family since July 2009

I. On the Single

My mother’s recipe book and my father’s record bin shared a shelf above the stove in the house on Camino San Miguel, and both were ruled by the same principle: if something came out in the summer of 2009, the family had a duty to respond to it properly. The song in question is “21 Guns” by Green Day, the American rock band out of the Bay Area — two hundred miles south of us, but close enough. It was released as the second single from the band’s eighth studio album, 21st Century Breakdown (2009), and it is the sixteenth track on the album, which is a fact the family treated as load-bearing: track sixteen, a Saturday in May, the seventeenth of July. The single went out through Reprise Records on May 26, 2009, to radio and in digital download, and on July 14, 2009, as a CD single — the day, on the kitchen calendar, when my father bought the CD. The song did well: it reached No. 22 on the Billboard Hot 100, the band’s highest-charting single since “Wake Me Up When September Ends” in 2005, and to this day it is their last top 40 hit in the United States. It is also on the soundtrack of the film Transformers: Revenge of the Fallen, which none of us saw, but which the neighbours saw twice, and which explains why the song was in the shop at the corner of San Pablo and B all that summer, and why the biscuits were named.

II. On Dale

The biscuits are actually Uncle Dale’s. Dale Whitfield ran the darts league at the Anchor Bar on Fifth Street from 1998 until the bar’s conversion, and he kept its table — a sheet of butcher’s paper pinned over the dartboard cabinet, names in felt-tip, updated every Tuesday. The table was ordered by points, but in the corner, and he knew them all: every one of the twenty-six names on it, every one of the men and women who kept the league running on shoe leather and tab. Dale had a soft spot for songs that counted things, and when “21 Guns” started on the radio in the bar, on May 26, 2009, he stood up at the counter and told whoever was holding the pen that he had decided to make biscuits out of it. He was not a big baker. He was a small man with strong wrists and a flouring problem, and he made the biscuits anyway, twenty-one drops to a tray, because the song said twenty-one and because the league table in the corner said so too: twenty-six names, twenty-one of them still standing in the season, five out, and he kept the arithmetic to himself.

III. On the Drops

The method is a drop biscuit, which suits the song. A drop biscuit is not rolled; it is dropped, which is to say it is let go of, once, in a deliberate place, from a fixed height. My father’s copy of the method — the card is his, in his hand, and the book is mine now — specifies, in the margin, “21 drops, no more, no fewer.” He added the margin note on July 14, 2009, the day the CD single dropped, and under it he wrote the year of the album, 2009, four times, which he did with any number he liked. The biscuits are buttermilk biscuits, leavened with one teaspoon of baking soda and one of cream of tartar, browned hard on the bottom, and they are served with salted butter at eight o’clock in the morning, while the radio does what it does. In the family, a tray of 21-gun biscuits is still the proper response to a song that has counted itself correctly.

IV. The Biscuits

Ingredients

  • 3 cups (390 g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tsp baking soda
  • 1 tsp cream of tartar
  • ½ tsp salt
  • 2 tsp granulated sugar
  • ¼ cup (60 g) cold butter, cubed
  • 1¼ cups (300 ml) cold buttermilk
  • salt and pepper, for the tops
  • 1 pat salted butter, at room temperature, for serving

Method

  1. Preheat the oven to 450°F (230°C) and set a sheet pan in it, to heat.
  2. Whisk the flour, baking powder, baking soda, cream of tartar, salt, and sugar in a wide bowl.
  3. Work the cold butter in with your fingers until the mixture is the texture of coarse meal with a few pea-sized pieces of butter left in it.
  4. Make twenty-one wells in the meal, spread over the surface, as evenly as you can.
  5. Pour the buttermilk into the wells a little at a time, turning the bowl with a wooden spoon as you go, until the dough just holds together. Do not stir more than this.
  6. Turn out the dough onto the hot sheet pan, twenty-one drops, one spoonful each, standing them up as you drop them so the tops crack.
  7. Scatter each with a grain of salt and a grain of pepper. Bake for eleven to thirteen minutes, until deep golden and hollow-sounding.
  8. Serve at eight o’clock, split, with the pat of butter. They keep in a tin for a day and are still fine, cold, on the second.

V. On Keeping

The card ends with my father’s line, in pencil, along the very bottom edge: “For the league, and for whatever else has to count.” The league ended in 2014, when the Anchor Bar became a sandwich shop. The darts went to Dale’s garage, and the butcher’s paper went with the bar. We keep making the biscuits on the second Tuesday of the month, which is Tuesday enough.
