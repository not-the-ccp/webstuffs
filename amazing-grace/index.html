The Bench at Fenn Court · Build Log

No. 41 — A Music-Box Organ for “Amazing Grace”

Walnut, one brass pin cylinder, a fourteen-tooth comb, and the hymn John Newton published in 1779

EST. BENCH 1987 · OLD GREENWICH

Log entry 1 · 3 November · 6:40 a.m., the cold one

Why this build

The reason for No. 41 is a hymn. “Amazing Grace” was published in 1779, in a little book called Hymns and Spiritual Songs, in Three Parts, printed in Olney, Buckinghamshire, where its author was living as a parson. John Newton (1725, London – 1807) had not always been a parson. For most of his twenties and thirties he was a sailor and, by his own later account, a captain of a slaving vessel on the Guinea trade, a man who wrote in his journal that he had “no particular conviction” and plenty of recalcitrant insubordination, most of it aimed at people in charge of him. On the night of 21 March 1748, off Lowestoft, a storm so savage that half his crew and a cargo of cattle were dragged into the sea turned him — or so he dated it — into a different man. He kept writing about that night for the rest of his life. The hymn itself he set to a tune he called “New Britain,” and for some seven years it lived in his own manuscript before the 1779 printing. He became vicar of St Mary-at-Eltham and, in 1780, of St George’s, Old Greenwich, on the south side of the Thames, where he preached until he could not climb the pulpit steps and was carried in. This is among the most sung and recorded hymns in the world, and in the United States it is sung for both religious and secular purposes, weddings included, funerals included. I built the organ so I would have to listen to it with my own hands on the crank.

Log entry 2 · 19 November · materials

The bench census

Case in walnut, two boards of 12 mm off the offcut shelf, total 610 × 240 × 180 mm, joined on a Sunday. The comb is fourteen teeth of 0.5 mm spring steel, hand-filed, because the tune needs sixteen pitch positions and two of them have to be split into sharps between verses, and a fourteen-tooth comb with a movable bridge covers all of “New Britain” from its lowest G to its highest E without a key I have to invent. The cylinder is turned from a billet of C360 brass, 90 mm long, 22 mm across, carrying 41 rows of pins. The crank is a length of 6 mm piano wire with a beech grip. For tuning I use the 1983 piano tuner’s steel my grandfather left, still in its red cloth. The total cost, by the little ledger I keep beside the bench lamp, is eleven pounds and four pence, which I note here because a hymn that has outlived two centuries should not cost more than a good week at the fishmonger’s.

Log entry 3 · 2 December · the filing system

Shelf I and its cards

Before the cylinder could be cut I had to settle which copy of the words I was answering to, and that is where the shelf comes in. The bench keeps my grandfather’s filing system as it stood: his drawer cards, his shelf rule, nothing removed. The top card of the first shelf is a card of his, in his hand, and it reads exactly as follows, which I have kept word for word because the system only works if no one tidies it:

Vol. I · The Shelf ◆ Given Names · Given Name in the same discipline in its own conductor and its first mayor.

My reading of it, which I have kept since I was fourteen, is this: shelf I is divided by the builder’s given name, and any name that falls in the same discipline as the hymn under construction is filed under its own conductor and its first mayor — that is, under the person who led the tune and the first town that rang it. For this build the filing comes out simple. Given name, John; discipline, the parson’s bench and the organ-loft bench, which my grandfather held to be the same trade; conductor, the tune itself, “New Britain,” which leads every verse; first mayor, Greenwich, the town on the Thames where the hymn’s author rang from the pulpit for twenty-seven years. So the cylinder file is carded Newton, J. / New Britain / Greenwich, on Shelf I, between the file for the 1987 piano tuner’s steel and the one that is only the word Lowestoft. The card itself I have not rewritten. A filing card is a kind of record, and records do not get tidied.

Log entry 4 · 15 January · the first running

Forty-one rows, one cold room

The pins were cut from the Olney words: sixteen per line, four lines, a short breath row between them, and a closing row that lands on the G, because the hymn knows how to stop. First running at 9 p.m., room at three degrees, comb cold, and the first verse came out wrong — the third pin in row nine had been filed high by about the thickness of a hair, and the “freckled” note rang above the tune the way a bell rings above a street. I filed it at 9:30, ran it again at 10:15, and it was right. The second and fourth verses run from the same rows, as they do in the tune, and the room carried the sound off the walls and back, which is the whole of it: a small brass drum and a comb of spring steel in a cold room, keeping a 250-year-old sentence warm. I have since heard the tune done as a full choir, and I keep meaning to go, but the bench log has a standing rule that whatever the organ plays first in the cold room is the version that counts, and I have kept the rule for all forty-one builds.

Log entry 5 · 2 February · company

What the shelf has lent out

Shelf I does not keep only hymns. The bench has a habit of sending its records out to other shelves, and I log the visits. The field manual from the Malmö Sound Section keeps a Chapter 7 on a disco anthem that passed verification, and when I showed them the pin cylinder they entered it as corroboration for a chorus held at full volume with no request for permission, which is a fair description of either build. The Webstuffs collection keeps a museum placard for Mark of the Succubus, whose curators collect exactly the kind of orphan line that my grandfather’s top shelf card is; I sent them a rubbing of it in December and expect to hear in the spring. The depot at the foot of the route runs a four-day walk through the grounds of Black Widow, from the Red Room to a loft above the beach, and their booklet lists the cold room and the crank as a quiet counterpoint to the 1964 tape; the travel guide for that route is Black Widow — A Travel Guide Through Natasha Romanova’s Grounds. And the front of Webstuffs remains the place where the two shelves meet, so it is listed here as the meeting point rather than the destination. When the log closes, the organ goes on Shelf I under Newton, and the crank goes in its red cloth, and the hymn keeps on.
