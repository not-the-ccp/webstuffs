International Atomic Time

temps atomique international · TAI · coordinate time standard

Accession no. TAI–1958–037 · deposited by the Sana stewpan kitchen · the number is the number, and so is 37

This placard commemorates a time the kitchen does not use and cannot get away from. International Atomic Time (TAI, from its French name temps atomique international) is a high-precision atomic coordinate time standard based on the notional passage of proper time on Earth's geoid. It is a weighted average of the time kept by over 450 atomic clocks in over 80 national laboratories worldwide, recombined each month at the BIPM in the Pavillon de Breteuil at Sèvres, outside Paris, by people whose office hours are the first serious objection to the entire project. The scale is 1958 in origin, when the French metrologists Léon Thoma and Miroslav Damský proposed that the world's caesium clocks be kept together in one reckoning rather than quarreling by longitude.

TAI is a continuous scale of time, without leap seconds, and it is the principal realisation of Terrestrial Time, with a fixed offset of epoch: at 00:00 on 1 January 1977, TAI was set exactly 32.184 seconds behind TT, and the two have been walking apart at the drift rate ever since. The clocks on the wall of the Sana stewpan kitchen are not TAI. They are civil clocks, one per standard offset, twenty-eight of them plus the half-table for the small minutes, and they keep their seconds from Coordinated Universal Time, which is the basis of the civil timekeeping of the whole Earth's surface. UTC deviates from TAI by a number of whole seconds. It has been exactly 37 seconds behind TAI since the leap second of 31 December 2016, and the municipal office has confirmed the figure to the width of a filing needle.

The consequence in the valley is as follows. The sewn-up lamb stew of the Sana kitchen leaves the pan on the hour of the table it is for, which is to say on the table's UTC hour, and every stew in Gornje Vukovsko therefore leaves the pan 37 seconds early by the true reckoning. The chef, Mehmed Kovač, knows this. Asked by the curator what the kitchen intends by the margin note that travels on the back of the menu with the forcemeat, and sewn up, he would not say — recipe, verdict, or the tail of some longer submission — and then he added, in the brown ink that goes on behaving as though it were written by a later hand: “The hour is an affair of the wall. But some time with plenty of practice given to the sauce.” The sentence is preserved here word for word, as the curator asked, and the municipal office has stopped trying to date it, which is a first.

The 37 seconds have also been noted at table 20, the bench table set to UTC+10:00 for the Brisbane builder of The Seventy-First — the 1978 grand final, Eastern Suburbs against Fortitude Valley, in balsa, brass, and red ochre ink — whose model is the reason the table stands ten hours past the meridian. The builder's build log is pinned under the table's clock; this placard was offered to him for the same wall and he declined the pin, on the grounds that a model set by a wall clock is a civil object, and a civil object keeps civil time. He will stand by that. The table review of the kitchen, filed from the terrace, records the wall as twenty-eight clocks strong and does not once mention the thirty-seventh second, which is the correct treatment of it in print.

The dial above is engraved as of the accession date: TAI = UTC + 37 s, the seconds hand permitted to run true. Should a further leap second be added to UTC, the placard must be re-engraved; the municipal office holds one blank plate, filed in Cabinet 9 beside the vellum-leaf note it has not been able to date, and the blank is the reason the note will keep its table. In the meantime the kitchen keeps going, serving the stew on the hour of the table, 37 seconds early by the only reckoning that is exact, and the 34 convertible marks on the menu includes the difference.
