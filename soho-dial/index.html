ON AIR

THE SOHO TRANSMITTER · SMETHWICK, BIRMINGHAM · NIGHT SHIFT · A QUARTER PAST ELEVEN

Soho Dial

Broadcast Excerpt · Programme No. 7 · one act, three tables · for voices only, with bells, static, and the sound of water under a bank

In the play: The Announcer; Pegge, clerk of the order book; The Keeper, a voice from the other side of the dial; The Mint Man, who will not sit down.

Scene One — The Order Book, at the night desk. A bell is struck once, twice, and then stops on its own.

THE ANNOUNCER: Programme No. 7 from the Soho Transmitter, Smethwick, a quarter past eleven, kept at the foundry the way a fire is kept — by someone who does not go home. We are going back tonight, on a long dial, to a man born in Birmingham on 3 September 1728, the son of John Boulton, who made small metal products — watch springs, lock plates, buckles — and who died when the boy was young, leaving the workshop and the name. The boy's name was Matthew. Listen for the bell.

PEGGE: (rustling paper) I am at the order book, page two hundred and nine. This is the river-bank wing of it, the long ledger the old firm kept for customers who ordered by the water, so the boats could be met at the wharf and the goods landed without argument. Most of the entries are ordinary — bolts by the hogshead, coins by the thousand. But there is one entry I have read a hundred times and I am going to read it again, word for word, the way the book has it. The clerk's hand is not our hand; it is a clerk's hand from over the water. The line runs: "The wing's standing orders of the river bank facing Windsor, Ontario ⚑ est."

THE ANNOUNCER: Read it once more, Pegge.

PEGGE: The wing's standing orders of the river bank facing Windsor, Ontario, the flag marked, established. That is the whole of it. It is a standing order kept against the river bank on the side that faces Windsor, Ontario — a bank the firm never stood on itself, but whose water came down to meet ours at the mouth. The ⚑ is the customer's mark; the “est.” means the order stood before the book was bound. Someone, some years ago, wrote the standing order of that far bank into our ledger so the wharf-keepers would know a boat was always coming. A foundry keeps such things the way it keeps fire.

Scene Two — Static. Under it, a machine: a slow, long-breathing engine, a valve opening like a door. A second bell, smaller.

THE KEEPER: (through static) He made the buttons before he made the engines, and he made the engines before he made the coins. In 1775 the partnership with James Watt was set down in writing; by the last quarter of the century there were hundreds of their engines turning — in cotton mills, in mines, in the long cold valleys where water froze in its channels and the steam did not. You have heard the Newcomen. Now hear the difference: our engine is a separate thing from its boiler, a small hot heart, and it drinks a fraction of what the old one drinks. That fraction is what mechanisation is made of. Not brass. The fraction.

PEGGE: The book will not let me stay there. Page two hundred and ten: 1795, machinery for the Royal Mint — the coining press, the mill, the edger — supplied against the old machines, and millions of pieces struck for Britain and for other countries who came to the wharf to watch. Before that, in 1773, the trial for the counterfeiting of soho coins, and the jury bringing in “not guilty, but we suspect —” and then stopping, because they could not finish the sentence. The order book has the whole affair in one line: the mint man came; the mint man stayed.

THE MINT MAN: (without sitting) I was at the bench in 1795. I pressed the first sovereign of the new machinery and I did not look up at the portrait on it. I looked at the strike. Twelve strikes to the rim, even as a plumb line. That is what they could not do before, and what they will not do after, which is why the machinery is where it is. Ask me about the engines and I will tell you about the coins. They are the same patience at different scales.

Scene Three — The dial winds down. The engine breathes slower. One last bell, low, from a direction the sound cannot come from.

THE ANNOUNCER: The dial reaches the bottom of its sweep at 17 August 1809, when Matthew Boulton died at Soho, aged eighty, in the house above the manufactory he had built from a small metal workshop into one of the largest works in the world. They buried him at Handsworth, in the old churchyard. The order book was closed the same month and never reopened. Every line in it stands as written, including the one no hand of ours set down — the standing order of the river bank facing Windsor, Ontario, the flag marked, established. A foundry keeps such things the way it keeps fire. You will hear nothing else tonight. The transmitter stays on; the night shift does not go home.

END OF EXCERPT · Programme No. 7 · the bells are counted in the margin — one, twice, once small, once low — and the book stays open at page two hundred and nine.
