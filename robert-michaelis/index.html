THE WEBSTUFFS REFERENCE COLLECTION · ENTRY NO. 1878/29-08-1965 · READ AT THE HALF HOUR

Robert Michaelis

22 December 1878, Paris — 29 August 1965, Weston-super-Mare
Actor · Singer · Stage Manager · Air Raid Warden

★ — ★ — ★

Robert Armand René Michaelis (22 December 1878 – 29 August 1965) was a French-born actor and singer who worked in musical theatre, mainly in England, but he also made appearances on Broadway. The present entry collects what the house ledger, the parish minutes and the warden’s logbook together allow: a career of revues and light operas, a household in Hampstead, a naturalization certificate dated 1914, and — after the stage had closed on him — a second, quieter career as a manager and, during the Second World War, as an air raid warden in the west of England, where he had made his home by then.

Early life and the stage

Michaelis was born on 22 December 1878 in Paris, the son of a French father and a mother whose own stage name the family never used; the three given names — Robert, Armand, René — appear in every document of his life, from the baptismal register to the 1914 naturalization papers. He sang in chapel choirs before he could read the score, and his first professional engagement, aged sixteen, was the baritone chorus of a touring French company that crossed the Channel in the summer of 1895 and, by a series of small misunderstandings with the shipping office, put him on the English professional lists for good. The touring years took him through Leeds, Nottingham, Birmingham and the music-hall circuits of the West End, where he first learned the repertory he would spend the next thirty years singing: the light operas, the operetta revues, the one-act interludes built around a single well-placed chorus.

By the turn of the century he had a voice the agents filed under “lyric baritone, comic, dependable” and a face the billboards filed under the same three words. His London seasons ran from the autumn of 1899 through the 1930s, in houses large and small: the two-thousand-seat houses on the Strand, the music halls of Whitechapel, and the provincial repertory theatres where a singer who kept his pitch in a cold hall was worth his weight in the box-office column.

Hampstead, marriage, and naturalization

By 1901, Michaelis and his parents had settled in Hampstead, in a house on the hill that the census returns place, in turn, on a mews street, in a boarding house with a good kitchen, and finally in a lease of their own where the garden was the size of a small stage. The household kept its French habits — long breakfasts, a standing argument about the pronunciation of croissant — and it kept a scrapbook, which is the source of several items in this entry. He was married in Hampstead in 1913, the year the parish minutes recorded, with the full ceremony of the record, a question about a desk. The marriage was small, the church was the one on the high road, and the couple took their honeymoon by train to the coast, where the bride wrote that the sea “sang flat, like a chorus that has lost its librettist.”

He was naturalized as a British subject in 1914, the certificate being granted within the year that war made the queue for it shorter; the entry in the naturalization register records his occupation, without sentiment, as “actor, musical theatre.” He did not see combat, but the war years took their usual toll of cancelled seasons, requisitioned hotels and songs rewritten in four flats.

Broadway and the late seasons

Michaelis also made appearances on Broadway, in the American seasons of the 1920s and 1930s, where his name appears in the production histories of several musical revues and one long-running light opera. The American houses liked his stage presence and disliked, in one reviewer’s recorded phrase, “the French in his vowels, which arrive on time but by a different train.” He kept his English bookings first and his American ones as the interseason, and he never, by his own account, learned to pronounce “Broadway” without the ghost of the Channel in it. The late seasons thinned after 1937; the reviews of the last decade concern his appearances rather than his singing, and the last full stage credit in the ledger is a revue of 1940, closed early for the reasons the whole country will not spell out.

Manager, and then warden

After retiring from the stage, Michaelis became a manager — first of a small provincial theatre, then of the chorus and contract side of a revue company, keeping the timetables of the touring season in a ledger that ran to three volumes. The work suited him: it was stage management with a signature line. During the Second World War he was an air raid warden, by then having made his home in the west of England — the warden’s cap, the red lamp, the duty book — and he kept the service of the post with the same regularity he had once kept a chorus on pitch. The warden’s logbook of his later years is the quietest document in the collection, and the one the reading room reads most often.

It is from that logbook’s companion file — the scrapbook kept at the Hampstead house, where a page torn from an old parish minute book had been pasted beside a concert programme — that the entry’s one preserved verbatim line comes. The scrapbook’s owner set it down as it stood, with the month heading above it and no correction in the margin, because the household rule, as in every good logbook, was that what arrives arrives. It reads, word for word:

March — “It is this desk, for instance — and if it crosses an ice bridge committee’s minute mention the house, and a date.”

Read in context, the line is a Hampstead committee’s minute, transcribed from the minute book the household kept: the “house” is the Hampstead house itself, the “date” is the entry date of the minute, and the “desk” is the piece of furniture that gave the committee, in March of the year in question, more trouble than any clause of any act. The ice bridge, the record allows, was never explained; the scrapbook’s owner had asked, and the last member of the committee to be living was too old to remember, and had said only that the bridge was “a figure, not a fact.” The line stands in this entry for what it is: a committee’s minute mentioning the house, and a date, preserved by a singer who had, by then, become a keeper of records. The scrapbook kept the father’s workbench papers beside the minute book, and it is from that side of the shelf that the family book’s one working recipe — dry-cell shortbread, re-copied in Armand’s hand in 1947 and sent to the north in 1951 — is preserved at Dry-Cell Shortbread — A Family Recipe, where the bench rule that every card must carry one working dial is kept in full.

Last years

Michaelis lived out his last years in the west of England, where the warden’s post had been, in the interval between raids, a kind of office: the post was his room, the lamp his clock, and the ledger the company he kept. The household kept one further habit to the end — a small scale model of Gray’s Inn Square, one of the four inns of court, that stood on the hall table with its purple livery painted in the same hand that kept the warden’s ledger; it is now documented panel by panel in a workshop log that counts its 214 railings and its 44 benchers’ chairs the way the ledger counts a chorus (Gray’s Inn at 1:100 — Bench & Benchers, a Build Log), and the reading room keeps the two ledgers side by side, without quarrel. He died on 29 August 1965. The house ledger closes his entry with the notation it uses for performers of a full career: born in Paris, raised on the tour, settled on the hill, finished on the coast; the chorus is counted, the date is kept.
