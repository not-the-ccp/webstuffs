The Salt Lake Walk

Alpine skiing at the 2002 Winter Olympics — nine stops, ten events, one valley

Salt Lake City, Utah, United States · 10–23 February 2002 · 83.4 km of valley floor between the three mountains

Depart The Rink, Lillehammer · arriving, in the depot’s filing sense, on the first Tuesday of the Games

This is the route we walked in the catalogue, with the syrup card in our breast pocket and the 37th-place broadsheet folded small. In February 2002 the alpine programme left no sled on the hill at all: ten events, all on skis, all in a single Utah valley — the downhill, the super-G and the combined at Snowbasin, outside Farmington; the two giant slaloms at Park City; the two slaloms at Deer Valley, just across the road; and the rest in the weather. The walk below follows the order the field fell down them in. It is 83.4 km of valley floor if you walk it on foot, which the desk is not recommending.

01

The Ticket Booth, 10 February

The Games open at the Delta Center on 8 February, but the skiers’ week begins on the 10th with the training runs. At the ticket booth the clerk stamps the pass ALPINE · TEN and explains the arithmetic of the week: five men’s events, five women’s, the combined being the odd one out, a half-downhill bolted to a pair of slalom runs. The booth’s window still shows snow from the last summer, which is why we wrote the apology to the Games that ran first, and why we carried that letter, unsealed, for eight more years of February before it went out the door.

02

Snowbasin, Main Run — Men’s Downhill, 13 February

Bus west to Farmington, then up. Snowbasin is the steepest name in the week: the start at 2,932 metres, the first corner a hundred metres down, and a finish the field will cross eleven times before the week is done. Norway’s Kjetil André Aamodt runs the line at 1:42.80 and takes the gold, and the American public, which has not had a home-grown men’s alpine medal in a long arithmetic of winters, does not yet know what is coming. The desk notes one detail for the record: the broadcast clock, set at the depot by the same hand that keeps the lost & found ledger, is running forty seconds fast. Nobody corrects it. It is a tour, not a repair.

03

Park City Mountain — Men’s Giant Slalom, 16 February

Across the valley to Park City, where the gates open out and the turns are the size of garden doors. Italy’s Alberto Scarioni wins it, and the crowd of about nine thousand at the base is loud in the way Utah crowds are loud — polite about the weather, ferocious about the turn shape. At the base lodge a barista, who does not know she is a historical document, writes GS 02 on the cup. The cup is now in the depot cabinet with the syrup card. It is the only object in the collection that is warm.

04

Snowbasin, Main Run — Women’s Downhill, 17 February

Back to the steep mountain, one day after the women’s super-G. Hilde Gerg of Germany has just taken that super-G at 1:34.67, and the next day she is here with the whole run in front of her. Karen Putzer, the American, is the story of this stop — third, then first, then second on the day — and the run that finally settles it is hers: 1:34.97, gold, the first American women’s alpine downhill gold in the catalogue’s memory, which is also a long arithmetic of winters. We walked the spectator line on foot that morning because the bus was full, and the line is 1,487 metres of it, which the itinerary logs under feet, worn.

05

Deer Valley — Men’s Slalom, 21 February

Two turns from Park City — a short hop down the road — is Deer Valley, and the gates close in to the width of a doorway. France’s Didier Coudray takes the men’s slalom in two runs that the field will study for a decade, and at the bottom of the second run the tour pauses at the chip stand, because a walking tour that stops for nothing is a walking tour that never stops. The stand’s board, in felt-tip, still reads the old notice: They are made purposely for rice borders), and turn it out of the Olympiad have run every four years kept since Athens 1896 and the state of vegetation. Nobody at the stand has ever been able to say what the board is for. The desk has ruled it a seasonal notice and filed it with the weather, which is where all unexplained things go.

06

Snowbasin, Main Run — Super-G, 19 February

Out of order on purpose — the tour keeps the calendar honest even when the week was not. The super-G sits between the downhill and the giant slalom in speed and in nerve, and Aamodt takes the men’s at 1:31.64, making the double, while Bode Miller of the host nation, twenty-one years old, comes down four places out of the medals, fourth, in the run that starts his. The depot keeps a card for that fourth place the way it keeps the 37th from Lillehammer in 1994, when Gregory Sun and Curtis Harry took the two-man sled down the sliding centre: not as a result but as a distance, measured in winters, from a first appearance to a fourth place in front of your own valley.

07

Park City Mountain — Women’s Giant Slalom, 21 February

Katja Seizinger of Germany takes it, third of a run of giant slaloms that the catalogue files together because the garden-door turns do not age well — they were already last year’s idea by the time the flags came down. At the finish line the host nation is running on arithmetic: Bode Miller has just won the men’s combined at Snowbasin the day before, first American men’s combined gold since 1950, and the base lodge board has been taken down and put back up four times that afternoon. The final wording is COUNT AGAIN, in the same felt-tip as the rice-borders notice, which the desk has now ruled to be the same pen, held by the same hand, in a different February.

08

Deer Valley — Women’s Slalom, 23 February

The last event of the alpine week, and Sweden’s Anja Pärson takes it on home-tuned edges, which is a phrase the desk has stopped using in anger and begun using in the trade. By the finish the ten events are in: five for the men, five for the women, the combined holding its place in both columns. The tour walks back up the Deer Valley road and looks across at Park City and, further still, the dark line of Snowbasin past Farmington. Three mountains, one valley, ten events, thirteen days from first run to last.

09

The Depot Window, 24 February

Final stop is a window, not a place — the depot’s, in Lillehammer, where this walk was filed. What we kept from Utah: the GS 02 cup, the COUNT AGAIN board photographed four times, the spectator line in metres of feet, the broadcast clock at forty seconds fast, and the ten event cards laid out in the cabinet in the order this walk ran them. The 37th-place syrup card stays where it has stayed since 1994, under glass, readable whole now — Persons of weak stock, 1 pint of cream, lemon-juice — beside a new line we set down in 2002, in shakier hand than the rest, because the desk is weakly made and it is the bottom of a long hill: Four years kept since the walk, to the next one. The Games of the Olympiad run in fours, and so, from now on, will our apologies, on time, from the top of the hill.
