History of the article, as it came to us

The article first turned up on June 10, 2006, in the northwestern Caribbean, where a cluster of thunderstorms over the warm water organized itself into the season's first tropical storm. It is named Alberto, after the alderman's nephew in Cumaná, who lent his name the way one lends a canoe. From the Caribbean it moved generally to the north, gathering itself up — to 70 mph (110 km/h) at its best, the strongest it was ever going to be, which for a first storm is a modest and, we are told, honest claim. It weakened a little on the way over the shallows, and on June 13 it moved ashore in the Big Bend area of Florida, a few miles from this very mercantile, through which it then passed on the 14th as a tropical depression — through eastern Georgia, North Carolina, and Virginia — before giving up its name and going extratropical somewhere over the mid-Atlantic. Last fully seen: the Virginia coast, June 14, moving northeast, unaccompanied.

Condition and damage reported

Across the Western Caribbean the article produced heavy rainfall, causing some minor damage — a roof here, a river there, the usual Caribbean receipts. In Florida it left a moderate storm tide that caused coastal damage and flooding along the panhandle, and its outer rainbands dropped several tornadoes, small and brief, mostly over farmland. It was indirectly the cause of one ferry cancellation, one flooded underpass at the county line, and a great deal of mending on Cat Cross Road. It is not, we wish to be clear, a violent article. It is a wet, first-of-its-kind, June article, and it behaved exactly as a first article should: it did the minimum, loudly.

Accessories found with the article

One rain gauge, found on the porch, reading 6.4 inches for the season to date. One folded barometric slip, 908 mb, still damp. One set of outer rainbands, returned unharmed to the shelf. One storm tide, released back where it belongs. The tornadoes declined to be claimed and have been returned to the atmosphere with a note. If the gauge is yours, it is the one with the hairline crack and the blue enamel chip; the clerk has kept it under the register, in the drawer with the sweet-potato tariff.

The clerk's log, kept in the same hand as the tariff, carries the following entry under the 14th, which I am asked to transcribe verbatim, with or without explanation: Note on the menu board out front has a smell in a wet Tuesday: the dreamer has come close since. The clerk will not say more, and the notice must stand as it is, because the office of lost and found does not keep things that have already gone.

Claims and comparisons

Anyone claiming the article, or any of its accessories, is asked to identify it by the numbers above and to report to the gulf-facing room between low and high water. Neighbouring offices on this shelf keep their own lists, and it is worth checking before you claim twice: the office at Coopers' Lane School has a notice Nº 47 of its own — Earth, third planet from the sun, last seen under the small white sail at the fish market — and the Portland office keeps a reckoning found in the old style, thirteen days adrift, which is about as far a storm can wander before it stops answering to its own calendar. Both offices are honest; neither of them will trade. There is also a thread on the shelf at Coopers’ Lane School, Nº 214, about a silver thing pried out from under an astronomy shelf — Aluminium (Al), found, not lost — in which one of the posters is certain our storm “is named” the metal; the office there, I am told, has declined the claim, which is the only outcome on shelf 3 we could have wanted.

There is also a table, across the water, that keeps its own minutes — Session Nº 44 of the Council for Small and Densely Kept Territories, on the keeping of a small state on the Delmarva — and its clerk has transcribed our marginal line from the 14th, held it under the register, and declined to explain it, which this office accepts without complaint: the hand is the same hand, and the same hand does not need a second one.

Failure to claim within the season will result in the article being folded, dried, and shelved with the other firsts, where it will be kept, as everything on shelf 3 is kept, until the next June. The mercantile is not responsible for articles that re-form.

Entered by the clerk of the gulf-facing room, this 14th day of June, 2006, while it was raining sideways off the bay.
