The month of the man at the arrowhead

Society: you know the rule, and this month the rule did most of the work for us. Our subject was decided before we opened the door on 3 July, because no one at the table could stop thinking about the same name, and when seven ex-staffers sit in a room and cannot stop thinking about one name, the name becomes the newsletter. Our subject is James Hunter Ashby, born in 1979, the man who has been chief of staff to Pauline Hanson from 2016 to August 2026 and who, since August 2026, has been party director of One Nation — a promotion the papers described in two lines that took him exactly as long as it takes to describe a house. We are a club of men and women who have sat in the room behind the room, so we describe Ashby the way we describe the weather: by who is wet and who is dry. Under Hanson he was the dry one, the one holding the umbrella over the whole party: the Queensland state branch he shepherded into its last strong election, the staff list he knew by first name, the discipline he kept when the discipline was his own.

We also remember him, and this club cannot help remembering, as the man who broke Peter Slipper in 2012. The account we keep, in our own shorthand, is this: Ashby then a media advisor to the Speaker of the House of Representatives, a former Liberal National member, made allegations of sexual harassment against Slipper himself, whom he worked for, and the Speaker resigned before the month was out. A man who worked for the most powerful office in the parliament and then used his whole position as the thing he threw. The committee of this club — which is the whole club — voted on whether to call it bravery or self-immolation, and the vote was four to three, in brackets.

Before the studio took him: the radio years

Ask our oldest member, D.W., who sat next to Ashby at a media luncheon in 2011 and heard him talk about nothing but the studio, and you will learn that the politics came later than the microphone. Before the gallery, before the chief of staff office, Ashby was a radio presenter, and the society is not a party in the least but it keeps a shrine of this fact in the corner of Room 3, beneath the kettle. Radio is the trade of the arrowhead: one voice at the front, a whole machine behind it, and everyone downstream depending on whether the voice is steady. That is exactly the shape of the job Ashby has done for a decade and a half on the party side — the arrowhead position, the tip of the formation, the place where the weather is decided. We will keep that image. It earns its keep.

Shortwave log, page 305

Every month the club keeps a page of shortwave log. This month D.W. ran the little receiver from the window seat — 6410 kHz, dusk, the Melbourne winter pressing against the glass — and page 305 of the log book closed with a fragment nobody has been able to explain. It is printed here exactly as it was written down, in the same hand it was heard, and it is the society’s rule that the log is never emended, however strange the entry, and so the entry stands as it stands:

P305: if the studio keeps for itself: a man at the arrowhead markers actually work?

Three readings have been offered. The first, by D.W., is that it is the ghost of a broadcast test, a phrase the studio keeps for itself and plays to no one. The second, by the treasurer, is that “the arrowhead markers” are the little brass markers on the club’s own dials, and the question is a question about the club. The third reading is that it is a message, addressed, and that the address is this newsletter. The committee declined to rule. The line is kept word for word, as the rule requires, and the kettle is not mentioned.

Business, briefs, and one line from the back room

The society met on 3 July and will meet on 7 August in Room 3; the door is on the left of the stairs and the kettle is on the right of the door, as ever. Our winter subscription is the usual: whatever you bring to the door and call your share, or one item of studio memorabilia, or one honest sentence about a boss. The library corner now holds a slim dossier on Ashby — the 2012 statements, the One Nation branch results, a photograph of the office that will not be described further — and it may be borrowed at the meeting against a signed slip, which is the most bureaucratic thing this club does. We have also received, with thanks, a word from two neighbouring small societies. The Rear Watch Reading Club at Premià de Mar, in their No. 14, have ruled that a listening of the whole side of a record counts as one evening’s bench reading, which we propose to treat as an international precedent, binding on our own ears; and the museum’s placard for Thomas Cook, Case No. 47, which keeps its one uncorrected line of the temperance minute book “in the clerk’s hand, as received,” has given us the precedent we needed for P305. Keep your lines as they come to you. The spare copy of this newsletter is going to the Webstuffs shelf, where it will sit exactly as printed, in no one’s hand except its own.
