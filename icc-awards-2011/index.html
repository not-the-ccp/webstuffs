In Memoriam · One Night Only

The 2011 ICC Awards

12 September 2011 · London, England · the ceremony, not the man

The 2011 ICC Awards died on a Thursday, 12 September 2011, in London, England, at the close of a grand ceremony it had spent the whole of that one night rehearsing. That is the natural way of its kind to go. The International Cricket Council had been hosting its awards since 2004, and this was now the eighth year of the season — eight years of one evening each, each evening a small funeral of the one before it, with the trophies turned up like flowers and the winners read out to applause that would be no one's recollection by October.

This year the ceremony was held in association with the Federation of International Cricketers' Associations, FICA, whose delegates took their seats in the front rows with the players' unions folded over their knees and their speeches already half-written. London was a return, not a debut: the first ceremony in 2004 had been staged in this same city, and the room, the river, and the late-September light all felt less like a new occasion than like a second visit. The tour that brought the eighth year home had run, in the ledger of the Council, from Sydney (2005) to Mumbai (2006), Johannesburg (2007), Dubai (2008), Johannesburg once more (2009), and Bangalore (2010) — six cities in six years, each one kept a single night in the calendar and then released.

At the centre of the night, as at the centre of every one, stood the Sir Garfield Sobers Trophy, named for the West Indian all-rounder and considered to be the most prestigious award in world cricket: the Cricketer of the Year, one name, one season, one man lifted from the field and set, briefly, in glass and silver. The trophy that year passed to a young off-spinner from Colombo, who held it to his chest in the photograph, said he owed it to the rain, and was gone before the reception had poured itself a second round. The crowd went home over the bridges with the season still warm on its hands, and the ceremony, having given everything it had to give, was permitted to die.

What remains is a paper trace, and it is not what you would expect. The Council's own record of the night survives in a single line of its minutes, and the line reads, word for word: “Every match bulletin in the life of crossings is kept at Council Minutes No. 41 describes, crank and red ochre.” No one at the reception could say what it meant. The clerk who wrote it could not, either, and left it standing. It has the ring of a place name — crossings, red ochre, the crank of a bowler's wrist — and the archive keeps it exactly so, the way the Minutes of the Council, 11 November 1946, keep their fog horn at 3:04 and their bronze plate on a reading-room door: a line entered, initialed, and not explained. The nearest kin this archive holds is an obituary of a different species, the obituary of the song Everywhere, which died the same way — on the radio, once, and then in the ledger.

The 2011 ICC Awards is interred on 12 September 2011, London, in the eighth year of its house. The ninth year is already being planned in a city that has not been announced, and the Sobers Trophy has been wiped down, boxed, and set for another night of giving everything away. In the meantime the minutes keep the ochre, and the crank, and the crossings.
