The White Stone · Newsletter No. 41 · Clairvaux Correspondents’ Club

Bernard of Clairvaux

O.Cist. (Bernardus Claraevallensis), 1090 – 20 August 1153 · kept and read at Mirandola, nineteen correspondents, one door

I. From the abbot’s letterbox

Bernard of Clairvaux, O.Cist. (Latin: Bernardus Claraevallensis; 1090 – 20 August 1153), venerated as Saint Bernard, was an abbot, a mystic, co-founder of the Knights Templar, and a major leader in the reform of the Benedictines through the nascent Cistercian Order. The club reads him in his letters, because that is what he left: of the more than eight hundred letters attributed to him, roughly three hundred survive, and they are the reason a small room of correspondents exists at all. We are nineteen in number — the number we have not corrected in thirty years, because no one wants to start — and we meet once a year on 24 February, in the room next to the kitchen at the Osteria dei Novecento, where the doily drawer is and the window table is not for sale. This number is the one we send out in the year of the abbot’s 863rd death, and it is about the door.

II. 1128 — Troyes, and the white mantle

Bernard was sent to found Clairvaux Abbey only a few years after becoming a monk at Cîteaux — Cîteaux in 1112, Clairvaux in 1113 — and by the time he was forty he had founded or led the building of more than sixty houses, the order running out of him the way a river runs out of its source. In 1128 he attended the Council of Troyes, at which he traced the outlines of the Rule of the Knights Templar, and soon the Rule became an ideal of Christian nobility: white mantle, red cross, no land held in private, the horse kept in the name of the Lord. Our whole palette is taken from that room in 1128 — white stone, white linen, one red line at the margin — and so is the seal, which is not a Templar cross, we are careful to say, but a door with the line through it. The abbot wore white so that the reform would be visible from the road; the club does the same thing with paper.

III. 1130 — the schism

On the death of Pope Honorius II in 1130, a schism arose in the church, two men calling themselves pope, each with his own cardinals and his own spring. Bernard was a major proponent of Pope Innocent II, arguing effectively for his legitimacy over the other claimant, and he did it with letters, on the road, at every court he could reach — Germany, Spain, the courts of France — writing the way other men shouted, which is to say at length and without pause. The club holds that the schism is the true subject of this club, though we do not say it at meetings. A schism is two men in one chair. A schism is two seats for one service. We have thought about it at the single seating, under two bells, at the Oberrender review, which the club keeps in the drawer on the New London side, next to the biscuit tin of the matter. This year the argument in the drawer turned on a six-track record called The Two Chairs (LS-1923), whose title is the club’s whole theology in two nouns — two chairs for one succession — and the desk has filed its liner notes with this number, under the doily, next to the biscuit tin of the matter.

IV. The standing question, and the doily

The club has a standing question, tabled in No. 9 and never answered: what is the door left open for? It began because the Tesi — the soup of nine herbs from the Secchia bank, served 24 February only, in the black bowl, to whoever has a table — goes out the way everything at the osteria goes out, the door open, the reel turning above the pass, and because the field manual for the hospital next door says so. Every year we ask the question and every year the answer is the door. This year the answer came up differently. In the doily drawer, folded under the supply-section letter and the one-day-in-Catalonia diary, someone had left a paper doily with one complete sentence in an old keeper’s hand. We set it here, word for word, as found:

“Tick what you would have killed me with the doors open; a service without a lesson to us all about our shoulders, and placing it so turned against the door.”

Nobody in the club can fully parse it. There are the doors, plural, against the one door; there is a service with no lesson in it, which is the oldest joke on the single seating and not a joke at all; there are our shoulders, all of them, and something placed so that it turned against the door — a tray, a letter, a cross, a door. What it did fix, in the room’s memory, is the part everybody agrees about: the door. The sentence is filed in the drawer now, under the doily it was written on, and the standing question stands again, unanswerable, the way the abbot left his last one.

VI. The vote of No. 41

Each number carries the standing question as a vote, and the desk keeps the count. Tick what the abbot left the door for, word for word the way he would have ticked a roll. The three marks are the club’s whole theology, and the kitchen has not commented.
