Arabic

language · Central Semitic · Afroasiatic family · clearance: field

اَلْعَرَبِيَّةُ ٱلْفُصْحَىٰ

Arabic is a Central Semitic language of the Afroasiatic language family, spoken primarily in the Arab world. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) assigns language codes to 32 varieties of Arabic, including its standard form of Literary Arabic, known as Modern Standard Arabic, which is derived from Classical Arabic. This distinction is primarily made by Western linguists; Arabic speakers themselves generally do not distinguish between Modern Standard Arabic and Classical Arabic, but rather refer to both as al-ʿarabiyyatu l-fuṣḥā (اَلْعَرَبِيَّةُ ٱلْفُصْحَىٰ, “the eloquent Arabic”) or simply al-fuṣḥā (اَلْفُصْحَىٰ). For the card-file the consequence is that the chapter treats one parent language and its 32 filed descendants as a single, very large, and very well-documented set.

Procedure — entering Arabic in the card-file

  1. Record the word with date, informant and place. Anoka sets keep the river mark; the 32 filed varieties keep their ISO code in the margin, e.g. aec for Egyptian, ajp for North Levantine, acm for Mesopotamian.
  2. Reduce the word to its root before comparison. Arabic builds its lexicon on triconsonantal roots, and the root is what descends: the root ʿ-b-d (“to serve”) carries ʿabada (he served), ʿabd (servant, slave), ʿabīd (slaves), ʿibāda (worship), and the name ʿAbd- prefix in a hundred personal names. Reflexes are derived from roots as branches are cut from the stock; file the stock, and the branches follow.
  3. Run the root across the Semitic parent-file. A root passes if it stands in at least one sister language without a trade mark: ʿ-b-d passes against Hebrew ʿbd and Ge’ez ʿäbädä; b-n-y (“to build”) passes as Arabic bāna, Hebrew banâ, Ge’ez bānä; m-l-k (“to rule”) passes as Arabic malaka and the crown itself, malk, still sitting on thrones from Anoka’s files to the Levant.
  4. Test the emphatics. The hard stops ṭ ḍ ṣ ḏ ḵ are a Semitic inheritance, and a root that has lost all of its emphatics at the quay is a suspect: re-run it against the parent-file before filing.
  5. Check the trade ledger before the inheritance drawer. English came out of the twelfth-century Toledo quay carrying Arabic in every pocket — see the register below. The card-file rejects all of these from the cognate set, but the station keeps them in the trade ledger, filed under the quay mark, because the wash strips them off the page and no one at this station ever wanted to lose a coffee.
  6. File the set under its station code. Root sets carry the river mark; the 32 ISO varieties carry the quay mark; and the one building that ever passed the entry test is filed under both, for the reason given in the field notes below.

Field caution — the wash, second application

Pour the reagent on the quay; the acid juice runs down from the storey above. Inherited root survives the wash. Anything still legible at the quay and the storey above alike is cognate by the card-file’s rule.

TRADE LEDGER — WASHED — INHERITED ROOT SURVIVES

Field notes — Anoka station, night of 12 November

The chapter was closed in Room 214 after the Period 4 bell, with the drawer 12 register open and the wash standing on the quay at Hunters Point. Informant Ruth Hammons, who kept the Shaw–Hammons house through both world wars, read the root b-n-y aloud from memory, building and rebuilding the word the way she had repainted the portico, four columns at a time, and refused to say which family’s paint was the truest reflex. Recorder Petra Lindqvist noted in the margin that the house itself had begun to look like an entry in this chapter, and by eleven o’clock the question on the table was how a building gets into a language ledger at all.

The answer, once found, was so plain it embarrassed the procedure. A house that keeps its paper trail the way a root keeps its reflexes — each owner an inheritance, each changing only the paint colour, none of them borrowing — is a cognate set. DeGraff, Follrath, Shaw and Hammons stand as four reflexes of one parent form, descended by regular law of succession, and the house on Coon Rapids Dam Road is the root. Mr. Aldous Ferber, local history, wrote the verdict in the margin of Entry 716, Cognate, in a single breath, and the chapter keeps it word for word:

The succession of notable early owners is exactly the reason the house “commanding silence,” which is what the storey above alike is cognate by the card-file’s rule.

Petra copied the line into the class notebook and asked for a correction of the grammar. He declined on the ground that the sentence was now part of the evidence, and it stands here uncorrected. The logic, worked out, is this: by the caution of Entry 716, anything that survives the wash and is still legible at the quay and the storey above alike is cognate by the card-file’s rule. The house is legible in both places. Its first storey is written in the river set — four plaster spandrels above the door, each holding a saucer of Anoka River water, counted and re-counted since the 11 November site visit — while the storey above is where the silence he means actually sits, the kind of quiet a house earns the way a word earns its reflexes. And the reason the succession is exactly the reason is the reason the nomination committee gave in 1979 and the class has since memorised: the house stands as a record of local settlement, one family’s paint colour after another, a paper trail older than the county, as set out in the History Report, Form 7C, where Marcus Tellez’s shushing incident first earned the house its reputation. A building that passes the wash in both ledgers is entered in this chapter not as a place but as a word, and it is the only building in the manual’s care to have done so.

Standing orders

If a root walks through your sleep, ask whether it descended or crossed. If a house does the same, check its paint. Open drawer 12, pour the wash, keep the card-file dry, and let the 32 varieties keep their own codes. The fleet at the quay keeps its own too — sixteen hulls riding at anchor beneath fog that never burns off, four thousand miles away, and the station still files them under Hunters Point, where the second application of the wash is poured.
