ENTRY · 17 October 1976 · BENCH CLEARED

The report on COINTELPRO went back to the school with a distinction and a question. The examiner had filed it in the house in the same manila envelope as Case 1989-LA-047, and Miss D. Farrow, at the term's end, had set the house one further piece of work: show me the difference, in object, between a roll and a blow. I took that to be a question about assassination, the subject the report never named, because the report's subject is the long and secret kind of blow — the one delivered to a movement, to a name, to a life, over years, without a witness, and without a roll kept for the dead. The other kind is the open one. The margin card on the bench says: assassination is the willful killing, by a sudden or secret attack, of a person, especially a prominent or important one, typically for political, social, or ideological reasons; assassinations may be ordered by individuals or organizations and carried out by themselves or their hired accomplices; the acts have been performed since ancient times; the one who carries them out is called an assassin. The build I set myself, then, is the two together in one frame: the roll, which is the roll of the Derby gate, and the blow, which is the one the company never had to keep a roll for.

ENTRY · 24 October 1976 · THE GATE

The two pylons came up first, because the specification says a memorial is not a thing to be looked at from a distance but a gate in the road, which the road itself goes through. The uprights are painted MDF, grained grey-brown with the kitchen's walnut finish, each standing a little over thirty feet at the bench's 1:87, on a base course with the date 1914–1919 in raised letters. Between them the gravel passage is real gravel, swept in at an interval I measured against the drip over the workbench, so the passage is laid down in the same forty seconds the drip has kept since before this log began. The station roof goes in the distance as a strip of black card on the bench shelf, and behind the gate I have put a strip of the garden, dark green, because a memorial must have a wall to its back if it is not to be seen from behind.

ENTRY · 2 November 1976 · THE DRUM AND THE ROLL

Inside the left pylon I have fitted a drum on a ratchet of twenty-eight teeth that turns one name at a time in order and no other order. The names come from the company's own roll as the specification records it — the 2,833, in the order the roll set them down, the way the bronze panels carry them — and they are entered on the drum under cover, the way the file is entered in the house, in whole. The panels themselves are another matter, and the specification is plain about them. Of the fixings, it says only: “The director of the company's own roll and no other.” I have no title there, so I have entered the names and left the fixings to that one desk and have not touched them. The drum is read at one name per click, in order, and the count in the base course is cut, not painted, so it may be worn by the same weather as the names it counts. At the end of the roll the ratchet stops, the needle lifts, and the side is finished.

ENTRY · 9 November 1976 · THE BLOW

The right pylon carries the other mechanism, and it is the one the log is named for. A single trigger, a wire spring and a clockwork drop, lifts the needle over the drum and lets one name card fall out of its row into the gravel passage, without turning the drum, without taking a number, and without a second visit. The point, which McIlwain says he understands though he does not say so, is the difference the whole build is for: the roll keeps its dead in order, one per click, the way the company kept them, and the blow takes one out of the order and gives it no number in the base course at all. I have pinned to the bench shelf the card of the open blows the drum has no room for: the blade among the senators, 44 BC; the single shot at Waterloo, 1815; the shot in the crowd at Buffalo, 6 September 1901; and the shot in Sarajevo, 28 June 1914, which the card notes is the blow that opened the war the 2,833 belong to. A blow of that kind is ordered or merely aimed, and its man is the one who needs no second visit, which is the whole of the definition and the whole of the craft.

ENTRY · 11 November 1976 · THE RUNNING

We ran the bench on the day the structure was five and a half years old, because a machine that keeps a day keeps it the way the specification says, without moving, painting or explaining. The drum turned from the first name in order; McIlwain stood at the bench and read the falling names the way the panels are read, from the road, in the direction of travel. At a name the house had agreed on, the trigger was pulled once, and the card dropped into the gravel at the forty-second count of the drip, and the drum did not move, and the count in the base course did not change, which is the whole of the meaning. The reel counter on attic unit No. 2 kept running through the running, as it keeps running through everything, and I noted its figures in the margin for the record: from 11,902 to 11,944, unasked, as it does.

ENTRY · 28 November 1976 · FAILURES

The spring of the drop broke on the seventh test and I rewound it at the bench, which cost me an afternoon and the kitchen's only spare pin. A name jammed at line 1,461 — a W. for the second of the roll — and the ratchet took it out sideways, the way a blow takes a man out of a row; I have left the card where it fell and have not re-entered it, because the roll is the company's and the jam is the machine's and neither should be tidied for the other. McIlwain asked, once, whether the machine was a picture of the program in the report, the long blow, and I told him the report is the picture and the machine is the bench study, and that the house does not explain its objects any more than the specification explains its kept line.

ENTRY · 14 December 1976 · FINISHED AND KEPT

The bench is finished and kept, as the house keeps things, in whole. The gate stands in its road, the gravel is kept free, the drum holds the 2,833 in the company's own order, and the trigger waits with its one spring, which is the kind of waiting the subject is made of. It is to be read once a week, one part per meeting, no flipping, the whole thing before the part. From the margin of the drum card, in a hand that is not mine, one line has been preserved word for word, and the bench has not attempted to solve it, the way the house does the work of a line the house did not write. It reads:

“The gravel is kept free, which is the only courtesy a gate can do to a man who is not in the roll.”

For the report that set this bench to work, in the same manila envelope on the same shelf, see COINTELPRO, School Report, History, Coopers' Lane School; for the specification whose pylon the left upright copies, the count and the gravel passage, see Specification No. 228,415; and for the index of the house, return to the Webstuffs index, where this log is entered under Bench, beside the reel counter, which runs through all of it without once being asked to.
