Build Log No. 12 · opened 29 January 2020 · ink: black, corrections: indigo pencil

A Corner Rig for Amazones Power

Turntable, amplifier, two speakers, one crate, and a record that arrived the morning after its release date

12.1 · The reason for the build

The record arrived on a Tuesday, the 29th of January 2020, the day after its release, and I did not have a way to play it. Amazones Power is the second studio album by Les Amazones d'Afrique, the West-African women's ensemble, and it came out on January 28, 2020 through Real World Records — almost three years after their debut, République Amazone of 2017. The sessions ran from May 2018 to April 2019, spread across three countries — Mali, France and Spain — and it was produced, like the first album, by Liam Farrell, the Irish-Parisian musician who records under the name Doctor L. What the liner notes spend most of their care on is the line-up: of the women who made the debut, only two came back — Mamani Keïta and Rokia Koné — and around them a number of new singers gathered, some from the continent of Africa, some from the African diaspora. A band that changes its faces almost wholesale and still sings as one throat is exactly the kind of record a room should be built to hold, and that is why the log exists.

12.2 · Parts received, and the one carton that spoke

Parts, as the invoice from the surplus house on Flanders Street itemises: one direct-drive turntable, second-hand, with a new cartridge fitted and aligned on Saturday; one tube amplifier, boxed, still wearing its shipping strapping; two bookshelf speakers, matched by serial number (L: 2241, R: 2242); eleven metres of speaker cable, the red-and-black braid I have wanted since the last build; and a crash pad, folded, because a corner rig in a shed is still a place where things fall. The amplifier’s carton was the odd one. On its back panel, printed small above the hazard triangle in a hand that was not the factory’s hand, was a line that no one at the surplus house would translate for me, which I keep here word for word:

Coat of Five Globes Deposited at the same nonsense at you as if the person who spots you.

I have sat with it, the way you sit with a sequence of moves before you lead the first one. My reading, and it is only my reading: someone is always the one watching from the ground, and the watching is part of the work. It hangs above the bench now, on a nail, cut out of the carton.

12.3 · Wiring, and the first spin

The wiring took a day and a half, which is long for two speakers, but the shed window draft was against me and the cable wants to be laid flat. Ground wire first, then signal, then the check that the needle is off before the power comes on — the standing order of every build in this log, and the one the last builder here learned the hard way. On the evening of the 31st I put the disc down. The opening of the side that carries the balafon came up clean, and by the second track the room had changed its mind about what it was for. I have listened to this album three nights running, start to finish, no track skipped, because the record is built like a traverse: it does not go up, it goes across, and if you stop in the middle you have stopped the argument. The new singers and the old two hold their places the way the two returning Amazones do, and the whole thing stays level the way a good traverse does — eleven metres of ledge, nothing to do but the holds.

12.4 · Cross-reference shelf

For the standing order on what it means to be watched while you work — the coil of red tape on the knife-edge crimp, the person on the ground who notes it in her own hand and will not say what she wrote — see the station diary of 14 July 2017, the traverse at the Arzl boulders, which I keep within arm’s reach of the amplifier because the shed and the bench have an agreement with each other. For the etiquette of receiving a piece that is performed with the listener rather than at him — no applause, book open at the rhymes, the fourteenth stanza never interrupted — see Chapter IX of the book of the Table of the Poets, which governs my listening as strictly as the standing order governs my wiring. And for the wider weather the album rides on, the West-African storm of chanted vocals and intersecting rhythms that came before it and paved the road from Lagos to Bamako, see the almanac entry No. 47 on Afrobeat, pressure high and holding, no front expected.
