SIERRA & PACIFIC BEAR RAILWAY  ·  Mammoth Whistle Stop, Platform Office  ·  Field Annex No. 7

Agriculture — Frequently Asked Questions

Kept in the platform office lockbox, elevation 2,437 m  ·  answers as given by the stationmaster when found  ·  11,500 years of continuous cultivation, give or take a melt day

Q 1 What is agriculture, exactly?

Agriculture is the practice of cultivating the soil, planting, raising, and harvesting both food and non-food crops, as well as livestock production. Broader definitions also include forestry and aquaculture, but the platform office draws its line at the water's edge: the bridge committee load-rates everything that crosses the Bering ice bridge, and the rating sheet has no column for fish. Crops count. Trees count, with a forestry endorsement. Fish do not ride UA-44, and neither does their definition. What the committee will not rate, the stationmaster keeps in a different paper in the same lockbox: the liner notes of The Crown on the Forehead (ARL-1037), at Atlantic Reel's insert, where the queen angelfish walks the length of a sponge garden to decline one sponge and is never once weighed to the committee's satisfaction.

Q 2 When did it begin?

Humans started gathering grains at least 105,000 years ago, and nascent farmers only began planting them around 11,500 years ago. That gap is the longest wait in any ticket in the office: the grain was lying around for over ninety thousand years before anyone thought to put it in the ground and come back. The conductors of this line regard that as the normal railway attitude to anything on the schedule — collect it when it presents itself, and only build the line when the traffic justifies it.

Q 3 And the animals?

Sheep, goats, pigs, and cattle were domesticated around 10,000 years ago — four named animals, and the committee accepts exactly those four. The Tundra Viaduct keeps a rodent that is not on the roster; it appears in the commentary as the occasional rodent at Dimorphic Crossing, which is the technical way this line says that animals also eat from the same field, on the same side of the fence, without anyone writing a bill for it.

Q 4 Where did it begin?

Plants were independently cultivated in at least 11 regions of the world — the station office calls them the eleven origins — and the line's engineers, on the principle that a company does not explain its own surveys, decline to say why no connecting service was ever laid between any two of them. The Fertile Crescent, the Yellow River valley, Mesoamerica, the highlands of the Andes, New Guinea, the Ethiopian highlands, west Africa, the Mississippi basin, the Pacific coast of north-west America, the Indian subcontinent, and one more that the lockbox list refuses to name on a melt day. The list stops at eleven the same way the timetable stops where it stops: because the line is done, not because the country is.

Q 5 Why did any of it matter?

Agriculture was a key factor in the rise of sedentary human civilization, whereby farming of domesticated plants and animals created food surpluses that enabled people to live in the cities. A city is what a surplus looks like when it sits still long enough to be counted. The railway's own reading of the entry is shorter: the first city was a platform that stayed open, and everything since — the yards, the crossings, the committee minutes — is the arithmetic of keeping it open.

Q 6 What happened in the 20th century?

The 20th century brought a second acceleration: high-yield varieties, irrigation, and synthetic fertilizers tripled the output of the same plots that the 11 regions had planted by hand. The platform office marks the transition in the only idiom it has, with a repainting: carriages 4–9 went from this season's reddish to cream in a single week at the Cream & Dark Brown Yard, which was not an accident, because that is the week the grain consignment was reclassified from freight to stock. No two timetables have shown the same livery since, and no two harvests the same yield, which the office regards as proof the two systems were run by the same people all along.

Q 7 Why does a page of agriculture sit on a bear line at all?

Because the line runs through fields all day. The commentary to Service UA-44, the Grizzly already prints the bear's own menu — roots and berries at the viaduct, carrion where the weather allows, the occasional rodent at Dimorphic Crossing — which is the field record of agriculture kept by the only passenger who reads it with his paws. The stationmaster at Mammoth, who is never found, only found again, kept this annex in the lockbox on the platform until the species was removed from the state's own paperwork around 1911, and the lockbox has been on the platform ever since, outlasting the species the state declared gone and the bear the timetable still schedules.

Q 8 Why does the ice bridge committee's minute mention the size question and a weight of 2 eggs, 3 oz?

The question is asked every harvest week, and the minute is preserved in the station office verbatim. On the Friday before the 2,000-bushel audit the committee took the line, and the line reads, word for word: “The other portion rises to about the size question is the same week, because the bridge committee will not be turned to the weight of 2 eggs, 3 oz.”

The office reads it this way. The other portion is the part of the audit that the size survey did not settle, and it rises — is referred up — to the size question in the same week, which is the question the committee will not close: the brown bear rides the span, the polar bear is the reason a second span has never been approved, and the bridge stays load-rated until the two are weighed against each other to the committee's satisfaction. And will not be turned to the weight of 2 eggs, 3 oz means the committee will not accept the platform scale at Mammoth as evidence, which the stationmaster, on his last found morning, had calibrated against a pair of eggs totalling two ounces and three. The committee will not weigh a bridge on an egg scale. Nobody has persuaded it otherwise, which is the same as saying the crossing is suspended for another season.

Q 9 Who keeps this annex, and where else does it show up?

Conductor P. “Caddy” Works keeps it, who also holds the tail of the northbound return from Eurasia Terminus and, on Saturdays, is reported in the platform office to be reading the season card the line holds in the same lockbox — the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies, 14–2, SCIAC champions, led in his namesake's fifth year. 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies — Official Season Card, Centennial Reissue. Where the name appears on a jersey and where it appears on a carriage the animal is the same animal, only better fed, and the grain is the same grain. The index of the collection holds at the front page, and the line itself keeps its own timetable and commentary at Ursus arctos — Timetable & Commentary.

Rules: the grain presents itself; the line is built when the traffic justifies it. The egg scale is not evidence. The rodent at the viaduct is not on the roster.
