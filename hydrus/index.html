Entry No. 114 — Hydrus, the Water Serpent

male water snake · deep southern sky · visible whole from the latitude of 35° S southward, circumpolar from about 40° S · best aspect: the sun low in the north, the glass cold, the log open

First — The Observation

Set the glass at the Cape in the height of their January, the sun sunk low in the north, and the serpent is coiled under the teapot of Sagittarius, head raised toward the knees of Centaurus, tail dragging along the milky water toward the Southern Cross. From Saltfleet it never rises at all — the loft glass, on the summer solstice, clears it by no more than a boat's height above the sea-line — and so this entry is kept, as our southern entries are kept, on the faith of the observers who do see it, with their barometers and our own.

The head is bright enough for a dark night: Alphair, the alpha star, stands at 2.08, and gamma, in the heart, at 1.82, the brightest of the whole line, with epsilon at 2.06 not far off. Below the fourth magnitude the body runs thin — Lacaille's table, drawn at the Cape in the two years before 1754 and published with the Bayer letters in 1756, gives the serpent 41 stars, of which three are in the head, twenty-six in the body, and twelve in the tail. That is a small serpent. It is, in the keeping of this almanac, a small serpent by design: the constellations of the southern waters were made to fit the water, not to fill it.

Second — The Record

The serpent was one of twelve constellations made by Petrus Plancius out of the observations of Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who had sailed through the deep latitudes and reported back what the sky did down there that no northern star-book had ever promised. Plancius set the new figures on a celestial globe of 35 cm (14 in) in diameter, and the globe was published in Amsterdam in the late 1597 or the early 1598, by Plancius and Jodocus Hondius, the first paper the world had of the serpent. Six years later it came into a book: the first depiction in a celestial atlas is Johann Bayer's Uranometria of 1603, where the serpent is drawn in ink, with Latin, in a posture that has not changed since. The French explorer and astronomer Nicolas Louis de Lacaille came down to the Cape after, charted the brighter stars with his own instruments, and in 1756 gave them the letters by which this page addresses them — alpha, beta, gamma, delta, epsilon, zeta, kappa, iota — so that a keeper in Lincolnshire may call the water serpent by its names and still mean the water.

Third — Signs and Seasons

  • a. When the tail of Hydrus dips below the cross-staff at Cape Point and the barometer stands at 29.84 and falling: a squall by the morning glass, from the south-south-west, two days of it.
  • b. When Alphair is due south at the eighth hour and the sea lies flat as a ledger: hold the log dry for seven days; salt the stores.
  • c. When gamma Hydr dims behind the milky band at mid-watch: rain of the gentle kind, good for the beds, bad for the gaffs.
  • d. When the serpent and the Cross stand together, whole, above the horizon at one watch: it is the height of their summer; the northern keeper should take the entry as it is given and not argue the latitude.

Fifth — The Keeper's Note

There is a marginal line in the 1756 table, in a hand that is not Lacaille's — I do not believe it is anyone's hand, so early is it — and the line reads, word for word: Why it matters, and the record before they are arranged, the fine spaces between them. It has taken me years to answer it, and the answer is this: the serpent matters not for the bright head, Alphair, but for the gaps. We keep the fine spaces between the stars the way we keep the space between the last frost and the first thaw — they are the weather, not the sun. The stars are only the pins; the water between them is the tide.

And for those who would weigh a fish named for the water against the water itself, the card box keeps Auntie Myung-sook's Porgy on the Tide-Line, Card No. 7, where the fishwife rules that no register is allowed to stand between the fish and its landing.
