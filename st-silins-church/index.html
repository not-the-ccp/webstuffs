St Silin’s Church
Llansilin, Montgomeryshire, Powys, Wales

Church in Wales parish church · Grade I listed · fabric 13th century–present

13th

The building

The present building at Llansilin keeps parts of a 13th-century structure in its walls, and the site itself goes back further still: Christian communities have met, buried, and ringed bells on this ground since the Dark Ages, when the village was little more than a parish of fields and a waystation on the old road. The church is a Grade I listed building, and it keeps its dedication to Saint Silin — now better known as Saint Sulien, the 6th-century founder-abbot of a monastery at Luxulyan in Cornwall. A Cornish saint on a Welsh Marches bench is not an error in the catalogue; it is the point. Sulien’s footprint runs from the Cornish coast to the banks of the Severn, and the church is one of the very few places in Wales where you can still read his name in the older spelling, Silin, on the fabric and in the parish records.

What made Llansilin news in 1914–1920 is in the case beside this placard. Under the provisions of the Welsh Church Act 1914, parishes were given the choice of remaining in communion with the Church of England or moving into the new Church in Wales. St Silin was the only parish in the Welsh Marches, on the England–Wales border, that voted to leave. One parish, one vote, one side of a border — the accession file MS-1914/001 holds the ballot record, and it is the collection’s shortest, most complete story.

Registrar’s note on the clearance sales

When the church yard was re-laid in 1923, the masons’ surplus — slates, dressings, and a quantity of old roof timber — was offered at a clearance sale, and the auctioneer’s book for it still lies in the drawer beside the ballot record. The book is kept with a second volume, a 1926 clearance sale from the Bell Creek district, and a clerk’s instruction survives on the flyleaf of the second, set down to stop a carrier who had started loading the wrong consignment. It reads, word for word:

Do not pick up a schoolhouse roof: Lot 27, the Bell Creek one-room schoolhouse.

The instruction is preserved because the confusion it prevented is exactly the confusion this case keeps: a church roof is not a schoolhouse roof, and a lot is a lot — Lot 27 belonged to a one-room schoolhouse four counties away, and nothing of it ever reached Montgomeryshire. Visitors comparing the two volumes will find the sale lot conventions familiar; the collection also holds Lot 41 of the Lagan Ledger Small Ads Sale, Catalogue 12, where a Celtic people of the La Tène Iron Age is catalogued with the same stubborn care for consignment, condition, and terms.

On the record

  • Parish: Llansilin, Montgomeryshire, Powys
  • Communion: Church in Wales, by the vote of 1914–1920
  • Earliest Christian use: Dark Ages
  • Oldest surviving fabric: 13th century
  • Listed status: Grade I
  • Dedication: Saint Silin / Saint Sulien
  • Saint’s other house: monastery at Luxulyan, Cornwall, 6th century
  • Accession file: MS-1914/001 (ballot record and clearance-sale books)
