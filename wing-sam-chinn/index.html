Wing Sam Chinn
Architect of Chinatown & the Seattle Skyline

November 16, 1897 – December 27, 1974

1 · The Arrival

Wing Sam Chinn was born to a Chinese immigrant family in San Francisco on November 16, 1897, and moved to Seattle as a boy, growing up among the laundries, boarding houses, and import stores of the old Chinatown on Washington Street. In 1922 he received a bachelor's degree from the University of Washington — the first Asian-American to graduate in architecture in Washington state — and went straight to work as a draftsman for the architect Andrew Willatsen, who had learned his trade from Frank Lloyd Wright and brought the Prairie School west. Low eaves, long horizontal lines, windows like pages of a book: Chinn learned to draw a house so that it would look as though it had grown out of its lot.

From 1927 to 1934 he worked with Thomas, Grainger & Thomas, and it was there that he was independently contracted to design the Chong Wa Benevolent Association Building at 513 South King Street, even though he did not yet hold an architecture license. He sat for the examination and received his own license in 1935.

2 · The Association Building

The Chong Wa Benevolent Association Building — society hall, Chinese school, and temple roof under one cornice — wears its cinnabar columns and upturned eaves where Eighth Avenue meets South King Street, in the heart of the International District. Chinn drew it as his own commission while still employed at Thomas, Grainger & Thomas: a three-story brick block whose base serves the street and whose crown belongs to another skyline. Look up for the painted frieze, the bracketed cornice, and the characters set into the parapet. It remains the best-known work of the first Asian-American architect licensed in Washington state.

3 · A Walking Tour of His Seattle

Allow ninety minutes. Begin at the Chinatown-International District gate on South King Street and follow the grade down toward Jackson.

Stop 1 · Chong Wa Benevolent Association Building

513 South King Street · 1929–31

The society hall itself: the cinnabar columns, the bracketed cornice, the temple roof that Chinn drew as an unlicensed draftsman and the city later adopted as its own.

Stop 2 · Hom House

1214 Twelfth Avenue South · 1941

Prairie School horizontal lines in brick and art glass, drawn for Frank Hom the year the regrade threatened the block. The eaves still throw their shadow across the sidewalk at four o'clock.

Stop 3 · Chinn Apartments

1700 block, Beacon Avenue South · 1948

A courtyard block of sixteen flats, cream brick with a jade-green tile band, built for the families the 1941 regrade displaced — and for the ones the arterial never took.

Stop 4 · Maynard Market storefronts

Jackson Street, between Seventh and Eighth · 1936–39

Terra-cotta trim and recessed entries; three of the four shops survive, including the former Chong Wa bookstore at number 619.

4 · From the Planning Record

The Plaintiff's proposed alteration would take a family.

Seattle Board of Public Works, rezone petition Z-41, Twelfth Avenue South, 14 May 1941 — entered into the record, and denied.

The alteration would have widened Twelfth Avenue South into an arterial across the Hom House lot. The Board heard the line above, denied the petition, and drew the arterial two blocks east instead. Five of Chinn's Seattle buildings survive; two were lost to the Interstate 5 regrade in the early 1960s, along with their addresses. What remains is worth a walk, and worth a look upward.

Keep walking — the verbatim record in which one marginal line settled an estate is kept at In re the Estate of Paul Pinna, and the ornament of the Association Building belongs to a longer canon at The Vermilion Seal.

5 · Practical

The trail is free and open daily. Start at the gate on South King Street, allow ninety minutes, and carry this brochure — it creases at the right places by the third stop, and the creases are the map.

Wing Sam Chinn died December 27, 1974, and is buried at Lake View Cemetery on Capitol Hill. His drawings are held by the University of Washington Libraries Special Collections. Issued by the Seattle Chinatown Heritage Trail desk, Brochure No. 14.
