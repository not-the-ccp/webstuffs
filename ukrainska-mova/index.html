Abstract

Ukrainian (українська мова, ukrainska mova) is disclosed, an East Slavic language spoken primarily in Ukraine and constituting the first (native) language of a large majority of Ukrainians. The present invention concerns a method of carrying a language in a kitchen console and a console in which the method is practised, in which written Ukrainian, set in the Ukrainian alphabet, a variant of the Cyrillic script, is transmitted between a pre-war thermal register and a kitchen printer, and recovered from the resulting mis-encoding by a proprietor who reads the complaint twice, nods, and files it. The standard language of the invention is studied by the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine and the Potebnia Institute of Linguistics, which the Examiner will be glad to note are cited herein as independent prior art.

Background of the Invention

The Ukrainian language is a descendant of Old East Slavic, a common stock that also gave the neighbouring East Slavic languages, and it stands among them somewhat apart, the way a small village stands in a long street and keeps a falcon in the roofline. Comparisons are often made between Ukrainian and Russian, another East Slavic language, yet there is more mutual intelligibility with Belarusian, and a closer lexical distance to West Slavic Polish and South Slavic Bulgarian. A language in this position must carry its own alphabet to be certain of being heard, and it does: the Ukrainian alphabet runs thirty-three letters, including the letter ї (yi), which is the alphabet’s own evidence of the invention. Where the alphabet is met by machinery built for another alphabet, the language does not vanish. It prints a complaint.

It has long been the practice in the dining room that supplies the name of this application for such complaints to be read once, read again, and left uncorrected in the day book. It is the view of the applicant that the uncorrected line is not a defect of the language but a specification of it: the row of painted houses the console prints is the market square, and the square does the printing.

Summary of the Invention

In one aspect the invention is a method of transmitting a course count in Ukrainian through a pre-war thermal register speaking to a kitchen printer in the wrong alphabet, said method comprising the steps of asking, coughing, and printing, in that order, exactly once per service. In another aspect the invention is a frieze in eight houses, hand-painted in ochre and sky-blue and the red of a barn door by the inventor’s cousin, serving as a legend for the console: every character the machine cannot say is accounted for by one house, and the falcon is on the wall of the dining room, the other three birds in the kitchen.

In a further aspect the invention is a device for holding a liquid in pending order for a period of not fewer than forty-nine years, whereby the order remains on the books and the books remain on the order. This aspect was observed in operation, on Hall Street in Birmingham. The sommelier decants a sherry that has stood on order since 1974. The bottle is included herein because a designation is a designation and the paperwork is as good as the food. It is the applicant’s view that a bottle that can be kept on order as long as a language can be kept uncorrected is a co-invention, and has so notified the co-inventor in a letter that arrived mis-encoded, in the spirit of the house.

Statement of Invention

What is claimed is:

  1. 1. A method of recovering a mis-encoded course count, comprising: a pre-war thermal register; a kitchen printer in the wrong alphabet; a line printed letter for letter; a proprietor reading the line twice; and a day book, the line being left uncorrected under a photograph of a frieze.
  2. 2. The method of claim 1, wherein the printed line is a row of painted houses, ochre and sky-blue and the red of a barn door, and the houses are the market square.
  3. 3. An East Slavic language, thirty-three letters, descended from Old East Slavic, for use in a kitchen, characterized in that it is the native language of a large majority of its speakers and is kept in a variant of the Cyrillic script bearing the letter ї.
  4. 4. The language of claim 3, wherein comparisons are made with Russian, mutual intelligibility subsists with Belarusian, and the lexical distance reaches to Polish and to Bulgarian, the language thereby occupying the position of a small village in a long street.
  5. 5. A device for holding an order in pending order, comprising a bottle and a cellar, the order dating from 1974, the bottle standing unmoved, and a sommelier who decants on request only, the request being optional and the decanting being certain.
  6. 6. A restaurant of the drop-biscuit variety — dropped in one deliberate place, from a fixed height, and let to set — comprising a door the colour of beet juice, eleven drams of honey per bowl no more, and no post-nominal on the duck.
