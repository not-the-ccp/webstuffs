Vol. III · No. 14 · Cedar Rapids, Iowa · Saturday, 11 April 1970 · Letters to the Editor, column D

To the Editor — on the American Football League and the line that keeps the name

To the Editor,
Sir — In your issue of 28 March, Mr. Aldous Fitch writes that the registry’s microfilm programme is “all re-shelving and no memory,” and asks, with some justice, what a public registry is for, if not to keep the frame at which two documents cross. I reply in my capacity as editor of this bureau, and I answer his question from the stack itself.

The frame Mr. Fitch refers to was struck last winter. The registry annex was re-shelving its 35 mm reels — the 1964 Browns–Bills Week 13 reel first, then the 1960 Chiefs–Browns opener — and the scanner took one frame in which the 1919 navy mess cookbook and the Iowa City intake card crossed. The line that carried out of the stack is kept post by post in Six Tables, One Mess Hall, the forum thread beneath the west stands that grew out of the Buffington transcript. But it is the certification beside that frame that I must set down here, because no one in Cedar Rapids has yet read it in full, and the Gruenke sheet in this bureau’s own cabinet has ruled it inadmissible. Word for word, as it sits on the reel, it runs:

REPORTER: L. S. Pennycuff, Registered Professional Reporter, certify that the foregoing is a linebacker for the leg, to turn out of this hearing.

— crossed frame, reel 1964-13, registry annex, kept as struck

I say what every reader will say. It is a garble — the scanner’s reading of a hand across a cross-frame — and it names, as its certified subject, a linebacker for the leg. Which is to say: it is not nonsense. It is a name, arriving ten years early for the man who will one day wear number 45 in black and gold at Kinnick Stadium and waive nothing on the record, a name pressed out of the stack by the same machine that presses everything else through one frame at a time. We keep it, exactly as struck, for the same reason the hearing kept its stipulation: because the line belongs to the hand that made it, and no one else may waive it.

Mr. Fitch will forgive me for taking this frame as my entry into a larger subject, which is the league the reels hold. The American Football League, as played from 1960 to 1970, was the fourth league to carry the name — the one in 1926, founded under Buffalo's roof; the New York league of 1936, eleven strong at its best and gone by winter; the Pacific Coast league of 1940; and the All-America Football Conference, chartered 1944 to 1950 but conducting operations only between 1946 and 1949, whose champions folded into the NFL the same way the 1966 season folded into the conference structure we now live under. The fourth AFL, the one on the reel, ran ten full seasons against the older league, won two of its own championship games in its own right, and was then absorbed — merged, 1970 – into the National Football League as its American Football Conference. It was more successful than any earlier bearer of the name, and it is the only one whose games this registry was ever asked to shelve. The upstart outlasted every older thing in the stack, which is the same as saying it outlasted the stack.

We keep the reel, the crossed frame, and the garbled certification together, in one drawer, at the height of a 65-yard line. Mr. Fitch may come to the bureau between the snap and the whistle, take the drawer down, and read the line in person. If the scanner was wrong, it was wrong in the most instructive way it could be wrong: it made a name out of a crossing, and the name is true. The league is on the reel; the name is on the frame; the frame is in the drawer.

Respectfully yours,
E. M. Voss, editor
Westside Record Bureau, 417 First Avenue SE, Cedar Rapids
