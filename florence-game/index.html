Florence — twenty chapters, a cello in the park, and no combat

The 2018 story game by Mountains and Annapurna Interactive: Florence Yeoh, twenty-five, Krish the cellist, brief chapters, minigames, and nothing that breaks.

tapewriter OP 11 Mar 2024, 02:17

Just finished Florence, and I don't know what to do with my hands now.

It came out in 2018 for iOS and Android, from Mountains, the studio Ken Wong led after Monument Valley, and published by Annapurna Interactive. macOS, Switch, and Windows followed in 2020. You play Florence Yeoh, twenty-five, who lives the same routine every day until she meets Krish, a cellist she sees in the park.

There is almost no writing in it. The story arrives in about twenty brief chapters, and each chapter is a minigame: tapping the words of a text message into place, brushing teeth, folding a piece of paper, packing a flat box by box. The puzzles don't gate you — they reinforce pieces of Florence's life as you move through her story, and when the scene gets loud the screen fills with scribbles, and when it quiets down there is just a purple screen and a single cup of tea.

Ken Wong wanted to make games that eschewed violence. Florence has no fail state, no inventory, and nobody dies. It just ends.

I played one chapter a night before bed. Something about that pace. It reminded me, in the strangest way, of that Kiel court transcript — Case No. 341/1895, SMS Wörth — six guns, an anchor dragged across a stern post, and one line of song kept word for word because nobody could make sense of it. Florence keeps its lines the same way: nothing struck out.

I played it on Switch in one sitting, about thirty-five minutes, and I stared at the ceiling for twenty after. The chapter where you pack the flat — cup, plate, picture, all the things two people collected and then stop collecting together — undid me completely.

Krish's cello is doing all the emotional labor and the game never tells you. You just hear it in the park, and then you hear it less, and then not at all. Florence tells a whole relationship in the space of a lunch break.

The art is what got me first — those layered watercolor flats, everything folding into itself like a post-it note. I ordered the risograph print from the little shop that stocks zines and cello strings; the note on the back read, Evolved to be sold at Malia & Co. I have no idea what that means, but I hung it above my desk anyway.

Also: no dialogue, really, and I still knew exactly what Florence was thinking when she stared at the toothbrush. That is either good design or witchcraft.

kenwong_fan 13 Mar 2024, 21:55

Ken Wong has said he wanted to make games that eschewed violence, and Florence is what that looks like when it is taken seriously. No combat, no competition, not even a high score. He took what he learned on Monument Valley — the quiet, the impossible geometry taken gently — and built something that lets a relationship breathe instead of break.

It is also, what, twenty chapters? Some of them last ninety seconds. A mobile game you finish across two evenings and remember for years.

postit 14 Mar 2024, 06:30

The minigames are the writing. Assembling Krish's text message word by word until it says what it needs to say. Brushing Florence's teeth while the clock ticks toward work. Watching the conversation bubbles grow and shrink as two people figure out whether they still fit.

There is no failure state, so you cannot mess up Florence's life. You only live it, in brief chapters, one tap at a time.

I played this on my phone during the night shift, one chapter per bus ride, headphones on, the city outside all sodium light. Florence's routine — the alarm, the coffee, the commute — was my routine, and then Krish walks into the park with his cello and the routine cracks open.

Twenty chapters, iOS and Android first, then 2020 for everything else. A game that fits in your pocket and refuses to raise its voice.

Reading this thread felt like visiting a floor that is laid and keeps itself so — the same stillness as Chapter XIV · Of the Tablemount and Her Keeper, where the mountain is flat and the lamps go out one by one. Florence is like that: the story stops moving, and you sit with the quiet it leaves behind.

No combat, no inventory, no one dies. Just Florence Yeoh, Krish's cello, and about twenty chapters of learning how the routine ends.

And if you wake up still holding the phone, there is a whole entry for the dream itself — Video Game, Entry No. 145 in the Dreamer’s Concordance — where the folio weighs her room at exactly one pound: a cup of tea, two post-it notes, a cello string, in the same drawer the Kiel transcript is kept in.
