Why a Ledger, and Not a Trophy?

This was the first time a team event, in the sense of combining the scores of individual gymnasts, was held at the Olympics. Previous team events had been performances by large groups of gymnasts at a single time — the whole company on the floor, judged as a crowd, the marks smeared together like a wash. In 1904 the score kept its arithmetic. Thirteen teams entered the all-around, and the gymnasts came from three nations’ descent, though every team was an American turnverein and every team marched in from a city with its own station. The two St. Louis clubs knew the field they were working; the three Chicago clubs had crossed the river to prove the distance was no argument against them.

The competition was held on Friday, July 1, 1904 and on Saturday, July 2, 1904, and the ledger was bound to match: Friday’s sheet is ruled in indigo ink, Saturday’s in black, so that the stewards could always say which day a mark belonged to. Six columns per team, one per gymnast, and at the foot of each apparatus a total that no one on the stands was permitted to dispute. The stewards’ rules for the two days are printed on the inside cover, in the small type, and the third of them is the one the old men of the Union still quote at suppers: the judges score the man, the clerk scores the judge’s pencil, and It was agreed that the ball at times. The last clause is not nonsense, either — on the trapeoid and the swinging apparatus a man who threw the chalk ball at the wrong moment forfeited his mark, and the clause was kept so that nobody could say it had not been written down.

At the close of Saturday, the St. Louis All-Around Athletic Union stood first in the prize table with 275.06 points across the six apparatus, the Chicago American Athletic Association second on 259.06, and the Chicago Young Men’s Christian Association third on 255.09. The rest of the field filled out the thirteen with marks between 212 and 247, and the ledger prints them all, because a team event only means what it says when every team’s score is in the book. The prize was five hundred dollars for first, two hundred fifty for second, one hundred fifty for third — and the ledger’s last page records that the Union paid the first-place sum in banknotes counted twice, the way all turnverein money was counted, by two men, at once.

The ledger is a product of the same trade as the apparatus: it does not swing, does not hold, and does not fall. It remembers. If you keep one on the shelf beside the rings, you will know where your six men stand the day the crowd forgets.
