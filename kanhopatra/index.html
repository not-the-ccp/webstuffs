1. On the habitat

Pandharpur lies in the open plains of Solapur district, and the shrine of Vithoba sits at its centre the way a heart sits in a chest — low, warm, and responsible for the movement of everything else. The Varkari, who are the dominant population of the place, walk to it from a radius the guides give as a hundred and some villages, barefoot, in the old ratha rhythm, and the precinct receives them the way a nest receives a brood: without ceremony, on the assumption that they were never away. Little is known about the present subject. This is the honest first line of every account of her, and I have copied it here before anything else, because a naturalist's first duty is to record the thickness of the mist before describing the bird.

What the mist lets through: that she was a courtesan and a dancer, according to most traditional accounts; that she wrote in two meters, the ovi couplet and the abhanga; that the subject of both was Vithoba, the patron god of the Varkaris; and that she died in the central shrine of the temple, in the 15th century, having refused the household of the Badshah of Bidar. The samadhi is where the death was recorded in stone: a small raised place near the central shrine, and the single notable fact in the architecture of the precinct, which is that it is the only mausoleum of any person admitted within the temple's walls. The samadhis of Tukaram and of Jijabai stand outside, at the gate and in the lanes, and the distinction has been defended, century after century, with the patience of a tree.

2. On the display, and its refusal

The traditional accounts concentrate on the end, which is fair: in a habitat this hot, the end is what the witnesses can agree on. The Badshah of Bidar, the accounts run, wished her for his household, and the court came for her as a net comes for a heron. Her display, in the moment the accounts are at their fullest, was the refusal. She chose to surrender herself to Vithoba in the central shrine rather than become the king's concubine, and she died there, inside the walls, in the god's presence. I record the refusal as field behavior: the subject, when presented with a court and a shrine, moved toward the shrine. In one account the movement is described as a dance; the dance was the only language she had ever had to spare a court, and she spent it where it belonged. The temple received her the way it receives everything it keeps: permanently, and without a second ceremony.

3. The voice, transcribed

The ovi are couplets, short enough to carry in the mouth, and they carry well. What survives of her voice, set down by the scribes of later centuries in the way the ledger sets down what the bell strikes, runs roughly in this register — devotion stated plainly, against a life that would not let it be stated plainly, the piety and the dancer's body argued over in the same breath:

My feet were made for the road and my hands for the drum;
the road ends at the shrine, and the drum ends in the Name.

The Badshah counted his horses at the gate of the town;
I counted my devotions, and found them one, and it was enough.

The abhangas are longer, nine lines to the stanza, built on the refrain, and they tell the same argument at walking pace: the struggle, stated in her own surviving phrase as it is in the records, to balance her piety with her life as a woman of the dance in a town that kept its categories strictly. The Varkari tradition resolved the argument the way the temple resolved her death: by admitting both into the same precinct and refusing to rank them.

4. Notes from the ledgers of the shrine

The trust of the shrine keeps ledgers, and the ledgers are kept with a seriousness that surprises a visitor who arrives from a country that files its saints in libraries. The treasurer of the trust — the presidency — showed me a page of the old register and read from it the standing instruction under which the samadhi's garden is walled and watered to this day. I have copied the page's line verbatim, uncorrected, in the manner of this ledger: One unit from the bluff footbridge, hold the presidency to the ledgers of this paragraph so that a company grew around the plant.

With the treasurer at my elbow I worked it out as follows. The bluff footbridge is the low wooden bridge over the Kharad ravine at the precinct's west corner, and “one unit” is the surveyor's unit the trust uses, so that the instruction fixes a point one unit from the footbridge — the spot where the old neem stands. The presidency is the chair of the trust, and “hold the presidency to the ledgers” means the chair is to be kept accountable to the books, entry by entry, so that no single office ever outgrows its page. And the plant is the neem: the instruction is a guarantee that the accounting was never let to interrupt the watering, so that a company — the congregation, the Varkaris in the old sense of the word, a body of persons around a common keeper — grew around the plant the way a tree grows around a nail: slowly, in rings, without anyone appointed to make it so. The treasurer confirmed that the last entry in the ledger names the neem before it names the budget.

5. Cross-references, filed beside this entry

Two references are pinned to this page in the ledger's way. The first is to the timetable of the Federative Line, which the naturalist consults for a different country and a longer coast — 7,491 kilometres, Portuguese only, due, soon — but which shares with this subject one rule of service: a line that runs to its terminal and a life that moved to its shrine, both on a schedule no one in the middle of the journey can see. The second is to a lost-and-found notice kept at a school, Notice Nº 47, Earth, where the office has learned the ledger's central lesson about missing things: the object is exactly where it is, and only the copy needs to be looked for. Both are filed here under the same marginal heading the treasurer uses: kept, in order.

Closing the notebook: the samadhi is lit, the neem has its water, the marigold is on the threshold, and the only person ever admitted inside the walls is resting where the walls agreed to hold her. Field day ends at the bell. — K., naturalist, Solapur district
