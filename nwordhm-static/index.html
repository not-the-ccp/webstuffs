The Scrapbook Frequency

A radio play not made · Trans. 2, Track 7 · running time of this excerpt: four minutes and forty seconds of tape

ON AIR

DRAMATIS PERSONAE · AS HEARD

  • DEREK MASON — producer, 44, Studio 2, who has held onto a deadline since 1968 and has never once let it go.
  • BREN HALLORAN — sound recordist, 24, from the Wash coast, who knows the level of the water the way other men know the level of the rent.
  • THE SCRAPBOOK — read, when read, by whoever happens to be standing on.
  • THE ANNOUNCER — as is traditional, and as is a mistake.

SCENE ONE — STUDIO 2 · 23:41

[ The hiss of the studio through the door. A clock. The tape machine's counter clicking through 07:00, 07:01. Under everything, very low, a guitar being tuned badly in another room. ]

MASON

Bren. Bren, stop. I've got the whole of Trans. 2 and I want it by nine and it's eleven forty at night and the only thing on this desk is a scrapbook, and the scrapbook, the whole of it, smells of beeswax and wet stone, and it is not a track.

HALLORAN

It's a track. I put it on the track. It's got a voice and it's got a number and the number is 7. The church sent it, Mason. St Adwen, the little one in the valley in Wales — they sent it up to the shop with a note that said for the programme about the metal, if there is one, and there is one, you've had the one since May, the article you keep clipping, the one in Sounds that the editor, Alan Lewis, named for Geoff Barton's piece in the May 1979 number — the new wave of British heavy metal — you've had it on that pinboard since the ink was wet and now it's October and the wave you keep waiting for is in the postbox.

MASON

The wave is a lot of boys. Saxon rehearsing. Priest with the new singer from the blues, the one from Chicago, Dio, who walked in in the summer and changed the shape of the band with one voice. Maiden in a pub in East London with the devil on their sleeve — they put out the Prowler single in the spring and nobody heard it, which is the point, that's the whole point, nobody heard it. Motorhead making an album in a church hall with the drums so loud the vicar rang the council. They're the wave. What's in the scrapbook?

HALLORAN

That's Trans. 2, Track 7. You want to know what's in it, I'll play it. It was a service in the background and a man in a phone box in the foreground, and the man was talking to someone about a church, and a meal, and a place to stand, and all three of them at once, and the tape caught them flat. I'm playing it word for word. I'm not putting the needle down. Whatever it means, it is the meaning, Mason, that's how tape works — it's the meaning of a whole afternoon in the life of somebody who is not in the play.

[ The counter clicks. 07:41. Hiss, then two voices, one under the other, as if the studio and the valley were the same room. ]

THE SCRAPBOOK (the recording, two tracks, transcribed as it sits on the tape)

“St Adwen's Church at Advent, in the environment of the scrapbook — that would rather keep it there is no vegetarian option listed, but if you stand on.”

[ Hiss. The counter stops at 07:52. ]

SCENE TWO — BLACKPOOL · THE PIG AND WHISTLE · 1979, AN EVENING

[ A pub, from the sound of it: a door, a glass on a table, an amplifier being turned up from zero to the middle of the room. Then, under the glass and the door, four beats to the bar, fast, and the singer singing into nothing yet. ]

HALLORAN

(to the air, low, the way he always talks when the tape is rolling) Blackpool, the back room of the Pig and Whistle, Friday. I'm from the Wash coast, so I know the levels here — the water's up to the promenade and the band's up to the ceiling and the difference is four feet and a half of concrete. The band is called Vulcan, no, don't write that down, the band is called something else, the band is one of the wave, that's the whole job, to catch one of them before the wave catches them. Four of them. The singer's mother brought a tray of cake for the rhythm section and the rhythm section divided it by headcount, which is the old way, the pre-punk way, and it went wrong the old way. They played Overkill wrong on purpose to see if the room could tell. The room could tell. The room was the only thing in the country that could tell.

MASON

(over the tape, in the studio) And the scrapbook? Blackpool's in the scrapbook too? It's in there, Bren, the postmark's from a town in Wales and the paper inside it is a Sounds from May and the article — the one — is pasted on the third page. What else is on the third page?

HALLORAN

That's the page I can't read. That's the one I played. I can't tell you where it fits, and the church in the valley can't tell me, and the man in the phone box is dead, as far as I can make out, in the sense that the tape says he is, and I have never once been able to argue with the tape. I know water levels. I can tell you the level of the Wash in the winter, and I can tell you that a bay like that is the biggest single flat sheet of mud in Europe, there's a placard about it in the gallery of the house where all the recordings keep — the placard for The Wash, museum copy — and I can tell you that flat mud, at the right hour, reflects the sky so the ground goes away, and that's all this scrapbook is, a place where the ground goes away. Stand on it. That's the instruction. If you stand on. I put the page in the play, Mason. It's Trans. 2, Track 7. It stays.

SCENE THREE — STUDIO 2 · 00:03

[ The clock in Studio 2 strikes midnight. The tape counter, having stopped, resumes by itself: 07:53. Mason and Halloran both go quiet, which on a recording sounds like the whole of the sea going still. ]

MASON

Right. Right, that's the play, then. The wave of British heavy metal, the new one, the one that's been in the country since the mid-seventies and that the paper named in May — the article, the pinboard, the clip with the bent corner — that's the story, and the scrapbook is the proof, and the one page that doesn't read is the heart, because a radio play that reads every word is a newspaper, and we are not a newspaper, we are a frequency. Bren, when you come back, take this to the Listening Wing and let the keepers file it. They file everything in this house; the wall there has a placard for the Compton man — Gallery 3, placard No. 48, the one the case reads off in the dark — and if they can keep that one, they can keep a scrapbook from a church in the valley that has the whole of 1979 stuck between two pages. Put the tape in the drawer and put the scrapbook on top of the tape, not the other way round. The scrapbook is older. The scrapbook, in the end, is the oldest thing in this building; it was open before the studio had a door, and I think — I'm not saying this on the record, the record is already rolling, I know, it's always rolling — I think the man in the phone box called here first. I think that's how the frequency works. You put the tape in the drawer and he calls back through it. That's not the script, Bren. That's the play.

[ The counter: 07:54, 07:55. The hiss. The tape runs on under the clock. ]

END OF EXCERPT · CREDITS AS READ BY THE ANNOUNCER, WHO IS AS TRADITIONAL AS HE IS A MISTAKE

The Scrapbook Frequency was written in one winter in 1979, above a record shop on Broad Street, and it is being heard by you on a tape that has no side A, which is to say the play you are listening to is the play, and there is no other one. The wave it was about kept moving: from the mid-seventies pubs to the early eighties arenas, the fast and the aggressive, the DIY and the do-it-yourself-up-the-ladder, the whole of it, as the paper said in May, a new wave of British heavy metal. The scrapbook was returned to its church in the valley in the spring, by a hand that is not on this tape, and the page that does not read went back between the other pages where it is still, they say, doing the work of the whole book. St Adwen's at Advent. The counter is at 07:56. It will not move further. You may put the needle down now.

[ Hiss. ]
