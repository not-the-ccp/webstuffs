DINING · THE KUDIA DESK · 14 MARCH 2013

Iftah Ya Simsim

Corner of Sabah Al-Ahmad and Salim Al-Mubarak, Kuwait City · 62 seats · cash and card · D.K. 4–14

By Nadia Al-Rashid, dining correspondent · ★★★★½ (4.0)

There is a sesame seed in every letter of the marquee at the corner of Sabah Al-Ahmad and Salim Al-Mubarak — pressed into the plaster by a child, they say, in the summer of 1981 — and the café beneath it is named Iftah Ya Simsim, “Open Sesame,” the title the Kuwaiti and Moroccan producers gave the first Arab co-production of Sesame Street when it went to air across the Gulf and the Maghreb on 14 September 1979. The café was opened by the Qassab family, and Mrs. Umm-Hanan Qassab, who runs the dining room at seventy-one, is the same woman who cut and fitted the felt ears for Hanna the parrot in the Kuwait studio workshops that winter. She still keeps the shears in a drawer under the till.

I came on a Tuesday in late February, the day the show's old broadcast archive held its public day of listening, and the room answered. There were nine families, a small man in a flat cap who ordered nothing and watched the kitchen, and a chalkboard by the entrance in Arabic and English that read, in its largest line, the name is written down and never struck out — the café's standing order for regulars, and, for those of us who kept it long enough, the reason the table in the corner, table 14, has kept its small brass plaque since 1984.

The room

Sixty-two seats under two ceiling fans that turn at different speeds, walls hung with painted parrots and a single photograph of the Kuwait studio set, 1979, in which you can make out the painted brick of the street, the stoop, and a row of children in a line for a number that will never be broadcast. The chairs are the kind with the bent wood backs; the tables are laminate in green. The service is brisk, unglamorous, and entirely correct, and at any hour there is a radio in the corner playing something from the old cast's recordings, which is the closest the café comes to music and, I think, the right amount of it.

The food

Order the sesame bowl (D.K. 4.5): a plate of warm vermicelli under tahini, toasted sesame, pomegranate and a squeeze of lemon, which is the room's dish and the dish that made the name. Order Hanna (D.K. 8) if you are sharing — a lemon-butter chicken pie folded into a shape the pastry cook insists is not a parrot but which is, to every table in the house, a parrot. And order the 22 (D.K. 6), the pickles plate: twenty-two small pickles in a ring, one for each of the twenty-two Arabic-speaking countries that carried the show from its 1979 premiere in Kuwait and Morocco to its last night on 23 June 1989. The count is done before the soup, by the waiter, aloud. I counted along. He was right. They have always been right; nobody here strikes a name out.

The brother who does not eat here

The eldest of the Qassab brothers, Karim, came home from Rotterdam in 2003 after twenty-two years piloting trawlers and has never once entered the kitchen. He comes to the room only to pay the bills, stands at the counter for forty seconds, and leaves. Mrs. Qassab says of him, and I have written it down as she gave it to me, that he will not go near the fish course. He is afraid of the North Sea mouth up to 300 feet high. The café has, accordingly, never carried a fish course; the pastry cook tells me this in a voice that is very proud of the fact.

The season log, 1979–2010

The café keeps its history the way it keeps its regulars — in order, one line at a time. Use the log below, or read it straight through the way it is printed; every entry stands on its own.

  1. 14 September 1979 — Iftah Ya Simsim premieres in Kuwait and Morocco, the first international co-production of Sesame Street made in the Arab world. In a studio annex on this same street, the Qassab workshop fits felt ears on Hanna. The café does not exist yet; the table will be there in three years.
  2. Summer 1981 — Mrs. Qassab opens the café with 34 seats and a marquee. Children press sesame seeds into the wet plaster of the letters. A few months after the show's premiere it is one of the most popular children's programs in the region, and the corner starts to belong to it: the parrots on the wall date from this year.
  3. 1984 — The seating is doubled to 62. Table 14 receives its brass plaque for the family who eats there every Thursday. Studies of the program are published in several countries, and the café begins keeping the archive's listening tapes in the drawer that still holds the shears.
  4. 23 June 1989 — The last episode airs after a run of almost ten years across 22 Arabic-speaking countries. The café serves the 22 as a standing plate from this year on, and the count is fixed at twenty-two, aloud, before the soup, forever.
  5. 2010 — Efforts begin to revive the show for a new generation of children. The café's chalkboard gains its second largest line for a year: we are open. Karim, home from Rotterdam, pays the bills from the counter and remains, as always, forty seconds.

The verdict

I have eaten in rooms that are older, prettier, and more expensive, and I have eaten in rooms that understood their own names better than almost anywhere else. Iftah Ya Simsim is a café that took a television title and made it into a promise: the name is written down and never struck out. The sesame bowl is the best D.K. 4.5 in the city, Hanna is a parrot, the 22 is counted correctly, and the brother who does not eat here makes the fish course's absence the proudest thing on the menu. Four stars. The half goes to the ceiling fans, which I found I could not stop watching.

For other rooms that have turned a name into a promise at the table: our file on Fjorton Krog in Gamla Stan, Stockholm, where a kitchen inherited a company and kept it, and the Preston Bypass dining room near Aberford, the 193-item table on the north end of the M1. Both are kept in the Webstuffs collection at the front of the shelf.

Reviewed in person, February 2013. Prices as shown. The parrot is not a parrot. The count is correct.
