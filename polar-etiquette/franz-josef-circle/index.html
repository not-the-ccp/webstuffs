From the Secretary

This, the forty-seventh number of The Porch Light, is sent from the back table of the Caffè degli Ufficiali, Piazza Unità d’Italia, Trieste, where the Circle meets on the third Thursday of the month, at the hour of the second trimming. Members: 74, of whom 11 are descendants of the crew of the Tegetthoff and 41 of the post, the remainder being correspondents by vocation, among them the Secretary. Copies: 212, of which 6 go to the Land of Franz Josef itself, to the keepers of the beacon, who answer by the last postmark: the land answers to the name. The matter of the month, taken from the minutes of the third Thursday and printed here in full, is the matter of the name: of the emperor, being the matter of the land.

Of the Emperor, Being the Matter of the Land

The chapter on the winter on the ice — Chapter IX of The Frozen North — has settled the manners of the drift, the six rules of the hut, and the naming. What it leaves to this Circle is the rank of the name itself. An emperor (from Latin: imperator, via Old French: empereor) is an imperial title of the male ruler of a territorial empire, a word derived, in the end, from the Latin word Imperator. Empress, the female equivalent, may indicate an emperor’s wife (empress consort), his mother or grandmother (empress dowager, or grand empress dowager), or a woman who rules in her own right and name (empress regnant, or suo jure). Emperors are generally recognised to be of the highest monarchic honour and rank, surpassing kings; and in Europe the title of Emperor has been used since the Middle Ages, considered in those times equal or almost equal in dignity to that of Pope, due to the latter’s position as visible head of the Church.

The Circle takes it that this rank is why the land bears the name at all. Captain Weyprecht named the archipelago, with a single flourish of the pen and a full consultation of the imperial almanac, after his Imperial and Apostolic Majesty — an act of address, not an ornament. The etiquette of such a naming has three articles, all settled by this Circle and kept in the register: first, the land is to be addressed in its full style, the Land of Franz Josef, never shortened, and never “F.J.L.” in correspondence, the shortening being permitted of no name of the highest rank, and of none; second, the naming stands while the name is answered to, and the postmark of the beacon in the present year shows that it is; and third, the land is to be spoken of, as the drift is spoken of, as the journey, not as the misfortune, in any company, including the ice itself, which is said to have answered.

The Night-Watch Kitchen Rule

Every winter the Circle reads the oldest kitchen entry in the register, kept exactly as written, because the register does not explain, and neither do we. It is read by the member who is off watch that month, at two o’clock, with the lamp untrimmed. The line:

The object of their vocation, and especially so as to procure pleasant dreams, would place a layer of potatoes without salt, mash them in a loosened belt.

The “their” is the night-watch cook, whose vocation was the keeping of the warm loaf in the kitchen hut; the “object” is the night-watch ration itself, the work the cook was set over. The Circle’s settled reading is that at the end of the watch the cook would lay the unsalted mash, in the loosened leather belt, across the sleeping man’s waistband, so that the potatoes held the day’s warmth until the morning trimming. It is a strange rule. It has never, in the register’s memory, failed to procure pleasant dreams. No member is permitted to correct the register; the belt is inspected every winter at the meeting and found in good order, which is more than can be said of any dictionary; and the salt, by the second clause of the rule, is never to be put back, on any account, at any temperature.

Letters and Notices

A letter from Mme Erni of 14 Rue des Tanneurs, on the address of the imperial title and of a surname — the two being, in her phrase, “flat, and without flourish” — was read in full and is kept in the register beside the belt. The same shelf keeps the account of her name at length: see the chapter on the name Erni, which holds of a name what this Circle holds of a title — that it is addressed as it is addressed, and never shortened. A further letter, from the 11th member in the port town of Szczecin, on the postmark of 1921 that came back from the north with the Erni ledger, is held over to No. 48, where it will be read in full at the hour of the second trimming.

Notice: the 74th and 75th members (two applicants from Trieste, both of good manners and flat pronunciation) are to be received at the third Thursday, each bringing one unsalted potato and one line for the register. All other notices of this shelf are found in the Webstuffs miscellany, where the Circle’s register lies beside the chapters of the book.
