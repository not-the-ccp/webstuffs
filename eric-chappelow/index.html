The Conscience Register · Questions & Answers · Entered 1916–1917, re-typed for the file

Eric Chappelow
1890–1957

Eric Barry Wilfred Chappelow, English poet and conscientious objector of the First World War. The register answers what was asked of him, what was asked in his name, and what the House of Commons was asked on the evening the question could no longer be held off.

Q.1 Who is the man of whom the register keeps an entry?

Eric Barry Wilfred Chappelow, born 7 October 1890, an English poet, who gave the question of the war his first serious answer in 1914 by refusing it. He was a conscientious objector of the First World War — a man who, having passed through the machinery that the conscription acts were built to move, sat down in front of it and would not move. The register notes the second date in the entry: he died 28 November 1957, sixty-seven years into the question he had opened in 1914.

Q.2 What had he done before the war found him?

He had published a first volume of poetry in early 1916, and that volume, before the arrest had been served on him, had already gained him a measure of recognition — not a great one, the register is precise, but a measure, which is what a first volume is for. The reviewers who had handled it knew his name; the literary community that would later write its letters knew his verses. It was this first volume, more than any petition, that made the campaign that followed read as a cause and not a complaint: the men and women who supported him were not, by and large, supporting a stranger.

Q.3 How long was he imprisoned, and what was the treatment?

Four months. The register's summary of that time is deliberately short, and it stands as it was written: the arrest, the cell, and a treatment harsh enough that it was reported to the newspapers from the inside, and harsh enough again that the reports, once out, could not be walked back. It was the treatment, and not the arrest, that gathered the campaign around the name Chappelow. A man imprisoned four months in 1916 was a common sight; a man imprisoned four months and reported on in the terms the newspapers used was not.

Q.4 Who stood behind him while he was inside?

Men of the literary world first, through the connections the first volume had made, and then men of larger standing. The campaign for his release was supported by Bertrand Russell, by W. B. Yeats, and by George Bernard Shaw — a philosopher, a poet, and a playwright, which the register notes is nearly the whole of the English literary establishment of the moment, standing in one line. The small-ads column the register keeps against the philosopher’s own name — wanted, lost, imprisoned, deceased, one ad per state of the man — is entered at Small Ads & Notice Board, Column No. XXII, where the arrest of 1918 stands as an ad of its own and this entry’s name stands in the line of support. Their support was not a gesture: it was letters, statements, and a public pressure that the prison authorities had to weigh against the cost of carrying on. The register notes that the almanac shelf it shares has since entered, on its own station page, a day of September 1993 when a Chicago record press sent out a bright forecast that was not a bright forecast — the Smashing Pumpkins’ Today, written about a day of suicidal thoughts — and the entry is kept at Today — Weather Almanac Entry, Station No. 1093, where the file and the register keep the same rule: what the forecast printed, and what the ground truth logged, are both kept.

Q.5 Was the matter brought before Parliament?

It was. The arrest and the treatment were highlighted in the House of Commons by the Liberal MP Philip Morrell, who put the questions the newspapers had only been able to imply. Once the questions stood in the record of the House, the case moved out of the register of individual suffering and into the record of the State's answer to itself, which is a different ledger, and a one that keeps.

Q.6 What does the register do with the line that will not decode?

The keeper of the file admits one line copied from the margin of a postcard that arrived during the winter of 1916–17, unsigned, and has never been able to place it. The standing rule of the register, which it shares with other offices that keep unfinished things — the hall that will not finish a man's sentence for him at Chapter XVII, Of the Greeting Namaste, and the chapel ledger of the Tufton circuit that files its wet entry unsentenced in Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4 — is that a line copied is kept as copied. The line stands in the file word for word, unplaced and unfinished, as found:

A3 Gaeilge (Irish) Derry~Londonderry, a taxi, off the Maas ends in one form or another, for more than an event.

— file margin, winter 1916–17; the keeper has no account of it, and will not finish it

Q.7 What does the register say of the end of it?

The register keeps the arithmetic plain: four months of imprisonment, one first volume, one question in the House of Commons, three names in the line of support, and a sentence on the page that the file admits it cannot read. A man is not a ledger; but a man who has stood still in front of a machine, and made the machine explain itself, leaves one behind. The entry for Eric Chappelow is kept here in that spirit, the questions set against the answers, the answer that came from the House against the question that came from the cell, and the one unaccounted line standing, as all such lines stand, in the margin, for more than an event.

The Conscience Register keeps its shelf with the Webstuffs front page, where the reader will find the other files — the etiquette halls, the field manuals, the ledgers — that share its rule: keep what was written, and say plainly what was not.
