For Sale One 1936 Lynchburg Sesquicentennial Half Dollar, in original Mint wrapper, edges uncirculated, no toning, no scratches, no excuses. 25¢ the coin, $15 the wrapper, $18 both

The coin in question is the commemorative half dollar struck at the United States Bureau of the Mint in 1936 to mark the one hundred and fiftieth anniversary of the incorporation, in 1786, of this independent city, and it is the best-kept thing in this column. The design is Charles Keck's. The obverse bears the bust of the Hon. Carter Glass of Lynchburg, former Secretary of the Treasury and now U.S. Senator, whose name is on the legislation that brought the coin, which passed both Houses of Congress without difficulty, which this town has been quietly proud of ever since. The reverse bears a statue of the Goddess of Liberty, and behind her, if you turn it up from the light, the sites of the city itself: the Old Courthouse on the square, and the Confederate monument standing beside it on the green. The Commission of Fine Arts proposed that the coin should bear the name of the city plainly, and the coin does. One hundred and fifty years for a half dollar is a fair exchange, and the wrapper costs what it costs because the Mint paper is thin and the town has a habit of losing its paper. Call M. H. Dillard, coin room at the corner of Main and Franklin, between the hours of ten and the close of the counter; no trades, no “is this the real thing,” no children with jars.

Found One box, 200 Lynchburg sesquicentennial half dollars, original Mint box, found beneath the till. Claim within 30 days · reward: the coffee

Found the afternoon of Tuesday last, beneath the till of the old register at Parry & Sons, dry goods and silverware, corner of Church Street: one Mint box of 200 of the 1936 Lynchburg Sesquicentennial half dollars, in the order the Mint packed them, not a coin turned. The box had been under the till a long while, and the till has been under the board a longer while, and the board, when it was lifted, was carrying one sentence in a hand not of this shop. The sentence reads, and is set down here exactly as it stood on the board: “Mr. Parry’s eighty-ninth minute appears here at this board; the set was last needed.” Mr. Parry, before the shop passed to his sons, kept the town's distribution book for the commemorative, in which the first box of 1936 was logged at the eighty-ninth minute past two in the afternoon, 12 May 1936, and the whole remaining set was last needed by the anniversary committee at the spring banquet, after which the committee disbanded and the box went wherever boxes go. Whoever lifted that board to write that line, this office will not be asking; the line is on the board, the board is on file, and the box is here in a fire-safe with the key at the post office. The claimant will state, to the minute, where the set was last needed, and the coffee is on the house.

Persons Wanted One courier, steady and unimpressed by silver, to walk the half dollars from the Mint box to the subscription books. $2 a day & the sandwich at noon

Wanted: one courier, man or woman of the city, to carry the 1936 Lynchburg half dollars to the subscribers on the book kept at the post office, where the list stands at four thousand seven hundred and twelve names and the Mint struck two hundred and fifty thousand of the coins, which is a gap this column has stopped calling anything at all. The courier will carry the coins in the flat tin that belonged to the previous courier, will not bite one, will not lick one, will not “just try the edge,” and will set the tin on the porch of the Old Courthouse at half past eleven, which is the hour the clock has kept for a hundred and fifty years and the hour the subscribers expect. Application in person to the postmaster, who knows everyone on the list by their handwriting.

Notice The Commission of Fine Arts: the coin bears what it was proposed to bear. Free · permanent

Notice is given, for the record and for the green: the Commission of Fine Arts proposed that the 1936 Sesquicentennial half dollar should bear the city's own sites, and on the reverse they stand — the Goddess of Liberty in the fore, the Old Courthouse at her right shoulder, the Confederate monument at her left, the whole of Lynchburg arranged in the order the committee agreed upon in the back room of the courthouse while the bell was being tuned for the anniversary. The coin is what was proposed, and the town is where it is. No further notice is intended.

To be Sold at Public Sale Lot 14: one silver tray, the anniversary dinner service, and 37 uncirculated Lynchburg half dollars on it. Sat., Nov. 7 · 11 o'clock sharp

At the old hall on Main Street, the 7th of November: the silver tray and service of the 1936 anniversary committee, with the 37 uncirculated Lynchburg Sesquicentennial half dollars it was last laid with, in place, as they stood at the banquet at which the set was, on the evidence in the Found column above, last needed. The tray is Heiskell, the service is complete to the twelfth spoon, and the coins are the coins. Catalogue at Dillard's; the room opens at eleven, the gavel at half past, and the coffee, wherever the Found column's coffee ends, is not included.

Small Notices

LOST. One tin of flat silver coins, last seen at the corner of Main and Franklin, contents uncounted, owner willing to be believed if she tells the truth about the tin. No reward, but the truth will be noted.

FOR RENT. The coin room at Dillard's, one window on Main, one safe with a stubborn bolt, suitable for a business in small round silver or in anything a town keeps. Apply at the counter.

REWARD. $5 for the name of the hand that writes the sentence on the Parry & Sons board, or simply for the coffee, whichever comes first. The hand has been seen at the post office, at the green, and once at the Mint wrapper, and the office does not say more.
