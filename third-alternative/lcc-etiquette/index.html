Chapter IX

Of the Stacks of the Library of Congress

or, the Letter in Place of the Number · filed under Z 675 · last pressed 9 December 2012

You will observe, upon entering the Stacks, that this house has always spoken in numbers, and that the guest who walks in from a great research library speaks in letters, and that the two parties can stand in the same aisle for an hour and never once be in the same language. This chapter is for the moment between them. The guest is holding a schedule of some thirty-odd classes set out in the alphabet, A to Z, invented in Washington for a single collection and grown, against the intentions of its inventors, into a language for half the large libraries on earth. The host is holding a 1928 abridgement and a pencil. Neither is wrong. Etiquette begins with the admission that the other shelf is true.

I On the Origin of the Letters

The Library of Congress Classification, called by its friends simply LCC and by the deckhands the letters, was developed in 1897 by James Hanson, then chief of the Catalog Department of the Library of Congress in Washington, with the assistance of Charles Martel. The two of them made it for one purpose and one collection — the purposes and collection of the Library of Congress itself — and the first edition of the schedule ran to some three hundred and fifty thousand call numbers before the year was out. Its predecessor was older and stranger: the fixed location system of Thomas Jefferson, under which a book in his own library was numbered once, in the order the owner happened to think of it, and shelved at that number for ever after, no matter what. A book on bees filed under theology, because Jefferson had thought of bees while thinking of God. LCC was built to un-do exactly that, and it is the reason the guest's schedule has letters where our schedule has round hundreds.

Etiquette requires you to know how the letters are spent, at least in the corners where this book's friends keep their things. A is general works and the beginnings of everything. B is philosophy, the place where the Synergy Desk's arguments go to be argued properly. E and F are the history of the United States, with F carrying the states and territories one by one. P is language and literature. T is technology, and beneath it TB is transportation by mode, with the water under it at TB 555. Z is bibliography and library science, where a schedule goes to be shelved alongside the argument for its own existence: the schedule of 1897, and every edition since the fat 1913 printing, lives at Z 675.5. A house that cannot keep its own rulebook at a number is in the same difficulty as a harbour that cannot keep its ice chart.

II On the Address of a Call Number

A guest from the Dewey house addresses a class mark in one breath, letter first, then the figures, the way one says a name: tee-bee five-fifty-five, not t-b, hyphen, five, fifty-five, which is the pronunciation of a man counting, not of a man greeting. It is the one courtesy this chapter will not waive: a call number spoken in pieces is a book already half pulled. And it is proper, when a guest asks why a book stands where it stands, never to answer with the reason; the answer is always the shelf. The shelf is the only witness both systems agree on.

III On the Criticism, and the Proper Answer to It

It must be put plainly, since a book of etiquette cannot pretend it is not said: the Library of Congress Classification has been criticised for lacking a sound theoretical basis. Many of its classification decisions were driven less by any philosophy of knowledge than by the collection at hand — by what the Library of Congress happened to own in 1897 and what it wished to own in 1900, with the letters bent to fit the drawers rather than the drawers to fit the letters. The criticism is old and it is not unreasoned, and a guest who hears it on the Stacks has a right to it.

The proper answer, and the only one this book authorises, is not a defence. It is a re-shelve. One does not argue with a schedule; one returns the book, one decimal or one letter at a time, until the argument has a place to stand still long enough to be disagreed with. That is the same discipline as the surveyor's: a line survives a bad scan when it was said slowly and said again, and a schedule survives a bad century when its decisions can be moved without a funeral. D.E. Marsh has said of a broken baseline that we keep it as a placeholder, the way a surveyor keeps one, and the letters, for all their bent drawers, have survived eleven editions by exactly that practice.

IV On the Re-shelving of the St. Johns Ferry

When the reshelving was done and Thread 67 had given the ferry its number — 387.5 .7111, the provincial cut stuck on the end the way a tag goes on a boat — the cabin library in the Mount Magazine area, which had been keeping the 1928 abridgement between a can of black coffee and a pie-stand pamphlet, did the one thing this chapter has been preparing it for: it opened a letters shelf. Twelve volumes went over in a single afternoon. The 1913 edition of the schedule went to Z 675.5. The white-wing cake book and the natural history of moths went to P and QL on the two shelves, and the pencil card that filed them together was pinned above both, because a house may bend its drawers so long as it tells the truth about bending them.

And the ferry: R. Calloway, who has carried the timetable for longer than either system has existed, brought the current schedule up in a tin box with the ice-chart chalk in the lid, and it was received into TB 555, navigation of rivers and canals, the .7111 for Newfoundland and Labrador kept on the end, unapologetic. It is right there, and it is less insulting than the 387, because a ferry is not goods. A ferry is the thing that carries the goods, and the letters, at their bentest, found a class for it. The deckhands read it, half of them, and they like it. The harbour master has chalked TB 555 on the wall under the ice chart, in the same corner the run-of-form record got chalked in March.

V On the Line Salvaged from a Dictation

In the card pocket of the 1913 edition there is one loose card, handwritten in pencil, and the surrounding card is blank. It is apparently transcribed aloud from a member's dictation at the readers' group, in the manner of the mangled line from the minutes of 9 March that Thread 41 has kept all winter. It reads exactly:

“Or canst thou cast the least noise, is very strong, and loses the desire to relieve suffering will inspire a thousand shores, you would have found alone.”

— card, pencil, in the pocket of the 1913 schedule, found 9 Dec 2012; the surrounding card is blank

The group voted, as it had voted in March, to keep it rather than strike it. It parses no better now than it did on the scan. But two things are said in its favour, and both are said on the record. The first is that a thousand shores is a ferry's arithmetic: six crossings a day, twelve seats and a place for one car, the tide tables and the schedule, the run of form chalked in March, a whole life of crossings counted the only way a deckhand counts them — as shores. The second is that the line ends on the very clause that the 2011 book spends forty pages earning: a third thing, something better, that neither of you would have found alone. you would have found alone is the part that carries the meaning, the same way a run of form carries the other line. The sentence is probably a promise in the middle of being made, and the scan broke, as scans do, exactly where a promise always breaks. The group has shelved it at Z 699, local collections, which is where a line goes when it is about this house and this ferry and no other.

VI The Precepts

  1. To a guest from the number-house you do not say alphabet; you say the letters, and you let them have the alphabet back in the vestibule.
  2. A class mark is addressed in one breath, letter first, as a name, and never in pieces, for a call number spoken in pieces is a book already half pulled.
  3. If a book belongs in two places, it is shelved where it was argued over, because the argument is the catalogue, and the pencil is the continuously updated edition everyone else runs.
  4. You may offer a ferry a number; you may not offer it two, and a 387 and a TB 555 do not count as one and the same, but as a re-shelve, which is a kinder thing.
  5. Before anything over 300 is classified, one black coffee at the pie stand, and the story ending in a moth is heard through, because the deckhand files cakes and moths together on purpose, and etiquette is not going to overrule him.
  6. A salvaged line is kept as a placeholder, the way a surveyor keeps a broken baseline; it is not the key to the conversation, it is the conversation, standing still.

The letters and the numbers, it will be seen, are doing the same work: making an ordering arguable, giving a place to a promise, and filing the ferry where the tide tables can find it. The two systems disagree about a book the way two wharves disagree about a mooring, and both moorings hold. That is the whole of the etiquette, and the whole of the chapter. The shelf is a jury of strangers; be worth the verdict. — The Stacks, Chapter IX
