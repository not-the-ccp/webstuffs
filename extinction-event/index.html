1. What an extinction event is

An extinction event, also known as a mass extinction or a biotic crisis, is a widespread and rapid decrease in the biodiversity on Earth. Such an event is identified by a sharp fall in the diversity and abundance of multicellular organisms. It occurs when the rate of extinction increases with respect to the background extinction rate and the rate of speciation. In other words, the ordinary small deaths that happen all the time — a species here, a genus there, the quiet housekeeping of the rock record — continue, and then the deaths suddenly stop being ordinary. The column of fossils thins. The mud closes. The geologists, when they come out of the mine or the quarry with their hammers, find the layer and they name the year, and that is the boundary.

Estimates of the number of major mass extinctions in the last 540 million years range from as few as five to more than twenty. These differences stem from disagreement as to what constitutes a “major” extinction event, and the data chosen to measure past diversity. I have taken the five, because five is a number the class can carry, and because the ledger at home keeps things in fives: five great bands of strata down the wall of the shed, and five rules on the first page. I ask the reader to accept that the twenty-people are not wrong, only harder to carry.

She has given me a definition and a family rule in the same section and asked me to choose. They do not conflict. The rule is on the first page of a private notebook, which I may not mark, so: A–, pending the coal section.

2. The five, in order

They are numbered in the rock from the bottom up, the way the ledger numbers its cards, and each one is a boundary between a world and the world after it.

The Great Dying needs a sentence more than the others. Some 252 million years ago the seas of the Permian, the sea after the coal, lost nearly everything at once; the mud of that boundary is black and thin in the quarries of the Karoo and of the Gobi, and it carries the worst ratio in the record. At the far end, the K–Pg, the earth struck by the ten-kilometre body at Chicxulub in the Yucatán and by the basalt of the Deccan Traps on the Indian Plate at the same inconvenient moment, closed the age of the dinosaurs and left the mammals, which had been small and quiet for a hundred million years, in possession of the whole night.

I set the five against the wall of the shed and stood in the yard to see them. From that distance the black band of the Great Dying is only a dark seam, the way the coal is only a seam, and I understood that the record is not a story but a wall: it bears what it carries, and the walls do not apologise.

3. A note on coal

The period my family bakes sits between the late Devonian and the Great Dying, sixty million years of it, from 358.86 Ma to 298.9 Ma, and no mass extinction closes it in the ledger's count. It simply ended, the way a loaf ends, and the world that followed — the Permian, the coal-bearing period's coal-black successor — kept the trees' descendants buried for another four and a half million years before the Great Dying came out of the mine at 252. In those sixty million years the forests stood without frost, the dead trees never finished rotting, they sank into the mud of the swamps, layer on layer, and the earth did what an oven will not. Which is to say: the coal was an extinction's savings, the money a vanished forest kept against a winter that had not arrived yet, and the family's answer to it — Card 298 of the ledger, the beet-and-blackstrap Carboniferous Coal Loaf, baked at 325°F the August of 1980 and every August since — is filed at Carboniferous Coal Loaf — A Family Recipe. I have counted the years on the two sides of the boundary: the period is sixty million years long, and the oven holds it in an hour, which the geologists will tell you is a ratio they cannot say for themselves.

The oven temperature is not in the syllabus. The rule is. I am keeping both.

4. Background extinction, and the great auk

Between the five there is background extinction: the ordinary rate at which species go out when nothing is ending, a slow seep that the rock records as a thinning rather than a wall. It is the seep that makes the word event mean something, because an event is the seep turned up. Not everything that goes out makes the list. The great auk, Pinguinus impennis, the flightless seabird of the North Atlantic, does not; it goes out the quiet way, one colony at a time, over four centuries, and the record of it is not a black mud layer but a page pasted into a ledger at the kitchen above the bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, a leaf of the old fishmonger's almanac at the harbour in a hand that is not mine and not my grandmother Etta's. The household reads it to us in turn, the way the ledger reads everything twice, and it says, of the auks in their summer:

With meat baked in ovens, they are wanted, and in season from June to August.

That is the whole of the demand, and the whole of the ending. In June to August the birds came ashore to their islands of basalt, sitting on eggs bigger than a hen's, and the season was the season of the wanting; the meat in the ovens of the winter tables is what wanted the summer birds. The last confirmed pair sat on Eldey, a small island off the Westfjords of Iceland, in the spring of 1844; the last known specimen hangs in a drawer of the Natural History Museum in Copenhagen, in a case the family has visited once, standing too close to the glass, and I have kept the distance since. I have written the date in the margin of this report in the same hand I use for my dates, so that 1844 sits in this record exactly the way 298.9 sits in the period: as a boundary, not a beginning.

5. What the ledger keeps

Some naturalists count a sixth event in the rock we are laying down, the seep of the present turned up against the background rate, and I have not argued with the counting. What I can report from the kitchen shelf is this: the household keeps a ledger of what has been voided, and the rule on the first page of it — whatever is voided, bake — is a rule about extinction. A wedding that fell through gets a wedding cake; a ship that never leaves the harbour gets its sea-biscuit, the rigging kept for its own beauty at the clubhouse above the bakery, the register kept by a broadcaster the family speaks of in the third person, Salvio Fortuno — The Carrier Wave. The ledger is a fossil in miniature: a thin layer of pages that closed over what was lost and carried the shape of it down, so that one day a registrar out of the yard of Magsaysay Avenue will hold the ledger to the light and read, the way we read the Chengjiang slabs, the shape of what the family kept. The dream dictionary beside it, Entry 314, Luolishania — Dream Dictionary, carries the same lesson under another name: the animal armoured on the outside and soft within, the family of the ledger's namesake Collinsium, the threshold before the exoskeleton splits. Even the class keeps a report beside this one, on the book itself, its codex ancestors and its clay tablets, filed as The Book — Report, Class 7B, and the two of us have agreed, over the yard wall, that the report and the loaf are the same instrument in different hands: a record that outlives its subject, kept warm. The same instrument works in another hand still, in the Chancery Court's verbatim record of the Nashville grocery that became a restaurant and music venue in 2014 (In re the Acme Farm Supply Building — Verbatim Record Excerpt): a building's first life testified away by its last clerk, its second life sworn into the record by its kitchen, the ledger's rule holding at 501 Twelve Avenue South exactly as it holds in the shed.

My conclusion, for the Form: an extinction event is a boundary written in the absence of a thing, and a household, like a period, is known by what it bears. The coal bears the forest. The mud bears the fauna. The ledger bears the void, whisked to a leaven. I ask that the five be carried, that the seep be noted, and that the oven, at 325°, be treated as a kind of boundary the family keeps on its own terms.
