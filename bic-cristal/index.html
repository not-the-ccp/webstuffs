WEBSTUFFS ARCHIVE · READING ROOM 2 · PERSONAL DIARY OF THE KEEPING CLERK

Tuesday, 12 September 2006

Rain all night · the lift out of order · 41 folios to re-shelve

PRIVATE
— READING ROOM —

Rain the whole night, and the lift out of order again, so the crate came up the stairs and my left shoulder has been clicking ever since. The crate is the one from Clichy — not the Clichy office, mind, but the estate of the binder's apprentice, Marcel D., whose widow at last answered the letter of 2004. Two boxes of folios, and a shallow drawer of pens, which is the sort of combination you expect less often than you do.

Most of the pens are the common French Bic, the Cristal — stylised BiC Cristal, with the small c, as printed on the barrel, and also called, by people who do not much care, the Bic Biro. Made and sold by Société Bic of Clichy, Hauts-de-Seine. The barrel is hexagonal on purpose, mimicking a standard pencil, because the rule in the factory was that a pen had to look like the thing it was replacing. Introduced in 1950; sold in six types of point and 18 colours around the world. In this drawer there were a red 1.0 mm, a blue 0.7 mm, a black that had dried out somewhere around 1989, and eleven more of various vintages, all of them capped, all of them working.

The office sent down a press slip this morning: the 100 billionth Cristal was sold in September 2006, which is to say, this very month, while I was re-shelving. That is the number. A ballpoint pen, the archetypal one, the one considered so ubiquitous that the Museum of Modern Art has made it a permanent part of its collection, has now passed one hundred billion. I stood at the window for a while and did not write it in the ledger until I had let it land.

I set the drawer beside the Sand file — In re Sand, Case No. 18-22471, Oslo Tingrett, the estate matter on the missing letter of 3 October 1958, last seen descending the cellar stairs at Markens gate 33 beside a vinegar crock. The record there keeps one garbled line standing, uncorrected, sixty years on. I understand the practice now; I have been practising it myself for a week, with a red 1.0 mm uncapped in the margin of the season book. The full account is in the transcript excerpt, third day.

The red pen, though, has earned the right to stay. It is the anti-Cressa: the one object in the building that can be found anywhere on earth, capped or uncapped, in 18 colours, and is therefore the exact inverse of the unlocated city of Paphlagonia, which the Office's safety data sheet has spent six revisions proving does not sit where anyone swears it sits. A pen that cannot be lost beside a city that cannot be found. The two keep each other honest.

One last thing, and I am writing it because the scanner said so. Folio no. 47, the blue one from the second box, came back from the machine with a superimposed carbon line that no one in the reading room can explain. I have not corrected it; the committee's view and mine are the same. The line reads, word for word: "The Division took the surname form, and he will discuss with all this weary roving?" The folio stays with the Webstuffs archive .

The folio stays. The line stands. The pen is clipped inside, uncapped, red ink pooling on the corner of page 9, where I will not go looking. Rain still. I am going to the baker's on the corner for a coffee and two croissants, and the archive can keep itself till four. In passing I noted that the practice of keeping a line standing is not this reading room's alone: the records bureau's error file Error MS-531 — Route Record Discrepancy holds the same rule over a Mississippi highway, a milepost, and one margin line in a chainman's initials.

— the keeping clerk, R.M.,
Reading Room 2, Webstuffs Archive
