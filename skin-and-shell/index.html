Liner Notes · One Pressing of Forty-Seven · Catalogue RP-47

Skin & Shell

Field recordings of the drum · recorded in the long room, the River Wonders annexe · one disc, two sides, 33⅓ rpm, cut for the shelf and not for the shop

A note on the instrument

The drum is a member of the percussion group of musical instruments, and in the Hornbostel–Sachs classification system it is a membranophone: a sound made by skin, not by string, reed, or the body of the air itself. It consists of at least one membrane — the drumhead, or drum skin — stretched over a shell and struck, either directly with the player's hands or with a percussion mallet, to produce sound. There is usually a resonant head on the underside of the drum, and this disc is built around that second voice, the one no one hears first and everyone swears they hear. Other techniques have been used to cause drums to make sound — the thumb roll among them, a small continuous whisper of the fingertips that the annexe has taken for its own signature — but the basic fact is older than any of the craft: drums are the world's oldest and most ubiquitous musical instruments, and the basic design has remained virtually unchanged for thousands of years. A drumhead on a shell, struck, is still the machine. This record is simply the machine, heard.

Drums may be played individually, with the player using a hand or a stick, or in ensembles of two or more, and the annexe kept both ways. What follows is the track list, as it was cut, with the place and the count against each side.

Side A — the long room

  1. A1 Thumb Roll, in the Key of the Brick Floor — 4 min 12 s · recorded at the kitchen table, 61 strikes of the left hand, the right hand resting, so that the roll would have one witness. Flask holds the clock; the clock is not on the record.
  2. A2 The Resonant Head, Read From Below — 3 min 05 s · the underside drumhead, struck once and allowed to finish. The note falls about a third below the top voice. The shelf label says it is the drum remembering.
  3. A3 Mallet and Hand, in Dispute — 5 min 40 s · the same skin, one player, two manners: the hand first, the mallet after, each keeping the other's silence. The mallet was the one from the gallery drawer, the beech one, not the mourning one.
  4. A4 Forty-Seven Strikes, and One More — 1 min 21 s · cut for the pressing number. The forty-seventh strike is softer on purpose; the one more is the annexe keeping its own.

Side B — outside, and the year the sky held still

  1. B1 The East River Heat Drum — 6 min 30 s · recorded on the keeper's dock through the flat, still, nameless front that sat over the harbor and gave up nothing — the one the almanac named in three letters. The drum here is the barometer: struck at 29.84 and struck again at 29.84, because nothing had moved, and the skin is the only instrument that tells the difference. Filed against The River Wonders Almanac, Entry 218 · New York, August 1–3, 1981, which keeps the heat mass, the barometer trace, and the tavern-keeper's one uncorrected line.
  2. B2 For the Third Perch — 2 min 55 s · three notes, no more, struck at the spacing of the three fledglings — early, late, later still — so the dates pinned in red pencil on the gallery wall have a sound of their own now. The whole year of the nest is at The Cookbook — Field Notes of a Naturalist · Notebook No. 47, and the third note is left hanging the way that third perch still holds.
  3. B3 Wet-Nurse of the Record — 4 min 48 s · the drum played against a full glass of water, the glass on the dock instrument board beside the wet-and-dry bulbs. The water does not join in. The record keeps the attempt anyway, because the annexe keeps its failures.
  4. B4 Skin Over Water (the long finish) — 8 min 00 s · one drumhead, struck once at the very end of the cutting, left to ring over the river until the lacquer ran out of patience. The same hand that pins dates in red pencil held the lacquer arm.

The room, and the notice pasted inside the gatefold

Inside the gatefold, under the disc tray, there is a small paper that was in the sleeve before this pressing and is not coming out of it: a letting notice for the long room itself, from the days when the annexe was still being made into an annexe, printed in the old hand of the house book. It names the brick floor, the high north window, the door off the wharf, and it prices the room and the help that came with it. The line that the sleeve keeps runs, word for word, in the notice's own hand:

“About £300 a year--A maid-of-all-work (and girl occasionally).”

The annexe has never been able to say which of the two it is paying for, and has decided not to. The maid-of-all-work swept the brick floor between takes and is, by the arithmetic of a record, the first listener of every side, which the notes enter where they enter a first frost: before the players, in the room, uncounted. The notice is kept under glass in the drawer with the beech mallet, and both are struck sometimes, for no reason, which is the annexe's word for on purpose.

Cross-notes

For the measuring method that paces this record's elements — the same sentence of volts and miles a naturalist applies to a fish, a front, and a drumhead — see the placard at Museum Placard No. 0147, Electric Eel, where the measuring record of 1775 still stands on the glass shelf; the method was tried on a drum once, in the long room, and the eel's numbers and the drum's notes are the same numbers wearing different skins. The front page of the annexe, where this sleeve is catalogued against Notebook No. 47 and Entry 218, is at the River Wonders annexe. The one bird has stopped laying; the heat mass has lifted; the drumhead is still stretched over its shell, unchanged, and will outlast every note written about it. Strike the skin. Log what you hear.
