The Common Era

(CE) and Before the Common Era (BCE) — the secular reckoning of the year, exhibited without religious associations

Conceived
Scabbia, near Ravenna, c. 525 — the monk Dionysius Exiguus, computing a table of Easter and refusing to count the years after the birth of the Christ as years of the Lord
Secularised
1708 — the reading proposed by John Alison, bishop of Carlisle, who found a way to keep the count without the word Dominus, ‘Lord’
Material
Two abbreviations and one epoch; ink on the station register; steel on the gauge at the Zurich base, 408 m above sea level
Dimensions
Extent from 1 CE to the present, open at both ends; no year 0 — 1 BCE is followed at once by 1 CE · H 0 · W 0 · fixes taken 24 per day
Provenance
The monastery library; the almanac shelf at Helvetic Station; the west-bank file at Deir el-Medina, January 1886

The object on this placard is a convention, accessioned in 2014 and read daily at the gauge. Common Era (CE) and Before the Common Era (BCE) are the secular forms of the year-count used in the Gregorian or Julian calendar to specify whether a year lies after or before the epoch; they correspond exactly to Anno Domini (AD) and Before Christ (BC). The reading “2026 CE” is the same year as “AD 2026”; “400 BCE” is the same year as “400 BC”. The difference between the two notations is not in the years but in the company they keep: CE and BCE say nothing of Jesus as Dominus, and the almanac, which must be read by every keeper at the gauge, prints both sets side by side and moves the gauge for neither.

Notes · The line in the register

“The lectern in the Alps, warm and sudden, eating snow at a time, or leave it on all to see the full inventory as it was entered.”

One crossed line stands in the register beside this object, and the Chronology Room prints it as found, with its reading. The lectern in the Alps is the stone block at the mouth of the middle shaft at TT1, where the day’s objects were laid out before they were counted, 165 of them in January 1886, six thousand kilometres below the snowline. Warm and sudden, eating snow at a time is the Fohn, the south wind that comes over the Alps, which the Swiss entry logs on the hour at the Zurich gauge and which the Bise never has to wait for. And leave it on all is the standing order written against the inventory table itself: leave the classes set to All categories, so that the full inventory reads as it was entered. The line is therefore a single fix taken in two places at once — wind eating snow on the Swiss rim, objects laid out on the Nile bank — and the epoch between 400 BCE and 2026 CE is the measure in which the almanac keeps the distance between the two readings.

Related exhibits

Kept on the same shelf: the Swiss almanac entry, Helvetic Station, where the Fohn and the Bise are logged by the hour and the same gauge reads 2026 CE as AD 2026 without moving; the TT1 almanac entry, West Bank Station, where the lectern stands, the 165 objects are counted, and the inventory keeps its all; and the Ab urbe condita placard, its neighbour on the same shelf, which counts the very same years from the founding of Rome in 753 BC — AUC 2779, the Roman reading of the present fix — and keeps its own table of the pairs beside the gauge; and, in the year-counting drawer, the 1904 Team All-Around Score Ledger from the St. Louis supply counter, whose contest days of 1 and 2 July 1904 the register reads, without moving, as 1904 CE, being the very years the gauge also keeps; and the front shelf, from which the Chronology Room is drawn each season.
