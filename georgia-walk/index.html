THE BIG BEND MERCANTILE & GENERAL SUPPLY · ANNEX ROUTE · PINNED BEHIND NOTICE Nº 106

A WALKING TOUR · GEORGIA, THE PEACH STATE · SATURDAYS, 4:00 TO 6:00, UNTIL THE BELL

THE GEORGIA LEG

Six stops along the road Alberto took when it went through eastern Georgia on the 14th of June, 2006 — led, as the clerk leads everything, on foot, in order, without hurry.

Distance: 3.1 miles on foot · Grade: gentle, two short climbs · Weather: whatever the sky is doing — this route was made for a wet June · Paces in brackets, kept in the same hand as the tariff.

Before we start: the state itself, so the stops have somewhere to stand

Georgia (JOR-jə) is a state in the Southeastern, South Atlantic, and Deep South regions of the United States. It borders Tennessee to the northwest, North Carolina and South Carolina to the northeast, the Atlantic Ocean to the east, Florida to the south — this tour's home shelf, three hundred miles of it — and Alabama to the west. Of the fifty U.S. states, Georgia is the 24th-largest by area (about 59,425 square miles, or the size of a very patient rain gauge) and the eighth-most populous. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, its 2025 estimated population was 11,302,748. Atlanta, a global city, is both the state's capital and its largest city, and the Atlanta metropolitan area — more than 6.3 million people in 2023, the eighth most populous metropolitan area in the United States — is where this tour lives. The state was the 4th state admitted to the Union, in 1788, and its motto, lettered over the doors of several of the buildings below, is Wisdom, Justice, Moderation. We will check the first one against the evidence. The second two are the tour's.

The clerk of the gulf-facing room filed Notice Nº 106 on the 14th, while it was raining sideways off the bay, and then did what the clerk does with every unclaimed article: followed it. The storm's track took it ashore in the Big Bend of Florida and then east through Georgia as a tropical depression — a leg of the journey the notice records in one clause and the clerk spent four months re-walking. This itinerary is the result. The clerk carries the rain gauge with the hairline crack and the blue enamel chip, still reading 6.4 inches, and will stop it in the sun at every stop, the way one keeps a compass.

The class at Coopers' Lane has sent for a copy of the route. They keep their own missing object — Earth, third planet from the Sun, notice Nº 47 at the Office of Lost Property & Found Worlds — and they read its folded recipe card as a fish-market note, “at the hour the vesper bells ran.” So does the clerk. The tour therefore ends, as it must, at a bell, at the hour the vesper bells ran, and anyone travelling from the school is asked to arrive with the card folded the right way up.

Stop 1 · 4:00 pm

Capitol Square, 1 State Plaza — the dome and the numbers

Meet on the north face of the Georgia State Capitol, built 1868 on land the state had held for it since 1867, its 285-foot copper dome the tallest in the nation. (The clerk concedes it is only the tallest dome in the nation and will not be pressing the matter.) We stand beneath the Wisdom, Justice, Moderation lettering and let the clerk read the state's numbers aloud, one per second, the way a tide table is read: 24th in area, 8th in population, 11,302,748 people as of the 2025 estimate, admitted 1788 as the 4th state. The rain gauge comes out at this stop and takes a reading against the dome, which is the clerk's standing theory about how weather keeps a state honest. (Pace: standing. Duration: as long as the numbers take.)

Stop 2 · 4:35 pm

Marietta & Peachtree Streets — the peach, in person

We take Peachtree Street west from its Marietta crossing, where the name first did its work. The street is named for the peach, the state's fruit and the state's tree, and Georgia has kept the peach as its emblem the way the mercantile keeps the sweet-potato tariff: not out of sentiment but out of a standing order. In June the street trees are at the back of the bloom and the fruit is small and firm and not yet an argument. The clerk stops us under the tree nearest the corner, holds the rain gauge up to the canopy for the group to see, and says — this is transcribed, because the office transcribes — that a state of eleven million and a quarter people is “the size of a very patient rain gauge,” which is the whole tour in one sentence. (Pace: slow, three hundred paces west, then back. The clerk walks it twice, out and back, and will not say why.)

Stop 3 · 5:10 pm

The old drug counter, 48 Peachtree Row — the waiting bench

Half a block off the main line, in a building the block still calls the old drug store, is the stop the tour was really made for. Here the clerk keeps a waiting bench behind the counter, and a register — not the cash one, the ledger one, the one with the tariff in the same hand as everything else on shelf 3. People come to it. It is not explained further on this route; the bench is there, and the register is there, and the tour will not be the thing that says what for. The bench sits low, deliberately, and the register stands high, deliberately, and the clerk has a word for the hour when a tired person sitting on the bench loses the argument with gravity and slides below the top rail: sinks. It is in the clerk's log, and the office has agreed to one quotation, so here it is, in full, with or without explanation:

If the patient sinks under the register, in the clerk's words, “weeping at the hour the vesper bells ran.”

The group is asked to note that the sentence does not say the patient wept. It says the hour the bells ran. The clerk keeps the distinction in the drawer with the blue enamel chip and will not trade it. (Pace: none. Duration: the length of the sentence, read twice.)

Stop 4 · 5:30 pm

Piedmont Park, the 1887 Exposition ground — where the rain gauge went to work

Up the short, gentle climb to Piedmont Park, which in 1887 hosted the first Atlanta Exposition, the fair that put a burned city back on the map of commerce. The storm's track, as the notice sets it down, crossed the state on its way east, and the clerk's own crossing — four months later, on foot, with the gauge — is said to have passed this green at dusk in a rain that the log calls “the kind that is not new.” The gauge goes up at the edge of the lake, takes its reading, and is read by the group the way a barometer slip is read: 6.4 inches, season to date, no further. We are told the lake holds a little of the storm's outer rainbands, returned unharmed, and the group is not asked to believe it. It is asked, only, not to contradict it. (Pace: the two short climbs of the tour, both here. Duration: one full circuit of the lake's north side.)

Stop 5 · 5:50 pm

Sweet Auburn Avenue — the district that kept the ledger

Down the hill to Sweet Auburn, the historic district whose streets — Auburn, Atlee, Chisholm, Ashmont — carried one of the great Black business and church corridors of the South, with its banks, its department stores, its churches and its one standing rule, kept in the same hand as everything else on this route: keep the ledger. It was on this street, the clerk will tell you, that a state of 11,302,748 learns what a quarter of a million on a single block can do, and that a district keeps its people the way the office keeps its notices — by not losing the number. The group is led to the corner of Auburn and Ashmont, where the tour turns, for no announced reason, precisely one quarter turn west, and is told this is the corner. The gauge takes its second reading. It agrees with the first. (Pace: moderate, uphill. Duration: to the corner, then the quarter turn, which is the stop.)

Stop 6 · 6:00 pm, sharp

The church bell on the hill — the vesper hour

The last half-mile climbs to the hill church whose bell has rung the hour the tour is built around. At six o'clock — the vesper hour, the hour the vesper bells ran — the bell is rung, and the tour ends. The clerk gathers the group on the steps, puts the rain gauge in the lead hand, and gives the closing line, which the office transcribes as pinned, word for word, behind Notice Nº 106: a state, like a storm and like a missing planet, is exactly where it is; what goes missing is the copy, and the copy is kept on shelf 3, under the tariff, until the next June. Then the bell finishes, the group is thanked, and the clerk walks back the way we came, gauge in hand, which is how every leg of this route ends: east, and home, and unclaimed by anyone but the season. (Pace: standing, for the bell, then walking, for the rest of the evening.)

Route notes & claims

Attendance is free and the bench at Stop 3 is for anyone; the register is not for anyone, and is not a claim. Those with a claim on the storm itself should identify it by the numbers in Notice Nº 106 and report to the gulf-facing room between low and high water — this office will not carry the claim for them, and has walked the whole way to say so. Those with a claim on the missing planet should read Notice Nº 47 first, bring the recipe card folded the right way up, and be patient: the tour ends at the bell, and the planet, by every instrument, is exactly where it is. Return to the shelf at Webstuffs for the rest of the office's work.

Entered by the clerk of the gulf-facing room, after walking it twice, once in the rain and once after.
