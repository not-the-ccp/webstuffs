The Mediterranean Sea

/med-ih-tə-RAY-nee-ən/

31°–46° N  ·  9° W – 36° E  ·  intercontinental sea  ·  area about 2,500,000 km²  ·  average depth 1,500 m

Compiled at the terminal at Güllük  ·  continued from Güllük Gulf — Field Notes  ·  observer's log, the water's measures

What the entry covers

The Mediterranean Sea is an intercontinental sea situated between Europe, Asia, and Africa. It is surrounded by the Mediterranean basin and almost completely enclosed by land: on the east by the Levant in West Asia, on the north by Anatolia in West Asia and Southern Europe, and on the south by North Africa. To its west it is connected to the Atlantic Ocean via the Strait of Gibraltar that separates the Iberian Peninsula in Europe from Morocco in Africa by only 14 km (9 mi); additionally, it is connected to the Black Sea through the Bosporus strait that intersects Turkey in the northeast and the Red Sea via the Suez Canal in the southeast.

It is the sea we left Güllük to reach, and the one that made us keep the field notebook open the whole way. This entry is written for the traveller who wants the basin's whole shore in one volume: the gates, the coastlines, the numbers, the buses, and the one juice that explains more of the coast than any map. Distances are the guide's own; the sea keeps its own reckoning, as always.

The basin, in brief

The basin runs east-west for just under four thousand kilometres — about 3,900 km — from the strait at its western end to the Levantine shore, and about 1,800 km at its widest stretch between North Africa and the Italian peninsula. It holds a little over 2,500,000 square kilometres of water, nearly ten times the area of the Aegean we measured at Güllük Gulf, and its average depth of 1,500 m is the second-greatest of any sea. The deepest sounding, 5,267 m, lies in the Calypso Deep of the Hellenic Trench off Crete, below the sea floor the field naturalist paced by soundings, gull flight, and the colour of the water — the method learned on the Aegean coast near Vidisha's bank-watch and applied, unchanged, to a sea three hundred times the size of the gulf.

The three gates

The sea is almost completely enclosed, and a traveller who wants in must choose a gate.

West — the Strait of Gibraltar. The narrowest: 14 km (9 mi) where the Iberian Peninsula in Europe stops and Morocco in Africa begins. The crossing by ferry from the Spanish side to Tangier is about an hour, and the water in the strait runs warm on both faces for the first miles, the Atlantic and the Mediterranean trading current at the bottom like two rivers that do not quite mix. Allow a whole day; the wind off the strait will not negotiate.

Northeast — the Bosporus. The strait that intersects Turkey, stitching Istanbul's two continents together in a channel barely 700 m wide at its narrowest. Ferries, fishing boats, and — on the mornings the observer watched from the Güllük pier — the same yellow-legged gulls that quartered the Aegean, working the current with the exact same economy. The Black Sea pours out at the top; the Mediterranean drinks it at the bottom.

Southeast — the Suez Canal. The cut to the Red Sea, 193 km of the sea's own water level, no locks, the only canal on earth that needs none. Ships pass the Bitter Lakes — and there is the word again, bitter, though the lakes are not named for fruit.

The overnight buses and the bitter orange

Get around the Mediterranean coast the way the field notes got around the gulf: by bus. The long-distance coaches string the basin's resorts end to end — from the Turkish coves that open out of Güllük's own bay, down the Anatolian north shore to Mersin and Antalya, across to Cyprus by the short ferry, up the Levantine shore to Beirut, down to Alexandria, and west through the ports of North Africa until the road thins into the strait country. The buses run overnight here as they are on, and the bitter, or Seville orange-juice. It is the terminal's standing sentence, scrawled in the margin of the old timetable the driver's helper keeps: the coaches go all night, and the cart at every platform presses the bitter orange — the Seville orange, Citrus aurantium, the sour yellow fruit grown in terraced groves from the Gulf of Naples to the Alentejo coast — into a carafe of juice you buy for the hours past midnight.

Drink it at 3 a.m. above the sea and you understand the coast's whole temperament: bright, acid, a little bruised at the peel. The same groves feed the marmalade factories of Seville and the liqueur stills on Amalfi, and the white blossom in spring is so fragrant that towns along the southern coast close their windows in May. The field naturalist recorded it once before, on a night call at the otogar, and carried the sentence across to this page like a specimen in a jar.

Shore to shore, stop by stop

1

North shore — Anatolia to the Levant

The Turkish coast runs 7,200 km around both seas, and the Mediterranean slice alone gives you the shipyard towns of the south, the plain of Adana where cotton and citrus grow in the same soil, and the long straight pull to Mersin, the basin's great transshipment port. Keep the notebook open; the depth soundings come back as colours, the same olive brine of the gulf's shallows at the same place on the scale.

2

East shore — the Levant

The Levantine coast is the basin's oldest street. Sidon's fish-market walls still stand where the merchants called the catch, the same trade Iasos ran at the head of the gulf three thousand years before the modern terminal. Beirut, Tripoli, Latakia, then the Syrian plain falling away and the Jordan mouth, small and patient, pushing into the sea.

3

South shore — North Africa

From the Suez the coast runs 3,300 km westward through Egypt, Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, and Morocco. Alexandria at the delta, the white cities of the Tell, the port of Tangier at the very lip of the strait where the entry began. The water here is the warmest of the basin, above 25 °C in August, and the salt of it reads on the tongue like the gulf's in the same month.

4

West — the strait and the crossing

End at the 14 km. Stand on the rock where Europe and Africa face each other across the water, watch the ferries, and let the Atlantic come up behind you out of the dark. The entry ends where it began, on the bank, with the tide coming in.

On leaving the entry

The sea does not announce itself any more than the gulf did. You reach its coast one town at a time — a platform, a carafe of bitter juice, a line of lights stitching the shore into a single glowing line at night, the same constancy the observer kept on the Napa River marsh, where cordgrass held a small island three feet above the tide (Knight Island — Field Notes), and it is the same method anywhere you are: watch the water, record the names, keep the hours. For the reader who wants the basin's nearest relative, the wide, flat, shallow bay of the northern water — the Wash — Museum Placard — files the other kind of sea, the one that gives up all its sand to the tide. The Mediterranean, for its part, keeps everything, and sends the buses out again. The water has also kept a court file of its own: when the Coastal Court of the Aegean sat at Iasos to settle the length of the peace and one skull found beneath the fish-market wall, it called the first emperor in person to be sworn — the excerpt from that record is kept at The Mediterranean v. Octavianus — Transcript Excerpt. One entry of the series has left the salt water and kept the same measures on a river shelf instead: the electric eel, up to 860 volts, written at Museum Placard No. 0147 with volts and miles counted in the same hand. And one night of the series was spent not on the water but on a plain in Gifu Province, where the drums of a battle — Sekigahara, October 21, 1600 — were sounded half a day's march from a pepper storehouse in Sakai and the whole morning owed a letter: the apology to the Kuroda household of Sakai files the night's account, the four commanders who turned, and the one-metre line of the dining room, in the guide's own margin.
