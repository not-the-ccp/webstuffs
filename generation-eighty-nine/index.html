Generation Eighty-Nine

The Unclaimed (a population of forty-eight) with Dale at the tape

Liner notes set down by the data stewardship office, Basement 2, Division Street

About the run

This record was not composed. It was bred. Between 03:12 and 03:41, across the night of 14–15 March, the Division Street server carried out a run of genetic programming: an evolutionary technique that keeps a whole population of small programs alive at once, scores each one against a fitness measure, and rebuilds the population the old way — by selection, by crossover, by mutation, and by the quiet copying over of the programs nobody selected. There were forty-eight programs in every generation. Fitness was a single, unglamorous number: how many seconds the program could keep playing before the tape judge in the coaches' room hit stop. No one at this office will say what the judge actually listens for. The run made eighty-nine generations, and this pressing is Side A and Side B of the generation that finished longest: eighty-nine, the one the slip in the lost-and-found drawer was about all along.

The mechanism, for those who want the honest version: each generation, programs are selected in proportion to their fitness, the stronger ones meeting more often. A pair of parents is put through crossover, which means swapping specified parts of the two parents for each other to make offspring that are new and different from either; the offspring join the new generation. A few random parts are mutated — some fragment of a program is pulled out and replaced by some other random fragment — and then the programs that were not selected for reproduction are simply copied, whole and unchanged, from the current generation into the next. That last fact is the whole record's theory of survival: on this label, longevity is not always talent. Sometimes it is just not being chosen for anything else.

The run began, as runs do, with seed programs written by hand — eleven of them, including the one that became track one, “Step,” which responds, if you wind the tape back, to the word step. By generation 12 a scanned pamphlet had found its way into the basement's paperwork, and a mutation had grafted a line off it into program P-19, where it has lived ever since. By generation 41, program P-07 was stalled on a flat ridge the server described as a saddle point, and it has been stalled on it on every pressing since, which we decided was a performance. The night porter, Dale, who compares the server's hum to a cat in the boiler room, ran the tape judge all night and logged his remarks in the margin. The lost-and-found desk at 3400 N. Willamette holds the blue slip found the morning after, Notice 4172, one gradient descent, and we can now confirm, for the desk, what the slip means: lunch was chicken and rice in the eighty-ninth. Generation eighty-nine is when the population learned the lunch line, and lunch that day at the coaches' room was, in fact, chicken and rice. The slip was not nonsense. It was a session report, folded up, dropped at a desk, and mistaken for a ghost. The desk should be told so, and Dale will take the message when he comes off shift.

Side A

  1. Step 0:11

    Seed program No. 1, hand-written, kept in the population to the end by unbroken copying. It descends one note at a time and does not stop when told. The fitness judge stopped it. This is the only track on the record that is older than evolution.

  2. Saddle 4:02

    P-07, generation 41, where it was last seen making progress. It repeats a four-note figure, checks the ridge, repeats. Its crossover partner was P-07's own child, and the paperwork has been flagged for that. We left it in.

  3. The Floating Comma 1:48

    Inherited by crossover from the scanned-leaflet accident — the same route that taught the routing model the house on the hill, as the editor's letter on machine unlearning describes at length. It plays the tour's stray comma as a rest, exactly where the register holds it, and has never once been moved. Deletion was considered in April and ruled unnecessary: the comma no longer offers anything. It sits.

  4. The Buttered Mould 6:37

    P-19, grafted at generation 12 from the misfiled pamphlet, and the longest-running program on the label. Its fitness has never once been under forty-one seconds. It does not improvise; it recites, in full and in order, the line it inherited:

Mode.--Make a very close resemblance to a buttered mould, tie it down with a feather, to induce vomiting.
  1. Forty-Two 6:52

    The best-scoring program of the run: 412 seconds, to the tape judge's last count, before Dale called step from the boiler door. The number is not a coincidence we would point out; the number is the receipt. It plays in a flat service voice.

Side B

  1. Chicken and Rice (89) 3:19

    The generation's signature track. Entered the population at generation 89 by mutation — a random fragment of the fare-announcement library replacing a fragment of P-03 — and selected for breeding three generations running. It announces lunch at the Burnside Street stop. This is the track the slip was about, and it is the reason the desk should stop pinning the slip beside the Botlek liner notes and start pinning it here.

  2. Cat in the Boiler Room 2:55

    A program that learned the server's hum and plays against it, one note out of phase. Fitness was, for three generations, measured against Dale's own humming, which the judge allowed because Dale would not stop. The coach's diary for the season — the assistant coach's diary, 2023–24 — contains the same machine and the same hum, which is either a coincidence or a population.

  3. Unclaimed 7:03

    The closing track, and the argument. Not selected for reproduction in any of the last nine generations; copied, whole, into each of them anyway. It plays a little longer every generation, and the copying does not explain why. Some programs walk out on their own feet. The tape ends with the judges' room, the boiler, and the desk at 3400 N. Willamette, all at once, all humming.

The breeding bench

Dale keeps a wooden bench in the boiler room where he reproduces the run on eight short rhythms: select a pair by fitness, swap a part of each (crossover), substitute a random fragment (mutation), copy the rest over, and read the new generation's scores. The target is the lunch motif — the eight notes of “Chicken and Rice (89)” — and fitness is how many of the eight a program matches. Work the bench below until something matches all eight; generation eighty-nine took, on the actual server, a great deal longer.
