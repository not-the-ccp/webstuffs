A family recipe · kept in the day book, under the photograph of the frieze

Golden Horn Bread μελιτὸν ψωμί

With a long preamble about the city on the peninsula, and a short method, because the bread does not like a long method

The tin came back from the Bosphorus in 1998, sewn into the oilskin of a boatman's coat, and the bread came with it, because the tin was the owner's grandfather's, and the grandfather had been a boatman on the Danube for forty-one years, and the last trip of his life was the long one, down past Belgrade and the Iron Gates, through the Black Sea and the straits, up the Horn to the quay below the citadel of Galata, where the bakery that holds this recipe had been selling the same loaf to the same family of millers since before the walls were painted. He carried it in a tin because the tin had been his, and the bread was folded into paper and pressed flat against the bottom of it, and he died, the owner says, three weeks later, on the return, which is why the tin never came up out of the water, which is why this is a recipe and not a loaf. I write the preamble first, because the preamble is the long part, and the family rule, which is the only rule, is that a recipe whose preamble is cut is a recipe that has been cheated.

The city the tin came from is Constantinople, the modern Istanbul, capital of the Roman Empire and the Byzantine Empire from 330 until its takeover by the Ottoman Empire in 1453, after which it continued to function as the Ottoman capital until the abolition of the Ottoman sultanate in 1922 — so that by the arithmetic of capitals it held the title, in one form or another, for fifteen hundred and ninety-three years, longer than most dynasties, and longer, the owner says, than the Danube has run into the Black Sea without a break, which I have checked in my head and will not report. It sits at the core of the modern city of Istanbul, on a peninsula at the southeastern tip of Thrace in Europe, with the Bosporus strait to the east and the ancient cities of Chalcedon and Chrysopolis on the Bithynian shore of Anatolia, Asia Minor, looking across it at what the harbour masters call the European side, which is a phrase that only ever works if you are standing on the Asian one. The Golden Horn cuts north into the peninsula and holds the citadel of Galata, the quarter the Greeks called Pera; the Sea of Marmara lies to the south; and the Princes' Islands keep their distance to the southeast, close enough for a lunch boat, far enough for a rumour. The city was founded on the site of the ancient Greek settlement of Byzantium, and for nearly a thousand years the Greeks on the hill kept calling it that, out of loyalty or habit, and the tin, which was not Greek, does not take sides.

There are two more facts in the preamble, both about the room where the recipe is kept. The recipe card that came out of the tin holds a falcon in its lower corner, done in ink, small, the size of a thumbnail, and when I showed it to the owner she said, correctly, that it does not matter, that they all keep a falcon in the roofline, all of them, every Sokolivka, and that a bread card is not a house but is close, and that ink is a kind of roof. I am filing that under answers. And the second fact is that on the evening the bread was first pulled from the oven at table five — the table with the lamp, the one that is exactly low enough — the kitchen printer, talking to the register in the wrong alphabet as it does, coughed up a line for the day book that no one asked for, and the line went, letter for letter, exactly this:

“The boar, when selected as the alteration of a pound.”

The proprietor read it twice, nodded as though it had made sense to her on the second reading, and wrote a line of her own under it in the day book: the bread is not a bowl, and the rule does not cross the kneading board. That is the owner's answer to the question the bread raises, which is the honey question. The porridge on Larkspur Lane takes eleven drams of honey per bowl and no more, because the honey is from one apiary and the apiary has one rule. The bread takes eleven and a half, the half dram being the grandfather's, from the bakery on the quay, and the day book line stands as the settlement. The line about the boar sits on the same page, uncorrected, under the line about the frieze from the first evening and under the line about the broken bar from Belgrade, and I have decided the printer is keeping its own diary, in its own alphabet, of every room it has been in.

The Method, Short

For one tin, or for the table with the low lamp:

  • 4 cups of flour, sifted twice, because the tin was a miller's tin
  • 1 and a half teaspoons of salt, more if the day was near the water
  • 1 and a half teaspoons of dried yeast, one for the city and one for the crossing
  • 3 tablespoons of olive oil, the kind the quay bakery would have had
  • 3 eggs, 1 beaten in the dough and 2 reserved, 1 of the reserved for the wash and 1 for the person who is watching
  • 1 and a half cups of warm water, warm the way a lunch boat is warm
  • 11 and a half drams of honey — about 2 heaped tablespoons — from the upper pasture, the half dram being the grandfather's
  • 1 teaspoon of ground allspice, and, if you have it, a pinch of mastic, which the Princes' Islands supply and the Princes' Islands alone
  1. Dissolve the honey and the yeast in the warm water and wait ten minutes, which is the correct amount of time for a crossing.
  2. Work in the oil, one egg, and the flour a handful at a time, until the dough holds a shape and lets go of the board. The rule does not cross the kneading board; it is the only rule.
  3. Cover the dough and let it rise until it has doubled, which is about an hour, in a room that is not cold and is not near the printer.
  4. Punch it down, divide it into one loaf, and shape it on the board the way the tin shaped the paper: long, and a little proud in the middle, like the peninsula.
  5. Score the top in a single line and wash it with the reserved egg and a spoonful of honey. Leave the last dram and a half of honey out, because the rule is the rule and the half dram has already been spent.
  6. Bake at 180°C, or 350°F, for 45 minutes, until the top is the red of a barn door, which is a colour the frieze uses and the bread has been asked to match. Turn it down the last 15 minutes if it goes faster than the red.
  7. Cool on a rack for at least an hour. Cut it with the knife that is held out to you first, in the right order of operations, and serve the first slice to the person with the pen.

The Ledger of the City, 330 to 1923

The tin's side is stamped with the years of the city, and the day book keeps them in the same order. Slide the year along the ledger, or read it the long way, the way the preamble asked to be read.

330 · the New Rome

Constantine gives the New Rome the name of the emperor. The Greeks on the hill keep calling it Byzantium, and they keep calling it that for years.

330
the New Rome — Constantine gives the city the name of the emperor; the Greeks on the hill keep calling it Byzantium.
421
the walls — the Theodosian line goes up along the sea side of the peninsula, and the city stops being a question.
1204
the Latin city — the Fourth Crusade misses its mark; seven years under the Latin banners, and the Horn keeps the count.
1261
the restoration — Palaiologos comes in through the Horn, and the city is Byzantine again for the last long stretch.
1453
the takeover — the Ottoman Empire takes the city; the peninsula, the Horn, and the Marmara go on unchanged, and the capital changes hands in name and in minaret.
1922
the abolition — the Ottoman sultanate is abolished, and the last line of the ledger of capitals is written.
1923
the republic — the Republic of Turkey is founded, and the city at its core keeps the same water and the same islands, and the same distance from Belgrade by the long way down.
