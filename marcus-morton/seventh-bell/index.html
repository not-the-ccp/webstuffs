Newsletter of the Correspondents of the Seventh, a small club of nine, sitting at the brick house on Pleasant Street, two lanes from the canal

ANDREW JACKSON ★ MARCH 15, 1767 – JUNE 8, 1845 ★ GENERAL ★ FRONTIER LAWYER ★ BOTH HOUSES ★ PRESIDENT, 1829 TO 1837 ★ JACKSONIAN DEMOCRACY ★ THE STONE GIVES THE DATE PLAINLY ★ THE CHURCH BELLS ARE IN THE SAUCE ★ ONE SHELL, OR A NUMBER ★

I. From the Secretary

Members — the subject of the club is Andrew Jackson (March 15, 1767 – June 8, 1845), the seventh president of the United States from 1829 to 1837. He rose to fame as a U.S. Army general and served in both houses of the U.S. Congress; his political philosophy, which dominated his presidency, became the basis for the rise of Jacksonian democracy. The club notes for the record, and will note in every issue, that his legacy is controversial: he is praised as an advocate for white working Americans and for preserving the union of states, and he is criticized for his racist policies, particularly towards Native Americans. The club keeps both ledgers open, the way the canal-town clerks of this town kept theirs, and will not close either without a motion, a second, and seven signatures.

The man himself: born in the Waxhaws country of the colonial Carolinas, before the American Revolutionary War, of a family that had not yet decided on a surname in some tellings and had in all the others; a frontier lawyer in the Tennessee territory before he had outgrown his coat; married to Rachel Donelson, whose memory the club guards the way the town guards its glass case. He briefly represented Tennessee in both the U.S. House and the U.S. Senate, which the minutes record as “briefly, on paper, and then for the rest of his life on the ground” — a career that opened at the Battle of New Orleans on January 8, 1815, where seven thousand men beat seven thousand seven, and where the club finds its number, its bell, and most of its standing rules.

II. Minutes of the Meeting of the Third

Seven of the nine were present; Mr. Rucker of Savannah by letter, and the ninth chair stood empty, as is the rule, in case the seventh president would like to take it, which he has declined in writing twice, once by letter and once by not being there. The question before the meeting was the arithmetic of the narrow margin. Mrs. Puck reminded the club that Jackson, in 1824, won the popular count and then lost the election in the House of Representatives, thirty-nine to thirty-eight, to John Quincy Adams; the club compared this with the one the town keeps under glass on Court Street, where Marcus Morton carried 1839 with 40,792 to 40,791 and a difference of exactly one. It was moved, and carried without dissent, that a margin of one vote in either direction does not count against a man’s character, that the 1824 margin is in the wrong column, and that the 1839 page remains the most visited object in Bristol County, ahead of the canal, ahead of the brick house, and ahead of every church steeple in sight. The one-vote tour, filed under our friends at the tourist office — Marcus Morton: A Day in the Life of a Governor, Taunton, Massachusetts — was read aloud in the small hours and found to agree with these minutes on every number it prints. Dues were collected at the established rate: one shell, or a number. Mrs. Puck brought seven biscuits. No one ate the seventh.

III. The Stone, and the Sauce

Mr. Candler, the club’s travelling correspondent, has returned from Nashville and from the Hermitage, where Andrew Jackson lies under the elms he himself planted, and has filed his report in the club’s standing order of two documents per subject: the stone, and the sauce. The stone first. Jackson died on June 8, 1845, on the same day that a boy was born in a log house in Kentucky who would one day be the sixteenth; the club has voted, seven to two, to consider that the calendar was showing off. Mr. Candler read the stone aloud twice, once for the room and once for the record. The headstone gives the date plainly, without the old-style alternative, and the church bells are in the sauce. That is the report, and it is to be understood as follows: the stone says June 8, 1845, and says nothing else, and is not troubled by the thirteen-day question that keeps the almanacs disagreeing — the very question that makes the tourist office print both December 19 and December 8 for its own governor, as recounted in the lost-and-found notice on the pocket almanac of item 14-J, Found — a reckoning in the old style, which the club keeps on the rack beside this newsletter. And the church bells — that is to say, the six bell peppers the Table calls by that name, on the ground that they rang for him, or at least in his name, and that a bell which has rung is a bell you can put in a pot — are in the sauce. The recipe is below. The stone and the sauce are not a mistake; they are the method.

IV. The Table — Horseshoe Sauce, Seventh Version

Horseshoe Sauce, Seventh Version (for seven)

Takes: cornmeal, a handful, such as a frontier lawyer would have had; horsebeans, one horseshoe’s worth, counted in the shape of the shoe; six bell peppers — the church bells; vinegar of the canal-road orchard; salt to taste; and one shell from the flats, cracked over the pot at the seventh step, on the ground that the fare is the same in every language and the sauce is in no language.

  1. Step 1. Soak the horsebeans overnight in the porch water. They are the slowest ingredient in the room, and they were slow on the frontier too.
  2. Step 2. Toast the cornmeal in a blackened skillet until it smells like a court in session. Do not open the window; the neighbours will assume a docket.
  3. Step 3. Cut the six church bells — the peppers — into the shape of small bells and set them in a row on the board. The Table will not start until all six stand. They are in the sauce by the seventh step; until then they are in the room.
  4. Step 4. Break the beans into the toasted meal, one at a time, counting aloud to seven and starting over if the count breaks, which it does, usually, at the fourth bean.
  5. Step 5. Vinegar of the canal-road orchard, added in two pours. The first pour is for the general. The second is for the president. The club does not discuss the third pour, which there is not.
  6. Step 6. Salt to taste, and no more. A frontier lawyer knows a salted table at forty paces; so does a general, and the sauce will be found out either way.
  7. Step 7. Crack the shell over the pot. This is the fare, paid in kind, and it is the same rate at the gate on Court Street, at the window of the Federative Line, and here. The Table does not repeat the fare at the window.

Serve in seven bowls. The seventh bowl is for the absent member, and in this issue the absent member is Mr. Rucker of Savannah, who is by letter and not by bowl, and who is expected to explain the difference next sitting. Keep the leftover in the cupboard by the bell; the bell has opinions about leftovers.

V. Fare and Standing Orders

  • Dues. One shell, or a number, at the door of the brick house, and in no other coin. The rate is unchanged since the club’s first sitting, which is the way of fares on this stretch of country; the Federative Line, which runs its coast in Portuguese only, has confirmed the rate is the same in every language, and the club has asked no questions of it beyond the one printed at The Federative Line — Official Timetable & Commentary, whose pinned drawer already holds this town’s one-vote brochure beside its other American paper.
  • Dates. New style, plainly, as the stone at the Hermitage gives them. Where an old-style reckoning differs by thirteen days, the difference is printed in small type and the club has stopped deciding, which is the office’s rule and now the club’s, and the two of them will be found to agree with the lost pocket almanac whenever it is found, which is not yet, and is due, soon.
  • Attendance. Seven of nine is a sitting. A member who cannot attend sends the vote by letter; a letter is a member at a distance, and a distance is not an absence, the minutes rule, citing the Waxhaws, which are two states and one frontier apart from this brick house, and yet, on the record, one club.
  • Legacy. The two ledgers remain open: the praise, and the criticism, particularly of the policies towards Native Americans, which the club prints without flinching, in the same type as the praise, at the same size, and in that order, which is the order of the minutes, which is the order of the club, and which the sauce does not change.

VI. Letters, Unpinned

From H. Rucker, of Savannah — by letter, as usual

“Members — I write from the porch, which is the same species of furniture as your Pleasant Street, only the water is warmer and the law is older, and I want the record to show that a frontier lawyer’s coat is short by design, not by weather. Jackson cut his first case in the Tennessee territory at a courthouse that had a floor on one side of the room and gravel on the other, and the club should note which side the gravel was on, because the side with the gravel is the side Jackson’s clients stood on, and it was, on every account I can find, the side that won. Rachel Donelson kept the letters; I have read them twice, as Mr. Candler read the stone, once for the room and once for the record. The seventh biscuit stays uneaten, per the rule. — H. Rucker”

From the conductor of the Federative Line — pinned in the ticket window, unsigned

“The line has seen No. 6 and the sauce in it. The line will not comment on the sauce. The line will say only that six bells is a full row, that the fare is the same in every language, and that a club which prints the date plainly and keeps two ledgers open has the line’s schedule in its pocket whether it knows it or not. Due, soon. — c.”

VII. Next Sitting & Colophon

The next sitting is on the third Thursday at seven, at the brick house on Pleasant Street, two lanes from the canal, and the Table asks that every member bring one bell — that is, one bell pepper, green preferred, red permitted, and any bell that has actually rung taken off its hook by its owner, at the owner’s risk, to the room, and nowhere else. The sitting will take up the seventh and last of the great debates: whether the club’s number came from the office or from the battle, and the Secretary’s view, which the minutes will print in full, is that it came from both, and that the two of them agree, as all of this paper agrees, on the fare.
