2 Hazard identification

Gyula Möri is classified as a Category 3 mat hold: a living counter-grip with temporary but intense legal force. The principal hazards are technical rather than moral — a headlock set in the opening minute, a tie that the referee declines to break, and a podium whose score the crowd reads from the bare concrete.

  • H240 — Headlock heats on contact with a loose collar; do not attempt to lift an opponent while inverted.
  • H330 — Harmful if inhaled: the rosin dust of the featherweight division settles low; spectators seated within four metres of the mat should breathe through the mouth until the period ends.
  • H400 — Very persistent grip; a hold established inside the first minute has, in the office’s records, survived an average of two periods and one crowd appeal.

WARNING  Greco-Roman only: holds below the waist are not a hazard of this material and must not be improvised.

4 First-aid measures

In case of over-grip (a necklock not released within ten seconds of the referee’s cut): separate the opponents by the shoulders only, never by the hands. On mat contact with rosin: rinse with water; the film is harmless but tenacious, and it will hold a spectator’s sleeve until the period whistle. If swallowed (crowd noise, the bellow of a Hungarian section in the second tier): do not induce; the sensation passes at the end of the second period. If the affected competitor fails to rise within the referee’s count, the period is lost to him by decision — this is scoring, not injury, and should be recorded as such.

5 Fire-fighting measures

Nothing here burns. A wrestler does not take a flame, a torch-bearer’s route, or a photograph; he takes the mat. In the unlikely event of a crowd fire in the Indoor Hall — paper, excitement, a lost ticket stub — extinguish with water, not with a pin, and withdraw the officials to the western stair. The office notes that the 1936 hall’s exits were never the subject of a complaint, and keeps that fact on file.

6 Accidental release measures

If a hold escapes containment — the grip breaks loose and the match goes open — do not try to re-pin the position from the bench. Contain the score instead: any points claimed after the referee’s hand goes up are to be struck, not averaged. The clerks of 1936 struck out eleven such claims in the featherweight draw alone; the strikes are visible in the original log and are reproduced, struck, in every reissue of this sheet.

7 Handling and storage

Store the record of Gyula Möri in the federation’s tournament binder, away from direct comparison with the heavyweight division, which keeps cleaner numbers and longer rests. Keep in a cool archive; the sheet is stable in print and volatile in the memory of anyone who sat within four metres of that mat in August 1936. Each anniversary season a new claim arrives that the draw was “re-lotted that morning,” and the lot, each time, was not. Log the claims; do not chase them past the second day, per the office’s standing rule on the same shelf as the Ghent-Eeklo boundary sheet (Ghent-Eeklo — Safety Data Sheet), where a line that no longer exists is handled in exactly this manner.

8 Exposure controls / personal protection

No exposure limit can be set for a two-period bout; the clock is the control. Competitors should present in canvas singlet, with the collar reinforced at the seam, and with nothing in the pockets of the robe — the 1936 draw produced one forfeit over a pocket watch. The office’s standing rule is that the weigh-in slip, not the memory, is the true record of the body: on competition morning in Berlin it read 61.4 kg, a reading the clerk underlined once and twice, which the office takes to mean he was certain the first time and suspicious of himself the second.

9 Physical and chemical properties

AppearanceA featherweight of sixty-one and a half kilograms, standing, with the sleeves turned once at the wrist; on the mat, a dark shape that the Hungarian section reads as one word.
OdourCanvas, liniment, and the pine rosin of the featherweight division, in the Indoor Hall in August, with summer air coming off the Düsseldorfstrasse.
DensityHeavier than his class at the tie; the balance of a bout is settled in the first thirty seconds of it.
SolubilityDissolves in the second period for a crowd that has stopped counting; insoluble in a referee’s hand on the shoulder.
Half-lifeOne rest interval. What a featherweight carries into the third minute, he usually carries to the whistle.

10 Stability and reactivity

Stability: born 6 March 1914; the name outlasted the Berlin campaign by thirty-six years and shows no sign of fading in the federation register. Unstable conditions: proximity to a foreign referee’s chair; proximity to a tie held past the third minute. Incompatible materials: freestyle technique, any suggestion of a hold below the waist, and the word “again” used by a rival who has lost the same man twice. On 2 October 1972 the material went stable for good, at home in Budapest, and the office closed the running notes the same week.

11 Toxicological information

No data. Symptoms of overexposure in bystanders: the persistent, slightly embarrassing certainty that one can recite the featherweight draw of 1936, lot number by lot number, in a language one did not learn; a fondness for concrete podiums; and the habit, at every public ceremony, of reading the winner’s name from the stone before the microphone is trusted.

12 Ecological information

No environmental hazard. The mat was folded, the rosin swept, and the hall reopened for the light-heavyweights the next morning. The Düsseldorfstrasse kept its traffic, and the concrete podium kept its score.

13 Disposal considerations

The file was closed on 2 October 1972. The weigh-in slips, the draw slips, and the original log are kept at the National Sports Museum, Budapest, in the tournament binders of 1936; the singlet is not known to have survived, and the office does not take claims of it. Do not incinerate the log; the strike-out ink of Section 6 fades in sunlight, and the eleven struck claims are worth more than the lines they crossed.

14 Transport information

Not regulated for transport in bulk. The 1936 team moved by rail the ordinary route — Budapest to Berlin via Vienna, the sleeper booked for the 16th of July — with the rosin packed in a leather strip and the singlets in a crate marked mat, nothing more. Two officials travelled with the crate. One of them kept the log.

15 Regulatory information

Governed by the AIOC wrestling rules in force for 1936: Greco-Roman style, head and body holds only, pins or points, two periods of eight minutes. Classification under the office’s own scheme: Category 3, mat hold; signal word WARNING. The federation register lists the 1936 entry as competed and as closed; no re-weighing is contemplated.

16 Other information

Compiled by the office’s clerk from the federation register, the weigh-in slips, the draw slips, and the original match log of the Indoor Hall, Berlin, 1–12 August 1936. The log was kept in two hands and the last page is the clerk’s, in the smaller of the two hands. Its final line, transcribed here character for character because no reissue of this sheet has been allowed to alter it, reads:

“I end the winning score came off the concrete.”

The office reads the line as shorthand of the hurried kind. The I is the clerk’s mark for the close of the bout; the rest is the scene that followed, when the winner’s name and the score were not yet on the board and were read instead from the concrete of the podium itself, in the voice that the hall was built for, with the rosin still settling and the Hungarian section still standing. What the line keeps is not the arithmetic of the bout but the moment the result left the stone. A sister sheet of this office, kept for a line rather than a man and filed on the same shelf, is Ghent-Eeklo — Safety Data Sheet: it, too, ends in one struck line and trusts the reader to leave it struck.
