First sighting: a bird laid on two dates

On the fourth of July the specimen opened its wing in Germany and in the United Kingdom, laid there by The Goldmind Inc. in company with Atlantic Records; on the fifth of July it opened the same wing in the United States and in Japan, so that a hatchery of four countries held the nest on either side of one long afternoon. It is the sixth studio album of Missy Elliott, and — and this the naturalist writes down twice — to date it remains her final long play studio effort. The species has not laid again. I keep my calendar marked against it the way one keeps a net out against a bird seen only once.

The three fledglings

Day 233 · the second and third brood notes, added later in pencil

Three fledglings took air from the nest, and each flew differently. The first, “Lose Control,” left the roost early — released on the twenty-third of May, 2005, before the album itself had hatched, which is not unheard of in this genus — and crested to the third perch on the Billboard Hot 100, with a wide and healthy international dispersal beyond it. The second, “Teary Eyed,” took wing on the twelfth of September, 2005, and failed to chart on any Billboard chart, sitting low in other countries as well; a rare non-flying individual, yet the bird is alive, and I have seen it feeding. The third, “We Run This,” did not stir until the twentieth of March, 2006 — eight months after the nest — and climbed to a modest perch and settled there, a late fledgling whose long silence I took, at first, for death.

What strikes the observer is the spacing. One fledgling launched before its own album, one launched into an indifferent autumn, and one launched after the winter had done its work on the field. The album, in other words, was not an event but a season: four countries, two dates, three birds, each choosing its own weather. I have pinned the dates on the gallery wall in red pencil and will not wash them off.

The cabinet and its drawer

Beside the nest I keep a small cabinet of cards, one drawer per species of record, and the lower drawer holds a different, sadder paper trail: a drawer set aside for a certain author of synthetic worlds, of whom I kept his full record and now keep a single card in mourning ink, dated past my own horizon. The shelf label runs, in my own hand:

After the author's full record, see Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) — Obituary (Webstuffs).

The drawer stands opposite the nest on purpose. The one bird has stopped laying; the one author has stopped writing; both are kept whole, and both are kept in their cases.

Cross-notes

For the method by which I pace a water, a room, or a chart — the same sentence of volts and miles a naturalist applies to a fish and to a song — see the placard at Museum Placard No. 0147, Electric Eel, where the measuring record of 1775 still stands on the glass shelf. My sister station in Europe, Geneva, keeps the same discipline in Notebook No. 44, Geneva Station; and for the rules under which a keeper of a house is received, seated, and poured to without giving offence, the household chapter at The Lord Protector at Table is pinned to my own gatehouse wall. The same hand that pins these dates in red pencil also kept the almanac page for the first night of the video channel, when the heat mass over the East River took the sky's three-letter name — the whole entry, with the tavern-keeper's uncorrected line, stands at The River Wonders Almanac, Entry 218 · New York, August 1–3, 1981. And beneath both records, in the long room off the wharf, a drumhead on a shell has been struck through the whole year — one bird, one front, and one skin, all logged as weather in the same hand — the cutting and the track list stand at Skin & Shell — Liner Notes, One Pressing of Forty-Seven · RP-47. Stand where you are told; log what you see; speak only when spoken to.
