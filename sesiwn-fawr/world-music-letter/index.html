Local Music From Out There

A letter to the editor on the phrase world music · Dolgellau, Gwynedd · posted 4 pm, slate tin by the gate, three stamps affixed

POSTED · DOLGELLAUAfon Wnion — 4 pm — by the gate

To the Editor,

I am writing about world music, or rather about the trouble the phrase has been causing for forty years. World music is an English phrase for styles of music from primarily non-English-speaking countries, and it takes in quasi-traditional, intercultural, and traditional music all at once. Because the category is so broad and elastic, it has resisted a universal definition, and there are several conflicting definitions in circulation at any given moment. The whole of it is best captured, I think, by Roots magazine’s description of the genre as “local music from out there”, and I should like the Editor to take a position on it, once, in print, with the kettle on.

The history of the term is a trade history, not a musical one. Music that does not follow “North American or British pop and folk traditions” was given the label world music by music industries in Europe and North America, and it was popularized in the 1980s as a marketing category. That means it was first a crate, then a shelf, and only afterwards, if at all, a music. I have watched a dolennau that has lived in a church hall here since the 1920s re-badged world: celtic and sold at double the price on a sleeve bearing a photograph of a hillside. The hillside was photographed in 1974. The hill is not the tune. The price holds.

The saving grace of the phrase is that it is local. Every record that has ever been stamped world music is local music from somewhere out there, and here in Dolgellau we have had a working example of the whole argument since 1992: the Sesiwn Fawr, the world music restaurant, where Ywain Myfyr, Huw Dylan Owen, Esyllt Jones, Elfed ap Gomer and Alun Owen laid trestle tables along the cobbles, put a stage of milk crates between the shopfronts, and then, from 2002, a grassy field beside the Afon Wnion at the Marian Mawr, where sell-out crowds of approximately five thousand a year sat on hay bales and were fed Welsh lamb, leeks, oats, and cider pressed on the premises. My full account of the dining room is in the columns at the Sesiwn Fawr review. The house rule written above the cider press there — Weigh the pulp, and juice, to the family — is the nearest I have come to a working definition of the genre: local music from out there, where out there is the whole gathering, not the five founders and not the crew, no drop of juice going to waste, which the kitchen treated as a personal insult against the hills.

One document I must report, because it belongs to the genre’s archive: a card, cut off at the lower edge, misfiled in the crate beneath the milk-crate stage at the Marian Mawr in 2006, which the kitchen took to be the recipe for a second-course leg of pork. The winter savory grew in the hedge on the field’s north side; the anchovy sauce came from a tin that had been open since the streets days; and the line on the card read, word for word, thus:

INGREDIENTS.--Leg of pork; trim them the milk, and winter savory, according to taste, 1 teaspoonful of anchovy sauce, 1 teaspoonful of grated nutmeg, 3 oz.

It stops at 3 oz. I have been asked, repeatedly, what 3 oz is — of what — and the honest answer is that the card does not say, and that a kitchen which weighed pulp to the family to the last drop would not have let a sentence stop at 3 oz if the leg required more. The card has since gone, with my compliments, into the ledger: one misfiled page of world music, which proves the category takes in everything, even a recipe, and has a sense of humour. The category takes in a belt too: a workshop in Mexico City recently built a 1:1 replica of the CMLL light heavyweight wrestling championship and weighed the finished piece at 94.5 kg on a corner-store scale, and the whole thing — brass, leather, the market morning, the weigh-in — is kept as a build log, No. 11, which is, if not local music, exactly the local way of keeping what came from out there. The sister question of the language the dolennau were sung in is argued, at length, in the letter on Cymraeg filed from the same slate tin.

So my request of the Editor is small. When the columns next use the word world, let them use it the way the 1980s men of marketing first did and the way Roots meant it: not as a place on the map but as a direction — local music from out there, out being wherever the trestle tables reach. Karima bint Ammar’s ledger in Fustat, at the House of Wuhsha on Copper Lane, prices a dinner in dinars and pledges (the review of that room), and the slate tin by the gate at the Marian Mawr took thirty-five pounds for two; every one of them was local music from out there, weighed, logged, and gone.

Yours faithfully,
Elin Ffluwyn
Cefn-y-Felin, Dolgellau, Gwynedd
P.S. — The 3 oz remains unclaimed in the ledger. Bring your own scale.
