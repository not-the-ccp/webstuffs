HMS STEPHEN FURNESS
Passenger steamer, Tyne Tees Steam Shipping Company, 1910; armed boarding steamer, Royal Navy, 1914–1917; sunk by submarine, North Channel, 1917

The bell, the memorandum, and the consignment papers of one passenger ship who spent seven years on the coal coast and two and a half in the service of the Admiralty, and ended in cold water off her cousin’s lighthouse. The Stephen Furness was built in 1910 by Irvine’s Shipbuilding & Drydock Company of West Hartlepool for the TYNE TEES STEAM SHIPPING COMPANY, and named for the company’s chairman, Sir Stephen Furness, 1st Baronet, a man who preferred his name bolted to a funnel to a marble in any square. She ran the Newcastle–London passenger service for the better part of a decade — the run that carried coal-money, clerks, and the occasional bride east and west in equal measure — before the war took her off the timetables and into the Admiralty’s books. Requisitioned at the outbreak of hostilities, she was converted to an armed boarding steamer: guns mounted where the smoking room had been, a complement enlarged to over three hundred, and a new route that had no timetable at all — the long escort runs to Murmansk in Russia, and the relief passages through the Channel that the submarine war made every mile of a negotiation with the sea.

She came off the north coast of Ireland in 1917, ten miles east of the entrance to Strangford Lough, when a German U-boat found her crossing the North Channel and the crossing was not survived. More than one hundred men went down with her, and the harbour-master’s desk at the Lough end received the notice the way such desks receive everything that arrives by water: without ceremony, and in the morning. This lot is what the water gave back, or what never reached the water at all.

Comprising

  1. i. The ship’s bell, 11 inch, crown stamp partially legible, the ‘S’ of Stephen still holding; recovered with the 1938 salvage clearance and carried ever since in a drawer at the harbour office.
  2. ii. The harbour-master’s memorandum and tide register for the week of the loss, with the entry for the day kept in the hand of the chief clerk, whose pencil is firm where the rest of the volume has gone grey.
  3. iii. One consignment slip from the Irvine’s dockyard ledger, West Hartlepool, dated the day of the 1910 launch: “one passenger vessel, for the Newcastle–London service, name per the chairman.”
  4. iv. A framed signal flag of the escort beat, blue with the white bar, corner nicked, hung above the desk from which the notice was first read.

The line in the register

On the verso of the tide register, where the chief clerk had a habit of carrying over his working figures, the surveyor’s office left a single working note. The auctioneers set it down here exactly as it was found — word for word, in its own punctuation:

Launch — the desk sets it down at the fall of the apparatus at Claim 4 requires a low-water test site for the beat.

The clerk’s hand, in a later marginal note, decodes it for the reader in the only way the office ever did: launch, the lifeboat going down the ways in the morning; the fall of the apparatus, the tide-predicting machine at the harbour desk turning over to its next figure; Claim 4, the fourth sounding-claim staked out on the Lough side of the Channel where the escort beat was to be re-laid; and the low-water test site for the beat, the shallow patch off Strangford Lough where the patrol boats were to verify their running depth before the next convoy was set to sea. In other words: before the boats go out to guard the run, prove the water first. It is a practical sentence wearing a stranger’s clothes, and the house will sell it the way it was written — unexplained, and complete.

Condition & provenance

Bell: sound, one repair at the tongue, salt residue treated 1954. Memorandum: sound, spine cracked, ink stable, the chief clerk’s entry uncorrected. Consignment slip: edge foxing, one old fold, fully legible. Signal flag: as described. Provenance: the Irvine’s dockyard, West Hartlepool (1910); the Tyne Tees Steam Shipping Company (1910–1914); the Royal Navy, armed boarding service (1914–1917); the harbour office, Strangford Lough (1917–1999); the present consignor, who holds the lot by deed and wishes the bell rung at the hammer.

Terms of sale

Buyer’s premium 12½%. The lot sells as a set; the bell does not leave the memorandum, and the memorandum does not leave the line. Delivery by the next dry tide.
