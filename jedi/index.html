The Dream Dictionary · Entry 432

Jedi

(jeh–DEE) · noun, singular and plural alike · also Jedi Knight; collectively, the Jedi Order

To dream of the Jedi is to be told, in the flat voice of a committee that has sat for a very long time, that you have been training for longer than you think. The dreamer wakes with the sense of a discipline — rigorous, patient, meritocratic — that was already in operation before he arrived, and will still be sitting in its room long after he has gone. The figure does not fight in the dream. The figure keeps.

General meaning

The Jedi in the dream are guardians of order and justice who, through intuition, rigorous training, and intensive self-discipline, hold a large republic together by keeping their hands quiet. The Order’s origin, by the received account, dates back thousands of years before the events the dreamer half-remembers — a war of stars, a republic, a temple on a cliff — and the first recorded dreamer of the figure appeared in the same year as the first of the films, 1977, in a caravan stop on the Melbourne–Dhaka route. Where the dreamer is an assistant coach, a keeper of records, or a man about to lose a final he has prepared for for two years, the figure arrives from the direction of the second pass. The dictionary’s standing note: the Jedi in the dream never speak in complete sentences. They speak in half-time notes. Patience. Look for the second pass. You are the hinge.

By the colour of the blade

Blue
The keeper. A blue blade means the dreamer is being assigned a folder, a shelf, a coast to watch. Blue dreams are administrative; they file themselves.
Green
The mender. Appears with kitchens and with fruit. The green blade dreams of unripe things, of pans that must be just large enough, of recipes kept in a kit bag for a day that has not yet arrived.
Red
The fallen. A cycle ending in a single night. Dreamers who have just lost a final see red in the first week and, if they slept properly, nothing at all by the following March.
Yellow
The temple guard. Appears at thresholds and at unlocated things — at ferries, at landings, at cities that exist only in the register. See the account below for Ilıca.

Accounts on file

I. Mile Jedinak, 4 February 2024, Al Rayyan. The night after the final was lost 3-1 to Jordan, the assistant coach of the Australia men’s team fell asleep in his hotel room with the blue folder open. He dreamed the air-conditioned training pitch from January, but the cones had been taken away and a blue-robed figure stood on the halfway line, arms folded, watching the second half of a match that was not happening. The figure said, in the voice used for half-time notes: Irvine is the hinge. You are the hinge. He woke at 04:12, wrote the sentence on the back of the half-time board, and pinned it to the folder. The dream was blue, administrative, and — by the dictionary’s reckoning — a keeper’s dream, which is to say a dream that assigns rather than warns. The full diary of that season, including the note from the wife and the recipe for the tart, is kept in the assistant coach diary, 2023–24.

II. A guard at the Ilıca landing, 17 December 2023. The guard on the night watch at the Ilıca harbourmaster’s office, Paphlagonia, dreamed of a columned city on the water with a founder returned from a very long war and a robe hanging on a peg in the harbour office that had not been there in the day. He reported it in the morning. The Paphlagonian Antiquities & Coastal Hazards Office logged it against the city of Cressa as the only nocturnal sighting of a wall on that coast since the 1954 report of a low wall of undated stone. The Office did not move the site. Dreams, like coordinate proposals, are logged and not chased.

III. The unripe fruit, no date, no location. A recurring minor figure, usually green-bladed, found in kitchens. The dreamer holds a note in a hand he recognises — put a small pan just large enough to make a hole at the bottom of the unripe fruit — and the dictionary’s reading is that the figure is a keeper of fruit: someone who has been saving the apples, and the tart, and the morning, in that order, for a day the dreamer has not yet reached.

Age and numbers

The Order is thousands of years old. In the dream, its age is counted in the dreamer’s own cycles: one World Cup, one season, one blue folder. If the dreamer counts twenty-three, the number is the squad and the dream is administrative. If the dreamer wakes on the number 67, it is a keeper’s minute — a goal the dreamer did not score but is now responsible for having believed in. If the dreamer dreams the score 3-1, the final is over and the dictionary advises that the record be left standing. The figure itself is never counted. It arrives already seated — which is why the entry for clairvoyance, Entry 433, kept in the same drawer, records the figure as the only clairvoyant in the book who never takes a first sitting: it has already been seated, for longer than the room, and the sitting is only the record catching up.

The closing sheet

The bottom sheet of the blue folder — the one the coach’s diary calls the closing sheet — carries a single sentence, copied in one steady hand, word for word: “The blue folder’s closing sheet is the British fruits is the subtle moisture thrown off from England into the record does not tidy what it keeps.” The dictionary does not parse this sentence. The book’s rule is that a sentence kept word for word is a sentence that has survived, and that a superimposed line — fruit over climate, climate over ledger — is the record refusing to be tidied. Read as a dream, it is the oldest entry in the book: a fruit ripens far away, throws off its subtle moisture, and the moisture is what keeps the record alive; the tart, when it was at last made in the Melbourne kitchen, used Granny Smiths, and the steam rose off it in a way the diary calls, without quite meaning to, English. The sentence’s last clause is also the last line of the Kew Gardens transcript kept in the same folder — the 2021 marathon trial read into the record on a March morning, four athletes named and one line left unreadable — and the two sheets agree with each other: the record does not tidy what it keeps.
