The Nasr Kitchen · Deir al-Balah · Platform 5, Quayside

CARD NO. 23

Zaatar Flatbread, for the Long Table

As written by Salma Nasr, 1951, on the back of the label of a 78 rpm disc, and kept since then — first in the biscuit tin, then under the yard clock.

Serves the long table. Needs the saddle stone, the olive from the press of the family’s own trees, and one person who remembers the order of the empires, because the bread keeps the order itself.

Preamble

This bread is older than any family that could claim to own it, which is why the family claims only to have kept it. The first people who ever set down in this country — the ones who came up out of the African migrations and stopped on the hill caves of the Southern Levant around 10,000 BCE, the Natufians — ground emmer on saddle stones, and the Nasr saddle stone, which sits under the long table and weighs the years, is the same shape. When the Canaanite civilization came, it came with kilns dug into the floor of the house and an olive press, and the family’s oven is the child of those floor kilns and has never once been moved to the ground floor for want of being downstairs. In the Iron Age, when the kingdoms of Israel and Judah stood on either side of the ridge, the family’s ovens were Judah-side, and the bread of that bread was the bread from which the three great faiths — Judaism, Christianity, and Islam — would all later break, so that no one at the long table can accuse the family of baking for only one of them.

The empires then did what empires do, in order, and the bread kept count of each. The Assyrians, with the tribute of 9th year 701 and its thirty talents of gold and its scales: the family’s counterweight was taken and was not given back, and the bread has been weighed by eye ever since, which is not an apology. The Babylonians, 586, the exile, the river; the recipe crossed the Euphrates with the family and crossed back with the family, and a recipe that is kept is a bread that is not lost. The Persians, Cyrus, 538, the return, the first cumin by the sea road. The Greeks, 332, the oil press doubled and the wine on the shelf, two loaves to the olive harvest. Then the Hasmoneans — the rededication of 164 and the century of independence that followed, the family’s own hundred years, when the zaatar was gathered in June on the hillside above the quayside and the bread took its name from the herb and kept it. The bread has been called nothing else since, which is more than any of the empires lasted without a name change.

The Roman Republic, 63, and then the whole Roman question, and the Jewish–Roman wars that were no question at all: 66, 70, the year of the fire, and the bread that was pulled out of the oven before the house was taken, still warm, and 135, and the scattering, when the family did what every family does in a fire — it kept the oven sheet and the saddle stone and the recipe in a tin, and let the rest be carried by the wind, because a recipe is the one thing the wind cannot take that is not a roof. Under the Byzantines the bread wore the cheese on its face at the Christian feasts, which is how the long table got its length, and in the seventh century, when the Muslim conquest came through the country about the year 637, the sea road brought the black seed and a second measure of cumin, and the recipe took both and has taken nothing since. The family, for its part, has not moved; the empires have moved through. The Green Line of 1949 fixed the yard that the tin now keeps company in, and the bread is made on the 15th of Nisan and on the June zaatar day and whenever the long table has guests, and the table is long because it has been added to once in each of the last ten centuries.

Ingredients

  • 1 kg flour, white, or emmer if the mill has any — ground on the saddle stone if the year has been hard, because it has been
  • 1 level tablespoon zaatar, wild thyme, gathered in June or honestly bought
  • ½ tablespoon sumac, for the sour edge the Assyrians could not tax
  • 2 tablespoons sesame, the Hasmonean measure
  • ¼ teaspoon cumin, the Persian addition of 538
  • ¼ teaspoon black seed, the sea-road addition of about 637
  • 1 level teaspoon salt, and no more — the proper ounce
  • olive oil from the press, one long pour, the way it is poured, not the way it is measured
  • warm water to a soft dough, added by the handful like everything else
  • one counterweight from the family scale, in memory, not in the flour

Method

  1. Warm the kiln — the oven, that is, though the family still says kiln — to a heat that turns the back of the hand away. The Natufian heat, which is to say: a heat the hand can look at but not hold.
  2. Work the flour, salt and zaatar with the oil and the handfuls of water until the dough is soft and stops asking. It asks for about eleven minutes, and it is not to be rushed, because it has waited ten thousand years.
  3. Cover and rest it one hour, under the cloth the family keeps for it, which is older than the cloth.
  4. Roll each loaf thin as the Green Line — one stroke, no thicker, the line that was drawn in 1949 and that the schedule has run to ever since. Score three thin lines in the face of each loaf, for the three faiths, and the scoring is the family’s whole argument on the subject.
  5. Brush with oil and press the sesame into the face, then the cumin and black seed in the last third, because they came last, and the bread is honest about order.
  6. Bake against the back of the kiln until the face blisters and the room smells of June. Do not open the door in the second minute of the baking, which is the minute of 70, the year of the fire; the bread knows that minute better than anyone at the table, and it will be offended by the look.
  7. Turn the loaves onto the long table, oil the face again while they are hot, and serve. The last loaf goes on the oven sheet, cut-side down, where it keeps the tin company, and the tin keeps the disc, and the disc keeps the card, and the card is this page, and the page is kept where the yard keeps things — under the clock.

Notes for the keeper of the tin

The card as you are reading it was written by Salma Nasr in 1951 on the back of the label of a 78 rpm disc pressed that same year — a Levantine orchestra, side A the orchestra, side B the long table — because the kitchen was out of paper and the label was the size of a card and had both sides. In 1953 the family brought the disc to the yard at Gaza City, Track 7, where the Green Line of the 1949 Armistice Agreements holds the whole schedule, and asked that it be kept; the yard keeps everything it is given, and it mounted the disc on the signal room wall, where it has run ever since at the speed of whatever record it was. In 1968 the disc cracked along the label seam, the way 78 rpm does, and the yard’s keeper entered one line with the dispatch, and the line is kept word for word:

“The remainder of the original disc carries, under the clock.”

— keeper’s line, signal room, Gaza City Yard, 1968; entered with the dispatch and never taken down

That is to say: what remains of the record — the label side, which is the side with the card on it — is still on the wall, under the yard clock, and it carries. The clock has been right since 1949, the way the yard is right since 1949, and the board that hangs above it — the departure board with the dispatch kept on it and the yard’s whole careful ledger of the Green Line, the Rafah gate that departs on request, and the sea that is always on time — is the yard’s own record, kept at Gaza Strip — The Green Line Limited, Timetable & Commentary, and this card travels with it. For the sister records of a quiet hour kept in the same careful fashion — a sleeve that carries one margin line word for word, a society that is an hour not a room, the mat unrolled at seven — see the liner notes at Ethel Lote — The Quiet Practice, LP D/2024, whose disc runs quiet all the way down, the way the groove on this one did. The card’s two sisters in the tin — the card no. 9 pie from the 30th of May, with its almanac sentence, and the Vedeno honey cake, card no. 11, with its jam jar — are kept at Wembley Sunday Pie — A Family Recipe, Card No. 9 and Vedeno Honey Cake — A Family Recipe; the three of them argue about the 15th of Nisan, the pie by clock, the cake by tradition, and the flatbread by the June zaatar, and the tin holds the argument without taking a side, which is what tins do. The register where the cards are entered is the front page at Webstuffs. If the tin is ever full, the flatbread is the one that stays: it is the oldest card in the tin, and the tin has been polite to the older cards for ten thousand years.
