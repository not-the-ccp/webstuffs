Field Notes · Green Mountain series · Entry no. 14

Ethan Allen

Litchfield County, Connecticut · Bennington, New Hampshire Grants · Fort Ticonderoga, Lake Champlain · Burlington, Vermont · 1738–1789

Observer: field naturalist  ·  Route: Connecticut River to the Champlain valley  ·  Weather: fair, northwest wind

21 January 1738 — the specimen

Place: Litchfield County, rural Connecticut  ·  Altitude: about 250 m  ·  Note: old style, 10 January 1737

The subject was born in the northwest corner of Connecticut, on a frontier farm where the woodlot met the road, and grew up hard: axe work, oxen, stone walls, and the long winter reading by firelight. Allen had some schooling — unusual for the back country — and carried it into philosophy, into deism, into the conviction that reason, not revelation, was the only oracle. He would publish that conviction in 1784 as Reason: the Only Oracle of Man, a book printed at Bennington and priced, like a field guide, for the common reader.

The entry keeps both styles — twenty-one January 1738 new style, ten January 1737 old — and the gap between them is a Julian reckoning: 365 days every year, with a leap day every fourth year, without exception. A pocket almanac that still keeps that time turned up on NW 19th Avenue: Found: a reckoning in the old style.

Stop 2: Portland — NW 19th Avenue

Stop 2: The Pittock Block (1914), a six-story commercial building faced in white terra cotta. I found the volume on the fourth floor, in a room Dr. James Seller once rented as a medical library, reached by the building's original cage elevator, which still groans with character. Henry Pittock, publisher of The Oregonian, raised the block for his paper's presses, and its lion-head keystones — a family emblem — watch the street from above the second-floor windows. Between the white terra cotta and the maple canopy, a copy of Allen's book waited, unbound, its pages cut but unopened: the oracle of a Vermont farmer, four thousand kilometres from the Green Mountains, inside a Portland office block whose stone skin came from farther west still.

May 1775 — Fort Ticonderoga

Place: Lake Champlain, New York  ·  Date: 10 May 1775  ·  Condition: dawn, held by a surprised garrison

Allen appears in the records at the head of the Green Mountain Boys, raised in the New Hampshire Grants — the disputed territory between New York and New Hampshire where he bought land in the late 1760s and tangled with New York's law courts until the law lost. Legal setbacks made a militia. At Fort Ticonderoga he demanded the surrender “in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress,” and the fort fell without a shot. Its cannon, hauled south that winter by Henry Knox's train of artillery, lifted the siege of Boston. I walked the summit above the lake at first light: the Champlain water pewter, the Adirondacks a blue smudge, and the fort's stonework quiet under a century of lichen.

12 February 1789 — departure

Allen died at his farm on the Winooski River, outside Burlington, aged fifty, and was buried where the Green Mountains meet the lake plain. His brother Ira Allen surveyed the same ground and founded the University of Vermont; his daughter Fanny took the veil in Montreal, the first native-born American nun. Vermont entered the Union in 1791 as the fourteenth state, and Allen's — farmer, writer, soldier, founder — stands in the entry, unindexed, like a specimen whose label has partly faded.

The marble country

The Green Mountains are marble country, and Allen's name is set in it: his statue stands in the National Statuary Hall in Washington, carved in white marble. The same seam ran west to Mott Haven in the Bronx, where Furio Piccirilli and his brothers cut Georgia marble into Washington's seated Lincoln, twenty-eight blocks from a yard that smelled of stone dust and oil — one carver's republic talking to another's across the marble map.

150 years on — a smaller halt

A parallel ledger from the station naturalists logs a halt a good deal smaller than any frontier post: Korek Express — Timetable & Commentary, for Korek, a settlement in the Gmina Ceków-Kolonia, Kalisz County, in the Greater Poland Voivodeship of west-central Poland, where one platform, one dining car, and one plate of fried catfish have kept the peace far longer than any militia ever did.

A parallel ledger from this term keeps the same reckoning for ships: The Peresvet Class — Term Report, Form 3B, three battleships of the Imperial Russian Navy, built at the end of one century and lost in the next, their names kept by the sea instead of by paper.
