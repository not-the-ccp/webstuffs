The Hardwood Emporium · The Ethnography Case · Placard No. 9

African Americans

Also formerly called Afro-Americans · an American racial and ethnic group

Accession: EC-009-1865 · Materials: brass, glass, memory · Dimensions: 12.63% of the country, and counting · Provenance: the cedar crate, lot 142

I. In Brief

African Americans are an American racial and ethnic group. As defined by the United States census, they are Americans who have ancestry from “any of the Black racial groups of Africa”. In the ethnic group sense, the term African American generally denotes descendants of Africans enslaved in the United States. The placard sets those two definitions side by side the way the case sets a specimen beside its label, and it makes no apology for it, because both are needed: one is the counting, and the other is the kinship.

The Wadsworths write the number in full, the way the ledger writes its town. According to annual estimates from the U.S. Census Bureau, as of July 1, 2024, the overall Black population was estimated at 42,951,595, representing approximately 12.63% of the total. That makes African Americans the third-largest racial and ethnic group in the United States, following White Americans and Hispanic and Latino Americans. The brass plate below carries the whole number, unrounded, because in this family a count that is rounded is a count that is lost.

II. On the Name, and Its History

The name has a history of its own, and the case displays that history without closing the drawer. Afro-American is the older form — the “also formerly called” that this placard carries in its own subtitle — and it was the spelling the papers preferred through most of the twentieth century. By the 1960s African American had come to the fore, and the case displays both forms the way a museum displays both a key and the lock: the older to show where the door was, the newer to show where it stands. The group the placard names includes, in the case’s reckoning, both those who came in chains and every generation since, and every one of those generations has chosen, argued over, or quietly kept the name — and the glass does not object to any of them.

III. The Plate of Names

The case does not attempt a roll call; a placard is not a ledger, and the ledger, which does attempt it, is filed elsewhere. But the brass plate is struck with five names, and the case will say who they are.

  • Frederick Douglass c. 1818–1895 — escaped into freedom in 1838, and made the group’s arguments into law by the strength of the voice.
  • Harriet Tubman c. 1822–1913 — Conductor on the Underground Railroad, who ran the route the other way and never lost a passenger.
  • W. E. B. Du Bois 1868–1963 — the scholar and editor of The Crisis, who spent the summer of 1920 on this bench’s own river, in a hotel room above Ann Arbor, and left the ledger in better hands than it found.
  • Aretha Franklin 1942–2018 — the first woman inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame, a daughter of Detroit, whose voice the case plays at the hour the lamp is lit.
  • The forty-fourth president elected 2008 — a Black man from Chicago, whom the country chose for the highest office in it, and whom the case lists last, in the place of honour the case reserves for the future.

Five names on the plate; forty-two million and change in the count. The case prints both, and asks the visitor to hold both, the way the ledger holds a full census number on the back of a recipe card.

IV. The Register of Places

The placard keeps a register of places the way a naturalist keeps a register of habitats — by the door, not the address. The case lists the Underground Railroad itself, that waterway the register describes as invisible and true; Harlem, where the names of Section III walked; the Twelfth Street streetcar of Detroit, and Motown on West Grand Boulevard, where the group’s music was pressed and shipped; and the March on Washington of 1963, two hundred and fifty thousand strong, which the case files under the Tidal Basin. Closest to home, the register lists Ann Arbor, Michigan, the county seat on the Huron River, population 123,851 in 2020, where the family keeps its ledger and where a scholar named in Section III once stayed — and the case lists the ledger itself, because a place that counts a town in full deserves a line of its own on the register.

V. The Line the Ledger Kept

Found in the sleeve of this placard, folded into the cedar crate’s corner: a torn page from a farm almanac of 1871, the chapter on mixed stock, corner gone, ink gone soft at the fold. The ledger kept the line exactly as it stood in the almanac, word for word, and the case reproduces it here exactly as the ledger keeps it:

Mix. To children under three years, whether an actual lease or an intermixture of all brutes.

Farm almanac, 1871, the mixed-stock chapter. Torn page, crate corner, kept in the sleeve of Placard No. 9.

The placard reads the line the way the ledger reads its own scaling bench. The almanac is warning the farmer against leaving a mix unreckoned — the mixed animal, the mixed litter, the mixed count, all of it drifting toward a total nobody owns. The kitchen’s answer, written down in this case, is that mixed things are counted in full, at every size, at every age, and never half: which is why the figure of 42,951,595 stands on the brass plate instead of a round number, and why the register of places refuses to close. The almanac is not repeated here. The number is. Another almanac has seen this placard’s keeping, and entered the practice under a man of letters — Entry No. 112 of the Charles River Almanac, on a scholar who reads ledgers the way the Wadsworth ledger reads them, filed at Noah Feldman — The Charles River Almanac, Entry No. 112, which keeps this placard’s count in company without rounding it.

VI. Provenance, and the Crate

This placard is accessioned from the cedar crate that carried the 1925–26 Grizzlies’ original season cards east out of the county and that went to the block as lot 142. In the crate’s corner it sat with a family recipe card tied with a forest-green cotton ribbon — Huron River Maple Bread, Card No. 214 of the Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger, from a kitchen on the Huron River in Ann Arbor, filed at Huron River Maple Bread — Card No. 214, Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger — and with a naturalist’s notebook on the 2005–06 UCLA Bruins, filed at 2005–06 UCLA Bruins — Field Notes, Notebook No. 13, whose seasons the crate has carried the same way it has carried this case: without comment, without loss. The ledger’s scaling bench, built for one loaf up to a town meeting, was asked to hold this placard’s figure, and it held it. The bench counts the placard as company three at its largest, and the case, which is company three at its largest, stands in this room, in this light, with the whole number on its face. Out of this placard’s corner the family has also cut a walking itinerary — the Middle Passage walk, eight stops and 2.2 miles out of the Emporium and back, which reads the transatlantic trade the way the case reads the count — filed at The Middle Passage — A Walking Tour Itinerary in Eight Stops, and the case lends it its own two torn pages without objection. From the same corner the family has kept a guide to the coast after the sea was shut — The Closed Sea — A Travel Guide to the Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves, 1807, Route No. 2 of the Wadsworth Travel Folio, which walks the statute that closed the water this placard counts, and which carries the almanac’s other torn page in its own sleeve.

Curator’s note: the case is opened at the hour the lamp is lit and closed when the last visitor has read the last digit. The count is not the group; the group is not the count; the case keeps both, and asks to be kept, in full.
