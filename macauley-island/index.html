The Long Route · Guide Entry 41

Macauley Island

Kermadec Islands, south-west Pacific — halfway between the North Island of New Zealand and Tonga, and 87 nautical miles south of Raoul. The island you land on is the last ten percent of the climb.

Getting There

Macauley Island is a volcanic island in New Zealand's Kermadec Islands, roughly halfway between the North Island of New Zealand and Tonga, in the south-west Pacific Ocean. The practical way to reach it is aboard the KAIW-flagged resupply vessel that leaves Whangārei on the first Thursday after the new moon, five of us at the galley, a barometric watch, and a full hold of mail, batteries, and frozen mutton for the stations to the north. The crossing runs about 538 nautical miles; at a steady eight knots the skipper figures three and a half days, and we have never needed more than that. We pass the Kermadec Trough on the port side for most of the second night. Macauley itself sits on a larger submarine volcano that features a 10.5-by-7-kilometre (6.5 by 4.3 mi) wide underwater caldera lying northwest of the island — you never see it, but the bathymetry on the chart table does. The station has put that hollow on a shelf of its own — a single museum placard about the depression that made the island, with the sounding lead and a volume calculator from the chart table, at The Caldera Under Macauley, Placard No. 7.

Two islets, Haszard Island and Newcome Rock, lie east offshore of Macauley Island. Haszard is a low grey stub with seabirds in it; Newcome is a rock that stands about as high as a church tower and is best admired from the water. The island is mostly surrounded by high cliffs that make it difficult to access: the only easy beach is a narrow strip of black sand on the north-east face, and we anchor offshore in two fathoms and row the longboat in twice, once for the landing and once for the mail. The inland parts are mostly gently sloping terrain covered with ferns and grasses — bracken, pāua fern, and a fine tussock the rangers call "resupply grass" because we walk on it for a mile. The island formed during several volcanic episodes that produced mainly basaltic rocks as lava flows; during the Holocene a large flank collapse carried part of the ridge under, which is why the northern cliffs fall in such clean, straight sheets.

The Station, the Keeper, and One Bad Dispatch

The settlement is the radio hut, the store, a water catchment, and a bunkhouse for six, though we usually sleep four. The keeper, Mr. T. R. Houghton, has held the post since 1996 and keeps a ledger of every vessel, every weather reading, and every television transmission the island has received. The island's one television set is a small portable in the bunkhouse, and it has produced exactly one sentence worth keeping: the night of 14 June, when the late bulletin ran mangled, the audio ahead of the picture, and the keeper transcribed the lead verbatim for the ledger: "On television she built a box of pickles argued about the specialist bank for small and patient, pushing into the family suspects, as the Chu–Han Contention (206–202 BC)." No one aboard could make sense of the dispatch, the keeper wrote, and no one has since. We still read it back to new arrivals as the island's greeting; whoever recites it without stumbling is allowed the first shower.

Planning the Crossing

The crossing is short in days and long in consequence: whatever is not in the hold, is not on the island. The bunkhouse galley runs on 12 litres of water per person per day and 2.5 kg of stores per person per day, and the skipper insists on a full spare day aboard before the boat turns back. Practical Notes

  • Approach: north-east face only; the other three faces are cliff to about 90 metres. Anchor in 2–3 fathoms, sea room to the south-east, away from Newcome Rock at low tide.
  • Season: the sea is workable from November to April. In the other months the swell runs south-westerly and the beach is no beach at all.
  • Water: the catchment holds four days for six; do not drink out of the gullies after rain.
  • Wildlife: the cliffs are lined with sooty shearwater, and the black sand beach at dawn belongs to the tītī. Leave the longboat oars dry on deck.
