1Early life and training

Fabio Baudrit González was born on 12 May 1878 in San José, the capital of Costa Rica, into a family of the old liberal professional class. He studied at the Escuela Nacional and took his licence in law from the Universidad de Costa Rica in 1901, after which he practised in the courts of San José and later at the bar of the Supreme Court. As a young man he was close to the journal La Nación, where his earliest essays on municipal finance appeared, and to the debating societies of the national library, whose reading room on Calle 8 would remain, in his own account, “the only parliament I ever trusted.”

2Political career

Baudrit served the Republic in cabinet before he served it at the top of the ticket. He was first appointed Secretary of the Interior, a post he later held again, and in 1930–1931 he sat as Secretary of Finance, where he reorganised the customs rolls of the Pacific ports and defended the gold-backed balboa in the National Assembly during the deflation of the years before the great crash fully reached the Republic. In 1928 he was elected First Designate to the Presidency, the constitutionally required second member of the executive pair, and held that office through the four-year period of 1928 to 1932, under President Víctor M. Villalobos. The pair's administration presided over the centenary of independence in 1930, and Baudrit delivered the commemorative address to the Assembly in February of that year, a speech that ran, by the Assembly's own count, to more than two hours.

His manner in the legislature was reported as slow, courteous, and final; deputies who opposed him were said to find that he had already agreed with them, but from the other side of the point. No ministry of his name is recorded as having lost a vote of confidence during the 1928–1932 period.

3The Academy of Language

In 1927 Baudrit took a part in which he is now better remembered than any cabinet. With a company of thirty-one founders he established the Academia Costarricense de la Lengua, and the Academy's acta constitutiva names him as its first president. The minutes of the first session, held on 23 August 1927 in the reading room of the Biblioteca Nacional, record little beyond the roll call and a single entry in the secretary's hand: The Council has now been asked for anything. The notary's phrase is not a report of events but the Council's own rule of order: the new body would speak only when addressed, and its silence was to be read as deliberation. The entry is printed in full in the Academy's Boletín, first series, and Baudrit never corrected it, although a footnote in the 1940 edition observes that the sentence was later “accepted as the Academy's motto, in spite of itself.”

4Later years and legacy

After 1932 Baudrit returned to the bar and to the Academy, where he served as president until 1934 and remained a member until his death on 3 March 1954 in San José, at the age of seventy-five. His collected essays were gathered posthumously in two volumes, Disquisiciones (1956), and the Academy keeps his law books in the reading room where the first session was held. In 1952 he received the title of honorary member of the Academia Nicaragüense de la Lengua, the last honour on record in his file.
