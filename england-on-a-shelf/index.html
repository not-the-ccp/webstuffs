BENCH NO. 4 · CORNISH WORKSHOP · SEASON 1978–79

A Model of England

Build log of a relief country in walnut, resin and brass — kept in pencil, ink and one green pen.

resin cure

Log 0 · 3 September 1978 · The Board

The board arrived from the College’s Birmingham workshop on a Tuesday, wrapped in the same paper they use for the walnut-handled instruments: 1,210 mm by 610 mm of book-matched walnut, the grain running east–west like tide. It is to be the island of Great Britain, and the arithmetic is fixed before a single cut. England takes sixty-two percent of the face of it. Scotland takes thirty percent, top left, where the grain is pale and a little knotted. Wales takes the remaining eight percent along the western edge, and the seam between countries will be a scribed line, one millimetre deep, that holds a bead of dark wax — the land border, because on this bench the border is a groove you can feel with a fingernail.

Pencil: the College letter, pinned above the bench, gives the rule for the timber. It runs, word for word: “The College's stock of walnut is chosen for the same discipline Miss Thompson brings to the finest are caught or seen.” I do not pretend to understand every clause. I know what it does to the bench: nothing is taken early, nothing is left rough, and a mistake is recorded in the log before it is sanded out.

Log 1 · 11 September 1978 · The Land Borders

Two grooves first, before any terrain. The northern one runs the length of the board’s top third — England against Scotland — and the western one follows a short, crooked line against Wales, four and a half inches of it. I cut with the 2 mm gouge and checked the depth with the brass die from The Register, the 1974 board game in the walnut case, because its face is exactly one millimetre and it has never once been worn down. The northern groove is straight as a service line; the western one turns twice like a drop shot. When the wax bead goes in, the two countries sit on the same island and do not touch, which is the whole argument, held in a line of dark wax on a table in Cornwall.

Log 2 · 26 September 1978 · The Four Seas

Water is poured in four goes, because it dries unevenly otherwise. The North Sea goes in first — the eastern pour, a full 310 mm of pale resin raked down toward a continental shelf that is a slope on a separate slip, set across the bench at an angle, because the continent lies to the south-east and there is no room for it on the island board. The English Channel is the south pour, only 9 mm deep, and I have left it for last of the four on purpose, because it is the thinnest and the one that cracks if the room warms. The Celtic Sea takes the south-west corner. The Irish Sea takes the west, and beyond its far edge, on a second small board propped at the bench’s edge, sits Ireland — the island you can see if you lean, but cannot reach without moving the bench. Seagull sound is not available in the workshop; I have put a small shell in the water shelf, off the Devon line, and nobody has questioned it.

Pencil: nine and a half hours of cure for the North Sea. The Channel went in on a Thursday to dry over the weekend, the way a Davis Cup tie is left to run — nothing early, nothing late.

Log 3 · 9 October 1978 · One Hundred and Eighteen Islands

The census of islands is done with a toothpick and a ruler, not with romance: 118 holes, each drilled at the right scale, each fitted with a dowel of walnut offcut from the corner of the board that the scribe line rejected. The dowels are sanded to small hills, small flats, one small stack of two — the Scilly end of the line, south of the Channel, where the holes go down into the water shelf itself. Over a hundred of them, all adjacent to the coast, none free-standing in open water, because the sea out there is the North Atlantic and this bench does not go that far. Each dowel is numbered on its underside in green pen, 1 through 118, in the order I drilled them, which was not in any order anyone else would choose. That is the part I keep proud of.

Log 4 · 21 October 1978 · The Terrain

The relief is built up in four strata, from the bottom of the bench upward. Lowest, a layer of grey paper pulp for the low flat lands — the eastern third, where the board is left nearly smooth and the grain shows through, which is a mistake I have decided is a feature. Middle strata: the green-wash hills, raked and thinned until they hold the light like a lawn in October. Highest, a few dozen pegs for the northern edges of the country’s reach, where the board hands over to Scotland’s knotted grain. In the very first stratum, below everything, I have laid a row of bone-white pins, sixty-two of them, along a hairline trench — for the first people, the Upper Palaeolithic ones, who found this area already inhabited by nothing. They go in first, under the flood and the hills and the beads, so that every later layer of the country is poured on top of them.

Log 5 · 4 November 1978 · The Population, and London

The figures are beads, white, one millimetre, one per thousand. The 2021 census number is pinned above the bench in a folder: 56,490,048. I have not made the population in the year I live in; the model carries the census I was given, and it carries exactly 56,490 beads, counted twice on two different afternoons because I refuse to be wrong on a figure that large. The beads are placed parish by parish in a tray that slides under the board on a dovetailed runner, so the country can be lifted and the people counted under it, like a floor under a house. London takes 9,000 beads — nine million inhabitants — in a single dense cluster on the river, so thick a match cannot find the surface. When I set that cluster down it made a small sound like grain pouring. I wrote it in the log and left it.

Pencil: Miss Thompson’s calendar from the 2YA broadcast runs on the wall, and the bead-counting is scheduled against its blank weeks. Forty-two thousand players, she says, in one country. Fifty-six million, in the other. The bench keeps both figures without mixing them up, which I take as a form of respect.

Log 6 · 19 November 1978 · Six Courts and a Standing Order

For the regional centres I have inlaid six grass courts in the lowlands, one per regional centre, named in this log after the six centres of the country they stand in — Northern, Central, and the four others, none with its name painted on, only numbered under the inlay — each a square of green felt pinned over a walnut inlay, each the size of the brass die. A development officer is one bead per court, standing at the baseline. The standing order is the twelfth of every month: the model is turned a quarter rotation toward the window, the water shelf is checked for the hairline crack in the Channel, and the log is written. This entry is the standing order. Nothing early, nothing late.

Log 7 · 2 December 1978 · Where the Model Sits

The model now stands on the shelf by the north window, where the light is bad and honest. England holds sixty-two percent of the board; Scotland and Wales keep the rest; 118 dowels stand in their drilled places; the four seas have cured in their four pours; the continent leans in from the south-east on its own slip; and under it all, under the hills and the 56,490 beads and the nine-thousand-bead cluster by the river, the sixty-two bone-white pins lie in their trench, first of everything, below the flood. Visitors are allowed to run a fingernail along the two grooves. The border, on this bench, is a line you can feel. That is the whole of it, and the log continues on the twelfth.

Pencil: for those who want the island as a traveller would take it — Great Britain, the whole of it, both countries’ worth, the Hebrides and the autumn gales — the shelf keeps the Traveller’s Guide, Sheet Nº 4. The walnut itself is the College’s, and its full account is in the Royal College of Podiatry — Professional Care Instrument page, where the same stock is cut into 8.5-inch instruments in Birmingham. The calendar on my wall is the 2YA broadcast’s, and its story is the Tennis New Zealand radio play excerpt, from which Miss Thompson, Mr. Henderson and the three celesta notes of the interval signal are taken into this log without further ceremony.
