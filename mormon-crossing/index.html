Palmyra, N.Y. → Kirtland, Ohio → Independence, Missouri → Nauvoo, Ill. → Winter Quarters → the Great Salt Lake · 1820–1847

Sheet 1 · Tuesday, the 3rd · 06:15, before the coffee had an opinion

The plan on the bench

The subject came to me the way these subjects do: by mail, from Meridian & Vale in Salt Lake City, Room 3, the Vault, in the wake of their Catalogue No. 112, the Pacific & Deserts Sale. I want the layout to be about the Mormons, and I want the bench to say why. So this is the account in short: the Mormons are a religious and cultural group related to Mormonism, the theology and religious tradition of the Latter Day Saint movement, started by Joseph Smith — born December 23, 1805, in Sharon, Vermont — in upstate New York, in the thick of the Second Great Awakening, that long period of the early nineteenth century when the region kept converting in waves. After Smith’s death in 1844 the movement split into several groups following different leaders; the majority followed Brigham Young, while smaller groups followed Sidney Rigdon and James Strang, and many who did not follow Young eventually merged into what is now the Community of Christ, led by Smith’s son, Joseph Smith III. The word Mormon typically refers to the members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, the largest branch — the one that followed Young west. That is the whole story on two fingers, and it is a story that wants a bench.

The layout is a strip, 320 centimetres long, running the road miles of about 2,100 from the western shore of Lake Erie country out to the lake — roughly two-thirds of a mile per millimetre, which is not to scale with anything and is honest about it. Seven stops. Seven sheets of this log to follow. The base is 3mm basswood and it still smells of the cut, which is a problem I do not fully understand and intend to ignore. The same mail brought a box at a different scale I am not yet touching: a 1:72 campaign set of the Roman–Parthian war of the Armenian succession, 58–63 — Corbulo’s pontoon bridge across the Euphrates, the siege of Artaxata, one grey-card storm — which is listed on its own product page alongside my own layout.

Sheet 2 · Saturday, the 7th · the first three stops set

Upstate New York, and the temple that started as a promise

Stops one and two. Palmyra, the town in upstate New York where the Smith household came to live and where the Second Great Awakening was still loud enough to hear from a farm kitchen; I gave it a steeple with the weathering sanded off — new wood, new fervour. Then the Harmony farm, where Joseph, then a boy, cut stone for a house that was never finished; I modelled the unfinished walls honestly, at 1:87, which is the only way to do an unfinished house in scale: leave the top course of balsa unglued. Kirtland is stop three, and it is the one I have re-glued four times. The movement moved there in 1831, and the first temple of the movement went up in the 1830s — a small, square, two-storey thing with a glass cupola, built in a frontier town that could not yet afford to look like it could afford it. The mob that broke down the doors in Clay County in 1833 I have left to the logbook and not the layout; some weathering is weathering.

Sheet 3 · Wednesday, the 11th · 21:40

Missouri, the white city, and the jail at Carthage

Stop four, Independence, Missouri: the place the movement first tried to call home, and then lost in 1838, when the state’s militia drove the saints across the river to Iowa in a month of frozen snows. I built the river wide — four millimetres of clear epoxy, which is wider than any river in this region has any right to be at this scale — because the driving out was wide. Then Nauvoo, Illinois, 1839: the white city on the Mississippi, twenty thousand people and the temple begun again, and by 1843 the city was the largest on the layout and the one I keep losing to reflections. What the layout cannot hold is June 27, 1844, Carthage jail, the mob, the two shots, Joseph Smith dead at thirty-eight — the date that the whole strip pivots on. I marked it on the base with nothing at all: a small square of undecorated wood between Nauvoo and the river. The emptiness is the modelling.

Sheet 4 · Monday, the 16th · the split, cut with a scalpel

Three leaders, three towns, one word

Here is the knife work the subject asked for. In 1844 the movement is one; by 1846 it is several. I laid the split as a fork in the track at the east end of the layout — the only fork, which is the whole point. The majority, and with them the name as it is still used, followed Brigham Young west to the mountains and the lake; that line goes on to stop seven. Sidney Rigdon, Smith’s old First Counselor, kept the church’s head in the old country and set up headquarters near Kirtland; his line ends at stop two, at the temple I re-glued four times. James Strang went smaller and stranger: a prophet on Beaver Island, out in Lake Michigan, which I built as an island the size of a grain of salt with a steeple on it that you can only see if you lie on the bench. And the rest — the ones who would not follow any of the three — I have not given a line at all, because their line took a generation: they drifted, and many of them eventually merged into the church of Joseph Smith III, Smith’s eldest son, now the Community of Christ. The largest branch, the one the word Mormon still names, is Young’s. The fork is cut, the three lines sanded, and the logbook carries the arithmetic of it.

Sheet 5 · Friday, the 20th · the long middle, in paper pulp

Winter Quarters, 1,477, and the lake

Between Nauvoo and the lake the road passes a stop I did not expect to feel: Winter Quarters, 1846–47, the muddy village on the Missouri where the company overwintered and got its shape — tents first, then the first sod houses, the first wagons with real axles. I gave it twelve tents and no weathering, because the weather had already done its part. The first pioneer company of 1847 took the road out of there: 1,477 people in 570 covered wagons, following the Platte and the streams and the maps that were mostly wrong, and into the valley on July 24, 1847. The final stop on the strip is the Great Salt Lake, and I made the water the right colour, which is to say a colour I keep having to repaint. At the west end of the lake I set the first small meetinghouse, the steeple left sanded, like Palmyra — the layout begins and ends the same way, which is either a mistake or the whole design. The wagons themselves are the work I am proudest of: fifty-seven of them at 1:87, each one with a separate axle, because 1,477 people do not share an axle. The bench strip that runs the whole company down the layout is below, if your browser will run it; the stops in order are Palmyra 1820, Kirtland 1831, Independence 1838, Nauvoo 1839, Winter Quarters 1846, the plains 1847, and the Great Salt Lake, July 24, 1847.

Sheet 6 · open · the letter

The drawer, and what the drawer held

After the sale I wrote to the firm at Meridian & Vale about the scrapbook fragment, Lot 47.5 — the one line in the Petersen Papers that the family could not explain, the one that sits fast. I asked them who had written it, and they asked the family, and the family wrote back. The firm’s cover letter of the 20th of May is on the bench, and their one line of report on the family’s answer is this, and I quote it because it is the truest sentence that has come through this site’s post this year: “You have asked nine times, as the three who correct me do. She will answer, and I have asked the treasurer to open the drawer.”

It arrived the day after, so I know the drawer opened. The catalogue says the archive formed over thirty years in office drawers, one departmental move, and a bookshelf the family calls the long argument — and it was one of those drawers, the family’s ledger drawer, that came open. In it: one tin, in which were three stivers and a folded slip that read, in a hand I will not name, M. R. — settled in full. I know what that means, and the only person on this shelf who does is the member from Premià de Mar whose 11 September diary records the blackbird at the market stall, the merla, who pays in stivers and who, on the day she left, owed the stall keeper exactly three stivers — the member who has already asked the treasurer to open the drawer and been told do not. She has been told to do it, and the drawer did it, and the debt is settled in full in a ledger drawer in Cedar City, which is either a coincidence I intend to ignore or the whole design. In the same drawer, under the tin: one brass meetinghouse, the size of a thumbnail, the steeple sanded, the patina worn to the colour of the lake I keep repainting. It fits on the strip at stop seven, next to the balsa meetinghouse, and it is the one piece on the layout I did not make. The log will be closed when the wagons reach the lake. They have reached it once, in the strip below, on a Friday in the rain, with all fifty-seven axles turning. The drawer is open. The debt is settled. The steeple keeps.
