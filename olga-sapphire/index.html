The Kyoto Season

A game of classical ballet in eleven cards, one variety floor, and twenty-one seasons

For 1–4 players, ages 14 and up · 60 minutes per season · No previous grace required

1 · The Box and the Board

The box holds the board — a single variety floor printed in black line on cream card, its edges marked Nihon Gekijō, Ginza, Tokyo — eleven Season Cards, twenty-eight Class Cards, four dancer tokens in ivory, rose, slate and gold, a small brass metronome that actually ticks, and the teaching ledger, a forty-page booklet with genuine ruled pages for entering your students' names. The board is not a stage in the old sense. It is a floor of about one hundred and forty square metres under a chandelier that has never once been turned off, and the whole of the game is played standing.

The player who takes the gold token takes the part the game is named for: the company's principal, a Russian-born ballerina and choreographer who arrived from Moscow in 1936 with a case of theoretical and pedagogical materials, a passport, and a habit of teaching the barre at eight in the morning exactly as she had been taught to, in Leningrad and Moscow, long before she was twenty-six. Everyone else plays the corps: the students, the second principals, the variety singers who keep getting the intermission wrong.

2 · Setting Up a Season

Begin every season on the card dated 1 May. The principal places the metronome at the downstage-left corner, at 66 — the tempo the manual calls house tempo, the speed of a floor settling after the matinee. Each player chooses a home corner of the floor. The ledger is opened to a fresh page, and each player writes in the name of the student they will coach this season; names are bound for the life of the ledger and may not be re-entered in any later season of the same box.

The eleven Season Cards are dealt one face up, in order: May — First Public Class, June — The Diplomat's Dinner, July — Fire Drill, Full Company, August — The Kyoto Season (the card the game takes its name from, played face down until it comes up), September — New Footwear, October — The Variety Review, November — The Critic from Osaka, December — Twelfth Night, No Ballet, January — The Barre at Eight, February — One Student Left, and March — The Long Rehearsal. Two players may play the whole arc in one sitting; three or four should stop at the end of each year and close the box.

3 · A Turn: Count, Turn, Teach

A turn has three parts, and every part is counted out loud in eighths, the way the principal counted in the class room, because the game's whole logic is that ballet is arithmetic performed with the feet.

Count. Draw a Class Card and read the exercise aloud: five port de bras, six relevés, eight plié, or some product of that kind. You perform it by moving your token that many squares across the floor, one square per count, and the metronome decides whether you are on tempo. Arriving on the last count in the same corner you left is worth one credit; arriving elsewhere is worth nothing and costs the square you stand on. This is the game's repeated-addition rule: a turn of eight plié is eight single steps, taken honestly, and a player who tries to jump the product simply returns to the corner and starts the count again.

Turn. On any card whose name contains Turn or Revue, the principal may declare a pirouette: four consecutive counts in which the token travels one square on each count, turning a quarter of a circle, and ending in a corner. A completed pirouette is worth three credits and it is the only move in the game that may not be contested. The rules are explicit on this: the floor does not argue.

Teach. Once per season, each player writes their student's name into the ledger beside the exercise that student has now passed, with the date and the count. A student who appears on three separate pages of the ledger is released: their card is returned to the box, and the player who released them takes the student's token into the corner of the floor as a standing credit, worth one credit per season for as long as the game is played. Students released by the principal's own teaching are entered in the principal's hand, and the ledger keeps them under a separate ruled heading: First Class, Ginza.

4 · The Principal: 1936 to 1957

The gold token has one card the other tokens do not get, and it is played only once, in any game, in whatever season the player holding it chooses: The Materials. On this card the principal sets the theoretical and pedagogical materials down on the floor, and from then on every student released in the game enters the ledger automatically, without the three-page rule. The card's front bears, in small type, the whole of the principal's biography, and the game expects the player to be able to read it aloud at the table, once, at the start of the season in which it is played: born 28 June 1907; classically trained at the Leningrad State Choreographic Institute and again at the Moscow Choreographic School; performed in Russia until her marriage in the early 1930s to a Japanese diplomat, Takehisa Shimizu, by whom her name in the ledger runs as Midori Shimizu; agreed in 1936 to move to Japan, carrying the classical discipline with her and establishing the field by which her students would later be judged; employed by the Nihon Gekijō variety theater in Tokyo from 1936 until her retirement in 1957; the name Sapphire retained as the one the theater knew her by; dead 20 June 1981.

After 1957 the principal retires with the season in which the game's year count reaches it, and the gold token passes to the player with the most credits. The game is not over. A retired principal still keeps the floor: she may declare one pirouette per season on any player's turn, and every corner she occupies becomes a standing credit for the player holding the token who is standing in it.

5 · The One Card Kept as Found

The box also contains a single blank-faced card, Card 12, which has no name, no exercise, and no rule attached to it. It is printed on the reverse with one line salvaged from an early proof of the teaching ledger, and the rules are clear that it is to be read aloud once when it is drawn and then filed face down where it came from, unexplained. The line reads, word for word, exactly as it was found in the proof:

The author was born in Epworth, Lincolnshire, the twelve of the Invention The present application is cross-shelved with the harbour once was, and the record.

No player scores from Card 12. The metronome keeps 66 while it is read. It is the only card in the game that the manual declines to translate, and it is the reason the ledger in the box has a blank page — page thirty-nine — where its line was meant to be entered.

6 · Winning

A season is won by credits, but the game is won by the ledger. When the box is closed for the last time — whenever that is — the players open the teaching ledger and count the students entered under each hand. The player whose students fill the most pages wins the game, takes the box, and keeps the names. Credits decide seasons. The ledger decides the rest.

If two players' ledgers are even, the tie is broken by the oldest date of release entered in either, and the older date wins: the floor keeps its order. The metronome is left at 66. The chandelier is not, of course, turned off.

7 · Errata and Shelf Notes

Second edition, printed at the back of the theater: the first edition's August — The Kyoto Season card is to be kept face down until drawn, not face up, as misprinted; the word relevés is spelled with one s throughout, on purpose; and the brass metronome, if it loses a tooth, is to be re-timed by the nearest watchmaker and not replaced. For players who want to keep their products in arithmetic rather than on a floor, the Thread 214 of The Aden County General settles the case of multiplication as repeated addition at the bench, with an 84-slip tray and a 109 km leg, which is the same counting this game does with the feet. For a walk among the houses whose rooms the game borrows the quiet from — fir floors, tower windows, a physician's cabinets still labeled in their owner's hand — the Bates–Seller House Walking Tour keeps the itinerary in five stops, and the full index keeps everything else in its place.
