Two Nights Out of Time

A diary kept in November 1993, the week the television sent the thirty years back into a single street and then, on 27th, gave them all back to me.

● REC  19:30:00 · SP · TRACK 02

Friday 19 November 1993 · rain, a cold square, one mustard plant

The announcement came at the kitchen table while the kettle was still deciding. A special for Children in Need, two parts, 26th and 27th of this month, and it is Dimensions in Time: Doctor Who and EastEnders in the same hour, made to mark the thirtieth anniversary of the first broadcast, and — this is the part I wrote down three times so I would believe it — with every surviving actor who has ever played the Doctor. Sylvester McCoy, Colin Baker, Peter Davison, Tom Baker, Jon Pertwee, all of them at once, plus a scattering of companions I will not try to name before I see them and get them wrong. Doctor Who has been off the air since 1989; the anniversary special they were building in its place, the multi-Doctor picture called The Dark Dimension, began development and then quietly folded, and so the anniversary will be served to us warm instead of cold, inside a soap. Kate O'Mara is the villain. She is the Rani, the scientist from the 1986 serial who has spent three years between serials waiting for exactly this kind of weather. She traps the five of them in a time loop in Albert Square. I underlined traps and loop separately, like they were different animals.

Tuesday 26 November 1993 · part one, 5:15 to 6:00, the queue outside for the appeal

We had the set turned up past polite by the time the square appeared. The Rani's loop closed at exactly 5:15 in the afternoon of the day she set it, and it closed again, and again, each Doctor walking the same cobbles toward the same corner of the pub, each of them arriving with a different face and the same bad news. Five Doctors in one square and the soap's regulars — the bar, the steps, the flower stall — going about their day as if a century of science fiction had simply moved in and been asked to keep the volume down. What I keep thinking about is the choreography of it: five men of very different sizes and temperaments, one woman in a long coat holding all of them the way a knot holds a rope, and a street that could not be more ordinary if it tried. The mustard plant on my own windowsill, charlock yellow, has been blooming for two weeks. The family in the valley keeps a whole walk for that flower — eight stops beside the Secchia, 4.6 km of it, from the porch lamp to the hill, The Mustard Walk — and I found myself, in the middle of a time loop, checking whether it was still in the pot. It was. The square was not. The plant, by the botanist's count, had already promised small fruits at the top of the stalk that a November weather would not keep, and the Concordance files the non-arrival under Fruit, Entry No. 216, which calls it the dream's only true and terrible fact: in the loop the fruit does not come, and that is not disputed, only footnoted.

Wednesday 27 November 1993 · part two · the lake

Part two, and the loop opens the way a fist opens: slowly at first, then all at once, the seconds unspooling out of the square one by one, the Doctors walking backwards out of their own afternoons, and the Rani left with her instruments and her reasons and the long silence a laboratory makes when the experiment has ended and someone has to be the one to say so. The appeal got its number on the final button, the same night, and the phone box at the end of the street is, I am told, now a landmark, which is how I understand it has always been. I went for a walk afterwards, past the boathouse, to the small lake the script keeps referring to — the Rani's instruments are said, in the production notes I read at the counter, to have been calibrated on its water. There is a margin line in the notes, in someone's small, careful hand, and I have copied it here because I cannot reword it and I have stopped trying: The etiquette of the film inserts a curious companion to the lake, the lake. A companion, then — a companion of the character, a companion of the camera, or of the water. The lake is written twice, which may be the whole of it: once as the place, once as the thing that keeps the time. I stood there a long time. The lake does not loop. It is the one honest hour in the square, and I think that is why the script could not decide which of the two was which, and wrote both down, the way the diary writes both down, the way the loop wrote the 26th and the 27th and then let us keep the 27th. Whatever a machine does with memories — the letter in the paper this week about unlearning them, To the Editor, on machine unlearning, from 3400 N. Willamette, where a model was taught to forget a house on a brick hill and kept a 412-line receipt — whatever a machine does, a lake keeps everything the water was ever shown, and a diary keeps whatever the square did. That is the whole of the entry for tonight: the loop is open, the appeal is in, the mustard is in the pot, and the lake has the time.

P.S. — Jon Pertwee, in his afternoon, asked a regular where the loo was. The regular answered as if he were late for work. That is what a time loop feels like from the outside. Someone is always, somewhere, late for work.

Set in November 1993, kept since. Two parts, two evenings, five Doctors, one square, one lake. From this shelf: The Mustard Walk, the eight stops the flower gets when the square is empty, and the front page of Webstuffs, where the rest of the week is filed.
