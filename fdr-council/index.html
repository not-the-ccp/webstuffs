The Council of the Long Season

Convened where the river keeps its record · sitting in the record room, Massachusetts Hall, Cambridge

Minutes of Special Session No. 44

The subject of the session: Franklin Delano Roosevelt · January 30, 1882 – April 12, 1945

One record only · the seal is kept in the clerk’s hand until the vote

No. 44 The convening

The Council of the Long Season was convened in special session in the record room at Massachusetts Hall, Cambridge, on the first cold week of April, at a little after the hour the council keeps for a president’s last reading. Present: the Clerk of the Folio (chair), the Keeper of Slips, the Forecaster of the Charles, the Compiler of the Charles River Almanac who holds Card No. 112, and the Compiler of the Tripoli Coastal Weather Almanac who holds Card No. 63, who came by way of the ghibli and had the dust still on her folio. The Clerk read the quorum rule aloud — five names, four of them willing to be entered — and the session was declared open with the barometer at a slow fall, which the Forecaster noted is the pressure the council finds under most of its longest records.

The Clerk entered the subject for the record without ornament: Franklin Delano Roosevelt, born January 30, 1882, died April 12, 1945, at Warm Springs, Georgia, in the afternoon, of which more presently. He was the 32nd president of the United States, served from 1933 until his death in 1945, and is the longest-serving president in the country’s record and the only one to have served more than two terms. The Keeper of Slips entered the figure the council keeps for the length of the holding: 9,999 days at the desk, one day short of the round number, and no amendment.

No. 44 The four terms, entered as weather

The Forecaster moved that the session’s record be kept the way the almanacs keep theirs — by season — and that the president’s four terms be entered as four seasons of one long year. The motion carried without division. The first two terms, 1933 through 1940, were entered under the heading the Depression: the country coming out of the banks closed and the barometers broken, the fires on the porches, the letter from the desk read into the evening air, and the weather of a season in which a whole nation sat down at its own kitchen table and listened. The third and fourth terms, 1941 onward, were entered under the heading the War: the night of December 7, 1941, the second term abroad, the councils in the winter rooms across the water, the long nights, and the record of a season the Forecaster described, in the council’s grammar, as the worst year the instruments have ever asked to forget.

In the margin of the folio the Clerk entered the earlier weather of the subject’s own record, as the almanacs enter a station’s history: member of the Democratic Party; New York State Senate, 1911 to 1913, which the Forecaster noted was a single short season, the kind the record book rarely keeps; 44th governor of New York, 1929 to 1932, which the Keeper of Slips noted was the season the storm arrived and the subject stood at the window; and, before any of it, Harvard University, from which the subject graduated in 1903 — the same town, the same river, the same bank where Card No. 112 of the Charles River Almanac is kept, filed at Noah Feldman — The Charles River Almanac, Entry No. 112. The Compiler of Card 112, present, confirmed the river had not changed its bank in the interval, and the council recorded the fact without comment.

No. 44 The slip and the old rule

It was then the hour for the slip. The Keeper of Slips brought forward the paper the council keeps for the end of a holding — a slip, not a card, torn from the morning paper of a Georgia afternoon, the corner gone soft at the fold, found in the front cover of the record book the way other slips have always been found, and not dared removed. Before the reading, the Clerk recited the old rule the council has kept since the first session, word for word, as it stands in the first folio:

Standard of the slip to the extent of surface.

That is the rule, and the rule is the whole of the rule: a slip is entered only to the extent of its surface — no more of the day than the paper holds, no more of the weather than the ink shows, no amendment after the surface is read. The Keeper of Slips read the slip, from the top of the paper to the bottom, without a pause, and the minutes keep the reading exactly as it was made: at 1:35 in the afternoon, at Warm Springs, the holding ended. The room did not speak for the length of one barometer’s breath, and the Clerk entered that stillness under the heading no note required.

No. 44 The minaret clause

The Compiler of Card No. 63 moved, and the council took it up, that this record be compared with the minaret of the Murad Agha Mosque at Tajura, which fell in 1901 and was rebuilt in its own place in the twentieth century, the Murad Agha Mosque — The Tripoli Coastal Weather Almanac, Entry No. 63 keeping the account. The clause of the motion ran: that a record which outlives its holding is not lost but rebuilt, that the thing that fell is entered once and the thing that stands is entered once, and that the two entries share a place without sharing a date. The motion carried four to zero. The Forecaster added, for the margin, that a minaret is one long season and a president is four, and that the council has on this occasion recorded four where the record book had always entered one, and that this is not an error of the instrument.

No. 44 The vote and the closing

On motion duly made and seconded, the Council of the Long Season resolved that the record of Franklin Delano Roosevelt, 32nd president, four terms, 9,999 days, be entered in the last folio in full, be sealed by the clerk’s hand, be read aloud once at the winter solstice, and not afterwards; and that the slip of the Georgia afternoon be kept under the rule standing in the first folio, to the extent of its surface, and no further. The vote was taken by name. Cary, aye; Whitfield, aye; the Compiler of Card 112, aye; the Compiler of Card 63, aye. Four to zero, the seal pressed, the minutes closed.

So entered in the folio, and sealed at the hour above, in the record room, Massachusetts Hall, Cambridge, with the Charles holding its ice one season later than it should, and the ghibli, forty degrees away, keeping its own account.
Signed, E. R. Whitfield, Clerk of the Folio

No. 44 Margin notes, kept word for word

i. The barometer read 29.91 falling at the hour of the vote; the Forecaster has entered it beside the reading of December, which is the month the Charles River Almanac reads the record book aloud, once, at the solstice.

ii. The council notes that the subject’s family — the Delanos and the Roosevelts of New York, both old, both keeping their own ledgers — is the nearest form this record knows to a barometer with two dials, and keeps the note for that reason alone.

iii. The Keeper of Slips warns the next compiler: do not enter the slip under weather. It is paper. It is kept to the extent of its surface, and the surface is the limit of the record.

iv. For the practice of the almanacs this council compares its folios with — Card No. 112, the subject’s own station on the Charles, filed at Entry No. 112, and Card No. 63, the mosque whose minaret was rebuilt in place of the one that fell, filed at Entry No. 63 — the two compilers are thanked for comparing readings, and the seal is not asked to explain itself.

Council of the Long Season — Special Session No. 44, Franklin Delano Roosevelt, 1882–1945. One record only; corrections in pencil, and only by the clerk’s hand. This session is read aloud once at the solstice and not afterwards. The river keeps its own counsel.
