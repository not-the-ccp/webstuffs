The Normandy Weather Almanac

Entry No. 311 · September 1106 · station: Tinchebray, département de l’Orne, duchy of Normandy · recorded 28 September, by candlelight, from the almanac keeper’s table

►► 28 September 1106 · north-northwest wind 12 mph over the bramble · pressure falling from 1009 to 1001 hPa between the first horn and the last horse · no rain, no relief · one duke, one prisoner ·►►

Reading of the Day

The day began as the almanac had ruled it: high, thin, and moving. A north‑northwest wind at twelve miles an hour came down off the Perche hills and held all afternoon across the bramble and the corn at Tinchebray, the village that carries the other two spellings of its own name — Tinchebrai on one map, Tenchebrai on another — as though the day itself had been written down three different ways and no clerk could settle the record. The pressure stood at 1009 in the morning and was still falling when the last horse was unhorsed. The almanac marks it a clear sky; the chronicle of the same hours marks it an invading force under King Henry I of England, and against him the Norman army of his elder brother Robert Curthose, the Duke of Normandy.

By the rules of this shelf the two records are the same record, and both agree on the weather at the close: a decisive victory for Henry’s knights, the capture of Robert on the field, and a wind strong enough to carry the news to Alenéon and to the coast by midnight. Henry imprisoned his brother in England — first at Devizes Castle in Wiltshire, then moved south and west into the Welsh marches — and the almanac notes, for the benefit of later entries, that the weather over the tower of Cardiff Castle stayed dry and unfriendly all the way to Robert’s death there in 1134: twenty‑eight years of it, the longest running forecast on the shelf.

The Stove Log

A rule of the Normandy almanac, kept since the first volume: whatever the camp cook’s pot says, the stove log says it word for word, and the almanac does not parse it. The Normans’ camp ran on one great pot, and the pot’s own log for the 28th keeps a single line from the cook’s hand, in pencil, smudged with lard. It did not watch to see how a quiet man about the pot looks like it is not a disposal chute. The keeper of the shelf has read that line eleven times, as the cook’s other line has been read, and files it under unreadable, kept. Whatever it is, it is the weather of the camp that day, and the no‑eraser rule says it keeps its line.

Notes From the Shelf

For the rule that a reading, once ruled, keeps its line even when it has ruined a whole family of them, the keeper cross‑files this entry with Thread No. 54 on The Correspondents’ Board, where the fourth of the family, ruled out of the weather of the house, turned out to be the one who kept the ledger anyway — a brother’s ledger, in a way, and the same wind that took Curthose had been ruling that one for thirteen years before it. For the discipline of reading one card badly for twenty‑seven copies and keeping every one, the shelf points to Thread No. 896 on The Standing Ovation Board, where card K‑1754 has never once been read clean and the error handler is not sorry. Both boards agree with this entry on the method: the weather is not edited in public.

The barometer on the window at Alenéon stood at 1001 hPa at the hour the almanac was closed for the day. It is still falling. The next entry is filed at the front of the shelf, where the keeper’s notices hang.
