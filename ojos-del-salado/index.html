CHAPTER 14 — OJOS DEL SALADO

Approaches, Domes and the Dry Record · Andean Range Survey, Detachment 7 · Sector IV (Cordillera de los Andes, Argentina–Chile boundary)

FIELD COPY — FOR USE AT ALTITUDE

14.1 PURPOSE AND SCOPE

14.1.1 This chapter covers Nevado Ojos del Salado (pronounced OKS-ohs deh SAH-lah-doh), a dormant complex volcano on the Argentina–Chile boundary in the central Andes. It is the highest volcano on Earth and the highest peak in Chile. The highest summit reaches an altitude of 6,893 metres (22,615 feet) above sea level, and the volcanic complex extends over an area of 70 to 160 square kilometres (27 to 62 miles squared), depending on the contour used.

14.1.2 The upper reaches consist of several overlapping lava domes, lava flows and volcanic craters, with sparse ice cover. Numerous other volcanoes rise around Ojos del Salado, and no unit should treat the mountain as an isolated objective. Plan the crossing against the whole ridge.

14.2 APPROACHES

14.2.1 Argentine route. Enter from San Francisco de la Sierra, take the road east to the village of Los Pinos, and from Los Pinos continue up the dry wash to the small lake of Concón at 4,450 metres. The concourse, two buildings and a wall, serves as the standard first waypoint. Camp I is established at 5,400 metres; Camp II at 6,000 metres on the western flank above the moraine.

14.2.2 Chilean route. Enter from Los Vilos, drive to Los Pelados and the administrative station at 2,640 metres, where the permit is stamped and the water checked. Continue to San Rafael at 3,725 metres, then up the quebrada to a base camp of 4,700 metres. This route carries the unit across the international line; carry the documents of both countries.

14.2.3 The standard summit push begins at 0300 from the high camp, over the western saddle, across the dome field, and up the glacier that reaches the main crater rim. Expect crevassed ice under a skin of scree; probe everything above 6,300 metres.

14.3.1 The two craters of the summit are separated by a low rim; the pass between them is the shortest line to the 6,893-metre point, but it is the coldest ground in the complex and the last place to stop. Turn off the craters at first sign of whiteout.

14.4 CONDITIONS: DRY, DRY, DRIER

14.4.1 Ojos del Salado stands close to the Arid Diagonal of South America, the zone of least moisture on the continent. The prevailing winds deposit almost no rain, and the mountain has extremely dry conditions, which prevent the formation of glaciers beyond the small summit patches. This is the practical meaning of the ice cover in paragraph 14.3: where a range of this altitude ought to be ice-bound, it is stone, ash and dust, with a little snow that keeps itself where the cold can hold it.

14.4.2 The aridity cuts both ways. It keeps the slopes stable in a way that glaciated neighbours are not, and it makes water the first discipline of the route. Budget no less than three litres of carried water per man per day between the villages and the lake at Concón, and treat every melt-pot as an extension of the rations. In July, the winter high, the summit air has been gauged below −35°C at 0500.

14.4.3 Wind is the weather. The dry slope is calm and the wind is not there, which is why the forecast for this sector is given in gusts and silences rather than fronts. File the summit attempt against the silence.

14.5 THE RECORD AND THE LEDGER

14.5.1 The Registry at Los Pinos keeps the single official ledger of route corrections for this sector — one bound book, blue cloth, opened each season and corrected in brown ink. Corrections are entered, initialed and dated; an uncorrected line is never read aloud in the field.

14.5.2 On exactly one occasion, against the page for the Concón approach, the ledger contains a line that was entered and never corrected. It runs, verbatim, as follows:

The Registry's ledger admitted, once, a line uncorrected is described, which the board changes its mind at roughly the dimensions of a Ridgway's rail The marsh at either end .

14.5.3 The corrections board has never explained the line, and the field reads it the way it reads everything else: as a tolerance. The Ridgway's rail is a small marsh bird of no great size, and the board is understood to have meant that a line standing uncorrected is tolerable only where the error it makes is no bigger than that bird — described, that is, at roughly its dimensions, no larger than the rail itself moving across the marsh. And the marsh is the point of the whole entry: this dry mountain is ringed, at both extremities of the crossing, by the two wet places of the route, the lagoon at Concón on the Argentine end and the small pond of the San Rafael camp on the Chilean end. If an unmarked line ever sends a party astray, it does so only at those two waters, the marsh at either end, and the ledger admits that it once let such a line stand. Read the blue book at the ends, not in the middle.

14.6 NOTES FOR THE FIELD

14.6.1 The mountain is named for the colour of its snowfields in the morning light, which the survey reports variously as a pale, watery blue; the villages give it the name without argument and the ledger does not correct them.

14.6.2 For the discipline of the approach, the detachment files this chapter beside its own town chapters, where a river line is a boundary in the same sense the saddle is one here: see the companion Field Manual, Chapter 9: Huddersfield — Approaches, Rivers and the Spring Gathering, for the reading of approaches where water carries the boundary instead of stone. For the supply run that feeds the concourse at Los Pinos each season, the detachment borrows the running schedule of the Service TC-7, the Toothcomb Limited, fifth edition — Antananarivo to Etosha Halt, with the naturalist's commentary kept as a model of commentary on a route that should not need one.

14.6.3 The detachment's reading shelf keeps one entry that is not a manual: a school report on The Lives of a Bengal Lancer, the 1935 siege film of the British Raj, filed here because its garrison — one troop, two field guns, no ambulance — holds its headquarters the way a unit holds high ground against odds and weather.

14.6.4 Return routing: from the Chilean end, out by Los Vilos; from the Argentine end, out by San Francisco. The detachment files both routes dry, in the blue book, corrected in brown ink. Beside the blue book the detachment keeps one loose leaf from the field: Newsletter No. 27 of the Hooptie & Frill Club, Redding, California, which likewise keeps a single uncorrected line and reads it, per its standing order, as a tolerance — the ledger's only companion that admits a line can stand.
