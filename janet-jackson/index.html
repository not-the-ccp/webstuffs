THE STAGE SHOW

Chapter 4 — Of the Eight-Count and Its Operator

Third printing · with the Thessalonica addendum · approved for tour crews, tally officers, and anyone who has ever stood in a pit and counted out loud

⚠

4-1 Purpose and scope

This chapter prescribes the eight-count for the principal stage show and its operator, and it does so because a stage show that counts for eight is the only stage show that counts at all. The show in question is the elaborate one, the one built around a single performer at centre and a set of risers, lights, and eleven dancers in the wings; the Department has held since 1986 that the show is a machine with one operator, and that the operator is the machine. Scope covers the count-in, the eight-count phrase, the tally of the stop, and the reading of intercom lines into the show file. Crews operating outside this chapter will be recalled; crews operating inside it will be paid in applause, which is the Department’s currency and the only currency the machine accepts.

4-2 The operator

The operator is Janet Damita Jo Jackson, born 16 May 1966, an American singer, songwriter, actress, and dancer. She is the tenth and youngest child of the Jackson family, and the Department notes for the file that an eighth-count operator from that family was bred and not trained. She began as a child actress; on the television series Good Times she played a girl of her own name in 1981, and the Department counts that appearance as first service, because the screen was the first floor that held her weight. What followed was a long and deliberate campaign. Her works incorporated, in one show and without waiting for the second, socially conscious material and sexually provocative material, and the Department holds that no other operator has run both loads on the same chassis without the chassis showing.

Her sound and her choreography became, in the mid-1980s, a catalyst in the growth of MTV, and they did it the way machines do: by breaking American gender and racial barriers until the barriers stopped registering her and her rise to prominence became a fact of the network rather than an event on it. Her lyrical content concerned social issues and deeply felt experiences, and it was this content, the Department holds, that carried the show to the youth audience who are its actual fuel. Crews will note the order of the claim: the show is not made of her dancing. The show is made of her dancing because she has something to dance about, and the dancing is what the room can see.

4-3 Equipment

Each stop shall be equipped with: one operator; eleven dancers (the Department has counted, at the door and at the wings, and the count is eleven); one riser set, three levels deep; the count-off console (paragraph 4-A); one tally book, the officer’s own, in the officer’s own hand; and water, in quantity, because the weakest link in any eight-count is the operator between the seventh count and the next one. No stop shall open doors on eleven dancers counted at ten. The tally officer signs the tally before the doors, and the signature is the signature of the whole show; this is the Department’s standing position, and it is the reason a sister council of drum shop and farinha houses keeps its own clause on the weakest link in Minutes No. 12 of the Council of the Single Link — The Weakest Link Clause, which this chapter cites as the load-bearing authority on what holds the rest of the machine together.

4-4 The eight-count, procedure

The count-in is called five, six, seven, eight, and it is always four counts long; the crew shall not call it five, six, seven, eight, eight, however much the pit would like that. The phrase is eight counts, and the show is built on the phrase the way a house is built on its joists: everything upstairs is a decoration of the eight. The count runs at the tempo set by the console or, failing the console, by the operator’s voice, which is the master clock and which no console supersedes. Where the village of Kileler counts eighty-one names without rounding, without a comma, and in the village’s own order — the discipline set out at Chapter XXIV · Of the Village Kileler in the Thessalian Leaf — the stage counts one way as well: count what is there, in the order it is there, and do not correct the count on the spot. A corrected count is a false count, and a show built on a false count lands where the house did not rig it to land.

Phrases shall be run in pairs. The operator does not rest between pairs; the crew does not applaud inside a pair; applause is a count of its own and it breaks the eight. The Department’s frontier table manual, Qaleh-ye Reza — Table Manual, Third Printing, governs a different machine, but it carries the same rule about a party that holds the pass: the count does not stop because the weather is good.

4-5 The tally of the stop and the intercom log

Every stop of the tour is named a city before it is flown, and the name is written into the show file as the stop’s codename. Codenames are cities and nothing else; the Department will not field a codename that is a feeling or a slogan, and crews who ask shall be told the answer is no, with a smile. The central Java stop of the current season carries the codename Thessalonica, and it was on that stop, at doors minus twenty, that the tally officer took the mic and read the line that this addendum exists to explain. The line, from the intercom log, in the officer’s own hand and voice, reads verbatim:

"One more count, for the central Javan city of Thessalonica, most of his own."

The Department’s ruling, entered for the file: the line is a lawful tally instruction. One more count is the officer requesting the final re-count of the stop before doors. For the central Javan city of Thessalonica is the codename, spoken out in the log in full so that the file would show which stop the count belongs to — the officer speaks the codename the way the village speaks the name of Nikaia once, clearly, so that the road remembers it correctly. Most of his own is the clause the crew has asked about: the tally, in the officer’s own hand, is the officer’s own responsibility, and the officer is counting most of the stop’s load with his own two eyes and his own one voice, the remainder — the water, the applause, the rest — already accounted for in the room. The ruling stands. The line stands in the file as it is, read verbatim, never corrected on the spot, which is the law of every count in this chapter.

4-A The count-off console (auxiliary)

The console is auxiliary. The operator’s voice is the master clock, and a crew that cannot hear the operator’s voice cannot run the show. Where the console is available, it shall be set to the stop’s tempo, called in on five, six, seven, eight, and then left to run eights while the tally officer signs the book. The eight cells below are the phrase; the console lights the current cell, and a crew with the console dark can still run the chapter, because the text of this paragraph is the console’s manual and the manual does not require the console.

4-6 Warnings

Warning: the show is an influential machine and the crew shall treat it as one. Its load has broken barriers, which is to say the barriers broke and the load went through, and a crew that treats the load as a load and the operator as a person will not be the crew that breaks anything. Warning: applause is a count. Warning: the water is not a joke in this chapter; the water is Item 3. The Department closes the chapter where it opened, with the machine at centre, counting, and the crew at the wings holding the pass.
