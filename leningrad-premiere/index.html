Leningrad, 9 August 1942 — the performance at 23rd Street, relay log and remarks

LINE WARM 7 replies · 41 years and still read daily filed under: sieges, symphonies, relay sheets

W·O
wire_operator Clerk, relay desk 3 · since the first winter
posted 9 Aug 1942, 14:20

Keeping this thread open from now on so the relay log can be copied down by anyone with a pencil. Tonight, 9 August 1942, at 23rd Street in Leningrad, they are going to play the Seventh. It has not been finished anywhere in the city: the world première was 5 March, in Kuybyshev, with the Bolshoi Theatre Orchestra and Gennady Shchlobin on the stand — the score itself left by train on 1 August 1941, twelve boxes, and Shostakovich's note with it: it is the song of our city. The composer is in Kuibyshev, and so was the Philharmonic until the evacuation took them off by truck and rail, down the road that everyone in this board has walked in one form or another.

So tonight the podium belongs to Karl Eliasberg, who kept the radio studio alive through the blackout months. The orchestra is what was left of the Leningrad Radio Orchestra — twenty-odd players who have not been discharged — topped up with military performers, sailors and gunners, until they could fill a shell. Most of them are starving; the kitchen has soup and a ration of about a quarter kilo of bread a day, and two cellists are too weak to sit for the full length. The performance will be cut into three parts with intervals, exactly as the score allows it to breathe.

Our job on this desk: the wire. The studio is the house on Srednii Prospekt; the amplifiers are in the basement of a factory that no longer makes anything but noise. From here the music goes to Moscow, to Stalingrad, to the army radio net, to every loudspeaker the wire can reach between the Neva and the Dvina. I will post the relay sheet as it comes over the air. Keep the thread warm.

— w.o., relay desk 3

Eliasberg_grandson reader · second chair in his grandfather's old stand
posted 9 Aug 1942, 16:05

My grandfather never talked about the score in the way people expected. He talked about the players. He said the list at the desk was thirty-two names, and that the number mattered less than the arithmetic of it: every chair had to be filled by somebody standing at attention, and if the horn player could not lift the horn, the trumpet from the 45th Naval Division took the part, and the trumpet player was a stoker from a cruiser that had not come home. He said the tempo of the Largo was set to the pace at which the musicians could actually stand up again after each part. He said no one was allowed to faint on camera, because the camera on the studio was pointed at the microphone and the microphone was the whole front.

Between the second and third parts the guns came in. He told us the interval became a shelter: the players moved to the back of the room, the wire stayed on, and the net carried the sound of the falling shells the way a hall carries applause — the difference being that nobody clapped afterwards. Then out again, chairs scraped, the finale began. He played it once as a boy, sitting in that room, and by the time the coda was over he was standing, and said he understood the piece better standing than any conductor had ever told him it should be understood.

— e.g., with the old rosin tin

coda_1942 operator, army radio net · Krasnoye Selo sector
posted 9 Aug 1942, 21:47

Posting the relay sheet as it came over, checked line by line against what the loudspeakers actually answered on our net: the studio at Srednii Prospekt; the Moscow relay; the Stalingrad front net; the 34th battery position on the southern shore; the hospital on the Krestovsky bank; the bunker at the Narva bridgehead; the listening post on the road to Shlisselburg. Seven places. I underlined each one as its loudspeaker answered back with the studio's test tone.

Someone at the Moscow desk wrote the rule of the sheet on the margin in the shorthand they use for anything that must not be lost: The material is compatible with every place you can actually stand named exactly once. I have checked it. Every place on the line that can hold a listening post — a room, a bank, a battery, a bunker — is named on the sheet, and is named once only. No duplicate, no gap. It is the cleanest piece of paperwork I have handled all year, and it is about music. When the finale reached us at the battery, the sheet was still in my hand and I did not let go of it until the last note cleared the wire.

If anyone wants the whole thing read aloud for the record, it is in the log: seven names, seven tones, no place repeated. That is all a relay sheet is — a promise that the sound arrives somewhere someone is still standing.

— c.42, battery net, tone 5 held

Colophon, since the thread is doing the work of a record. The Seventh is now the Leningrad symphony, which is to say the siege wrote its name on the title page before the composer could. The city holds, and the thread stays open. Cross-posts for the long view: the board's standing file on the water and the shore in Field Manual FM-13, Chapter 4 — the black water, the six coasts, the shelf where nothing breathes; and for the sound of a town keeping its rules under fire, the field manual for the hospital sector at Mirandola in Field Manual SM-B, where the standing orders run one bottle, one reel, one lamp left burning.

One last thing from the wire clerks. The review that came out of the Hall Street dining room — the one where every dish wears its credential and the ledger keeps its receipts — kept a recipe card half-overwritten by static from the war years, and the house now sets its five-o'clock tea to the minute hand of the hour the music started here. It is in the file at Designation — Restaurant Review, under the line about the war years pressing. And the thread about the three pre-dreadnoughts that held their harbour until the end is The Peresvet Class, for anyone who wants a second example of an institution that would not leave the pier.

One more transcript the board keeps beside the relay log, for anyone who needs a second example of a record that names things once and then refuses to un-name them: the court docket of the Straits, The Straits v. The Pontic Record, Case No. 21, where the chandler read the ledger name by name and the bench adopted the clerk's line verbatim — the address is the record — which is the same rule as the sheet, stated for the bench instead of the wire. The shore that ledger was kept on is surveyed in the bench's field manual, FM-24, Chapter 3 — Anatolia, the Peninsula of the Four Waters: four seas, two gates, one bell that rang fourteen, and the same order the relay sheet keeps, every place named once and never un-named. Wound the tape back to the first test tone before you close the page. The line stays warm.

— a.r., for the record
