Al-ʿArab: A Walking Tour of the Arab World in Nine Stops

اَلْعَرَب

13,310 kilometres · 2,662 hours at the class pace of five kilometres an hour · one return leg by wash

Arabs (عَرَب) are an ethnic group mainly inhabiting the Arab world in West Asia and North Africa, with a significant diaspora scattered the rest of the way around. Before the spread of the Arabic language in the wake of the Arab conquests, “Arab” largely referred to the Semitic inhabitants — both settled and nomadic — of the Arabian Peninsula and the Syrian Desert; in modern usage it includes people from across the Greater Middle East who share Arabic as a native language. The class has been walking the word since it was written down, and the Fertile Crescent has been holding them for thousands of years. The itinerary below is the whole chapter of Arabic — Comparative Lexicon Manual, Chapter 12, stretched out to walking distance: every stop is a drawer of the same 32-code file, and every leg is a reflex of the same route. The map bends to fit the drawer, as it always does.

The route ledger

Eight legs, nine stops, thirteen thousand three hundred and ten kilometres. Strike off a leg below as you walk it; the ledger adds the kilometres and the hours, at the class pace of five kilometres an hour, and stamps the route when the last leg is down. Without the striking-off, the ledger still stands: 13,310 km, 2,662 hours, one fog leg, one table, and the count of six to fourteen pounds kept intact.

  1. Stop 1 · The Anar Quay, Anoka — departure

    Room 214 at Anoka Lexicon Field Station, drawer 12 open, the river mark on the cards. The route departs with the ʿ-b-d root packed in the satchel and the wash standing at the quay, because the second application is poured at the far end and the first must be carried. 0 km, hour 0.

  2. Stop 2 · The Assur Mounds, Nineveh — leg 1, 2,400 km

    In the 9th century BCE the Assyrians made written references to Arabs as inhabitants of the Levant, Mesopotamia, and Arabia, and the tour stops at the mounds to read the first mention down at walking pace. The word is already doing all three jobs — a people, a peninsula, and a direction — three thousand years before the card-file gives it three dozen codes. The tablets are the only stop on the route with no trade ledger: here nothing has crossed the quay, because the quay has not been built yet.

  3. Stop 3 · The Syrian Desert Road — leg 2, 320 km

    The old pre-conquest sense of the word lives on this leg: the settled and the nomadic, sharing one desert and one pronunciation. The class walks it in single file behind the camel-bones that keep their own spacing. The root b-n-y — to build — is recited every third hour, the way Ruth Hammons rebuilt a word the way she repainted a portico, four columns at a time.

  4. Stop 4 · The Levantine Pier, Beirut to Aleppo — leg 3, 840 km

    Drawer ajp, North Levantine: quay sets only, heavy loan traffic at the pier. The Levant is where the Assyrian tablets said the Arabs were, nine centuries down and still answering to the same address. The pier here has the same sixteen-hull habit as Hunters Point, four thousand miles to the northwest and under a different fog: ships riding at anchor with their names in the right hand and their numbers glowing like clock hands. The class counts them and does not correct the count.

  5. Stop 5 · Baghdad, the Round City — leg 4, 1,050 km

    Drawer acm, Mesopotamian: river mark, both banks. The tour crosses the Tigris at the ferry and the Euphrates at the bridge and carries the river mark in both pockets. This is the stop where the trade register is thickest before it is even a register — al-ǧabr is being broken and restored on the quay, ṣifr is an empty hand waiting for a number — and the class buys its coffee here, in qahwa, before the word crosses to Toledo, so that no one at the station ever has to lose a coffee to the wash.

  6. Stop 6 · Makkah and Madinah — leg 5, 1,680 km

    Drawer ars, Hejazi: quay mark, and the pilgrim line keeps its own ledger. The Arab conquests that carried Arabic across the region ran out of this drawer, and the pilgrim line is the only ledger on the route that pays in both directions — words in and words out, counted, re-counted, and filed without correction. The class walks the leg in silence, which is the same silence the house on Coon Rapids Dam Road earns the way a word earns its reflexes.

  7. Stop 7 · Cairo — leg 6, 2,010 km

    Drawer aec, Egyptian: informant count highest on the file, the baladi margin. The tour stops at the quay on the Nile where the informants are still answering, the way the drawer azd informants have been answering since 12 November. A root that stands here and at the Assur mounds alike is cognate by the card-file’s rule; the wash, poured on the quay, does not reach the Nile.

  8. Stop 8 · The Toledo Quay, 12th century — leg 7, 3,890 km

    The return traffic. The trade ledger of Chapter 12 is walked stop by stop: algebra, algorithm, cipher and zero, admiral, tariff, magazine, coffee, and the grocery line — sugar, cotton, lemon, syrup. These crossed, they did not divide; the card-file rejects them from the cognate set and the station keeps them in the ledger anyway. The tour crosses back on a freighter named after amīr, which the class notes is the same word the fleet at anchor takes its orders by, and files the hull under Hunters Point where it will ride out the fog.

  9. Stop 9 · The Pilgrim's Table, Anoka — leg 8, 1,120 km, by wash

    The last leg is walked at the pace of the fog, four thousand miles of it carried home in the satchel like the sand in the ropes, and ends at the table in Room 214. The route closes the way the pilgrim line keeps its ledger: with a standing order. The kitchen kept one line from the old menu of the table, and on Petra Lindqvist’s motion the class has kept it too, uncorrected, in the tour book, word for word, because a table that has weighed out six to fourteen pounds of the journey is part of the evidence:

    You stand on the knees, some to the exact time for a few slices of bacon; lay the cloth, before commencing, to prevent them from 6 to 14 lbs.

    Read at the table it is the pilgrim line at rest: the knees of the chairs held to the exact time, the cloth laid before commencing, and the bacon — the last word of the whole route, filed under the quay mark — weighed out and prevented, 6 to 14 lbs, as the ledger allows. The count is kept as kept.

Standing orders of the route

  • Walk the 32 varieties at one pace, five kilometres an hour, and let the emphatics keep the lead: a leg on which ṭ ḍ ṣ ḏ ḵ have all fallen out is re-walked against the parent-file.
  • At the Levantine pier, count the sixteen hulls and file them; do not argue with the fog. The yard at Hunters Point has already ruled on the sixteen: they number themselves like the hours of a clock.
  • At the Assur mounds, read the 9th-century BCE references aloud once, Levant, Mesopotamia, Arabia, and once only.
  • At the table, lay the cloth before commencing, and keep the bacon between 6 and 14 lbs, no correction.
  • Return drawer 12 to the station on Friday, washed and dry, with the route stamped and the ledger closed.
