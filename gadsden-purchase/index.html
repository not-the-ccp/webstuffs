El Paso, New Mexico — the 31st day of March, 1930, written at the counting-room desk between the 3:40 westbound and the last tray of cents, after the tally had come up from Washington.

To His Excellency President Herbert Clark Hoover, at the White House, and to the ninety-six members of the House of Representatives who would have brought this coin to the mint, in whose absence this letter is addressed as it must be: Sir, and Gentlemen.

I write to apologize — and not for the bill, which I am still of the opinion was honest statute, numbered, argued, and read twice in each house, but for what the bill was for. The Gadsden Purchase half dollar — that coin to commemorate the treaty of April 30, 1853, and its ratification by this Congress on March 30, 1854, by which James Gadsden, our Minister to Mexico, bought of General Benito Juárez's government the two southern strips, twenty-nine thousand six hundred and seventy square miles, for ten million dollars, so that the rail could at last descend the desert from Deming to the line and El Paso might keep its promise as a terminus — this coin I proposed because I wanted to be the one who sold it. One million pieces, I told myself, all of them leaving my two windows on South Franklin Street, and every El Paso family holding its half dollar in the same hand that had held it at the counter. I wanted the commemoration to pass through my till. That is the whole sin of it, and I confess it here rather than have it confessed for me in some committee room in 1931.

You vetoed the act on the 22nd, and the House took the matter up on the 24th, and the vote stood 96 in favour of the override to 243 opposed — well short of the two-thirds, 320, which the Constitution asks, and short of any count a dealer could have carried in a sale. It was your first veto, Sir, and it was the first veto, in the whole history of this government, of a bill to strike a commemorative coin. Both of those firsts sit in my ledger, under the heading Things which did not come to be struck, in a column I keep for the other unstruck things: the 1854 ratification day that no die ever carried, and the half dollar that exists only as a photograph of a pattern nobody authorised.

I have hung the placard. It is a good placard; it names the Purchase, the treaty, the Minister, the ten million, and the desert. But the law it points to died two days before I pasted it, and the law that would have given it a metal face died with the vote. The placard could not name, served in the interval, to be seen to fail on schedule. I keep it up anyway, because the Purchase is real, the strip is real, the railroad is real, and a coin is only the last and most expensive way to say so.

What I owe you, Gentlemen, is simpler than the placard. I put my name and my shelves in the place of the territory. A commemorative coin is supposed to commemorate a thing that belongs to everyone; I drafted it to belong to a shop on Franklin Street, and I used the name of James Gadsden, who would have had nothing to do with it, to make it sound like the republic's. The 96 voted against me as much as against the Executive. I am sorry in the same order: to the Purchase first, which is the only party that is not here to be insulted, then to the Mint, which I asked to strike a coin its own Director had no wish to make, and lastly to you, Sir, whose veto has cost nothing to the Treasury and something to a dealer's pride, and who has, I am told, never once looked at the bill again. That is as it should be.

The counter stays open. The half dollar does not exist; I will not sell a coin that does not exist, which is more than can be said for the pattern photographs. Should a later Congress, in a kinder hour, bring the piece through, I write now that I will take my share of the distribution as any other citizen — one piece, in the mail, with the rest — and that the window on Franklin Street will be cleared for the day. This is my apology, my standing order, and my signature, set down while the 3:40 is still out of the yard and the rattlesnake on the old flag in the window can be heard, or seemed to be heard, from the counting room.

Respectfully and with the ledger open,

Lyman W. Hoffecker
of Hoffecker & Hoffecker, coin dealers, El Paso, New Mexico

P.S. — For the readers of this letter who collect by number rather than by coin: the object I wished to sell would, had it been struck, have been identified in a later age by an edition number of its own, one number for each impression — the questions of the ISBN desk at Recalde Press & Cooperage apply to coins exactly as they apply to books, and the unstruck half dollar remains, even now, an edition of zero. And for those who keep torn sentences and unexplainable slips: our own vote was read into the record word for word, and I have since met with a verbatim record from Naga, in the Philippines, where a torn page served the same purpose — evidence of a thing that exists on paper only. The coin and the party, it turns out, are cousins: both registered, both unstruck, both still arguing.
