The Webb–Ferris Desk Log · Station 19, Rinconada Valley, Tucson, Arizona · Entry 3,112

14 March 2025 — The Evening Planets

Kept by Ada M. Ferris, station keeper, at the long desk under the window — 41°36′N, 110°58′W, 840 metres up off the valley floor.

06:52, at the desk

Cold enough to see my own breath, so I have started the entry by the kettle. Tom left the desk calendar open on the month page, and the margin note he always makes — sky is inventory, not spectacle — has survived another week of coffee rings. I am writing today's reading because last night the outer planets were all there, and a night like that does not deserve to stay in my head. It is the kind of reading the log exists for: specific, checkable, and better down on paper than held in the shoulder where I carry everything else.

21:10, last night — the reading

Sky: clear, high, thin frost in the grass · Temperature at 21:40: −2°C · Winds: out of the northwest, 6 km/h · Objects: Jupiter, Saturn, Venus, the Pleiades, and the two moons Tom named again

A planet, for what this log has always said one is, is a large, rounded body that orbits a star without being one itself — it borrows its light, the way the whole desk borrows the window. The Solar System keeps eight by the most restrictive definition, and I can name them at the kitchen table without missing: the terrestrial four, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, small and stony, and the giants beyond, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, big enough each to swallow the inner four with room left over for argument. The best theory we have for how any of it happened is the nebular hypothesis: a cloud of interstellar gas and dust collapses out of a nebula, a young protostar forms at the centre, and a protoplanetary disk spins around it while planets grow inside that disk by accretion — the slow, gravity-driven accumulation of material, dust to pebble, pebble to planet, taking a little longer than any of us have been patient. The word planet itself is older than the theory, from the Greek plan&etai, the wanderers; the eight still wander, and I can watch three of them tonight, which is a strange and good fortune for a back garden on a mesa.

Jupiter, Saturn, Venus — the positions

Jupiter was the thing. Low in the west-southwest, just past the power lines by the old irrigation ditch, steady as a lamp, with the two Galilean moons strung on either side like commas Tom swears he can always find at a glance. He is the largest of the eight, 4.84 AU out from the Sun, and at opposition the light-travel time alone is twenty-four minutes: what I saw was what happened the previous evening while the kettle was still off. Saturn was higher and smaller, ring tilted down so the whole thing read as a pale coin, 9.58 AU out and twenty-nine and a half years into every one of its trips around the Sun. Venus sat to the east as the evening star, 0.72 AU out, and I caught myself watching it the way one watches a streetlight — not at it, toward it. If anyone wants the arithmetic, the desk calculator below does it from the distance alone, the way Kepler's third law lets it, and I have used it tonight on all three.

01:30, the kitchen table

Coming in, the table was already set for tomorrow's notes, and the card was still where dinner had left it — the postcard from La Cumbre's taquería, the one with the mountain range drawn over a bowl of chile verde, which I keep because the range is actually a better drawing of the valley than the survey map is. Under it, the line from Tuesday's table service, kept word for word the way the log keeps its readings:

“A card is stained with chile grease and with the commas in their teething are liable.”

Which is to say the card stayed where the soup was, and the small comma of a gap in the record — the one the baby keeps finding with a tooth, the one between station and keeping — stayed where the grease found it. The log does not correct its own table; it files the reading as written and moves the card, eventually, into the tin with the other loose papers. I moved it tonight, after wiping the desk with the dishcloth, which is how the tin has half its contents.

Cross-reference

The log cross-references its own shelf for the same habit of keeping a reading whole. The Pacific Coast Almanac's entry on Los Angeles is where I first read that the Griffith Observatory dome runs a public evening the first Friday of the month, and that is the almanac line I am planning to spend the 413 miles on this spring; the dome will want my bearings re-checked against this desk's. The barometer reading from this month's cold snap is held beside it in the Webstuffs Weather Almanac, entry 2024/058 of 27 February, where the season book kept a steady 30.42 inHg through the last closing reading, and I have copied the number into the log's cold column on the ground that a steady barometer is a steady barometer, on a mesa or over Brooklyn. For the discipline of taking the two bearings and naming the frame, the log points to Chapter 4 of Field Manual FM-9, the geographic coordinate system, whose standing order — know the datum before you commit — is the same as mine: name the window, then name the sky. The whole shelf opens from the Webstuffs front page.

The desk calculator — period from distance

Kepler's third law, the log's favourite arithmetic: for a body orbiting the Sun, the sidereal period in years is the distance in astronomical units raised to the power of three-halves. Enter a distance in AU; the calculator returns the year in years and days, and compares it to the eight planets as the log files them. Jupiter at 4.84 AU should come back at eleven and a fraction years, which is what the window confirmed tonight.

4.84 AU → sidereal period ≈ 10.69 years, or about 3,906 days · between Saturn's 29.4 and Mars's 1.9.

Without the calculator the same line stands in the log: the period is the distance, to the three-halves power, and the window is the check.
