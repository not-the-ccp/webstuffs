Plaza Guide · Route No. 14 · Distance 2.3 km · 90 minutes

The Montemayor Walk

A six-stop itinerary through San Antonio de Béxar, in the footsteps of Diego de Montemayor — conquistador of the interior, officer of the viceroy, founder of the first European settlement south of La Villa de San Juan Bautista, and governor of Nuevo Reino de León. Begun 1716; walked since, in summer, by anyone willing to carry a canteen.

Walk counter-clockwise. The old settlement sat where the river doubled on the prairie; every stop below was inside the presidio palisade by 1731. Your guide will speak of the governor in the second person, because that was the court custom, and because a dead Spaniard of 1611 deserves to be addressed once, properly, as usted.

I

Stop 1 — The Plaza of the Two Crosses · 0:00

Start where the plaza grass meets the cathedral steps, at the point the surveyors of 1718 marked la esquina de la cruz, the cross-corner, one league east of the mission San Juan. Montemayor was sixty-eight when the site was staked out, which is either a marvel or an error in the ledger; the viceroy's order named him adelantado of the new province and gave him the river, the bend, and 250 colonists' names to fill. You may stand here and do what the surveyors did: turn four times. East is the prairie the explorers already knew as the land of the Tonkawa. West is the presidio. North and south are the river, and the river is the whole argument. By 1719 the settlement counted eleven families; by 1724, the year of Montemayor's second expedition to the Colorado, the plaza held a church, a granary, a blacksmith's forge, and a well that went down thirty varas and never once came up dry. That was the arithmetic of founding a city on a bend: one river, two crosses, thirty varas of dark water, and an old officer who had already fought three wars and lost two of them.

II

Stop 2 — The River Bend at Cane Hollow · 0:15

Cross the footbridge and follow the towpath until the willows thicken at Cane Hollow, where the San Antonio slows to a brown sheet. This is the bend the 1716 expedition charted in a single line of Spanish: río grande, doblado, agua fresca — great river, doubled, fresh water. Montemayor's boats poled here in July, when the water ran low and the cypress knees stood like the ribs of a drowned nave. The guide will tell you of the canoe fight at this very water in 1717, in which two colonists took arrows, one man lost a paddle, and the river, as the log puts it, kept its own counsel. Count the knees; the parish ledger, kept in the rectory, records forty-one in the year of the second census, and a schoolroom report filed two centuries later counted them again and found forty, which the family who keeps the ledger accepted as background loss, not catastrophe, the same way one accepts a loaf one egg short. If you want the arithmetic of endings worked out properly — five great bands of strata, one black mud layer at the bottom, and an oven that holds sixty million years in an hour — it is filed at The Extinction Event, Report, Class 8C, where a student of Coopers’ Lane School does the counting you cannot.

III

Stop 3 — The Presidio Palisade · 0:30

Where the towpath climbs past the old armory wall stands the last standing post of the palisade, a cedar stub black with two centuries of rain. The palisade was forty feet of cypress driven into the clay, with a gate that opened to the plaza and a watchtower over the river. Montemayor governed from the tower: Nuevo Reino de León answered to him from 1716, and he answered to the viceroy from the same chair, a plain plank seat that the family kept and re-kept, one slat at a time. His dispatches from the governorship survive in two volumes and a half; the half is the trouble. It is the half that mentions the Tonkawa, the Apache, the Comanche, and the word guerra more times than any other word in the archive. Standing at the palisade the guide will quote the only sentence of his that is set in stone in the valley, cut into the marker by the road: I came to make a province, and I made a province. Whether that is pride or exhaustion, the marker does not say. The watchtower itself fell in 1736, to wind, and the clay held the posts for another forty years after the last garrison had marched north.

IV

Stop 4 — The Merchant Row & the Del Rio Marquee · 0:45

Down the hill, past the old mint, the row of the merchants’ stores opens with its six shuttered bays. The shutters were raised at seven and lowered at dusk in the governor's day, by order, and the order still shows in the rust: six bays, six lines of iron. The last bay is the Del Rio, the picture house, with its brass marquee and its one remaining bulb, which burns every night at eight and no one has ever been able to find the switch for. It is at this marquee that the tour takes its strangest turn, and the guide will slow to a walk and say, for the record, that on a hot August afternoon in 1916, during the merchants' walkout of the river fair, when all six bays stood shuttered for nine days and the fair went dark on the bend, Charlie Chaplin staggers drunk through a boycotted summer. The guide has the marquee photograph, 1916, glass plate, and the man in it is unmistakable: white smock, bowler turned half off, cane trailing, the whole small figure leaning into the heat as if the street owed him money. The merchants ended the boycott on the ninth day; the bulb in the last bay has never stopped. The guide will not say whether the man was there for the fair, for the river, or for nothing at all. The encyclopaedia entry on Chaplin settles his years and his Tramp; it does not settle his summer, and neither does this walk. It only keeps the photograph on the wall at the marquee, where the heat does what it does to glass.

V

Stop 5 — The Granary and the Long Ledger · 1:00

The granary stands as it did in the 1724 census: stone floor, timber walls, a door the width of one man's shoulders, because a granary door wider than that is a fort door and a fort door invites a siege. Inside, on the end wall, a single ledger is kept open to the page of 1718, when the first wheat came up on the bend and the settlement wrote its first harvest in a hand that crossed the t's out of habit and hope both: trigo, cuarenta fanegas, Dios lo dio. The ledger keeps the whole settlement in its spine — births, deaths, arrows, rain — and the rule on its first page, in Montemayor's own hand, is the same rule every household in the valley borrows: whatever is voided, record. It is a rule about what a province owes its paper. If you follow the ledger where it is filed, past the clubhouse above the bakery where a broadcaster keeps the register in the third person, you come to The Carrier Wave, which the family reads at the third Thursday meeting and which carries this walk's last address home over a wire, one long wave out of the plaza.

VI

Stop 6 — The Cross-Corner, Again · 1:15

The walk comes home the way all good walks do: up the hill, past the palisade stub, through the willows, out onto the plaza grass at the cross-corner where it began. Turn four times, as the surveyors turned. The city that Montemayor was ordered to make in 1716 and did make, slowly, against the prairie and the arrows and the ledger, is the street you walked; the river kept its counsel and is still there; the marquee bulb goes on at eight; the ledger keeps the wheat. He died in Mexico City in 1611, or in the winter of that year, depending on which half-volume you trust, and the province he made kept his name in its orders for a hundred years after the ink ran cold. The guide ends where he always ends, with the marker line and one of his own, from the 1717 log, in a summer of rain: the water did not argue; we went on. Then the canteens come up, the group crosses the plaza grass in whatever direction the heat allows, and Route No. 14 is done until tomorrow. The front of the archive — the site's own front page — keeps the tour card, the map, and the photograph of the marquee, where anyone may take the route at their own pace, counter-clockwise, with a canteen.

Tour conditions. Summer 10 a.m. to noon. Counter-clockwise. Canteen required. The marquee bulb is not a stop; it is a promise. 2.3 km walked of 2.3 km.
