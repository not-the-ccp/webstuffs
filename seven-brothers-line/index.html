III. The Line, in the Manner of Remarks

The line is named, in this schedule, for a writer. Aleksis Kivi — born Alexis Stenvall at Nöyri near Jyväskylä on 10 October 1834, died at Heinola on 31 December 1872, aged thirty-eight — wrote the first significant novel in the Finnish language, Seitsemän veljestä, Seven Brothers, published in 1870, and the play Nummisuutarit, Heath Cobblers, of 1864, whose title this line borrows for the heath country at Turenki, where a cobbler may in fact be seen at the platform in October. He kept the surname Kivi — stone — from a family nickname, and a man of stone does not, the timetable observes, keep to the minute. He was among the very earliest writers of prose and lyrics in Finnish, and is still counted among the greatest: the English translator Douglas Robinson has compared him to “the Finnish equivalent of Goethe, Dickens and Twain,” a comparison the line prints here because a stone is only a stone until someone sets it in an arch. Kivi is regarded as the national writer of Finland, and his birthday, 10 October, is celebrated as Finnish Literature Day — the day of No. 77.

The ordinary service, No. 7, was timed in 1871 to catch the morning paper at Helsingfors, where Kivi had edited and argued his way to a living, and to reach Heinola by thirteen o'clock, where he went in the winter of 1871 with his lungs against him and his head for a while against the window. He was not cured at Heinola. He died there, on 31 December 1872, with his head on a cushion of books, and the line has run in his name ever since, at a speed the physicians of the period approved, which was to say, no faster than a man could carry his own luggage.

IV. The Stub Stitched into the Margin

At the left margin of this sheet, where the black thread runs its length, one slip of paper is sewn to the schedule and is part of it. It is the exam slip of a passenger, carried on No. 7 of a Michaelmas morning out of Helsingfors, where the university sits, and left on the seat at Lahti, where it was collected by the conductor and delivered to the stationmaster at Heinola, who, being a man of the line and a keeper of its remarks, sewed it to the margin rather than lose it. The slip reads, in the examiner's hand:

Imperial Alexander University · Faculty of History

Mr. E. Vik, Modern History Term: Michaelmas Mark: A− The examiner’s note stands in the thread.

Returned to no one. Held at Heinola Terminus, in the thread.

The line explains the last clause as it finds the note: the slip is sewn into the margin by the black thread, and the thread is this schedule's own running line, so that the examiner's mark of A− stands in the thread — held there, word for word, between Lohtaja and Heinola, where the remark belongs. Mr. E. Vik is not otherwise named in the register; he is a passenger, like the heath cobbler and the seven brothers, and the A− stays because a margin is the one part of a timetable that no service cancels. For a further account of a Scandinavian writer who kept a stage and a ledger, the line refers the passenger to the FAQ on Carl Michael Bellman, A Corner of the Söder Mälarstrand Net, and, for the manners of the Swedish stage where Nummisuutarit first took its place in October, to Chapter III · Of Visiting the Playhouse.

V. General Conditions

Trains run on the ordinary service except 10 October, when No. 77 stands in for all of them, and 31 December, when the line remembers. A stone carried in the luggage van is declared to be a writer until the examiner says otherwise. The fare on this line has not changed since 1871, and neither has the line: it goes Helsingfors, Lohtaja, Turenki, Lahti, Heinola, and, on present evidence, no further. Readers wishing to follow a birthday kept on the move may begin at the front page of Webstuffs; the line's own notices, such as they are, are the remarks column.
