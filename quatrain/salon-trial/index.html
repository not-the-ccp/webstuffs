Consulate of the Town & Territory of Salon-de-Provence

Transcript, Book 41, folio 112 · excerpt · entered in the clerk’s hand

Before the Consular Court, Tuesday, the 17th day of June, 1563

Plaintiff Michel de Nostredame, apothecary, of Salon v. Defendant Jean-Baptiste Pichot, printer & bookseller, shop beside the church of Saint-Claude

folio 112 the morning session, nine of the clock

THE CLERK. Present in the gallery: Master Étienne de Villeneuve, consular judge; the consuls Guillaume Aubert and Pierre Renaldin; Joseph Ferrier, clerk, the undersigned; the plaintiff, who came from the apothecary’s shop on the Rue de la Bourse with a mortar bag still smelling of saffron; and the defendant, who came with a satchel of type-cases and a manuscript. The bench was cleared. The record may open.
THE JUDGE. Let the record open. The cause is this. The plaintiff claims authorship of a work printed in this town in the year 1555, entitled Les Prophéties, being nine hundred and forty-two quatrains — that is to say, four-line stanzas — bound in nine centuries, the tenth century missing by the count, which the plaintiff calls a gap and the defendant calls a feature. The defendant confesses that he has set a second impression of the book in his shop, and in that impression he has added two hundred and forty-two quatrains, numbered 701 to 942, which he calls the seventh centurie and which he claims to have written at a river table in 1556. The plaintiff says the whole book is his. State your case, plaintiff.
THE PLAINTIFF. My lord, the book was printed here, in Salon, in 1555, and every quatrain in it is mine. I am an apothecary, not a prophet, but the four lines are mine the way the pestle is mine. Let me add, before it is asked of me, that my father’s family came to the Christian faith a generation before I was born, in December 1503; and I put that in the record once, so it need not be put again.
THE DEFENDANT. With respect, my lord, and with respect to the faith of a man’s father’s father, which is nobody’s business but God’s: I confess I have set the type. I deny that I have stolen it. A quatrain is a quatrain; four lines are four lines; any man with a ruler can build a frame, and I built mine by the river where the light comes in at a slant.
THE JUDGE. Then let the manuscript speak. Clerk, read the heading of the piece in the satchel.
THE CLERK. (reading) In the name of God. A vessel of the gulf shall turn for bread at the mouth of the river, and the centurie shall be counted without the tenth. Fact, fact, fact, omen.
THE PLAINTIFF. I have read that quatrain. It is not mine, my lord — it is yours, sir, and you have set it in my book with the rest. The whole seventh centurie is of that breed: fact, fact, fact, omen, but the omen is borrowed, and it is borrowed from a ship.
THE JUDGE. The plaintiff calls a witness. Name and trade.
THE CLERK. A maker of the four-line stanza, of the shed behind a bakery; known to the record as the Builder, keeper of a build log for the quatrain, which the plaintiff has produced as exhibit C, with a margin note in pencil reading LINE 1 STEM / LINE 2 FRAME / LINE 3 FRAME / LINE 4 STERN. His own account of the form is kept in the Quatrain — Build Log, to which the record refers.
THE BUILDER. My lord, I build quatrains the way my neighbour built a ship, plank by plank. A quatrain is four planks: the first line is the stem, and it sets the meter and announces the rhyme; the second and third are frames, and they carry the argument; the fourth is the stern, and it lands the poem with a keel. I have read the defendant’s two hundred and forty-two. The frame is right and the stern is wrong: his stern lands on the frame’s rhyme, which is how you know a man has not paced the room. And where his quatrains rhyme on the word “me,” I confess I cannot say whether that is skill or the rhyme my shed has banned, though I lean toward the ban.
THE JUDGE. The second witness.
THE CLERK. Marguerite Chabot, wife of the plaintiff.
MARGUERITE CHABOT. He wrote them at the shop table, between the mortar and the door. He studied at the University of Avignon for a little more than a year, and the plague closed the doors on him, so he left with his books under his arm and his hands still clean. He went after that to Montpellier to take his degree in physic, and he has never stopped trying. When the pestilence of 1546 took the town, he walked it street by street as physician, and came back famous, which he hates, and the quatrains he wrote four lines at a time, the way a rigger works one shroud at a time, and kept them in a paper envelope so the vinegar in the crock could not get to them.
THE JUDGE. The record will pause to take exhibit D. Clerk, what is it?
THE CLERK. A sheet from the office of the examiner, Specification No. 85–0023, Method and Apparatus for Sustained Televisual Presence, filed in that office’s reckoning on the inventor’s own date — the full sheet is kept at the Specification No. 85–0023 in the archive, and is cited here as exhibit. The plaintiff has marked one line of its field transcript, entered verbatim, the record of that office closing the entry without translation. The line, read aloud:

“Keep them from the stranded whale, accurate hints may be sliced thin.”

Field transcript, demonstration No. 2, entered verbatim; the office’s record closes the entry without translation.
THE JUDGE. The record will ask the meaning of it.
THE PLAINTIFF. My lord, in the third week of May a whale stranded in the Rhône near Tarascon, and it is there still, and the fishermen of the town go to see it the way a town goes to see anything that will not get up. “Them” are the nine hundred and forty-two quatrains of the book. The whale is the second impression in the defendant’s shop, into which I have watched him slip his two hundred and forty-two by the piece, the way the whale took the river: slowly, and with great weight, and with no intention of moving. As to the second half: I am an apothecary, and I have cut rhubarb root and saffron and ginger for thirty years, and I can slice a root thin, and I have cut many a hint of a remedy thin and sold it by the grain. But a quatrain is not a root. It is the defendant who slices thin — he takes the centurie apart, cuts out the quatrains, sells them loose at two sous a sheet and calls them hints. The accurate ones may be sliced thin, the line says; but the line also says keep them, and the keeping is the whole of it. Four lines held together make a frame; four lines sold loose make a shilling, and I am not the man who signs for that exchange.
THE DEFENDANT. My lord, I ask that the line be noted as not translation, that office having closed its entry without it, and that I be judged on the manuscript and not on a whale.
THE JUDGE. Noted. And on the manuscript, then. The record has heard the maker of the form, the wife, and the pestle. The bench finds as follows, and the clerk will enter it: (1) the two hundred and forty-two quatrains in the satchel are excluded from the second impression, and the manuscript is delivered to the consulate to be bound into this book’s annex; (2) the book shall stand at nine hundred and forty-two quatrains, no more and no fewer, the gap of the tenth century being kept open, as the plaintiff calls it, or the feature, as the defendant calls it — the record declines to choose; (3) the defendant’s claim of authorship over the seventh centurie is entered against his name, and set aside with the question of the plaintiff’s father’s father, which the record is not bound to hear; and (4) the line of exhibit D is entered in the transcript verbatim, and the record of this court, like the record of the office, closes the entry without translation.
THE CLERK. The bench adjourned at ten of the clock. The whale at Tarascon, for the record, was still in the river. Entered, Joseph Ferrier, clerk, in his own hand, with the saffron still in the air of the gallery.
