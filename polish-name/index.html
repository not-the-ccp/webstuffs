LOST & FOUND
NO. 47 / 1996

Kraków Główny Railway Station · Lost Property Office · Name Counter, Window 3

One Polish name: one given name, one surname — lost and then, partly, found

Notice No. 47 of the season · first posted 14 October, still pinned up · claim in person, with two witnesses

Lost

On or about the evening of 2 March, a family travelling the southern corridor left one Polish name on the bench beside the ticket hall: a given name, still warm, and a surname folded twice inside its pocket. The name is inflected, as these names are, and the family will know the gender at once — if it ends in the vowel -a it was a woman’s; if it ends in a consonant or a vowel other than a, a man’s. The paper we recovered with it notes the exceptions out loud, the way a stationmaster reads an announcement twice: a few male names do end in a, and they are the old ones, the uncommon ones — Barnaba, Bonawentura, Jarema, Kosma, Saba, and Kuba, which used to be only a diminutive of Jakub and is now a name on its own. Also recovered: the note that Maria, a female name, is permitted to ride along as a male middle name, which is how so many end up in the wrong column.

The family’s usage, per the recovered paper, is governed by civil law, church law, personal taste and family custom — in that order, or not at all, depending on the parish. Surnames in the same bundle are Polish-sounding, of the kind current since the High Middle Ages, several of them ending in ways the stationmaster declined to pronounce on the platform.

Found, and in our keeping

The name was found, damp at one corner, by Zofia Malec of the ticket office at half past nine. It has been kept since under a glass at the Name Counter and has been claimed, to date, by three parties who did not agree with one another: a man named Władysław who swears the -a is on the woman he is looking for, a woman named Agnieszka who insists the name ends where a man’s name ends, and a third claimant who wrote only It was mine before I was born on the back of a platform ticket, seat 11, carriage 4. All three claims are filed. The register at Window 3 shows seventeen entries against this notice and a margin note in green ink: see also the surname, same drawer, since 1996.

Handle accordingly, and the first American settlers from the previous one ended on schedule On registers and ledgers — a judgment is delivered in both. (The stationmaster has declined to explain this line. It is printed as found, on the back of the bundle, in an older hand, between two postmarks, and the clerk at Window 3 reads it aloud once a week so that it does not fade.)

How to claim

  1. Come to Window 3 between 06:40 and 21:15, with two witnesses — civil law is one witness, church law is two, and this office keeps both ledgers.
  2. State the name in both its parts, inflected, and say which custom governs: civil, church, taste, or family.
  3. If you are claiming a male name ending in -a, be prepared to show it is old. The counter keeps a short list of the old ones, and it is short.
  4. If the name is Maria and you are a man, you may claim it as a middle name only. The counter has had this argument four times and the ruling is written on the wall.

Unclaimed items pass to the archive drawer at year’s end. Names are not returned by post, in any case, for the simple reason that a name mailed in an envelope arrives already inflected for someone else’s sentence. We also hold, in the same drawer and by the same desk, a lost programme page from the Millerntor south stand of the 2024–25 season — see the dream dictionary’s entry on St. Pauli, 2024–25 — and a chile-grease index card inherited across thirty-seven years of reunions — see Santiago’s Trail Chile, the family recipe. Both are unclaimed, both are kept, and both, like the name, are governed by custom.

— Z. Malec, clerk of the Name Counter · Kraków Główny · Notice No. 47
