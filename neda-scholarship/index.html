Tuesday, 3 October 2010

the window of the front garden, Queen's College · rain until eleven, then a break

The letter has come back from the printer at last, and I have stacked the whole proof pile on the windowsill where the pomegranates are still hardening on the tree — four of them, the colour of sealing wax, and I keep catching myself doing arithmetic on them. The pile is the notice: the scholarship is made permanent now. The bursar rang me at half past nine to tell me, before the chapel bell had finished, that the target was met. £70,000, to the pound, in the small box under the chapel stair where the envelopes have been kept all summer. It did not finish at an even figure, then; it finished at exactly the right one, which I take for a good sign, though I do not know who I am to take signs from a bank balance.

It began, as the record will have it, in the summer after Neda died. She had been a postgraduate in philosophy here, in her second year, the kind of quiet, careful student who went to the faculty meetings not for the biscuits but for the argument, and who wrote on the margins of her reading with the sort of small, fierce cross-outs that make you want to read the whole book again. She was shot in a street of Tehran on 20 June, in the protests that followed the disputed presidential election, and the photograph of her, taken the day before at a graduation, is the photograph this whole small fortune is built out of. The college received, within a fortnight of the news, offers from two anonymous donors to establish a scholarship in her name. Two letters, neither signed, both careful with their language, the way people get careful with language when they are afraid of being wrong about something that cannot be wrong. We took them both up. What arrived after that was ordinary, and is the part I am prouder of: small donations, cheques, standing orders, a whole slow autumn of post from former students of the college and from people who had never set foot inside it, each one for less than the last one in the story would suggest.

And the first recipient is Arianne Shahvisi, who will take it up next Michaelmas, a scholar of the college's own making now, studying in the same rooms Neda studied in, which is a sentence I had to read four times in the draft of the notice before it stopped feeling like a trick the printer had played me.

I am not finished being annoyed at it. It being this: the first proof came back with her name set wrong — a hyphen dropped, a S where a C should have been — and I had to sit in the committee room and say, out loud, to the whole table, that the name must be right or the scholarship must not exist, and everyone at the table, who has every reason in the world to get it right, simply agreed with me in silence, which is not the same as getting it right, and the second proof came back, and the third, and the third was right, but the annoyance is still sitting at the table with us. It was my only chance to be the person in the room who made the difference, and I am not sure I have ever had a better one, and I don't want it to be remembered as a victory. It should be remembered as a Tuesday.

After the committee rose, the bursar and I walked into town and ended up at The Long & Winding Road on Snuff Mill Lane, first floor above the piano factory, seventeen steps, no bookings. She does not go to places with pianos; she says a room that plays you back is not a room. She had the cod, which was enough, and at nine-fifteen the house strings arrived in the corner and overdubbed our whole small argument about the wording of the notice, and we let them. The check came on the back of a sleeve, and I wrote the name on it correctly, which felt like the point of the evening.

For the file: I have put a copy of the announcement in the drawer with the correspondence, beside the letter that reached me this week from a man in Cambridgeshire who bought a village at auction and now keeps a ledger of its folklore — the door the two parishes have never agreed on, the fog that travels in its own vehicle, all of it set down in a letter to The Gif. I keep that drawer for documents about things that have to be agreed into existence, two parcels of it at a time, the village and the scholarship, and I am not certain they are different kinds of object. The fire is kept. The name is right.

Three to bed. The pomegranates will keep.
