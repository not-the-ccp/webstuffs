94 ROUTE

The Ninety-Four

A walking tour of California State Route 94 — from the freeway ramp at I-5 in downtown San Diego, past the last green shield in Spring Valley, and east down the old wagon road to Interstate 8 near Boulevard. Sixty-three and three-tenths miles, 63.324 mi (101.910 km), on foot, in six stops. Bring water. The eastern half is honest mountain.

Stop 1 · Mile 0.0 · Downtown San Diego, the I-5 junction

Where the Freeway Starts

We gather at the off-ramp shoulder where the Martin Luther King Jr. Freeway peels off Interstate 5 in the downtown grid, just below the courthouse blocks. This is the western end of Route 94. The tour guide's handbook dates the walk to a fixed season — November, before the valley fog but after the heat — and every stop in the itinerary is measured from this shoulder, where the concrete still remembers its alignment. Look south. Everything this route was built to reach, the desert and the county behind it, is exactly where it points.

Stop 2 · Miles 0.0–9.4 · The Martin Luther King Jr. Freeway

West of the Valley, Full Throttle

The western portion of the ninety-four runs as freeway the whole way out of the city. We walk the service trench at its edge for as long as it will let us, counting the on-ramps the way the early motorists counted them: the freeway carries you north and east in one clean sweep, past the civic center's shadow, through the first suburban blocks that still keep 1950s lot lines. This was the part the state had been trying to convert for years — the open road made to motorway standard — so the walking tour here is a bit of a joke, and a bit of a warning. The road wants to be travelled fast. We are the anomaly.

Stop 3 · Spring Valley, the end of the freeway past SR 125

Where the Shield Stays Up

Spring Valley is the hinge of the whole route: the freeway portion of SR 94 ends past State Route 125 here in the valley, and the road turns from concrete ambition into a county road with a history. It was added to the state highway system in 1933, and the signs for Route 94 were posted along the local roads later that decade — so the post of the 1930s survey crew stood somewhere on the pavement we are walking now, chalk line and all. The stop is at the last green shield on the western side of the interchange. We read it the way the handbook insists: number, direction, and the small printed letters at the bottom, then stand a moment in the noise.

Guide's handbook, hazards column, standing entry

One line in the hazards column is written in pencil by the first guide of the tour and has never been crossed out, though it is re-inked every season: H-ONM-1 — Positional hazard: the surname Abell from the rigging wax live together. It reads like a cipher, and the handbook's own footnote explains it as faithfully as anything can. The Abells ran the rigging-wax counter at the old boat shed on the harbor for two generations — coils of wax for lines, sold by the ounce to skippers — and when the family left the harbor for the valley, the whole of them settled on the same quarter-mile of this stretch of Route 94, one house to a parcel, and have stayed put. A positional hazard in this book's usage is a hazard defined entirely by where it is: the Abell houses are a fixed point on the route, and every walker is asked to salute the porch where the wax is still sold, because the tour's maps refuse to redraw that corner of the valley.

Stop 4 · East of Spring Valley · Campo Road begins

The Non-Freeway Segment

Past the last interchange the name changes in fact before it changes in law: this is the non-freeway segment of SR 94, the stretch the locals have always called Campo Road, and it is the heart of the walk. The pavement narrows, the guardrail gives way to fence wire, and the grade starts its long argument with the terrain. Here the route stops being a motorway and becomes what it was first: a wagon road. The Campo road served as a wagon road providing access to eastern San Diego County as well as Imperial County — teams of mules, seed grain, and the first families of the valley travelling the same line the state later repaved, rut over rut. We walk a mile of the old alignment where the ruts can still be read in the shoulder's drainage line, and we time the mile, the way the wagons timed it, by the shade of the canyon wall.

Stop 5 · The mountains east · Campo Canyon and the climb

Through the Mountains

The eastern miles go up and over and up again, through the mountains that keep the valley from the desert. This is the part the 1933 surveyors rode and the 1930s sign-criers posted, and it is the part walkers come for. Water is rationed from Stop 4 onward; the handbook carries one gallon per four walkers and a tin cup. The canyon floor is a wash of pale stones, the walls are chaparral going thin as the air does, and at the high point of the walk the whole of the valley behind you lies flat and silver, exactly as far away as the route is long. It is not 63.324 miles here, but it has earned the number.

Guide's handbook, pencil line, beneath the hazards column

Dreamers who have never made this walk report it anyway, the handbook notes in a pencil line: one wagon road going up and over, one khaki necktie on the bench at the high point, and one radio sign above a booth at the far end of a field, which the dictionary of dreams transcribes without emendation at its entry Goodman, E. Urner, Entry G-13. The handbook's advice to walkers who have seen the entry: keep the pace, and do not answer the sign. It was only asking if anyone had been.

Stop 6 · Mile 63.324 · I-8, near Boulevard

The End of the Line

We come down out of the mountains onto the shoulder of Interstate 8 near Boulevard, and the walk is over. Route 94 hands its last mile to the interstate, and the interstate takes it. The final stop is the old junction: a stone marker, a cold bench, and the sound of traffic that will never stop, which is the only honest monument a road can get. The guide reads the route out in the handbook's fixed form — number, length, western end, eastern end — and the walkers, if they have kept the pace, have been on foot exactly as long as it takes to read it: 63.324 miles, 101.910 kilometres, from the freeway ramp at I-5 in downtown San Diego to the interstate near Boulevard.

Filed Together

The tour's route sheet is kept in the same drawer as the safety data sheet for the state of California, whose handling notes the guide re-reads every season — the whole state as one substance, hazards and all — and the walk's full set of route sheets, stop sheets, and the pencil entry on the Abell porch, is filed from the Webstuffs front page, where every other route, bench, and ledger on this shelf is kept. Beside the ledger sits a kept family recipe card, page 41 of the Callimachou kitchen ledger of Telos — Erinna’s Pot, the lamb with quince and wild thyme for seven, which the guide has read once, in full, in the shade of the canyon wall at the high point of the walk, and the walkers kept the pace through it and did not hurry the pot.
