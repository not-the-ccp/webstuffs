Station No. 714 · Arzl im Pitztal · the keeper’s diary, summer book

Thursday, 14 July 2017 · entry kept in pencil, corrected in chalk

The Traverse at the Arzl Boulders

12 km/h north-east, nineteen degrees, dry rock, excellent friction

Out before the roosters, with the pad on my back and Maren from Imst behind me, down the valley road and up the footpath to the limestone benches. She carried the chalk; I carried the red-and-white crash pad, the one with the repaired corner, and the pair of shoes in 43 that are not mine — they came up out of the loft when I was sorting the loft, and they fit, which I will not explain. This is bouldering, and it is the clearest of the climbing: no rope, no harness, no belay, no one above you deciding whether the system holds. The problems are small — most of them less than six metres, twenty feet — so there is no altitude to argue with, only the stone, the sequence of moves, and the ground, which you have agreed to meet.

It is not the same as free solo, which is also done without ropes, and it does not pretend to be. On the free solo the fall is the whole question; here the fall is a working assumption and the mat is the settlement. Maren said we should chalk like the station chalking its instruments: enough, and no more. The air smelled of cut hay and of the dust off the bench. We set the pad where the line of the traverse would take us, four metres out from the long face, and left it there like a verdict.

Problem 14 is the long bench itself: a traverse, which means we climb horizontally, from one end of the problem to the other, across eleven metres of ledge with nothing to do but the holds. It is a boulder problem in the truest sense, because the rock does not go up at all. I did it four times. On the first three I stopped at the eighth foot, where the crimp is worn to a knife edge, and on the fourth I did what the standing order says: counted the seconds of the line, divided by five, and went again on the answer. It held. Maren, from the ground, noted it in her own hand and would not say what she wrote.

While the sun moved off the bench I was sorting her route cards out of the middle binder, because they had come loose again, and between two of them was a slip of paper in a hand I know, headed, word for word, For a novel that once held up by everything in it. Below the line, in pencil, is a list of the things that hold up a problem in its place: the foot, the crimp, the tape, the person who spots you. The novel, for the record, is the carpenter’s whale book that came through the sale as Lot 27, the Bell Creek Township Papers, from the tin that travelled west in 1894 — the book that once held up a Burt County schoolhouse roof, and that a Nebraska district found it worth two Saturdays. A novel and a bench; both stand or fall with the whole of their sequence, and both are held up by everything in them, which is why the slip is filed where it is.

One more thing, and I am recording it because it belongs to the lot and not to me: around the knife-edge crimp at the eighth foot there is a coil of red tape, laid smooth, older than the polish on the stone. It is the same red as the tape of the 42nd podium, coiled in its box under Lot 714, the Eiter papers, and laid the way she lays everything: exactly, and without explanation. The catalogue advises bidders to leave the first day’s tape inside the back cover of Madame Ching alone; it does not say what to do about tape found on the rock, and I have decided not to, on the ground that a line that holds, holds. The day closed, as the almanac says, clear by evening. We brought the pad down the valley road with the chalk inside it, and it rained an hour later, which the rock had no part in.
