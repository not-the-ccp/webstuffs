Abberton Reservoir — the water, the dam, and the one line in the warden's log

M. Petherick · Layer de la Haye · post 1 · 3 May 2024, 06:41 · warden emeritus

Putting the facts on the bench for the summer visitors, because every May someone walks down to the sluice road asking the same questions. Abberton Reservoir is a pumped storage freshwater reservoir in eastern England, near the Essex coast. It covers 700 hectares — 1,700 acres of water under a dam, which makes it the largest body of freshwater in Essex, full stop. Most of the water is pumped up from the River Stour; the reservoir sits higher than the river it drinks from, and the pumping is the whole trick. It was constructed between 1935 and 1939, when the Essex and Suffolk Water people were still building things to last a century, and today it is owned and managed by Essex and Suffolk Water, part of the Northumbrian Water Group. The nearest postbox is the one at Layer de la Haye, and the reservoir lies 6 km (3.7 mi) south-west of Colchester.

The part I want on the record: in the Second World War the surface was mined to deter invading seaplanes, and it was the practice ground of the RAF's No. 617 Squadron, “The Dam Busters”, who ran their approach practice over the water before the bombing of the German dams in the Ruhr. Bombers, night work, the full formation, over our fish. And in the warden's fire-watch log, in the entry for the night of the ninth run, there is one garbled radio line transcribed in the clerk's hand, and the board has decided — minutes of 11 April, vote 5–2 — that it stands exactly as written. I will not paraphrase it here. Post 3 has it. Read the thread before you argue with the clerk.

Re: the pumping

G. Okonkwo · Colchester · post 2 · 3 May 2024, 07:12

Pumped storage, as Petherick says, means the Stour water is lifted, held, and released on demand — the reservoir is a battery wearing a mudbank. The Stour comes out of the High Weald hills and runs to the coast near Harwich, and the Stour line takes it, filters it through the chalk, and drives it up to the top of the reservoir. It is a long way of keeping Colchester and the coast supplied. Worth remembering when the mains run low: the water in the kettle climbed 700 hectares of hill to get there.

Re: the warden's log, the line in full

R. Fenton · clerk, Layer de la Haye branch · post 3 · 3 May 2024, 08:03 · transcriber of record

Transcribed from the fire-watch log, night of the ninth Squadron run, clerk's hand, single pencil, corrected nowhere. The entry concerns the bell on the sluice hut, the standing rule of the watch, and the radio set in the office the two of them used. The line as heard, and as it stands in the log:

The bell, the office of the two of them borrowed from the clerk's standing rule is on fire?

It has been asked, many times, what it means. It means the clerk wrote down what the night watch reported: the bell, and the office, and the standing rule — the one the two of them had borrowed from the clerk for the whole of the watch — and somewhere in the static the word fire came out of the set with the question mark still on it. No. 617 Squadron was over the water that night, and nothing in the hut actually burned. The board keeps it word for word because it is the only line in the log that the war left in our hand. If you are going to quote it, quote the whole of it, question mark included.

Re: the mines

S. D'Abruzzo · Maldon · post 4 · 4 May 2024, 19:48

Minor correction, respectfully: the mines were laid across the approaches to deter seaplanes, and the water was cleared for the Squadron's runs under escort, not after. The difference matters to the fishermen, who will tell you the 1943 spring run of roach was the best on record, and to the board, which has never found a reason to disbelieve the fishermen. There is a project on the table to increase the reservoir's capacity — higher bank, more pumping, more battery in the hill — and the fishermen have been asked to say what they think. What they think is in their own thread, and it is not kind.

Re: walking the perimeter

H. Whitlow · Halstead · post 5 · 5 May 2024, 11:27

For the summer walkers: the perimeter path is open, the sluice road is not, and the old practice line of the Squadron runs from the south-east shore roughly east-west, which is why the north shore is quiet at dawn and the south shore is never quiet at dawn. Six kilometres from Colchester is a pleasant morning out; take the water at the village taps, not at the reservoir, and mind the bank on the north side where the chalk shows through in dry summers. If you hear the bell on the sluice hut, do not go up and ask why. The standing rule says it is rung when the water rises, and the water rises for reasons the board does not discuss in public threads.
