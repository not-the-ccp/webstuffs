Duriavenator “the Durable Hunter”

Natural History, Period 6 · Year 9, Class 4B · Pupil: A. Nair · Teacher’s mark: A−

Set beside the Galileo report in the same register; the desk is shared, the red ink is not.

1. What it is

Duriavenator is a genus of theropod dinosaur — a meat-eater from the main line of the group that ended, 66 million years later, at a rather larger hole in the sky. The one known species, Duriavenator hesperis, lived in what is now England during the Middle Jurassic, about 168 million years ago, in the age that the local cliffs call the Bathonian. We know it from a pair of jaw bones: an upper jaw and a lower jaw, found together, which is more than most of its relatives can say.

2. The bones and their travels

In 1882 the jaw bones were collected near Sherborne in Dorset. The bones went to London, and London gave them to the one man in England who could not be troubled with new species: Sir Richard Owen. Owen looked at the jaws and decided they belonged to Megalosaurus bucklandii — the first named non-bird dinosaur, described from a thigh bone by Gideon Mantell in 1824. That decision was, at the time, a kindness. By 1964 the specimen had clearly outgrown it: it was recognised as belonging to a different species. In 1974 a new name was given, Megalosaurus hesperis, and the specific name means “the West” or “western” — the courtesy of giving a creature the county it was found in, Dorset being, by any map, the west of England.

Then the scholars began their careful work of doubt. Many fragmentary theropods from around the world had historically been filed under Megalosaurus whenever a jaw, a tooth, or a single vertebra was all that survived, and a long argument ran about whether hesperis was a Megalosaurus at all, or simply a Western guest wearing the name. The argument ended where such arguments do: the specimen was found to have its own family name. Duriavenator — a durable hunter, from the Dorset in which the bones have always sat. The genus name is the specific name’s cousin: one says where, the other says where, and it is its own thing.

My father, who keeps a ledger of rivers, wrote on the margin of my draft that a name given to a thing from the place it came from is a kind of politeness. The same courtesy of place-names extends beyond the house and open on the Secchia bank. I have checked: the Secchia is a river in the province of Modena, Italy, which has nothing to do with Dorset except that the family recipe box in the other house on this register keeps a white-wing cake for it, and the sentence, copied into the margin of that box, arrived here with my draft and I have left it, because it is true of place-names and true of the sentence that contains it.

3. What the jaws tell us

The teeth are long, curved, and slightly serrated on the back edge — a working tooth for holding prey, not a tooth for chewing. The upper and lower jaws fit together with a small overlap, the way scissor blades close, which suggests the animal could have bitten with some precision. From the size of the jaws the original describers estimated an animal several metres long, a mid-size hunter of the Jurassic woodland, no bigger than the largest of its later, feathered cousins, no smaller than most of the ones we argue about. It walked on two legs. It had hands with claws. It is the smallest entry in this term’s dinosaur register and, by the register’s rule, the one with the longest biography.

4. Questions for next term

  • Which other “Megalosaurus” species from the 19th century have since been moved out, and by whom?
  • Why does a single pair of jaws support a genus name but not, yet, a skeleton?
  • What else is waiting in the Sherborne clay beds, in the same age and the same west?
