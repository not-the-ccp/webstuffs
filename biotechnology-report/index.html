1. What biotechnology is

Biotechnology is a multidisciplinary field that involves the integration of natural sciences and engineering sciences to achieve the application of organisms and parts thereof for products and services. The term was first used by Károly Ereky in 1919 to refer to the production of products from raw materials with the aid of living organisms. Ereky imagined pigs fattened on sugar beet and cheese grown in vats; today the same idea runs through laboratories that measure liquids in millionths of a litre.

The core principle of biotechnology involves harnessing biological systems and organisms, such as bacteria, yeast, and plants, to perform specific tasks or produce valuable substances. A cell of Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a miniature brewery: it turns grain into beer and flour into bread. A bacterium can be persuaded to make human insulin. A plant can be taught to carry its own insecticide.

2. How it works

  • Fermentation. Louis Pasteur showed in the 1850s that fermentation is the work of living microbes. Breweries, bakeries and cheese dairies still run on this principle.
  • DNA and the gene. The structure of DNA was described in 1953; in 1973 Cohen and Boyer moved genes between bacteria, and in 1983 Kary Mullis invented PCR, which copies a fragment of DNA billions of times over.
  • Gene editing. CRISPR-Cas9, developed in 2012 by Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, cuts DNA at a chosen address and lets a cell repair it with a new sequence.
  • Gel electrophoresis. DNA fragments are pushed through an agarose gel at one hundred volts and separated by length, so each band can be seen and measured.

3. What it is used for

Biotechnology has a significant impact on many areas of society, from medicine to agriculture to environmental science. Synthetic insulin has been made by bacteria since 1982, and the hepatitis B vaccine is grown in yeast cells. In agriculture, Bt cotton carries a bacterial gene that kills bollworms, and golden rice was bred with beta-carotene in its grains. In environmental science, bacteria digest oil spills, and Ideonella sakaiensis, a bacterium found outside Osaka in 2016, eats PET plastic.

4. Field visit: the clean room

In April I visited my father's laboratory in Edinburgh's BioQuarter. Helix Nanotechnologies, which he co-founded in 2014, makes programmable molecular diagnostics; its first product was a CRISPR-based point-of-care platform for RNA virus detection, deployed across three continents during the 2026–2028 respiratory pandemic. He held seventeen patents. The room smells of isopropanol and hot plastic, and the bench rules are laminated: no open drinks, no pens outside the logbook.

His bench rules live in the lab archive as a chapter of its field manual, where the clean room is treated as a climate of its own, the instruments carry standing orders, and the white-wall fluorescent is, by house tradition, a harbour lamp . My father's own record — Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) — Obituary — tells how he paced that clean room dictating dialogue into a voice recorder while the sequencers spun.

My mother, Dr. Sanna Rajaniemi, is a computational biologist at the Roslin Institute. She says the genome is a text and we are only learning to read it.

5. Dream log

3 June 2029

I fell asleep over an electrophoresis gel and dreamt the lab archive. I wrote it down exactly as it arrived, because Dr. MacLeod says a dream written down is data:

In the dream stands in the lab archive as a radio play at Kingpin of the head, and the white-wall fluorescent is, by house tradition, a harbour lamp.

I think Kingpin of the head means the radio play my father kept clippings about — Joe Kelley, the Kingpin of the Orioles — and the harbour lamp is the clean-room light, which he called that because Edinburgh's docks lie downstream of the BioQuarter. The Dream Dictionary keeps its own season under the Millerntor floodlights — St. Pauli, seventeen home matches, a south stand singing one sustained chord from August to May — and I wonder whether a clean room is just another kind of stand: seventeen steps from bench to sequencer, and someone always running when the whistle blows.

6. Conclusion

Biotechnology changes what we eat, how we are healed, and how we clean up after ourselves. It began with Ereky's word in 1919 and it continues in vats, clean rooms and dream logs. The organisms do the work; we write the instructions.
