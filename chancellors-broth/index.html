Card No. 42 · The House Ledger of Methods

The Chaudrée de l’Hôpital

A Pigeon and Turnip Broth of the Chancellery Kitchen — set down from the card kept in the blue recipe box, in Aunt Éloise’s hand, with her preamble written out long, because she said it was the only part of it that mattered.

The Preamble, as set down before the cooking begins

The card is ruled in blue ink on the wrong side of a 1961 telephone directory page, and the preamble takes up four of its five lines. Aunt Éloise wrote: “You do not cook this until you have cooked the man first, and the man is Michel de l’Hôpital, called the Good Chancellor, born in 1506 at the house his father kept in the old parish, and dead at Paris on the 13th of March, 1573, at sixty-six, which is the exact age at which a broth like this stops improving and begins merely existing.” It is a fair rule for a stew, and I have never once disobeyed it.

Michel de l’Hôpital, or l’Hospital, as the card insists on spelling it in the first line and nowhere else, was a French lawyer, diplomat and chancellor who outlived the Italian Wars by exactly the right margin and spent the whole of the early French Wars of Religion trying to talk France down from the edge of the table. His father was a doctor, and a doctor in the service of Charles III, Duke of Bourbon, which meant that Michel’s early life was not spent in France at all but in exile, first at Bourbon’s court and then at the emperor’s, in the great household of Charles V, where a boy of a minor duke’s physician learned the size of the world the way you learn it from a servant’s staircase: by climbing it every morning. When his father afterwards entered the service of the House of Lorraine, the boy came under the wing of Charles, Cardinal of Lorraine, and it was in the Cardinal’s patronage network that a foreign-educated exile’s son was folded back into French affairs one favour at a time.

The favour that mattered was a marriage. Michel married Marie Morin, a woman whose dowry carried, among other things, a seat in the Parlement of Paris, and with the seat came the work of a man who signs the king’s anger. In 1544, over the surrender of Boulogne, he drew up the charges for the king against the defenders who had given the city up, and the card has a little star in the margin beside the word “charges,” because Aunt Éloise’s mother used to say that a man who can write a charge for a king can also write a recipe, since both require the whole truth about what happened in the pot. After Boulogne he took a role as a diplomat — treaties, embassies, the long carriage roads between courts — and the rest of the line ran as the card gives it: master of requests, keeper of the seals, and at last Chancellor of France, in which office he met the Wars of Religion with two edicts, the Edict of April 1563 and the Edict of Saint-Germain of 1570, which the card calls “the broth and the water, in that order,” and which held France together, however imperfectly, on the principle that two ways of praying might share one king and one pot. He was never a soldier, never a favourite of either party, and the card notes, in the star-ink of its last line, that the only battle he ever won was the one held in the Chancellery’s own courtyard in 1562, with a musket and a door, defending the house that held the seals — and that even that one he won by talking first and firing last.

That is the man. Aunt Éloise’s rule stands: cook the man, then the broth. Set the pot on the fire before you read the recipe, because the broth must be on the fire by the time you have finished, and the card itself closes with a line I have never been able to make up, which the family has therefore agreed is an instruction and must be obeyed whatever it means: “We ran the rifle pit and no one in which she proposed to bear.” The ruling of the house, given at the 1987 winter supper by the unanimous consent of nine eaters, is that it means the broth must be carried to the table in the copper before a single chair is pulled out, and the eldest woman of the house proposes the first course and bears it. We have never argued with a line we cannot read. It has never once been wrong.

The Cooking, Card No. 42

For one deep pot, serving eight at the long table
2pigeons or one plump poularde, singed and washed in cold water, joints of the poularde left on the bone
12small turnips, scrubbed, not peeled — the card says the peel is “the exile, and you keep him”
4quarts of cold water from a heavy kettle; salt to the taste of the parish
1bundle of savoury herbs: thyme, one bay leaf, and the parsley you have not been wasting
1head of garlic, unpeeled, split at the top
to ordera knob of butter for the bread, and the bread itself, torn, not cut
  1. Set the pot on the fire. Do not begin reading until it is set. The man is 1573, the pot is present.
  2. Put the bird in the cold water, turn up the fire, and draw the scum off as it rises, four times, because the charges of Boulogne were drawn in four articles and a broth, like a charge, improves with the scum taken off.
  3. When the water is clear, add the turnips, the herbs, and the garlic, lower the fire to the simmer a Chancellor keeps, and leave it to talk to itself for the whole of a long afternoon — no less than four hours, no more than the patience of one man who could hold two ways of praying in one room.
  4. Taste at the hour of the two edicts: once, at April 1563, once, at 1570. The second tasting must be better. If it is not, salt is the only argument permitted.
  5. Draw the pot from the fire. Wipe the lid. Run it to the table in the copper before a chair is moved.
  6. The eldest woman proposes the first course and bears it. The line keeps its line.

Kept in the same ledger

Card No. 41 of the house ledger, the Ribat Loaf, a date-and-almond loaf of the Almoravid dynasty, shares the same rule of the fire prepared for the entire session and never once stopped; and the kitchen two waters further down the road keeps its own dark card, the Siege Loaf, Card No. 7, built to the relay sheet of 9 August 1942. Both of them, like this broth, cook the man before the food. The ledger as a whole is kept on the front page, where the other things worth keeping live.
