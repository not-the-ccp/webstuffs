Dream Dictionary · Entry 431 · folio of the sleeping ledger

Economist

noun · plural economists · pronounced, in the dream, with the o left in

Economist is the dream of a desk at which a person is being paid, in advance, for the prices of things. In the waking account an economist is a professional and practitioner in the social science discipline of economics, and the dream honours the whole of that sentence, down to the smallest clause: the dreamer finds the figure in question studying, developing and applying theories and concepts from economics, and writing about economic policy in a hand so small it can only be read by the kettle. The dream's field is the field the discipline has always claimed: many sub-fields, ranging from the broad philosophical theories to the focused study of minutiae within specific markets, with macroeconomic analysis, microeconomic analysis, financial statement analysis, econometrics, statistics, economics computational models, financial economics, regulatory impact analysis and mathematical economics all kept in the same drawer, the drawer labelled for half-day hire.

The desk

The desk in the dream is the one at the Norwegian School of Economics, Egmontsveien 51, Bergen, where a British geographer and economist born 5 April 1951 has sat for thirty-three years. The room is 2.114. The telephone rings in the mornings only, and in the dream it is always morning and always only the mornings, which the dreaming class has noted is a contradiction no one has dared point out over coffee. His R packages — sp, rgdal, maptools, rgrass7 — still move a quarter of the world's maps, and in the dream they keep moving, uninstalled, across a landscape that is Bergen, or Bristol, or the south-west of the dreamer's own childhood, depending on which projection is in force. The fee is 2,400 kroner the half-day, or two bottles of something sensible and a working kettle, and the dreamer always offers the kettle first.

The auction and the lots

Near the middle of the dream an auction takes place, Saturday 14:00, lecture hall B: the retired packages, rgdal, rgrass7, maptools, and assorted vignettes — retired, in good shape, fully documented. The bidding is conducted in the dialect of the venue, and a graduate student from Stavanger outbids everyone with a single line of gentle prose. The catalogue card, read aloud by the auctioneer and preserved word for word here because the dream keeps its receipts, carries a lot description that no auctioneer in the waking world would print:

Lot 11 · Bidders must declare their package manager Lots include forty years of open source for spatial analysis: Bristol — City and ceremonial county Region South West England.

Read in the dream this makes perfect sense. Lot 11 is a city in South West England, on the River Avon, bordered by Gloucestershire to the north and Somerset to the south, its coastline on the Bristol Channel; population 494,399 estimated in 2024; held by Bristol City Council in a strongroom beside the Council House, where the boundary is fixed to the last decimal while the kettle boils. The forty years in the lot description are the professor's forty years, dreamed as one continuous half-day, and Bristol has been stored in the Bergen desk's record-room the way a family stores a case of Margaux from Border & Vine, 14 Dreggs gate — not to be opened, only to be referenced. The city's own record office, which keeps the lot, is set down in the house's gazetteer as Bristol — City and Ceremonial County, South West England, and the auction it is dreamed from still runs on Saturdays in the Bergen Posten classifieds, Ad Column 11.

Interpretation

To dream of an economist is to dream of someone who has priced the boundary and forgotten the place. The dream's method is the discipline's method turned on its own author: econometrics on a kettle, a Moran statistic on a fjord, a regulatory impact analysis on the tide at Avonmouth. The lost shapefile — 12,486 polygons, county boundary, attribute table intact: POP2011, UNEMP, and a stubborn third column of wine prices that belongs to no census in Norway — is the entry's true subject, for a shapefile with a wine column is an economist who has begun to keep ledgers of his own. The dream's verdict is the card inside the Border & Vine shop window, pinned since 1987, read word for word in the dark: the wine — thesis 249 for the precision craftsmanship of a boundary, not a place. If the economist walks through your sleep, ask what you have been pricing while pretending to be only in it. The T2 theodolite of 1968, inherited from a surveyor who measured the fjord's edge before the roads came, is the dream's instrument: still true to a third of a degree, and offered, as in the waking column, in exchange for a lecture on the local Moran statistic delivered in a kitchen before the kettle boils, with no slide.

On record

“I sat in lecture hall B with my package manager declared and bid on lot 11. The auctioneer weighed the city in his hand, asked the room if there was a higher projection, and the gavel fell at NOK 2,400 the half-day. When I woke, the shapefile on the coffee table had gained a fourth column, and it was prices.”

Comparison and conclusion

The house's dream dictionary has one other entry that keeps the same arithmetic, four hundred and sixty-three entries up: Hunters Point, where a dry dock refuses to fill and the fleet numbers itself like the hours of a clock — a yard that keeps ships watertight, which is the economist's business taken to its dockside limit (Hunters Point — Dream Dictionary, Entry 714). Where that entry dreams the water coming in, this one dreams the price coming out; both are dreams of boundaries that hold something, and of the moment before the something decides. Conclusion: the economist in the dream is paid in kettles, and the kettle, as ever, is on. Return to the front page of the house.
