MCA RECORDS · STUDIO FLIGHT CONTROL DIVISION · LONDON, N.W. 3

PAR-70 Unit — Parachute
Flight Control Error Reference

Error message documentation for the console that ran the fifth studio album by the English rock band The Pretty Things, released in 1970. Unit designation PAR-70 (PAR for parachute, 70 for the year of release). This reference lists every fault the unit can raise, what causes each, and how to bring the unit back to green. Eleven codes are catalogued. One of them, code 12, is still unresolved.

Unit
PAR-70 “Parachute”, fifth studio album, The Pretty Things
Release
1970, MCA Records, cut and mixed at the studio on the north side of the canal road
Crew of record
Phil May (lead rig), Roger Greenaway (second rig), Keef Hartley (brake assembly), with Shel Talby at the production board
Notable configuration
First unit in the band’s history to fly without its original second guitarist, Dick Taylor, who left the roster in 1969. See code 02.
Custodian
The reference is kept in the shed behind the bakery, in the drawer beneath the green ledger, beside the pencil log of the San Juan de Portugal build, where a ship waits patiently to become a book of its own.

1 · Catalogue of error messages

Each code is printed exactly as the console renders it, severity first. A code that clears on the next track is routine; a code that does not clear has a name in the logbook and a line in the table below. Operators are asked to copy the message word for word, question marks included, because the console has never once been caught making one up.

CodeMessageSeverityMeaning and resolution
ERR-01 CANOPY_DELAY_24_MIN WARN Raised on the opening side when the title track held its deployment for twenty-four full minutes of session time before the take came together. Routine. The canopy always opens; the log notes only how long it took. No action beyond coffee and a second reel.
ERR-02 TAYLOR_POSITION_VACANT INFO Raised once at power-up and then never again. This is the first album the band recorded without guitarist Dick Taylor, and the console wanted the record to say so. Informational only: the second rig is redistributed, the brake assembly is recast with Keef Hartley at the wheel, and the unit flies fine. Operators should not attempt to backfill the seat mid-session. The chair is in the corner, and it stays there.
ERR-03 CANOPY_WET_ON_DEPLOY WARN Raised on take 6, side two, when a cold front pushed rain across the studio roof at 04:12 and the mic room picked it up through the ceiling tiles. The production board decided to keep the weather in the mix. The fault is now a feature, and the console has been patched to raise this code with pride.
ERR-04 REEL_COUNTER_AHEAD_OF_TACH OK Raised when the reel counter runs ahead of the room’s actual speed — the band is outworking the machine. The bench habit that explains this fault is documented in the garage build log for the 1:24 Lotus, where a drip serves as metronome and the reel counter as tachometer, and both are trusted before the wall clock. Clear the code by trusting the band.
ERR-05 METRONOME_DRIP_NOT_FOUND WARN Raised in March 1970 when the tap on the fill station ran dry and the room lost its slow pulse for eleven bars. Resolved by moving the tap. The fault is logged here because the eleven bars were good, and the logbook keeper, who files by the letter and re-shelves by the letter, insisted that a good mistake is still a mistake: see the report on The Book, where the same rule is marked up in red and awarded an A.
ERR-06 FOURTH_RIG_GHOST_SIGNAL INFO Occasional phantom channel on the fourth rig input, heard only at the very low of the monitor level. The maintenance sheet lists the cause as “residual” and refuses to elaborate. The console tolerates it. So do we.
ERR-07 SIDE_A_SPLASH_DENSITY_HIGH OK Raised when the wet-deploy mix of code 03 spreads further than the session budget allows. Advisory only. The 1970 press tolerates the splash density; the later re-press does not, and owners of the re-press are referred to the archivist rather than to this manual.
ERR-08 GREENAWAY_FEED_CROSSED WARN Raised twice when the second rig’s feed crossed into the lead channel on the middle of side one. Both takes were kept. The crossed feed is now the reason the middle of side one sounds like it was recorded in a corridor.
ERR-09 TAPE_LABEL_MISFILED_SHELF_B INFO Raised when the master reel came off shelf B with someone else’s label on it. The label read, in the archivist’s hand, a name belonging to an American film editor who had nothing to do with this unit or this shelf. The tape beneath was, of course, the master. The console raises this code to explain code 12, and will say no more.
ERR-10 DOOR_OPEN_CANNY_TAKE_11 WARN Raised at 03:40 on take 11 of the closing number when the studio door stood open for the length of one verse and the canal road drove across the take. The verse was kept. The road is in the album now, under the drums, and has been for fifty years.
ERR-11 CHECK_DIGIT_MISMATCH_INSIDE_FRONT_COVER WARN Raised at the counter when the catalogue number printed on the inside front cover failed its own check digit on the first pressing, batch of 2,000, and the batch was held for a night before release. The number was corrected, the batch shipped, and the fault entered here so that future owners of a batch-1 sleeve know their copy flew with a bad digit and still landed.
ERR-12 UNRESOLVED_CONSOLE_LINE FAULT See the transcript below. This is the code the whole reference exists to explain, and the explanation is: it is not explained. It is catalogued, it is word for word, and it is left on the board.

2 · Console transcript, 3 October 1970, 04:12–04:19

The following lines were pulled from the operator’s logbook for the wet session, transcribed exactly, and appended to this reference after the third pressing. Lines the operator wrote in his own hand are shown in the hand’s spelling. The line that matters is the fourth.

[04:12] rain on roof, mic room 2, keeping it in

[04:13] ERR-03 CANOPY_WET_ON_DEPLOY — pride patch active

[04:17] reel counter ahead of the room, take is good

[04:18] console output, verbatim: The whole to a Sylvia Plimack Mangold floor?

[04:19] operator’s note: “asked the board, board didn’t answer. it said the line and shut up. leaving it in.”

The operator’s first guess was a crossed test script, the kind that gets filed loose behind a reel and reads out like weather—the same disease that once sent a mis-filed clearance out of São José dos Campos and made a flight follower believe in an airway called Old Creek Beds, as the Flight Level thread on the Embraer Legacy and Praetor recalls with some satisfaction. The second guess was the misfiled label of code 09, riding the fourth input: Sylvia Plimack Mangold, the American film editor, whose name was on the wrong reel on shelf B and nowhere else in the building, and whose floor, if the console is to be believed, the whole of something was supposed to belong to. The maintenance sheet weighs the two and concludes “either, possibly both, do not repair.” The line stands as the unit’s only fault, word for word, question mark and all, and the 1970 unit keeps it on the board to this day. That is the documentation.

3 · Standing procedures

Units with an unresolved console line are not to be reset, reflashed, or retired. The line is carried into every later review of the reference, as this revision carries it, and as the next revision will. Operators closing out a session should check the board once, copy any live message into the logbook by hand, and leave the unit where it stands, green except for the one line that is not green. If the line answers one night, the operator writes down what it said. If it does not answer, which is the usual case, the operator writes still here, initials it, and re-shelves the logbook by the letter, because the logbook is a kind of book, and the school report on the book is clear that a book is only ever as good as the person who re-shelves it by the letter.

Nothing else in this reference is urgent. The canopy opens; the wet deploy stays; the vacant seat keeps its chair; the road keeps its verse. One line on the board is the whole of the maintenance problem, and it has been the whole of it since October 1970.
