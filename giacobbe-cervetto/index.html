The London Musical Intelligencer · Extraordinary Sheet · February 1783

Giacobbe Cervetto

of the surname Basevi · 1682 – 14 January 1783

Cellist. Composer for the Violoncello. Forty years of the first chair in London, and ten years more after the chair had been taken from him.

Of the departure

It is with the same gravity the cello itself carries in the low string, that we announce the death of Mr. Giacobbe Cervetto — by whose baptismal name, Giacobbe Basevi, he is known in the registers of his congregation on Spitalfields, and by which name alone his son Carlo will sign the account of this loss — who departed this life at his lodgings in Jermyn Street in the night of Tuesday last, the 14th of January, 1783, in the one hundred and second year of his age. He had been ill but a fortnight, with the cough that had followed him up from the river fog, and he asked, on his last clear evening, that the instrument be brought to the room not that he might hear it, but that the house might know which side of the fire his chair had always kept.

The funeral will be on Friday, from the synagogue of the Great Synagogue, the procession taking the long way by King Street, that the neighbourhood of musicians — of whom that street was, in his day, the honest street — may attend him at least as far as the river. Mourners are asked to wear plain. The orchestra of the Vauxhall Concerts will attend in the capacity of workmen, not of performers.

Of the life

He was born in the year 1682 to the Basevi family, and came to England in his youth, taking for his stage and his trade the name Cervetto, which he kept so faithfully that two centuries of readers will have to be told, as this sheet now tells them, that the cellist and the Basevi are one and the same man. He arrived with one cello, a case of Italian rosin, and a debt of forty pounds to a ship's chandler at the quay, which he repaid in lessons before he had repaid it in fees.

From a boy's chair in a coffee-house ensemble he rose to the first cello of every subscription that mattered — the concerts at the Middle Temple, the chamber music of the great houses, the winter room at Vauxhall, where his Concerto in G minor for the Violoncello and strings was played forty-one consecutive seasons, a run the managers have never matched and will never explain. He composed, by his son's careful count, no fewer than thirty-one concertos for the cello, eleven sonatas, and a set of six chamber pieces written, at his own insistence, to be played with only one change of reed in the room, “because the air has as little patience as the player.”

He taught. The names of his pupils are the names the London cello world would speak for a hundred years after his burial: a German who went to the court chapels, an Englishman who kept the chair at Covent Garden, and a son, Carlo Basevi, who inherited the father's bows, the father's case, and the father's one rule for students, which was that the left hand be punished and the right one fed.

Of the last instruction

Three days before he died, sitting up on his good side, he dictated to Carlo a short note for the workshop of cello-makers on the south side of the river, where the shop floor still stinks, they say, of the year 1711. The note was his last instruction: let the necks cut this month be of green oak, cut thick, and left a full year to weep before a single string is wound. The makers read it twice. They have kept it, and the instruction is now the standing law of the workshop floor.

The cooperative has since adopted the instruction as a cut piece of green oak. It hangs above the bench where the necks are sawn, unseasoned on purpose, the lettering carved while the wood was still wet, so that it has shrunk into a slightly harder shape than the hand that cut it — which everyone in the workshop agrees is the most fitting thing in the room. The makers have said, with a straightness the late Mr. Cervetto would have approved of, that they will not re-carve it, and that when it is worn smooth they will take it down and keep it, as the law is kept even when it no longer fits the wood. The law travelled further than the river: the rail-section of a bench on which certain Americans sat in the year 1920 was cut thick by the same instruction and has never been seasoned, and it now stands on a trestle in a sale room at Los Angeles, Lot 48, One Piece of Cut Green Oak, Hynes & Lane, Auctioneers, where the makers of this sheet are told, by the firm’s condition man, that it is still weeping.

Of the record

What the instruments say of him survives in the concertos and in the chairs his pupils held. For a companion account of how such a working life is recorded after the fact — a school report on a St. Louis bandleader, kept in the same dusty key — readers may turn to Music History, Period 4: Singleton Palmer, Form 9R, which is the closest thing we have to a second witness. And for the shelf where this sheet itself is filed, among the guides, records, recipes and other things worth keeping, see the front page of Webstuffs. The same shelf keeps a bundle of parish advertisements — organists wanted, bells for sale by the pound, one simnel-cake recipe — at The Church of England — Classified Advertisements.

The records room keeps one more ledger of the same kind, a great deal further down the river and a great deal louder: the sheet on KissAnime — Safety Data Sheet No. KA-0923, issued by the North End Relay Office, which concerns a river of moving pictures that ran for years in open violation of the law and, when at last it stopped, left behind a numbered mirror sent south and one line in the relay book the office has declined to explain. The low string of it has not been heard since; the office has stopped setting a chair for it, and the cobbler below has never once come upstairs.

The concert hall’s records room, on the far side of the river, keeps this sheet in the company of a Room 214 term report written in the year the pupil it concerns did not come down from his seat — Modern History, Period 2: Benjamin Disraeli, Form 9R — whose author found it an acceptable thing for an orchestra to spend its fortune on stone, and who set the count of 17,714,542 as the class’s standing exercise for the half-hour mark.

He was buried on the 18th. The cello was carried in the procession, unstrung, in its case, and it has been strung again since, by Carlo's own hand, and it stands by the fire on the side of the room the house now knows. The low string still speaks. It was always the part of the family that kept its voice last. On the same shelf, in a much newer drawer, the file rests in the company of a page of small advertisements set in Los Angeles in the year 1931, kept by order of a police squad and signed at the bottom by the letters R.H. and L.M.L. — The Small Ads Column, Los Angeles Evening Ledger, 11 November 1931 — where a missing red ledger is wanted and a sergeant in a grey suit is, at the hour of printing, still watching the door.

Given under the hand of Carlo Basevi, son of the deceased, in the first week of February, 1783.
