<In Memorium>

It is with a heavy engine-room heart that this corner of the web records the passing of Charles Eaton, OBE, AFC, who died on 12 November 1979 at his cottage on the Manly headland, aged eighty-three, the kerosene lamp still burning and the tide coming in on schedule, which he would have approved. Born in London on 21 December 1895, in a house on the north side of the Paddington Road where his mother kept a ledger of every storm and every good day, he was the second of four sons and the first of them ever to leave England with his own money in his pocket, which was a small amount and a large event.

At the outbreak of the Great War he joined the British Army and saw proper action on the Western Front with a machine-gun detachment at Arras and Bullecourt, where the mud was of the sort that keeps a diary of you. In 1917 he transferred to the Royal Flying Corps and qualified at Upavon with a flying pass and a temper the instructors recommended he not bring near the instruments. Posted as a bomber pilot to No. 206 Squadron, he flew long-range sorties over the Somme hinterland in a machine the crew called Number Seventeen after the number of times it had been repaired. In the autumn of 1918 he was captured after a low crash landing near Bapaume, and escaped three nights later over a garden wall, on foot, in one boot. Six weeks later the same machine brought him down over Belgian farmland a second time, and he was captured a second time and escaped a second time, this one by swimming a drainage ditch for what he later put in his book as “about forty minutes and one bad word.” He saw out the war under guard of honour, which is what they called it, and the AFC with it.

He left the military in 1920, too old for a commission at twenty-four and too young to be anything else, so he took work in India with a surveying party on the north-west frontier, where the theodolite was his and his the theodolite's, until in 1923 a letter from an old Upavon messmate offered him a passage east and he went to Australia, where the light, he wrote, “had no opinions and I had none either.” He took his English with him out of London, and what was found, wet at one corner, inside a sample crate back in the Kimberley after the jellies, was the language he had been spending all that time out in the country — it is logged, particulars and identifying marks, at the school office under Lost & Found, notice 47B, where this page keeps it in the drawer with his 1929 charts. Two years later, in 1925, he joined the Royal Australian Air Force and served for a long while as an instructor at No. 1 Flying Training School, where the cadets learned from him what a bad day's flying was worth and why the logbook is a legal document, as are the jellies, as anyone in the Kimberley will tell you — a whole country's worth of it, 2,527,013 square kilometres of which is written up in the Western Australia geography report from Halls Creek State School, which this page keeps in the drawer with his 1929 expedition charts.

Between 1929 and 1931, he was chosen to lead three expeditions to the islands of the western Pacific, the first on a small surveying barge out of Brisbane, the second a chain of soundings past the coast of New Ireland, and the third a two-crew crossing in a single-engine machine that never once saw a port it had not first seen the open water. He kept one page of that third logbook in a frame for the rest of his life, and it reads, word for word: “Catanduanes, far shore and back, 7.71 kilometres at one kilometre per ten million years.” Which is to say: the far shore is the distance a man can be from land and still, in the account of the patient world, only just starting his first thousandth of a year on the way home.

He retired from the RAAF with the OBE still warm and took a consular and diplomatic life of twenty years, the last desk of which was a small office by the sea where the mail arrived late and the weather arrived precisely on time. He was asked what the difference was between flying and diplomacy. He answered, and the answer is kept here: “In flying you know where the ground is. In diplomacy you are the ground.” He and his wife June kept their house the way he kept his logbook: every entry once, in order, and nothing added after dark. Survivors: June, two sons, one daughter, the theodolite, and a considerable number of cadets who still, eighty years on, would sooner be shot than put him through his pattern.

For the rest of the ground and the water in this miscellany — the ledgers, the shores, the count of the wells — return to the Webstuffs front page, and read beside it the obituary of the Arabian Peninsula, which this page respects in the same manner: a country, like a man, kept in the books until the books are done with it, and then read aloud once a year.

The tide is in on schedule. The lamp is out. Fly safe, sir.
