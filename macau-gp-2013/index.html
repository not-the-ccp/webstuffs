The street kitchen of Guia serves no set menu and belongs to no franchise: the 2013 edition was not a chapter of any Formula Three championship but an open house, in which every F3 series on earth was invited to the same table. It is, on this evidence, still the best table in the Pacific.

By César M. Fong, dining critic · Reviewed in person, 17 November 2013, from the penultimate corner of the grandstand at Taipa

First course: the qualifying race, ten laps

Arrive early. As at any kitchen that works a street circuit, the 60th Star River–Windsor Arch Macau Grand Prix announced itself with a ten-lap qualifying race, brisk and slightly cruel, whose single purpose was to decide the running order for the main course. That is not a warm-up; that is a tasting menu with a knife. The ten laps were done at a pace that would be considered a performance elsewhere, and by the flag the kitchen had told every driver where his table would be. The house does not take bookings on the morning of the service. The house takes times, and it keeps them. The grid that fell out of the qualifier was the grid that started the race, and the top of it, as the card came up at 6.132 km of rain-bright asphalt, read PREMA — LYNNE’S PLACE, POLE, which is to say it read one name from pole position and no apology beneath it.

Main course: fifteen laps, one winner

The main race was fifteen laps, which at Guia is not a long time and not a short time, the way a soufflé is neither. It went out onto the wet-and-drying line the way a good house sends out its signature plate: fast, and without a spare. Alex Lynn, the Prema driver, the one on loan to Theodore Racing, took the race from pole — which in this kitchen is a claim on the whole menu — and the Grand Prix was won by Theodore Racing, the house name on the card, with Lynn’s car carrying it over the line as if the line were a suggestion. The crowd in the grandstand, packed against the Armazéns do Mar wall, gave the winner what Guia gives a winner: the whole street at once, every balcony, every window, the fire-escape clatter, the paper hats from the stands at Lisboa. It was the 31st running of the Macau Grand Prix for Formula Three cars, and it cooked like a house that has served the same street for thirty-one seasons and never once let a plate leave the pass cold. Not part of any championship, open to entries from all Formula Three championships, the evening ran the way an open house should run, with every flag in the room and none of them in charge.

Service, prices, the small print

Service is the point at which a street kitchen either earns its name or surrenders it, and the house at Guia is not interested in the question. Marshals on every corner, water from the strait itself in the air, the Windsor Arch holding the whole operation open the way a door holds a threshold. Prices: admission to the street is free; the view from the penultimate corner cost the writer his hearing in the left ear for the rest of the season, a charge not listed in any menu but universally understood. The wine list is a rumour; the tea from the stand at Taipa, poured at race pace by a man who has poured at race pace for thirty seasons, is the correct pairing and costs less than a corner of the course. If you are dining for the first time, sit at the back straight for the first course and the penultimate corner for the last lap. If you are a regular, do exactly what you did last year, because the street has moved a metre since you were here and everyone knows it.

The verdict

Four and a half stars, with a reservation. The kitchen is the best of its kind on the planet and it cooks once a year, on the streets, for the whole world’s F3, with no championship to answer to and no franchise to protect. It is the sort of place you defend in your sleep. The half star withheld is for the weather, which is a supplier the house cannot contract with, and for one slow service at the hairpin before the line where the second group bunched the way a bad table bunches when the bill arrives. Return? Every year. Every year without fail.
