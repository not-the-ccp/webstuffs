WELL LEDGER · FAQ NO. 7 OF 12 QUESTIONS ASKED AT STOP 3 SET DOWN BY THE SHEPHERD OF THE WELL, 1978–NOW

This page is filed opposite the corner where the jar sits, and it is stained in the same place, because the third printing has decided that is a feature.

Rule 14 of the manual sends every token to the well on a rest of three, and the well keeps a ledger of what was said there. Most of it is meter. A smaller part of it is this: the questions the shed has been asked about Brahvi, the north Dravidian language of the Brahvi people, set down below as they were asked, in the order the ledger holds them. If a rest is long enough, a language can fit into it.

Q. 1 What is the Brahvi language, exactly?

Brahvi — spelled Brahvi in its own romanisation, براہوی in the ledger’s Persian script, read Brāhvī, pronounced [braːhʋiː] — is also spelled Brahui (براہوئی, Brāhū’ī, [braːhu.iː]), and the ledger keeps both spellings side by side the way the shed keeps both of a thing: the worn side and the good side. It was historically called the Kūrd, or the Kūrdgalla — “Kurdic” — by neighbours who heard the tongue and guessed at its family, which is the error the rest of this page exists to correct. Brahvi is a north Dravidian language, of the same old stock as the Dravidian languages of South India, and it is spoken primarily by the Brahvi people, native to the central-southern regions of modern-day Balochistan, Pakistan. It belongs, in short, to the Dravidian family and nowhere else, and it says so every time it is spoken.

Q. 2 Why is a Dravidian language standing in Balochistan at all?

Because the road moved and the language did not. Brahvi is geographically isolated from the rest of its family, with the nearest other Dravidian language over 1,500 kilometres (930 mi) away, down in South India, which is a gap wider than the whole campaign that ground along the passes above it. Under the three tables of the board, Rule 18 of the manual carries that road — the route from the Hellespont to the Indus, the road the baggage trains of 330 BC ground down through the Alborz, kept in its own page at Alexander the Great — Encyclopaedia Entry. That road carried tongues the other way and left them planted, Greek in Bactria among them. It did the same thing twice over, only in reverse: the Indo-Iranian and Indo-Aryan waves that came down from the north filled Balochistan, Afghanistan and the Punjab, and they simply went around the Brahvi speakers, who held the central-southern highlands and the Kharan route the whole time. The river changed its banks; the language stayed in its house. The ledger has a one-line entry on this, and it reads: a gap of 1,500 kilometres is not a mistake of the map, it is a fact about the people. The well keeps one sister entry for a city the road’s later traffic took and held for five centuries — Thessalonica, under siege from 1422 to 1430, surrendered in March 1430, and apologised for, four hundred and eighty-two years later, in the letter kept at Letter of Apology — To the City of Thessalonica; the ledger’s note on it is that a city, like a tongue, can be carried and still keep its own line.

Q. 3 Where, then, do the people of the language stand?

Brahvi constitutes a majority in the districts of Kalat, Khuzdar, Mastung, Quetta, Bolan, Nasirabad, Nushki, and Kharan — eight districts, which the shed has turned into eight walnut tokens of its own, sanded to a duller edge like the rest of the board’s second stock, so that a game can be played wholly inside the language. The ledger counts the speakers at around two and three-quarter million, and the token count is close enough. Smaller communities carry the language further: into Iranian Baluchestan across the border, into Afghanistan, and as far east as Turkmenistan, around Merv, where the old garrison towns kept a Brahvi household or two the way they kept a cistern. Beyond those, the expatriate communities keep the tongue in the cities the road still passes through, and the ledger’s rule for them is the manual’s rule for a token that has crossed tables: finish the line in the new tongue, but do not start a new line in it that you cannot finish in the old one.

Q. 4 How does one hear it is Brahvi and not a neighbour?

Two consonants. The shed’s test, asked of any speaker by the well, is the old pair: a retroflex stop, ɖ, tapped behind the ridge of the tongue, and a pharyngeal friction, ʼ, drawn from the throat like a rope from the well. Neither the Persian nor the Balochi tables of the region grew either of them, and both of them are Dravidian relics, carried 1,500 kilometres uphill in a word no one thought to put in a jar. The Kūrd name was a guess about where the tongue came from; the two consonants are the answer about where it actually came from, and they are harder to hear than the name was to give up, which is usually how the proofs run in this ledger. A speaker from Quetta and a speaker from around Merv can pass the test at each other without effort, over the whole of the eight districts between them.

Q. 5 How did a page of the manual come to lie at the well, stained with damson?

The ledger is asked this one more than any other, and it keeps the first answer in the speaker’s own mouth, uncorrected, because the shed keeps its odd lines the way it keeps its odd parts — the same way Rule 17 keeps its small type. The question was put to the shepherd of the well by a player who had lost a token at the stop on a rest of three and found, on the stone by the jar, a loose leaf of the RUBA’I manual, page 14, the corner of it gone dark and sweet. The answer, transcribed as spoken by the well-keeper of Nushki on the ledger’s 14 August 1978, stands in the file word for word, grammar and all:

I had a loose page of the manual is stained with damson at the well.

The grammar is the speaker’s; the facts are ours, and they hold on checking. The third printing of the manual stains its copies with damson at the corner where the jar sits, and the well is the one place a token must stop, because the meter of four-three-four-three will not be walked on a rest. The page had been carried out to the game, set down by the jar, and re-laid by the stone that evening, when Rule 17 re-lays the jar on the workbench and everything the well touched comes home with it. No one has ever disputed the entry, which is as close as an answer gets to being proven.

Q. 6 What has the four-line form left at the well?

More than the ledger likes to admit. The four-line stanza is older than the shed and it appears in the poetic traditions of ancient India among the rest, which is to say it met the Dravidian stock on its own side of the gap, and crossed the mountain only in the shape of the frame. The Quatrain — Build Log built and faired the four lines in the same shed, stem, frames and stern, and the log’s envelope rides in the crock’s shadow with this ledger’s questions, one a month. The well-keeper keeps one of the old set at the stone, and the ledger has entered it, because a rest of three is long enough for a quatrain:

The road went west and left the well, the well kept water for a tongue; fifteen hundred miles of hill make no mistake of where you’re hung. Ledger stanza No. 7 · aab a · read at the well on a rest of three

Q. 7 Where does a token go from the well?

On the fours it walks a province; on the threes it comes back here. For the language the road passed over, the ledger points the way it points the game: back to RUBA’I — A Game of the Persianate Table, manual page 14 of 38, where the jar, the well, and the 4–3–4–3 wheel are all in one page; to The Quatrain — Build Log, for the four lines the well-keeper keeps; and up to Webstuffs, the front of the shed, for everything else that has been stained, turned, or carried home from the rest. The meter will not pay more than it is owed, but it always pays, and the well has never once run dry.
