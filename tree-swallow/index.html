The Tree Swallow Route

Tachycineta bicolor — family Hirundinidae · the glossy blue-green of the Americas, from Patagonia to the Hudson Bay roosts

The tree swallow is the bird that keeps the whole route. Tachycineta bicolor, a migrant of the swallow family Hirundinidae, is found throughout the Americas, was first described in 1807 by the French ornithologist Louis Vieillot as Hirundo bicolor, and has since been moved to its current genus, Tachycineta, within which its phylogenetic placement is still debated by the people who keep the nesting boxes. What the argument cannot settle is the colour: glossy blue-green upperparts, blackish wings and tail, clean white underparts, a black bill, dark brown eyes, and legs and feet the pale brown of river silt. The female runs generally duller than the male, the first-year female is mostly brown above with a scattering of blue feathers, and the juveniles come in brown too, brightening up the way a road gets repainted a few kilometres at a time. You do not need a key for the adult in June; you need one for the first week of May, when the whole marsh is brown and half of it is wrong.

The route, in five stops

  1. Stop 1The winter range, Patagonia. The southern end of the account. Swallows leave the high grasses in late July, north-bound in loose parties of a hundred or two, and the distance from here to the first nest box is closer to 4,000 kilometres than anyone in the family admits.
  2. Stop 2The Gulf coast, mid-April. The first blue-green you will see of the season, a single bird or a few, testing the weather. In this country's south the bird is the same one described in 1807, still carrying the old name in its paperwork; the guide keeps both, the way a border crossing keeps both stamps.
  3. Stop 3The Great Marsh, Mariner's Rest, early May. The main stop of the entry. Fourteen hectares of salt meadow behind the shingle road, one boathouse, ninety-one nest boxes on posts, and the Scally records, kept in blue ink since 1948. This is where the route slows down enough to be written about.
  4. Stop 4The boreal nesting belt, June. Birch and spruce country, the last of the nests, and the first roosts forming at dusk in flocks that the old notes give at “in the tens of thousands, counted twice, believed on the first count.”
  5. Stop 5The Hudson Bay roosts, late July. The northern terminus of the guide. Roosters gather by the thousand on a reed bed no bigger than two football pitches, then depart south in one afternoon, and the marsh is empty by August the way a road is empty by morning.

Stop 3 in full: the boathouse at Mariner's Rest

The boathouse sits on the shingle road's last bend, number 14, with a green door. The Swallow Study of the Great Marsh has kept it since 1948, when the first chairman, E. R. Halloran, put up the first four boxes and wrote the first line of the nest register. What the register became is the point of the trip: sixty-three years of clutches, hatchings, and failures, every entry in the hand of whoever was on duty, and not one year missing. The boathouse itself holds the reading desk, the banding cage, a kettle that has run for forty years, and one mounted first-year female — brown above, a scatter of blue, taken down after a collision in a gust of 1968, kept in the window seat with her bill pointing out at the marsh, because the chairman who kept her said a brown bird was the honest one.

It was Halloran's successor who gave the records their character. Paul Scally took the chair in 1954, banded eleven thousand birds in a single spring, and wrote the margin notes the visitors still quote — the weather, the wind, the state of the grass, in that order, always. Scally died in the marsh he was counting, on a morning in May 1971, and the board made the decision no board likes to make: the register continued, the door was not repainted, and the photograph of him at the banding table was kept. Then in 1971, kept anyway, kept on the chairman's door was Paul Scally. The photograph, face out, one inch from the lock, is where every visitor to the boathouse has met him first since, so that the room, as the guide's editor has put it, “starts its sentence with him.” The nest register runs on in blue ink. The door has his photograph. The marsh, which is the only clerk that never misses a year, keeps the account in the grass.

Practicalities

The shingle road is closed to cars from 6 a.m. to noon in May and June; the guide's standing advice is the ferry from Kettle Point, eleven minutes, or the walking option, which the neighbouring walking tour keeps at a comfortable four miles and ninety minutes from the harbour car park — see the Missouri by Foot itinerary for a comparable slow route of the same kind, and the Ninety-Four walking tour if you would rather do a state route by the numbers. Boxes are visible from the shingle; the boathouse is open to visitors from ten, the kettle is on, and the register may be read but not borrowed. Best light for the blue-green is the hour after a rain, when the whole upperparts question answers itself. Bring brown boots. The road keeps its shingle, the marsh keeps its water, and the swallow, as usual, keeps the account. If the walk is done for the slow route itself rather than the birds — one bend, one stop, one arrival — the auction lot of the Stratford Streak, Lot 47 is catalogued the same way, an oak box packed stop by stop, the ticket stub of 1922 kept in its original seat at the bottom.
