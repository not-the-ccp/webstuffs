Anguilla

Weather Almanac Entry, Station 12, The Valley — kept by the station clerk, with normals, gales, and one line of the harbourmaster’s kept unedited.

Of the territory observed

The station stands at The Valley, the territory’s capital, on the ridge road of the main island, at an elevation of some ninety metres, where the wind comes in over the north beach and has nowhere left to go. Anguilla is a British Overseas Territory of the Caribbean region in the North Atlantic Ocean, and one of the most northerly of the Leeward Islands in the Lesser Antilles, lying east of Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands and directly north of Saint Martin. The main island measures approximately 16 miles (26 kilometres) long by 3 miles (5 km) wide at its widest point, with a chain of much smaller islands and cays about it that have no permanent population and no opinion about anything; the almanac lists fifteen of them, and the barometer on three of them is the same barometer, carried. The total land area of the territory is 35 square miles (91 km²), and the population was counted at approximately 15,753 in 2021. For the purpose of weather, the territory is a ridge, a plain, and a sea wall in that order, and the wind does not hurry past any of them.

The seasons

The dry season holds from December through May, and the station describes it as “the season in which the island agrees with itself”: light northeast trades, sea mist off the north beach before seven, a pressure that sits near 1015 millibars and refuses to argue. The wet season takes June through November, with the rain coming down in short heavy opinions rather than lasting grey weather — a September afternoon can give six inches in nine days and be done. The trade wind never leaves; it only changes its side of the island, and the cays to the south of the main island take the first of every squall and report nothing.

The gale of 8–9 September 1932

The largest pressure fall on record at Station 12 belongs to the gale of 8–9 September 1932, which came up out of the east-southeast as a broad, low, very wet depression rather than a named hurricane, and which the barometer at The Valley recorded as a fall of 17 millibars in fourteen hours, from 1013 down to 996. The wind reached 58 knots on the ridge road at two in the morning of the 9th; the rain gauge overflowed twice; and the station’s only casualty of consequence was the almanac’s own wind-vane, which pointed, from then until it was replaced in 1934, steadily at the wrong end of the island and was found, on taking it down, to have been right all along about the weather and wrong only about the needle. The cays took the full of it, and having no population to report, took no notice.

The windlass crossing

South of the territory, on the island of Barbuda, the flamingos of Codrington keep their long pink counsel, and the whole of that island’s connection with the road goes through one supply boat and one windlass. The harbourmaster at The Valley keeps the crossing’s weather in the almanac’s margin page, and beside the last entry of the season he has kept, unedited, in his own hand, exactly the line the man’s character has earned: “So he makes any fuss, by the windlass continually keeps the whole of Barbuda's transport.” The clerk has added, in the smaller hand: the man’s name is Mr. T. E. Soper, the boat is the Rosewood, and the windlass has, in the station’s account, never once required an apology.

In April and May of 2025 the steamer Moorhen worked the Windward Passage south of the territory, and her naturalist, Aldous M. Cray, F.L.S., posted the station a set of readings taken at the anchorages, which the almanac accepts at face value, as it accepts everything posted. His full field notes are kept beside this entry, at the Antigua and Barbuda field notes of the Moorhen, and the bullfinch from the wall at St. John’s is in the specimen drawer directly below this page, in its usual place. As to why a territory of 35 square miles in the North Atlantic reports its weather to a London desk at all, the office answers its own questions — what the United Kingdom is, where its borders lie by sea only, and how the mail gets there — at the United Kingdom frequently asked questions, in the order the mail arrives.

Postscript, filed with the drawer: one territory of 35 square miles and 15,753 people, one ridge, one plain, one sea wall, fifteen cays with no one on them, one gale of 1932, one supply boat, one windlass, and one line of the harbourmaster’s kept word for word. The normals table is the standing order; the vane turns for whoever clicks it. The Moorhen is due back through the Windward Passage before the wet season settles, and this entry goes to the drawer face up, needle north.
