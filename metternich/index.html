The Continental Fauna Survey · Hadramaut Station · Field Season 1849–1850 · Notebook No. 12

Princes metternichi

Common names: Klemens von Metternich; the Prince; the old bird at Schönbrunn. · Full binomen of record: Klemens Wenzel Nepomuk Lothar, Prince of Metternich-Winneburg zu Beilstein, 15 May 1773 – 11 June 1859, aged 86, the longest-lived specimen the desk has ever filed.

I. Station and weather

First light over the wadi, 3rd of the dry season. The wind comes off the escarpment with the smell of cumin and cold stone, and I am writing on the terrace of the family’s winter ground, where the Metternichs keep a low stone house for the months the Vienna court turns away from them. The valley runs east toward the sea, seven hundred and twenty kilometres of it, and no passport officer in the Empire has ever refused the household an entry here, which I note for the record: a creature of their size carries its own border.

The family is in the courtyard now. The father of the present generation feeds dates to the parrots from the lower wall, and the child with the brass telescope is being corrected by an uncle about the bearing of the meridian. The family's small telescope points at Wadi Hadramawt's sky. I asked leave, and they gave it gladly; it is a three-inch objective, hand-ground, with a finder ring worn soft by one thumb in particular. I will say more on that below.

II. Description of the species

Princes metternichi is a large, sedentary statesman of the Austrian Empire, German by birth and Vienna by long residence. The adult stands tall, wears the evening coat to breakfast, and moves through a room the way a heron moves through reeds — slowly, with the whole body already decided. The voice is quiet and famous; the pen is faster than the voice and twice as cruel. Colour above: dark, unchanging, the same coat for forty years. Colour below: pale, with the family arms of Metternich-Winneburg, the small lion of the House of Winneburg in the first quarter. The ears are excellent — they heard the sound a treaty makes when it is about to tear, and no ear in Europe was better — and I file the species deliberately beside the brown long-eared bat of the Cocking bore, another long-eared, low-voiced animal that keeps its ledge and lets everything else cross the valley.

Diet: ambassadors, ministers, gazettes, and — in great quantities — other people’s drafts of letters. The stomach is not the notable organ; the liver is. One specimen, observed at Strasbourg and Mainz in the 1780s and later at the court, digested the French Revolution for twenty years without a symptom.

III. The territory: Wahlenstein and Schönbrunn

Born 15 May 1773 at Wahlenstein, in the county of Rhaupach, son of a diplomat named Klemens von Metternich — the father, whom the household still keeps a portrait of on the long wall — and educated in the order of Strasbourg then Mainz, the bird of these notes entered the Austrian service young and stayed in it for life, which is the first fact of its biology. From 1809 it was foreign minister; from 1821 chancellor; and it held that territory until the spring of 1848, when the revolutions came up the street like a flood and the specimen, in its eighty-fifth year, walked out of the palace at dawn with a letter of resignation under its arm and was seen last, by the stablemaster, getting onto a southbound omnibus without an umbrella.

Three decades as the centre of the Concert of Europe. That is what the papers call it; I call it flocking. The great powers of the continent assembled at congress — Vienna, Aix-la-Chapelle, Troppau, Laibach, Verona — and met, and spoke, and dispersed without a war, the way starlings hold a murmuration over a roost. The specimen was the roost. When the roost left in March 1848, the flock did not break, but it never once found the shape again.

IV. Night observation through the small telescope

After supper I took the instrument, as promised. I must confess the evening was not spent on the wadi. I pointed the three-inch at the north, toward the latitude of the Empire, and worked through the list the family keeps for the season — the old household star list, the one in the red cover, which is a list of the powers of Europe set down as constellations, one star per capital, and I say this plainly because the list is a fact of the house and the house is a fact of the wadi. Austria held the centre of the field; Prussia and France stood either side of it, low and bright; Britain was the faint, distant star everyone kept losing in the upper haze; Russia was the one that rose late and stood very still.

It was, I think, the instrument’s finest hour. A small telescope, hand-ground, in a valley seven hundred kilometres from the nearest chancellery, showing the same figure the specimen had drawn with letters and dinners and one very long pencil — the balance, held. The child came back at midnight to reclaim it, said one polite word in German, and took the eyepiece without complaint. I will note in the survey’s ledger that the specimen, now in its seventy-seventh year at this writing, was asleep in the low stone house and had dreamed, by the family’s reckoning, of the Congress of Vienna; the uncle said this at breakfast, and no one contradicted him, which is the only way it could have been said.

V. Departure and last entry

Twelve months on, 11 June 1859, the word comes down from Vienna with the mail: the specimen is dead, at its house on the Prater, aged 86, and the court has ordered the black. The family’s winter ground keeps the black on the long wall, the portrait of the father still over it. The telescope has been oiled, the eyepiece cleaned, and the red-cover list put in its drawer.

I leave the valley on the south road tomorrow. In the ledger I enter the species thus: sedentary, long-lived, well-fed on other people’s letters; one territory, one flock, one balance held for thirty years with a pencil and a voice that never raised; and one small telescope in the wadi, pointing, night after night, at the sky it once kept. Princes metternichi: the desk files it under the longest-kept ledges, in red ink, and leaves it alone. A note to the survey’s readers: the same hand that counted this specimen once counted the presence of a stage family held for thirty years under one surname, and that record stands in the patent office’s file — see the Specification No. 85–0023, examiner’s copy — while the survey’s own front bench and station list are kept at the head of the Continental Fauna Survey.

Margin, in a later hand: the species is not to be confused with its son, Richard, a good bird but of the next season. · Ears, as in the Cocking bore animal, are the diagnostic. · The balance is not a personality. It is a posture held so long the bones take its shape. · Vienna, 1814–1822: the flock in full figure. ·
