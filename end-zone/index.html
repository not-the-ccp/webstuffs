Facilities Route No. 4 · After-Dark Permit · Cedar Rapids, Iowa

The End Zone

A self-guided walking tour in eight stops, surveyed after the 6 p.m. game of 14 November 2024, when the stands were empty and the lines were still warm.

Guide of record: W. R. Ashcom, Facilities Cartographer · Total route: 318 metres plus one item by mail · First stop at seat 45A, west stand · The route begins where the privilege hearing put its numbers on the map, and it ends where a raking rake put a blue sheet in a drawer.

Stop 1 · 0 m · West end line

The End Line

Meet the route at the end line, the last straight of the field and the boundary that the rest of this tour will spend the evening explaining. The end zone is the scoring area of the field under the gridiron-based codes of football: the area between the end line and the goal line, bounded by the sidelines. There are two of them, one at each end of the field, each on the opposite side, and each of them a place where the clock stops caring. Walk to the chalk and you will feel the difference in the concrete a half-yard in. The zone is the field’s last sentence.

Stop 2 · 40 m · West stand, row 45

Seat 45A

The route climbs the west stand to seat 45A, the seat where Cam Buffington once sat as a recruit before he wore the number himself. With 3:17 left in the second quarter of the 14 November game, he left this field on a stretcher, and on 22 November his lawyers stood in the courthouse with the In re Buffington transcript beside them and said the sentence this tour is still chewing over: the public interest is not a waiver. From 45A the end zone looks exactly the size of a decision that belongs to one person. Take in the view; the sealed records in the annex are the price of the view.

Stop 3 · 88 m · Perimeter, all four sides

The White Line

Walk the perimeter. The end zone is bordered on all sides by a white line indicating its beginning and end points, and that white line is the whole law of the boundary: where it is, the zone is; where it is not, the ball is dead. Facilities repainted the line on 29 October, and the fresh paint still smells of turpentine at dusk. The tour file stamps this stop the whites, three weeks on, because by the day the route is walked the line will have aged exactly three weeks, which matters to one marginal note at the end of the itinerary and to no one else, but the survey keeps every such number.

Stop 4 · 131 m · Four corners

The Orange Pylons

At each of the four corners of the zone stands an orange, square pylon, a visual aid to players and to the broadcast, its flat side to the sideline, its tip two feet off the concrete. Stand between two of them and you are inside the geometry that a field goal must thread. The survey notes a fact the pylons themselves will not volunteer: prior to around the early 1970s, flags were used instead to denote the end zone, white cloth on wooden poles that the wind kept straightening back out. The square replaced the waving. Nothing on this tour is less ambiguous than the pylon, and that is why it is orange.

Stop 5 · 176 m · East end, crosswalk

Goal Area and Dead Line

Cross to the east end and change your dictionary. The Canadian rule books use the terms goal area and dead line instead of end zone and end line, and a man from Toronto can say dead line to the end line for two seasons without once meaning a deadline, which is the kind of error that costs a crossing of the border a whole sentence. The latter terms are the more common in colloquial Canadian English anyway; the rule books keep their spelling, the crowds keep theirs, and the line on the ground keeps both. If you arrived from the U Sports schedule, you are already speaking the east end’s language.

Stop 6 · 240 m · Registry annex

Locker 7B

The route passes the registry annex, where the sealed records of the Buffington matter rest in Locker 7B until further order, catalogued communication by communication, no blanket assertions admitted. The tour does not open the locker. It stands in front of it for as long as the lock takes, which is a full count of ten, and it notes the rule the lock enforces: the zone can be described by anyone, but what is kept inside a boundary belongs only to the person whose decision the boundary is. Provenance first, character second, as the judge said on the record.

Stop 7 · 318 m · Goal line, east

The Scoring Area

Finish the metres at the goal line, where the end zone earns its name. It is a scoring area, not a decoration: a ball that crosses the goal line inside this boundary has carried the season a further step, and the pylon pair at its corners exists so that everyone in the building, including the camera, knows exactly where the carrying stopped. The survey stops here and turns the page, because the last stop of Route No. 4 is not a place on this field. It is a footpath, and it is four thousand miles away, and it is reached the only way a footpath that matters is reached: by mail.

Stop 8 · by mail · Mount Lofty Road, the Adelaide Hills

The Footpath Behind the House

The itinerary’s last stop is a weatherboard house on Mount Lofty Road, half a mile from a gate, where in September 2003 a woman raked a blue fixtures sheet out from under a gum leaf and kept it in a drawer between the tea tin and the letter box key for sixteen years. The In re Hatherell record carries her account in her own voice; the tour file carries one further line that the account never used, signed E. M. C., in the same unhurried hand that wrote the tick: I was raking the footpath behind the house before the whites of 3 weeks, if your acquaintance is not a waiver. The survey parses it, post by post, the way the registry parses a sealed record. The whites of 3 weeks are the end zone lines of Stop 3, repainted 29 October and three weeks on by the day the route is walked: the raking happened in the time before the repainting, in the week when the line was still last year’s line, and so the finding, wherever it was made, belongs to an older boundary. And if your acquaintance is not a waiver is the hearing’s stipulation carried across the map: knowing a name, a hand, a player in a public uniform, is not the same as holding the right that the name carries; acquaintance does not waive, and neither does a footpath. The tour ends where it began, at a boundary, and it closes with the note folded twice, the blue side down.
