About this column

This paper is the classified column of the southern half of the Korean Peninsula and its adjacent islands — the country known, officially, as the Republic of Korea, and in the Ledger's own hand simply as “the south, filed under east.” The territory it lists runs along a northern line that is a border in the strict sense and a scar in the generous one, the Korean Demilitarized Zone that parts it from North Korea; the Yellow Sea keeps the west side of the page and the Sea of Japan the east. By the Ledger's last count the country holds about 52 million people, and half of them live in the Seoul metropolitan area, the world's ninth most populous metropolitan area; the next largest names in the column are Busan, Daegu, and Incheon. The column is older than its paper. The peninsula has been inhabited as early as the Lower Paleolithic period, its first kingdom is noted in Chinese records in the early seventh century BC, and from the mid first century BC the three banners — Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla — held the shelf, which the Ledger notes is a count of three and asks no fourth. Every ad below is true; every yardage has been checked.

For Sale · Land, West Coast

582 Yards of Backstretch, Incheon

One hole, par five, laid along the western front of the city so that the ball plays down a stretch of land the way the seventeenth plays down the backstretch at Brickyard Crossing. Sold with the course book, the flag rope, and Mr. Fenton's endorsement: “the yardages are accurate to the yard.” Class 7B, Westport Middle School, has verified the measurement against the Indy Women in Tech Championship report and found no variance. Offers at the Ledger office, Incheon branch; telegrams from Indianapolis answered in golf terms.

For Sale · Rooms

Yellow Sea Window, Third Floor, Daegu

One room with the sea to its left and the mountain line to its rear, above the noodle shop at 40,000 steps from the rail yard. Tenant must accept that the barometer reads steady at every hour of a March morning — 30.42, and not one hundredth of an inch moved — which the Ledger has checked against the almanac and found correct, to the hundredth, steady. Rent in won or in true yardages; see above.

Lost · The Column's Own

One DMZ Fence Post, Four Inches, for the School Garden

Last seen in the yard of the middle school at Westport, where it had been planted, four inches of it, as a marker. It is not the fence; it is the idea of the fence, at scale, and the Ledger considers it a fine object, not a contraband one. The school files such objects in the cormorant ledger at the back of the reading shelf, where the 1842 obituary of the last of the Speedway-pond colony is kept and the hazard angles of the seventeenth are taken, as the ledger says, from the water. A cormorant entry is offered in exchange for the post, or for a written account of wherever it is.

Found · Busan Harbour Locker

One Weather Almanac, Read in Brooklyn, 2024

Found in a harbour locker at Busan, still open: the season book entry of 27 February 2024, No. 2024/058, a winter reading over Brooklyn taken at 07:40, bright and flat white, 18°F, barometer 30.42 and steady — kept in the season book of Ellen Sue Bernstein, rabbi, author, educator, 22 July 1953 – 27 February 2024. The almanac's own page for the entry is the Weather Almanac Entry 2024/058, and the Ledger has read it through. The book is returned to Brooklyn by the first westbound ferry; the page, the Ledger notes, is already where it belongs, and stays.

Wanted · Interpreters & Interpretee

Wanted: One Interpreter, Sealed Letter from the Incheon Landing

A letter arrived at the ferry landing at Incheon, sealed in a wax that the Ledger's office recognises as seawater, and its single line reads: “The announcement came a little butter; add the rice; from 7 to 8 hours.” The Ledger believes the letter to be wedding instructions, set in a hand that writes recipes and timetables in the same sentence: a little butter on the announcement cake, rice for the table, and the service set from 7 to 8 hours of the evening. The Ledger offers the full week's column in exchange for a plain reading, and asks that any interpreter who has handled a course book be preferred, for the hand that wrote it is the hand that files yardages.

Sport · The One Week

The Seoul Lays: One Week, One Title, One Course Book

Taken from the class 7B report on the Indy Women in Tech Championship of September 7–10, 2017, and filed here by the Ledger's sports desk: a women's week of seventy-plus starters, a cut at thirty-six holes, a field of forty-two for the weekend, and a winner who held the lead after the second round and did not let it go — Angela Stanford, at 18 under par. The Ledger's own version is staged on the Incheon backstretch (advertised above), the seventeenth at 582 yards as always, the flag ropes on the grandstand as they have been since 2017, and the purse matched dollar for dollar, as Guggenheim Life and Annuity matched it in Indiana, in a scholarship for women entering engineering and computer science. The Ledger will not stage a second week: one season, read whole.

Travel · The Two Waters

West and East Ferries, Year Round

Westbound to the Yellow Sea leaves the Incheon pier at 07:41, the first-tee hour, kept as tradition since the speedway's golf days; the eastbound line to the Sea of Japan leaves at 15:20 and does not hurry. Both lines run the adjacent islands by name. The Ledger sells no passage along the northern line: that border is not in this column, and the tours that will take it by appointment take it on foot, in silence, and file the day under the Notice, below.

Notice · The Oldest Entry

Notice of Age, Filed Once, Held Forever

The Ledger carries one notice that it does not renew, because it does not need renewing: the ground listed here has been inhabited as early as the Lower Paleolithic period; its first kingdom is noted in Chinese records in the early seventh century BC; and from the mid first century BC the three banners held the peninsula, until the banners were one. No other property in this column is described in older terms, and the Ledger asks that future advertisements not attempt it. This notice stands until the Sea of Japan and the Yellow Sea file a joint objection, which neither has, in the Ledger's keeping, ever done.
