SEABIRDS TRIBUNAL
OF THE NORTH ATLANTIC

First sitting · Chambers at the Westman Dock · Heimaey, Vestmannaeyjar, Iceland

Case No. SEA-1997/014

In re the Decline of the Atlantic Puffin (Fratercula arctica)

Held 3 March 1997, 10:04 a.m. – 12:41 p.m.
Record opened by order of the bench.
Transcript set by the clerk’s office, ink and corrections in the margin.

Before: Judge Sigrid Halldórsdóttir, presiding. Panel of seven. Senior registrars Muriel and Reyes of the record office, abstaining rights noted.

For the colony: T. Vík, of the cliffs. For the fishing interest: M.Ó. Skaalæ.

The Clerk: In re the Decline of the Atlantic Puffin (Fratercula arctica), Case No. SEA-1997/014. The Seabirds Tribunal of the North Atlantic in first sitting. Judge Halldórsdóttir in the chair. The record is open.

The Chair: The court takes up the species record. The subject before the court, as the clerk will have seen, is the Atlantic puffin, Fratercula arctica, also known as the common puffin, a species of seabird in the auk family. It is the only puffin native to the Atlantic Ocean. The two related species, the tufted puffin and the horned puffin, are found in the northeastern Pacific, and their depositions are not before this court, though they are noted for the record as heard nowhere, in the way the Pacific hears things.

The Clerk: Noted.

The Chair: The witness.

Examination of Dr. Elias Fennick, warden, Westman Islands colonies

Counsel: Dr. Fennick, you have held the warden’s post at Heimaey since 1974.

Witness: Since 1974, yes. Twenty-three seasons.

Counsel: And in your first season, how many birds did you count in the burrow lines of the Westman Islands?

Witness: Twenty thousand, four hundred and twelve pairs, over the three cliffs. I keep the book. The book keeps its own weather.

Counsel: And this season’s count?

Witness: Nine thousand, eight hundred and sixty pairs. The count is closed, and the count is closed.

Counsel: The court will have the distribution. The species breeds in Russia, Iceland, Ireland, Britain, Norway, Greenland, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, and the Faroe Islands, and as far south as Maine in the west and France, in Brittany. Correct?

Witness: Correct. And it is most commonly found here, in the Westman Islands, which is why this court sits in a dock instead of a room.

Counsel: A large population, then, and a wide range.

Witness: Yes. And yet the court has the table in front of it: the decline is rapid, at least in parts of the range. I have seen the burrows, and the burrows do not argue. A burrow that goes empty goes empty with the whole of the species’ silence in it.

The Chair: The record notes the beak in season at 55 millimetres, red with a grey base, and I observe that the witness has produced no counter-exhibit on the matter of the beak.

Witness: There is no beak in the box, your Honour. That is the point of the box.

Cross-examination of Hallvör Dóttir, skipper, capelin drifter “ÓLÍFINN”, port of Hafnarfjörður

Counsel: Skipper, the capelin run of 1997 — where was it?

Witness: I do not know where it is. The nets come up light and the log comes up heavy, and I have been fishing these two columns for nineteen years.

Counsel: And the birds?

Witness: The birds are where the capelin was. Ask the birds. The birds do not lie about it; they are too busy standing in it.

The Chair: The court will not ask the birds. The birds are the subject, not the witness.

Exhibit C — the list of fault lines

Counsel: The second registrars table the list of fault lines as Exhibit C.

The Clerk: Exhibit C is received, in full, in one envelope, sealed twice.

The Chair: Let it be read.

The Clerk: The list of fault lines, as it arrives: the Reykjanes rift; the Westman lines of 1853 to 1857, which last took the sea under the north burrow line; the fissure east of burrow 65 on Drangarnes; the seam under the dock itself, which the tribunal has been sitting on this morning. The list arrives complete.

Counsel: And does it explain the decline?

The Chair: I will take that. The list is complete and explains nothing. It is received for the record and for no other purpose, which is the record office’s whole trade.

Vote on the rating

The Chair: The question before the bench: whether the species record warrants, at this sitting, the rating vulnerable. Those in favour, signify. * * * The record reads as follows: Carried 7–0, abstaining 2, the abstainers being Muriel and Reyes, who keeps the list of fault lines that arrive complete and explain nothing.

The Clerk: Entered, word for word, the way the list itself is kept.

Order and adjournment

The Chair: This court rates the Atlantic puffin, Fratercula arctica, vulnerable upon this record, with effect from the spring laying season, and orders that the Westman Islands count be taken again in ink in the first week of June, the warden to bring the book and no one to bring the weather. The sitting closes at 12:41. The record may be continued in the manner used by the tribunal’s sister courts; for the method of the verbatim record the clerk is referred to In re the Kew Gardens Trial — Verbatim Record, Case No. BA-2021/029, and for the standing of the species’ northern neighbours, one of which is recorded as lost and is therefore not counted in the table, to the lost-and-found bulletin kept at The Rink, Lillehammer — Lost, One Country (Kingdom of Norway), while the vessels named in the fisheries return are answered for in the naval register at HMS Agamemnon (1852) — In Memoriam.

The Clerk: So ordered. The record stands as read.

— Set down from the dock by the clerk’s office, with the corrections in the margin, both kept. —
