WALLACHIA & VĂCĂREȘTI VALLEY RAILWAYS

The Nine

Timetable & Commentary · Buhueni Estate to the Court Station, Bucharest

FIFTH EDITION · SUNDAY 1 AUGUST — THURSDAY 27 SEPTEMBER, 1859

Schedule of the Slow Service — Nine Stops, One Passenger of Note

The Nine is the slowest train on the Văcărești Line: nine stops, all-day pace, the only service that takes the morning from Buhueni estate and the evening to the Court Station at Bucharest. Passengers have kept a private timetable for it ever since the 1 August 1815 departure, when a baby of the Văcărescu family was set down at the first stop, Buhueni, and never once for the rest of her life missed a train she wanted to catch. The passenger in question is Marițica Bibescu, born Maria Văcărescu, afterwards Marițica Ghica (1 August 1815 – 27 September 1859), boyaress, unpublished poet, and Princess-consort of Wallachia between September 1845 and June 1848. The times below are her times; the distances are the line's own survey, measured in stanzas where the surveyor ran out of rods.

Stops & Commentary, by the Stationmaster

1 · Buhueni Estate — the departure that began everything

The Nine starts where the Văcărescu money begins, in a house that has always been too large for its silences. Maria is born on 1 August 1815, and the family counts her as another pair of scissors for the future. Her grandfather Ienăchiță Văcărescu has already been a politician and a professional writer; her father Nicolae is doing the same two jobs in sequence; her uncle Alecu will take up the pen next. A child who grows up with three writers in one household learns early that a sentence is a kind of station: you are either on it or you have missed it. The family, by the standing estimate of the mill book, is the richest boyaress house in the province, and the richest houses are the first ones the weather hits.

2 · Văcărești Mill — three writers in one house

The second stop is the mill that gives the line its name. The commentary on this stop is short because the house does the talking: politics in the parlour, the pen in the study, and the grain coming in through both. When the deaths come, they come in a batch, the way a slow train drops one carriage a day, and the child who survives them is, in the family's own accounting, orphaned as a child. The mill book for the bad years records only: the lamps were trimmed every night, all of them, by someone.

3 · Orphan Siding — guarded by relatives & friends

An orphan, in Wallachia, is not a child without a keeper; she is a child with several keepers, which is harder. The siding at stop three is where the line slows to walking pace so the relatives can pass the girl along the platform, the way one passes a lamp in the dark. The family friends named in the log are the ones the stationmaster insists on: the line would not have reached stop four otherwise.

4 · Ghica Court Halt — the Prince on the platform

At the court halt of the Ghica house, Prince Alexandru II Ghica takes on the girl as a charge, and the train picks up for the first time. He is, the log allows, kind to her in the public way and exacting in the private way, which is how all the good princes of the province travel. The platform at this stop is the only one on the line with a roof, and the roof is what she is kept under for the next stretch of country.

5 · Brâncoveanu Crossing — the philanthropist alights

Zoe Brâncoveanu, philanthropist, alights here to be, by her own choice, on the side of the track rather than in a carriage. She runs the small charities of the crossing, which is to say she gives the orphan a table and a name at other people's tables. The stationmaster notes that the crossing was rebuilt twice in the girl's lifetime and both times Zoe was standing in the road. Period sources, at this stop, begin to use the word beautiful with a frequency the surveyor found impossible to correct. They also, in the same margins, use the words vain and ambitious, and the Nine runs through, on time, and does not argue with the telegraph.

6 · Știrbei Junction — the 1834 wedding

The wedding is run as a station change, which is what it was: 1834, at the junction where the young Barbu Știrbei, the Prince's brother, has the first train on the new branch. Maria steps from the slow service into the branch line, the platform crowd makes the noise the crowd makes, and from this stop onward the passenger is printed on the ticket as Marițica Bibescu. The commentary adds, in pencil, the one fact the telegraph never managed to transmit: she was already a poet at the junction. She simply never published. The verses found in the carriage later are enough to fill a small station library, and the line keeps them in the lamp room, behind the 150-watt lamp, where the ink does not fade and no one reads them at the top of their voice.

7 · Bucharest, Court Station — 1845 to 1848

In September 1845 the Nine arrives at the Court Station with its passenger in the position of Princess-consort of Wallachia, and for the next two years and nine months she is the most watched arrival on the schedule. The commentary of the period is unanimous on the face and divided on everything else: the court gossips call her beautiful, the court rivals call her vain and ambitious, and the timetable office, which prints only what is scheduled, prints that she was on time. In June 1848 the line goes red. The revolution takes the branch, the junction and the schedule in that order, and the last entry in the Court Station book is a cancellation: departure 17:40, Princess-consort, not proceeding. Reason: the line has changed its company. That is the whole of June 1848 in the stationmaster's hand. The Nine runs empty for a while, and then it runs with her as a widow.

8 · Ghica Estate — the widow's stop

The last full service of the line runs between the Court Station and the Ghica estate, and the passenger is now printed, as the old friends insist, Marițica Ghica. She keeps the carriage, the verse and the lamps. The stationmaster's note is brief and has never been corrected: she read aloud in the carriage at night, to no one, in a voice the lamp could not warm.

9 · Terminus — 27 September 1859

The Nine reaches its last stop at 02:30 on 27 September 1859. What the passenger did at the terminus is the one matter on which the line's own books agree with the lamps: the lamps went out. The carriage was found the way the timetable found it, all windows open, the verses still in the carriage. The stationmaster struck the date into the book, set the board to service ends, no trains since, and walked the line down to the platform to turn off the PA one reading at a time. The board, however, had already printed one line of its own, and the stationmaster copied it down word for word, because that is what you do with a line the machine prints that you were not expecting: you keep it, clause by clause, and you explain it to the next keeper.

The Platform Legend, Kept Word for Word

The Supreme Court on Wellington, the Nine, or the Italian Republic, is a slow speed and a 150-watt incandescent lamp hung over the PA.

The legend was printed on the platform card the night the line closed, by the same two-mouth rendering fault the neighbouring lines keep blaming on weather. The lamp room holds the card, and the commentary below explains it the way the office explains everything: clause by clause.

The Supreme Court on Wellington
The first alias of the Nine in the old card index, entered by a clerk who spelled the court station's name the way he heard it. No one ever corrected it, which is how the train got its first alternate name.
the Nine
The working name. Nine stops, nine stanzas, one passenger. The index prefers it.
or the Italian Republic
The second alias, entered the year the passenger travelled to the peninsula and came back with the habit. The card index keeps both aliases side by side, as it keeps everything.
is a slow speed
The classification. The Nine is, on the schedule, a slow; it stops at all nine stops and argues with none of them. The legend confirms what the timetable says in figures.
and a 150-watt incandescent lamp hung over the PA
Hardware. The 150-watt lamp at Platform 3, the one the plate on this page reproduces, hangs directly over the public-address horn, which is why a line the machine prints arrives to the platform with a little halo on it. The legend is not a sentence about the train. It is the card's own service note, describing what the Nine actually is: a slow, and a lamp.
