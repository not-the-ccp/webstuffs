Sound source 93 metal tonewheels, 22 electromagnetic pickups, motor-driven (to 1975)

1 · Summary of the instrument

The Hammond organ is an electric organ invented by Laurens Hammond and John M. Hanert, and first manufactured in 1935 by the Hammond Organ Company of Chicago. The original Model A was marketed to churches as a lower-cost alternative to the wind-driven pipe organ, or as a substitute where a piano was the standing expectation; a parson who could not afford either was offered a cabinet the size of a large radio, with a bench, a pedalboard, and a sound that a congregation would mistake for the big machine at the far end of the nave. The gamble held. By the time the company had worked through its models — the A, the M-3, the T, the M-102, and finally the B-3 of 1955 and its church counterpart the C-3 — multiple generations of the instrument were in use, most of them voiced the same way: the player pulls a bank of sliding drawbars, each one a metal bar with nine notches, and the notch chosen sets how loudly a given pitch enters the sum.

Until 1975, sound was created the old mechanical way in an electric body: a motor turned a shaft of polished metal tonewheels, each wheel carrying one raised tooth or two, at a rate close to 201 revolutions per minute, and each wheel passed near an electromagnetic pickup whose output was amplified into a speaker cabinet. The B-3 holds ninety-three of these wheels in two banks — A and B — so the same note has two voices, and the operator can switch banks mid-phrase. The organ is commonly used with the Leslie speaker, a cabinet with a rotating horn inside, and the two together are what a jazz drummer means when he says the band is in. The instrument quickly became popular with professional jazz musicians in organ trios and quartets, and the popularity carried through the pop and rock decades that followed. Around two million Hammond organs have been manufactured, which makes it, for the price and the size, one of the best-sold voices in the history of the modern church.

2 · Anatomy, field notes

Approach from the left, as the operator sits. The drawbar section stands above the manuals in two rows — nine drawbars over nine — each row a bank, A on top and B below on a B-3. A drawbar out to notch eight is fully in; at zero it is fully shut; the notches between are the fractions, and a setting is spoken as a string of nine digits, e.g. 8-0-4-0-0-0-0-0-0, the house tone of a hundred thousand bars. The presets are a rack of flip switches that set the drawbars for you; the B-3 carries the full rack, ninety-two settings in the church tradition, and the field names for the famous ones are set down in Section 5. The 93 tonewheels sit in two columns behind the console; the sub-octave wheel is the fattest and the 1 3/5 wheel the thinnest, a pin of metal going round and round. The pickup for each wheel is an electromagnetic coil, twenty-two in all on the classic layout; a wheel near a coil is a voltage, and a bank switch is a short. The pedal is a 25-note board at the feet, with its own drawbars of four, and the pedals talk through the same amplifier as the hands.

The Leslie is not part of the organ and is the best part of the organ. The 147 cabinet carries a rotor — a rotating horn over a rotating baffle — with a slow speed and a fast speed and a switch on the organ bench or in the pedal box. Slow is the congregation; fast is the chase. A B-3 without a Leslie is an engine without a road.

3 · The drawbar console (working)

Pull the bars. Each column is one drawbar, left to right from sub-octave 16′ to 1 3/5; click a notch to set it, click 0 to shut the bar. When the setting matches one of the house presets, the readout names it. This is the whole craft in miniature: the organ is just nine numbers, and two of them make a trumpet while four of them make a church.

Setting: 0-0-0-0-0-0-0-0-0

5 · On the road

The B-3 is a road instrument, built for the rack in the van. On the Born in the U.S.A. tour of 1985 the E Street Band carried one, and the man who sat at it was Roy Bittan, whose first instrument was the organ and who played the chorus piano on the studio record of I'm Goin' Down at the Power Station in May 1982. In the studio the piano went in only on the chorus; on the road the B-3 rode in the same rack and did the rest of the work, swell set to slow, Leslie running, the organ carrying the band through the minor-key singles while Max Weinberg's drums did what the tape already proved they would do. The single itself is logged in full at the chapter head above — same year, same rack, same band — and this chapter is its machine-side record: what the piano played, the organ could have played a half-step louder, and often did.

6 · Errata and marginalia

The dealer's field guide of 1963, Appendix C, listed Sightings Outside the Jurisdiction — a table of places where the instrument was reported but not sold. The 1968 reprint deleted the appendix in its entirety, which is the only reason one line of it survives, in the 1963 copies, uncorrected and unretracted. The line, under the column heading Confirmed, reads, word for word:

In the Highlands of Scotland, herds of elephants.

No organ dealer in the Highlands has ever been found to account for it. The entry may be a printer's slip, a clerk's joke, or a genuine order the company quietly filled; the 1963 guide is now a curiosity item and the appendix rides with it, which is how field manuals end up keeping secrets. Filed here without correction, per the manual's standing rule that a line once printed is a line on the record.
