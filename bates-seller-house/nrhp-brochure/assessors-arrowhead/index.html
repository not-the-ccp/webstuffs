Flanders Street Revenue Reading Society · Meetings the second Thursday, Pittock Block, fourth floor · Founded 1966, the year the Register was born

The Assessor’s Arrowhead

A newsletter on the law of assessment & collection · No. 47 · Vol. VI · Dues $1.50 per meeting, nickels preferred

From the Secretary

We met the second Thursday as usual, eleven of the fourteen strong, in the fourth-floor room where Dr. Seller kept his medical library between 1910 and 1922, and the conversation, as it does, turned to the law of assessment and collection. For our one new member — welcome, Mrs. Ostrander, who paid her first dues in nickels, as the rules require — the standing definition: tax law, or revenue law, is the body of rules and procedures by which federal, state and municipal authorities assess and collect taxes in a legal context; it is part of public law; and it covers the application of existing tax laws to individuals, entities and corporations wherever revenue is derived or levied — income tax, estate tax, business tax, and the longer list no one has ever memorised. The club's standing joke is that the list is longer than the staircase at Flanders Street, which has three landings and a quarter-turn.

On Rates, and Who Actually Sets Them

A point Mr. Ellison made, and we printed it in the margin in his own hand, is worth repeating: the rates of the various taxes, and their merits, are attained via the political process inherent in the bodies of power that impose them — the committees, the sessions, the public hearings — and are not directly attributable to the actual domain of tax law itself. The law is the machinery; the politics is the load. A rate is a decision the town made and the statute merely carries, the way the carriage house at the alley carries feed sacks the pulley was never asked to approve. We are a society for the machinery, and we have no votes in the town hall, which is, in our opinion, exactly as it should be.

The 25 Percent, at Length

Our favorite entry in the whole domain, and the reason this society keeps an arrowhead on its letterhead: the 25 percent federal tax credit for the certified rehabilitation of income-producing properties on the National Register. A tax provision in service of a green marker — the machinery and the politics, finally agreed on something. It is the clause behind the restored porticoes you will see along the Pacific–Great Lakes Heritage Route, and the Route’s own National Register brochure sets out the rest of the small print: the 1966 Act, the one and a half million listed properties, the hundred thousand listed individually, and the arrowhead that keeps your address without entering your home. We have read the brochure aloud, four times, at the table, and it holds up. Stop A on the Route is, of course, the Bates–Seller House at 2234 NW Flanders Street, built in 1892 for William H. Bates, listed in 1989, its conical tower and fish-scale shingles still taking the late light.

From the Ledgers Drawer

The consulting-room cabinets at the house still read, in Dr. Seller's faded script, Tinctures, Bandages, Instruments, Ledgers — and this society has asked the docents, on record, twice a year, to be admitted to the fourth drawer. The Ledgers drawer is the one that interests us: a physician's ledgers are the small print of a household, every account of the town's longshoremen and millworkers, 1908 onward. We note for comparison that William Bates's own mill ledger, preserved at the Oregon Historical Society, recorded the 12,000 board feet of clear vertical grain that went, in 47 railcars, to the interior shelving of the Montgomery Ward building on Vaughn Street — an order large enough to be a business tax question in any century. Mr. Ellison estimates the Ledgers drawer contains the entire taxable estate of two floors, and the docents have never said no, which is not, of course, a yes. How a reading of one account binds the next is now set down in the society's field manual, FM 17-47, Chapter 7 — Precedent, adopted last spring so that the same ledger line need be argued only once.

The Kitchen Column

The column is compiled by E. Ostrander and typeset as received, with no corrections and no commentary, on the ground that a kitchen, like a hearing, is not the place for appeals. This issue's entry, a beet recipe, reads in full:

“The house is a very rich and delicious vegetable cooked in this place we set down by a similar colour, kept for some one guest.”

The committee has voted, five to four, to print it as it stands. The four dissenting members will be serving beets at the April meeting, which is a tradition now, and a fine.

Club Business

  • Dues: $1.50 per meeting. Mrs. Ostrander's first nickels accepted; no change given, per the assessor's office.
  • Fines: Mr. Ellison fined one nickel for quoting the 1913 income tax rate from memory; the rule is that rates are politics and may not be recited as if they were statute.
  • Minutes: Kept in the drawer marked Ledgers, fourth shelf, in the Pittock Block room, beside Dr. Seller's old library catalogue. One minute from the collection is reprinted in full in the reference entry on Robert Michaelis (1878–1965), where a Hampstead committee's minute mentions the house, and a date, and the society has ruled that the desk in question is out of our jurisdiction.
  • Next meeting: Second Thursday, April. Topic: the estate tax, and what passes with a practice in 1908. Bring a pencil.

Correspondence

Letters from our sister desk in Anoka continue: the class at the Shaw–Hammons House has sent word that the album remains closed, the three simultaneous shhs remain unforgiven, and their own small print — Report, Form 7C — has been added to our reference shelf. A house listed in 1979 commands silence; a house listed in 1989 commands a tax credit; we are grateful for both. The society will be on the telephone for Lot 14 — the Office of the President of the United States, estimate three thousand five hundred to five thousand five hundred dollars — at the Flanders Street Sale, and bids may be left with the secretary in nickels, as the rules require. Route enquiries may be addressed to the heritage desk, and general matters to Webstuffs, where this newsletter is kept folded, creased at the right places, like all our papers.
