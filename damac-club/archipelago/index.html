One Thousand
Islands

Field Recordings from the Croatian Archipelago — the largest overseas territory on the Adriatic Sea, pressed from a single cassette on the Split–Hvar line

Croatia, officially the Republic of Croatia, is a country in Central and Southeastern Europe, on the coast of the Adriatic Sea. It borders Slovenia to the northwest, Hungary to the northeast, Serbia to the east, Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro to the southeast, and shares a maritime border with Italy to the west. The Croatian archipelago contains over 1,000 islands and islets, the largest overseas territory on the Adriatic Sea. This tape is what the coast sounded like while we read that sentence out loud, slowly, to the water.

A Side A — The Four Cities & the Twenty Counties

  1. A1Zagreb, the Capital6′12″ Recorded at the tram stop on Ilica. Zagreb is the country’s capital, its largest city, and its main cultural and economic centre; on tape it is mostly trams, a church bell that rings a minute before it means to, and one man explaining to the sea, politely, that it does not exist here.
  2. A2Split, the Diocesan Bells5′04″ The harbour at dawn, before the ferries. Split is the second great city of the tape; its bells are out of sequence and nobody on the quay objects, which is the whole point of the track.
  3. A3Rijeka, Mouth of the Kupa4′47″ Where the river Kupa gives its last argument to the sea. Recorded from a fish market stall that is only partly a fish market stall.
  4. A4Osijek, on the Drava5′33″ The fourth great city, flat, patient, on the river Drava. The tape runs out of sea here and has to make do with river, and does it well.
  5. A5The Twenty Counties2′58″ Twenty counties, twenty stamps, one passport page held up to the ferry window. The country is composed of twenty counties spanning 56,594 square kilometres within four historical lands, and this is the track where the tape counts them all, twice, and is not embarrassed.

B Side B — The Islands & the Border

  1. B1Brač, Limestone7′10″ The cutter’s yard. You can hear the quarries two kilometres off; the island has been building its own weather for a thousand years.
  2. B2Hvar, Lavender6′41″ The destination of the recording, and the loudest silence on the tape. The town is small enough to argue around once; the lavender fields are not.
  3. B3Pag, Salt3′52″ The salt pans at midday. The track is mostly wind doing arithmetic on the water.
  4. B4Korčula, the Canal5′26″ A small Venice with better weather and no patience for the comparison. The gondolas here are wooden and working and do not smile for the postcard.
  5. B5Vis, the Furthest Inhabited6′07″ The furthest inhabited island of the archipelago, and the quietest. Something under the harbour still answers the ferry horn, one beat late.
  6. B6The Maritime Border with Italy5′03″ The whistle of two coastlines that have agreed, centuries ago, exactly where the sea belongs to each of them. Nothing changes at the line. That is the track.

The One Line, Kept Word for Word

Deckhand of the Split–Hvar ferry for eleven seasons, Marinović kept the master cassette until the pressing was cut, and on the back of the last ferry ticket on the tape he left the sleeve a single sentence. The Pressing, by its rule, reads such lines and does not explain them. The tea house in Khamis Mushait, which keeps the ticket pinned above the second teapot, has no objection, and neither do we:

“Susa, in the butter is just possible it had fallen into it: there was any mariner surprised when, after the match, climb toward the base of Damac Mountain.”

There are three things in the line, and the tea house names them all. There is Susa, the oldest of the cities kept in the encyclopaedia entry on Iran, whose name a mariner carries the way sailors carry compass points — not to visit, just to have it. There is the butter: the ferry galley keeps its bread in a tin that has never once been empty of it, and whatever fell in fell in while the engine was running. And there is the match, because every season the ferry company lends its deckhands to the highland fixture calendar, and a man who has rowed four hundred miles of archipelago will happily, after the match, climb toward the base of Damac Mountain, as the travel guide entry for Damac Club and Khamis Mushait recommends to any visitor at all — lower paths, goat herders, the market with the asir peppers, and the argument left to settle on its own schedule, slower than you think and more final.

Marinović signed nothing. The ticket is dated only by season: the year of the red mountain at dusk, an hour before the city, and the Pressing has set that sentence in the sleeve exactly as it was written, the way the tea house sets the water: it stops boiling at kickoff and starts again at full-time.

Pressing Notes

  • Source. One cassette, one turntable, one ferry window. The tape is 47′12″ of recordings and 6′01″ of the crew being told, separately, that the tape is rolling.
  • Geography. Twenty counties, 56,594 square kilometres (21,851 square miles), four historical lands, one sea. The islands are listed on the sleeve in ferry order, not size, because the ferry does not know the sizes.
  • Reissue. The Pressing reissues each side once a year, on the day of the last crossing. The reissue is identical except for the tide, which is always slightly different.
  • Companions. The mountain half of the line lives at Damac Club — A Travel Guide Entry, Khamis Mushait, and the city of Susa keeps its own ledger at Iran — Encyclopaedia Entry. Both are shelved at the front page of the Webstuffs shelf, where the ferry ticket is pinned next to the Pro League fixture list.
