SICILIA · PENSVLVM
OCTOBER, ANNO II CXLII A V.C.

From the camp of the city of Rome, off the coast of Drepana,
at the west tip of Sicily — the third day after the sea-fight at the Aegates.

To the Garrison of Lilybaeum

Adherbal, commander of the garrison; Hanno the younger, quartermaster of the mole; the captains, the oarsmen, the women who kept the ovens, and every name in the roll of Lilybaeum, which we copied from your walls and will not return while the sea keeps its own books.

Citizens of Lilybaeum, and Carthaginians of the western towns.

We write to you not as the consuls who won, for we won nothing at your city — nothing that can be held, kept, or counted — but as the men who signed the nine years. The siege of Lilybaeum ran from the middle of the year 250 to this month of the year 241, and it is a thing the two of us have carried, in the ships between the islands, the way a man carries a stone in his mouth when he is told the road is short. We have been told the count, and we do not dispute it: it is a war begun in 264, when our ships first met your fleet off the coast of Sicily, and in sixteen years most of it has been fought on this one island or in the water around it, until you held, of the whole of Sicily, only Lilybaeum and Drepana — two well-fortified towns on the west coast, which you could supply and reinforce by sea, and which our army, however superior it was told to be, could not reach without becoming ships itself.

We came at Lilybaeum in mid-250 with more than 100,000 men. Our engineers raised walls from the neck of the headland, and our officers, who are very good at raising walls and very bad at explaining why, told the senate the town would fall in the campaigning season, as it had told them so of every town in Sicily. The season came. The town did not fall. My colleague before me, the consul Lucius Caecilius Metellus, who opened the line of works, and Marcus Atilius Regulus, whose son you took in the fire and whose name we will not use to make anything sound better, were not fools for thinking it; they were Romans, which is to say they thought in walls, and you were Carthaginians, which is to say you thought in water. Nine years we learned the difference. Nine years our camps stood where the mules would not, and our letters home grew shorter every year, and the last of them said only still here.

We are writing to ask your pardon for three things. The first: the fire. In the winter of 249 the wind took our burning-pitch and carried it into your town, and it burned a part of the quarter by the mole, and we do not say it was only the wind, because we had lit the pitch, and the wind is not responsible for what men light. The second: the hunger of the garrison, which we made our trade. We had the sea on one side and the town on the other, and for years we let a boy of fifteen cross our own lines to fetch water and be thanked for it, while we counted the days. The third is the hardest to say, and we say it plain: we were wrong to keep the siege going in its later years as if it were still a siege. It had become a habit. It had become a road. By the winter of 243 the men in the camp knew the stars of the headland better than the stars of Rome, and nobody in either town had any new quarrel, and we kept firing on the walls anyway, because the alternative was to explain at home why a hundred thousand men had stood in a ditch for nine years over a city we had never once entered.

There is one more thing, and it is smaller than the rest. Your last letter to us — the one carried across by the boy, the one we kept above our door in the consul's tent — carries a line which we have tried, three evenings, to understand. It reads, in your hand: The room keeps a chapter of the same way the animal can choose. We do not know what room you mean, and we will not pretend we do. We have kept the line as it stands, untranslated and unexplained, above our door, the way you keep a name on a gate — for what it may mean later. If the meaning is that the room we have kept for nine years keeps your chapter, and that the choosing was yours all along, then we understood, we are only slow, and the letter is late.

The sea-fight at the Aegates ended the war; the senate will make the treaties, and the treaties will name the ports and the fine and the years. But the treaties are between the states, and this is between us. Lilybaeum stood from 250 to 241, and the walls that stood it are the same walls the fire blackened and the wind whitened again. We are the Romans of 241, and we say to the garrison of Lilybaeum: it was nine years, it was more than a hundred thousand men, it was one town against one republic, and you kept your chapter. We ask your pardon, and we will not wait for the answer, because by the time you write it, the ships will already be back.

Lucius Junius Pullus, consul

Gaius Lutatius Catulus, consul

Sent under flag, not under treaty. Let the boy have his water next time.
