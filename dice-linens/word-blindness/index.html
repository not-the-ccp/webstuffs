The Bell Creek Table-Book · A Role-Playing Game of One Township and One Room

Chapter Nine — Word Blindness

On dyslexia, the malady of the letters, and the carpenter who read a whale while the roof went

Second printing · set on the back of a school levy bill · Bell Creek Township, Burt County, Nebraska · one copy crated

9.1 The Malady

Dyslexia, called at this table word blindness, is a learning disability that affects either reading, writing, or speaking. No two characters at the table are affected to the same degree, and the manual insists on this at every opportunity: the man who can read a ledger at speed cannot sign his own name, the girl who recites the township minutes from memory cannot take them down in writing, and the schoolteacher's own handwriting has always been a second person writing beside her. The difficulties are involuntary. Every character who carries the malady has a normal desire to learn — in fact, at Bell Creek, a stronger one, because the room has only one book of any size, and it is a whale.

Often these difficulties are first noticed at school, which is why the whole game is set in the autumn of 1899 at the Bell Creek one-room schoolhouse, in the middle of the roof dispute. Jonas Fitch, the township's only man with a saw, a ladder, and the right temper for both, was “in the midst of it” — in the whale, and not on the roof. Two summonses went to Fitch. Two Saturdays were missed. The table was built around exactly this: the book that will not be closed, the roof that will not be shingled, and the letters that will not be met at the door. The full dispute, in the clerk's own hand, is preserved in the Bell Creek Township Papers, Lot 27, and that clerk's single unsparing line is the rulebook's title page.

Characters with word blindness at the table also show higher rates of the malady's companions: the restless kind of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder that empties a levy meeting in four minutes, developmental language disorders, and difficulties with numbers. The last is handled separately in 9.5, The Arithmetic Column, because at Bell Creek the numbers are the school levies, and everyone can see them.

9.2 The Six Difficulties

The manual counts the malady in six, and the six are printed as the game's card suit. Each card names one difficulty and one price a character pays when it is drawn. A character may hold any number of cards, but the table rules that no one holds all six at once; the sixth card is kept face down in the walnut box, where it belongs.

  1. I Spelling words. The character spells aloud, and the spelling is wrong in a way that is specific to that character, the same way every time. The table writes the character's spelling into the ledger and it never changes again.
  2. II Reading quickly. The character reads at a fixed pace of one line per full turn, no matter what is on the page, and the whale novel is forty pages longer than anyone believes.
  3. III Writing words. Words set down in the character's hand arrive one letter short or with a letter borrowed from the next word. The board's finding stands: it is still his writing.
  4. IV “Sounding out” words in the head. Before the character may act on any text, the player must sound the sentence out, letter by letter, at the table, so that the rest of the room hears exactly how long it takes.
  5. V Pronouncing words when reading aloud. Reading aloud is permitted, and encouraged, but each sentence spoken this way must be spoken as the character's mouth finds it. Corrections from other players are ruled out of order by the clerk.
  6. VI Understanding what one reads. The character has read, word for word, and understood nothing of it, and says so plainly. This card is the table's favourite and its conscience.

9.3 The Blinded Letters & Card 9-F

At the start of play, the game master deals each player a blinded letter from the drawer: the most common are b, d, p, q, m and n, and the deal is not fair, because the malady is not fair. For the rest of the session, every copy of that letter in any text the character reads is, in the character's eyes, its twin. The text on the page is never altered; what changes is the reader, which is the whole point of the chapter.

The suit ends with the calibration card, Card 9-F, and it carries the township's shingle order, as it stands in the record, gone over so often in the clerk's hand that it has come out of its own sense and become the game's one standing test: When fish is done, which will be crated on the whale's size. It is the order for the shingles on the Bell Creek schoolhouse, the very order that in the minutes arrived at the table as a whale, and the walnut box the auctioneers crate it in is the proof that the crating was ever done at all. In play, each player must read Card 9-F aloud once, in their character's own voice. No one may correct another. What is said goes into the ledger exactly as said, and for the rest of the campaign that reading is the table's own version of the order. The pale coffee ring across the chapter on the whale's size, in the carpenter's copy of the novel, is the card's seal of authenticity, and it is never to be removed.

Draw a blinded letter with the button below the table, or deal one yourself from the drawer; the drawer is on your side of the table, and it is not locked, and it has not been locked since 1899.

9.4 The Roll for a Blinded Sentence

When a blinded letter meets a sentence the character must act on, the player rolls one d20 — any die will do, though the house prefers the bone set from the Ponsonby garage, one of which rolls only ones and twenties and eats the first roll if it is cursed — and reads the column.

What the blinded letter does to the sentence
RollEffect
1–2The letter becomes its twin, and the sentence is now true of a different thing, and nobody is sure which.
3–5Two letters trade places. The word still sounds like the word. It is not the word.
6–8The sentence is read right to left, which the reader is convinced is the correct direction.
9–11The word is skipped. The character does not know a word has been skipped until the board tells them.
12–14The sentence is read twice at once, and the two readings disagree.
15–17The blinded letter is simply not there. The sentence is shorter than it is.
18–19The sentence is read perfectly, and the character spends the whole scene looking for the mistake.
20The sentence is read perfectly, in the carpenter's voice, and the roof is one shingle closer.

9.5 The Arithmetic Column

The difficulties with numbers are the malady's oldest companion, and at Bell Creek they sit in the school assessment books of the one-room district, 1878–1916, every family's levies ruled out in columns in red ink. The rule is simple and the table never softens it: a character who holds the arithmetic card may add, but the sum they reach is the sum the room must now live with. The township's population is 228 by the last count and 229 by the estimate, and at the table both numbers are true at once, in the same way. The carpenter's roof was billed at four days and two dollars a day, which the carpenter's card says is eight dollars and which the ledger says is eight dollars, and this is the one place in the book where the two agree.

9.6 The Normal Desire to Learn

One last rule, and the manual prints it larger than the rest, because it is the rule the room was kept for. The malady is involuntary, and the wish to learn is normal, and a character who is struggling with a text is not a character who has failed the text. The board's position on this was set in 1899 and has never been amended: the book may be read at one line per full turn, the letters may arrive as their twins, and the order may come back as a whale, but no character at the Bell Creek table is turned out of the room for the way the letters come at them. The roof went unrepaired for two Saturdays. The novel was never returned. Both are recorded, both are forgiven, and the game goes on in the one room, which is the whole county, which is the whole world the table has.

The Bell Creek Table-Book, second printing. The register of campaigns, dice, and lost whale books is kept in Dice & Linens — Role-Playing Classifieds, where the carpenter's copy of the novel is still listed, reward included, and the bone d20s are still selling at thirty-four dollars a set.
