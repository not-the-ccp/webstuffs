Of the Class B Shelf

or, the Theory of Knowledge, for Deckhands · filed under B 165 · issued for the St. Johns run · 22 crossings of service to date

This manual is for the guest who has read Chapter IX, learned the letters, and then, standing at the second shelf of the second aisle, looked up at the plain brown spine that says only Philosophy and felt the aisle get colder. Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that examines the nature, the origin, and the limits of knowledge; it is called, in the trade, the theory of knowledge, and the schedule of 1897 gives it a shelf of its own at B 16, with its working drawer at B 165, foundations, theories, methods, where this manual itself is kept, between a book of moths and the 1928 abridgement. You do not need a degree to use it. You need a timetable, a tide table, and the honesty to say which one you were looking at when you believed what you believed.

1 On the Class Itself

The letter B was put in its place in 1897 by James Hanson and Charles Martel, for the same reason they put everything in its place: the Library of Congress happened to own the books, and the drawers had to fit. But B has always been the letter that fits last, because its books argue about what the argument is for. A book of etiquette may assume you know what a book is. A book of B will want to know how you know that, and it will not let you leave until you have said. The old fixed location system of Thomas Jefferson is remembered at this shelf as the founding incident: a book on bees filed under theology because the owner thought of bees while thinking of God. That is not a mistake. That is a method, and the method is association, and association is the oldest source of any claim a man has ever made, and the one this chapter exists to check against a tide table. When the cabin library opened its letters shelf and twelve volumes went over in an afternoon, B came over last, and the deckhands let it, because the deckhands know what a shelf is for before they know what a shelf is.

2 On the Three Kinds of Knowledge

The theory of knowledge sorts its subject into three kinds, and the ferry supplies all three in a single crossing.

The first is propositional knowledge, knowledge that a fact is so: I know that the run makes six crossings a day, twelve seats and a place for one car. It is the kind that can be written on the ice chart, and everything in this manual that is printed is of that kind. The second is practical knowledge, knowledge as skill, the knowing-how: the deckhand in charge can run a crossing in a bad swell the way no timetable can be run, and the difference between his hands and the schedule is the whole distance between the two kinds. The third is knowledge by acquaintance, knowledge as familiarity through experience: the deckhand knows the shore the way he knows his own face, without any proposition in it at all, and you cannot get it off him in a sentence, which is the field test for it. If a crossing can be taught in one sentence, it is a fact. If it takes a season, it is a skill. If it cannot be taught at all, it is an acquaintance, and you are in B's territory whether you filed yourself there or not.

3 On the Three Words

The epistemologist's working definition of knowledge is three words long: belief, truth, justification — knowledge as justified true belief — and the ferry gives the whole definition a day at a time. In the morning the deckhand believes the tide will come up at 4:40; that is the belief. It does come up at 4:40, within the quarter the schedule allows; that is the truth. And he holds the belief on the tide table, the ice chart, and a three-week run of form chalked on the wall beside it; that is the justification, which is to say, the reasons the man could give if asked, on the record, why he cast off when he did. Take the truth out and the crossing is a good story. Take the justification out and it is a lucky one, and no schedule will be built on a lucky one. Take the belief out and there is no crossing at all, which is why this chapter, for all its coldness, is written by people who believe the boat.

4 On the Five Sources, and Their Inspection

To discover how knowledge arises, the epistemologist inspects the sources of justification, and there are five of them at this shelf. The manual lists them in the order a crossing uses them.

  1. Perception. The look over the rail. The swell is what it is before the timetable is what the timetable says. Checked first, checked last, checked in between.
  2. Introspection. The hands' report to the head. What the tiller feels that the schedule never had. Not evidence for the harbour master, but evidence, and the manual does not forbid evidence.
  3. Memory. Last Saturday's crossing. The oldest source on the shelf, and the one a bad century does the most damage to; the 1913 edition of the schedule survives where last Saturday does not.
  4. Reason. The arithmetic of the tide table: high water, low water, the interval, the six crossings worked back from the 4:40. The one source that cannot be on the water at all.
  5. Testimony. The harbour master's word, the member's dictation, this manual. The one source that travels — and the one that breaks in the scan.

Caution 11.4. Testimony is the source this house runs on, because a ferry is a house of testimony: the timetable is testimony, the minutes are testimony, the 4–2 vote is testimony. Inspect it the way the surveyors inspect a datum. If you plot a fix on the wrong datum, you are two hundred metres out and you do not notice until you are in the reef; if you justify a crossing on the wrong testimony, you are three weeks of run of form out and you do not notice until the run is over. Re-check the source before you cast off.

5 On the Two Schools at the Bench

Two schools keep their benches at this shelf, and the board keeps both, the way it keeps the ferry and the moth.

The school of skepticism questions the human ability to attain knowledge, and it has a permanent seat at the readers' group, held by the member who has read every scan at 200 dpi and found the photocopied photocopy. The skeptic does not say the ferry does not cross; the skeptic says you cannot get outside your own crossing to verify it, and the timetable, being part of the crossing, cannot be the verifier. The bench is kept, on the record, because a shelf that has never been doubted has not been justified, and Chapter IV of the field manual for the charts says the datum is to be re-checked even when it is the one you cast off from.

The school of fallibilism says that knowledge may be wrong — that to know a thing is not to hold it fixed for ever, but to hold it while it can be checked, and to be the first to re-shelve it when the check fails. The St. Johns run is fallibilist by practice and by chalk: the three-week run of form was a knowledge, chalked on the wall, and it ended, and the harbour master did not strike the chalk, he dated it, which is the fallibilist's whole method in one eraser's worth of motion. Fixed location is the anti-school, and B's shelf is where a house keeps its anti-school on purpose, the way a harbour keeps the reef chart, because the limits of knowledge are on the chart, and you were going to find them at the shelf whether you asked or not.

6 On the Second Line Salvaged from a Dictation

In the card pocket of this chapter — a pencil card, handwritten, in the pocket of the 1913 edition, the one that went to Z 675.5 and lent its pocket back to B — there is a second line, transcribed aloud from a member's dictation at the readers' group, in the manner of the March line about the ferry that Chapter IX has already put on the record. It is filed here, at B 165, and it reads exactly:

“It was but some crumbs must have no attractions whatever beyond their mere suitableness to the ignoble monster primitively pursued in the reef.”

— card, pencil, in the pocket of the 1913 schedule; found with the first salvaged line; the surrounding card is blank

The group's first question was the same as in March: what is it for? The answer is this manual's own, and it is put down here as the working reading. What remains of a sentence after a bad scan is crumbs — a few words of the whole, the way the ferry line came down to if the St. Johns ferry and the run of form. And the rule of keeping them is stated in the card's own clause: the crumbs must have no attractions whatever beyond their mere suitableness. They are kept not because they please, not because they are beautiful, not even because they are true — for the truth of a crumb is a question the crumb cannot answer — but for their suitableness, which is the surveyor's word for a placeholder, the way a broken baseline is suited to the map it was on. Attractiveness is what gets a line misread; a line kept for its charm is a line already argued out of its shape. Keep it for the fit, and the fit will hold.

The harder half of the card is the second clause, and it is B's clause, the one that earns its shelf. The ignoble monster primitively pursued in the reef: the monster is the meaning — the meaning the recorder in the room was after, the thing a dictation is a primitive chase after, pursued with the oldest instrument on this shelf, the ear, before reason has a hand in it, which is to say, primitively. And the reef is where a wrong chase ends: the very reef Caution 11.4 is written about, where you are two hundred metres out and do not notice until you are in it. So the whole card, on the working reading, is an instruction in the limits of knowledge, which is the subject of this chapter and no other: you will pursue the meaning with the most primitive instrument you own, in the most dangerous water on the chart, and what comes back to you will be crumbs, and the discipline is to keep the crumbs for nothing but their suitableness — as marks, not as a meal, as a baseline, not as a verdict. It was but some crumbs. That is the fallibilist's whole confession, in pencil, with the surrounding card blank, and the group, voting as it has voted twice before, kept it rather than struck it, and filed it where a line about the limits of knowledge goes to be kept: at the theory of knowledge, second aisle, shelf B.

7 The Precepts

  1. Sort what you know before you argue about it: a fact in one sentence, a skill in a season, an acquaintance in no words at all, and the sorting is the work of the shelf, not the work of the hour.
  2. Justified true belief is the standard of the crossing; a crossing on any two of the three is a story or a luck, and neither will be chalked.
  3. Inspect the five sources in the order the crossing uses them — perception, introspection, memory, reason, testimony — and re-check the datum before you plot, even the one you cast off from.
  4. Testimony travels and testimony breaks; the timetable is testimony, and so is this manual, and the 200 dpi applies to both.
  5. The skeptic's bench is kept, because a shelf never doubted has not been justified; and the fallibilist's chalk is dated, never struck, because a run of form is a knowledge until the run is over.
  6. A salvaged line is kept for its suitableness alone, and has no attractions whatever beyond it: the crumbs are marks on the baseline, not a meal, and the reef is where the wrong chase ends.

The B shelf is the coldest shelf in the building, and the ferry's deckhands have made it warm the only way a working house can: by running the whole theory on a small tide, six crossings a day, and finding that the three kinds, the three words, the five sources, and the two schools all come around, one way or another, to the same question the harbour master chalks on the wall — how long will the run of form hold? — and the only answer the theory of knowledge is authorised to give is the one the chalk gives: until it is checked, and then it is checked, and the date goes on it. Knowledge, it will be seen, is a crossing, not a shore. You were never going to finish it; the etiquette is to keep the timetable honest. — The Stacks, Chapter XI
