The Lophophore Book of Manners · Atlantic Reel House Edition · Bound in sponge-cream with a blue fore-edge

Chapter IV.
Of the Moss Animal, or the Crown Worn Below the Waterline

In which the book of etiquette corrects the sleeve of ARL-1037, sets three courses for the water, and keeps one line of the ledger exactly as it stands.

§ 1. Of Station: the Guest Who Anchors

There is a first and a last piece of the book, and both concern the bryozoa — the moss animals, also called the Polyzoa and the Ectoprocta, and most politely referred to at the table as the house, because nearly all of them are, and none of them ever vacates. They are a phylum of simple, aquatic invertebrates, and a nearly sedentary one: the colony takes a footing on the substrate of a reef, a stone, a kelp frond, a ship's bottom, and the footing is the whole of the social life. There is no leaving. There is no arriving late, which the committee regards as the only perfect form of punctuality on record. The polyps are typically about 0.5 millimetres long — one sixty-fourth of an inch, or the width of a single note on the label's small hand — and the house counts them, to this printing, at 5,869 living species, each one anchored, each one present, each one having made the same decision on the same morning and not changed it since.

Rule the First. A guest who anchors does not change chairs. If the current changes the table, the guest does not; the guest is, by that time, furniture in the polite sense, and furniture does not take offence at being rearranged by water it did not consent to and cannot object to.

§ 2. Of the Crown (the Lophophore)

Every polyp wears the crown. It is called a lophophore, and it is a "crown" of tentacles — a complete ring of them, fringed and turned out like a hat at a wedding — and its whole office is filter feeding: the crown is turned slowly toward the current, the plankton is received, and the guest who is served moves on without being told. Here the book must say what the sleeve of The Crown on the Forehead said obliquely. The album at Atlantic Reel ARL-1037 took its title from the small dark rosette set into the forehead of the queen angelfish, Holacanthus ciliaris, the seal pressed on the envelope; but the survey boat Marguerite sat off the north shore of Andros in the third hour of the light, in the same sponge garden the album walked, and the hand that kept the names for the label wrote the lophophore in the margin three times before it wrote the rosette once. The true crown of the water is the crown of tentacles. The rosette is a seal; the lophophore is the hat. The house edition of the book of manners therefore records the correction, and the record keeps both crowns the way the log kept both spellings — the crown on the forehead and the crown on the current, and the misprint between them left exactly where it was.

Delia Marsh, producer of ARL-1037, is said to have heard the hydrophone and to have said, that's the bottom, that's the whole bottom, just play it; what the bottom was playing, on the mornings the hydrophone was over the side, was the sound of five thousand eight hundred and sixty-nine crowns turning at once toward the current. Track 3 of the record, the long walk down the sponge garden, was recorded in a garden full of them, and the garden, which declined to perform on the fourteenth morning, is thanked here for the refusal and for the company of the polyps, who did not decline.

§ 3. Of the Three Courses of the Water

The table is set in three services, and no guest is asked to cross the water between them. The marine course is the Stenolaemata, the marine bryozoa, who hold the reef and the warm bottoms from Nassau to Key Largo; the freshwater course is the Phylactolaemata, who keep a separate table in the streams and the ponds, most noted among the house for Cristatella mucedo, the small green guest that arrives on the back of a diving duck and is never asked; and the brackish course is the Gymnolaemata, the mostly-marine bryozoa, a few members of which prefer brackish water and are served at the join, at the estuary, at the place where the tide and the river meet in the middle of the sentence.

Most of the marine course sits in the tropics, where the album was cut; but a few members of the house travel farther than any other invertebrate the committee knows. A few are found in the oceanic trenches, at the bottom of the sentence, where the light does not reach and the crowns turn anyway. And a few are found in the polar waters, which is where the book of manners meets its sister volume. The frozen shelf of that shelf — Chapter IX of The Frozen North, of Winter on the Ice, Tegetthoff and Isabella, 1872–1874 — carries its own line of lophophore: in the icebound drift of 1873, when Captain Julius Payer and Captain Karl Weyprecht sat on Franz Josef Land in the third hour of a light that never quite came, the ice that broke off the lead each morning carried, under the first fresh snow, a crust of the small green and yellow kind, alive, and the men of the drift are reported to have saluted it, because there was no one else of that much company within four hundred miles who did not change chairs.

§ 4. Of the Ledger's One Line, Kept Word for Word

The last item of the chapter is the ledger itself. When the house edition of the book was bound, in the same small hand as the names log of ARL-1037 and the platform-office papers of the Sierra & Pacific Bear Railway, one evening of table talk was ruled off on the final leaf and left with its weakness stated. The evening was the commissioning of the chapter, the table was the Marguerite's, and the hand that wrote the line is the hand that carried the stationmaster's rule across the water — the rule that a conductor only builds the line when the traffic justifies it, set down at Mammoth Whistle Stop, Field Annex No. 7, in the annex that keeps the season card in the same lockbox. The line reads, word for word:

“The loose coils rapidly straightened out in freer and brighter repartees, you never to be reading the season card the line when the traffic justifies it.”

The book's own gloss, in the smaller hand, is this: the loose coils were the evening's conversation, which began coiled, the way the current coils round a polyp, and straightened out in freer and brighter repartees once the crowns had turned; and the second clause is an instruction to the reader, standing over the season card the line holds, to the effect that you are never to be reading it as the line is read at the platform office — as the line, at the moment when the traffic justifies it, and no moment before. The committee has not parsed the join between the two clauses. By the standing rule of the shelf, which the angelfish sleeve obeys and the annex obeys and this chapter obeys, the line stands as it stands, with its weakness stated, and the page holds.

Rule the Last. An unparseable line is not corrected; it is kept, the way the crown is kept on the forehead and the way the season card is kept in the lockbox, for the day when the traffic justifies it.
