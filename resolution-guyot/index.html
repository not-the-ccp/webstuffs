Chapter the Fourteenth

Of the Tablemount & Her Keeper

Being a treatise on the Resolution Guyot — formerly Huevo — in the Mid-Pacific Mountains, the manner in which a floor is laid and kept, and the conduct owed to a mountain that has been flattened, and therefore asks nothing of you except not to pretend it chose to be.

〰  〰  〰

No room in the house is more properly kept than one laid flat. The Resolution Guyot — which the older ledgers still call Huevo, and which is set down here under both names, the first with its place of honour and the second with its date of retirement — is a guyot, or tablemount, in the underwater Mid-Pacific Mountains of the Pacific Ocean. It is a circular flat mountain, rising 500 metres (1,600 ft) above the surrounding seafloor to a depth of about 1,320 metres (4,330 ft), with a summit platform 35 kilometres (22 miles) wide. The platform is the chapter's standing lesson: it is the largest table the house knows of that was never set, and it has been kept in that condition without a single complaint on file.

I. Of the Neighbourhood, and Where to Sit

The Mid-Pacific Mountains lie west of Hawaii and northeast of the Marshall Islands, which is to say the tablemount holds its place in the middle of a very large room, and the room's other furniture keeps its distance with good manners. A visitor who takes a bearing should give the address in the order the house gives it: the mountain range first, the two islands as the room's only named neighbours second, and the depth of the water third, because the depth is the one thing a visitor cannot arrange for himself. The chapter observes that the flat is reached by descent only; there is no route along, and the etiquette of the visit is the etiquette of letting go of the route.

II. Of the Old Name

The tablemount was formerly known as Huevo, which the Spanish renders as an egg, and the house keeps the name in the ledger not as an error but as a first impression: an island that had once been round, buoyant, and above the water, remembered at a distance in the shape of an egg before the water remembered it in the shape of a floor. The renaming is the chapter's first rule, and it is a rule about the visitor rather than the mountain: a name that describes what a place has been is not corrected; it is retired. Huevo is retired with thanks, entered where it first stood, and the newer name — Resolution, taken from the ship of the survey, of whom more presently — is set beside it in the place that belongs to the one who did the work. The house does not strike the old name through. Strikethroughs are for the menu when the kitchen closes; this was never the kitchen's business.

III. Of the Survey and the Ship

The flat was laid out and measured by the surveying vessel R/V Resolution, whose sounding lines were run across the 35-kilometre platform over the winter's campaign, and whose chart table carried the working copy of the chart the house now keeps. The vessel's log, as the house reproduces it in full at the close of the chapter, contains one line that the desk has declined to emend, and it is set down here, word for word, with its original phrasing left exactly as the ship's clerk wrote it:

The ship's log, final campaign entry

She now works for a flat floor, and the lamps are put out the night in 2010.

The house's reading is the following. She is the Resolution herself, and the flat floor is the summit platform across which her last soundings were run: the ship's whole remaining work, and the work she was chartered to finish, was a floor — a level of water that answers identically in every direction, the sea's own way of confirming a table is true. And the lamps: when a chart is closed, its lamps are put out, one by one, and the night in 2010 in which the final line of soundings was taken is the night the name Resolution Guyot came to stand in the ledger in place of Huevo. The entry is not a complaint about the dark. It is the ship's signature, and the chapter sets it down the way a house sets down a signature: at the foot of the page, in the hand that made it.

IV. Of the Hotspots and the Moving Floor

The last thing the chapter owes its reader is the truth about how the flat came to sit where it sits, and the truth is that it did not, in the least, intend to. At the time of its formation the tablemount stood in the Southern Hemisphere, and it was probably raised by a hotspot in today's French Polynesia, before the plate beneath it shifted the whole building — platform, walls, and all — north into its present position. The Easter, Marquesas, Pitcairn, and Society hotspots, among others, may have been involved in the laying of the floor, and the house sets them down in that order, which is the order the plate's work was done in, and which the ledger will not rearrange for convenience. The rule that follows is the chapter's heaviest, and it is the rule every visitor to a moved thing must accept: a floor that has crossed hemispheres does not owe its present position an explanation, and the visitor does not owe it one either. The flat is flat. The rest is the plate's business.

V. Rules for the Visitor

  1. Address the place as the Resolution Guyot, and keep the older name, Huevo, in the sentence where it is owed. A mountain renamed is not a mountain replaced, and the first name goes on before the second, in the order of the water.
  2. Give the depth before you give the height. To say it rises 500 metres is true; to say it does so at a depth of about 1,320 metres is the whole truth, and the house's rule is that at the table, one takes the whole.
  3. The 35-kilometre platform is a level floor, not a view. Do not climb it, do not describe its edges as scenery, and do not stand on the flat and call out toward Hawaii, which is the one neighbour in the room that will never hear you.
  4. When you speak of its formation, name the hotspot that raised it first — the one in today's French Polynesia — and then, and only then, the plate that carried it north. Cause before carriage, always.
  5. Leave the visit as the survey ship left it: the chart closed, the lamps put out one by one, and the log ended in the clerk's own hand, with no corrections.
