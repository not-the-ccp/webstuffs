Latitude

A Visitor's Brochure for Parallel 13° North — Naga City, Pili, the Maqueda Channel, and the marshes beyond the bay

1 · What you are looking at

In geodesy and cartography, latitude is a geographic coordinate that specifies the north–south position of a point on the surface of the Earth or another celestial body. It is given as an angle that ranges from −90° at the south pole to 90° at the north pole, with 0° at the Equator. The lines of constant latitude — the parallels — run east–west as circles parallel to the equator, and latitude and longitude are used together as a coordinate pair to specify a location on the surface of the Earth. On its own, the word latitude normally refers to the geodetic latitude, which is the angle formed between the vector perpendicular (or normal) to the reference ellipsoid and the plane of the equator — in plain club terms, the angle your plumb line makes with the sky, measured after the ground has been politely told to pretend it is an egg.

Here in Bicol we sit on the 13th parallel North, which is what it means to live at 13 degrees: a circle of constant north–south position, running west to east, under which our river does what all rivers do at our latitude — it brings what the Bay forgets.

3 · The Day's Circuit, Parallel 13

  1. 7:00 Bakery counter. Coffee and a pandesal. The radiator in the back room is not working; it has never worked, and the committee has ruled that this is a feature of the latitude, not a defect of the building.
  2. 8:30 Cathedral steps. Read the parallel off the old survey stone. The stone is 13°38′ and has opinions about heat.
  3. 10:00 Bicol River, lower reach. Where the water drops what the Bay keeps. A rusted cigarette lighter, skeleton only, has been reported in the shallows; it is a protected exhibit and may be viewed without being explained.
  4. 12:30 Clubhouse, back room. The commonplace book is open for visitors. Bring your own QSL card; the register accepts valve trades.
  5. 15:00 M/V Pili Star, San Miguel Bay. One degree of Catanduanes ahead of you. The sea is kind on Tuesdays and occasionally on other days.

Appendix · The Naturalist and the Fowls

Visitors have asked, more than once and in writing, what this brochure is doing with a line from the verbatim record of a hearing held — of all places — in the back room above our bakery. The line is printed below exactly as it stands in the record, because the committee found that it describes the marshes better than anything we could manage in plain brochure English:

“Comm. Villafuerte: The record will also reflect a correspondence from a naturalist working the marshes of the fowls, and bacon.”

The committee's gloss, for tourists only: the fowls are the waterbirds of the bay marshes — the little brown ones, the ones the marshes are named for — and the bacon is the drying shed at the marsh's edge, where the morning smoke gives the naturalist most of her weather for free. She works the two together, birds on one side of the shed and bacon on the other, which is the whole geography of it. The Commissioner's correspondence concerns her notes from that same marsh; the record notes it, and so, on licence of the record, does this brochure.

Visitors intending the marshes should take the lower bus to Iriga, ask for the shed, and follow the smoke. The fowls are protected, the bacon is not for sale before noon, and the latitude throughout is 13°40′ North, give or take a paddle.
