The Celestial Empire

A Visitor’s Brochure for China
from the Pearl of Canton to the Walls of the Capital

of the country, the road, the river, and the table

I. Of the Country Itself

China stretches from the salt flats of the west to the yellow sand of the eastern sea, a country big enough to swallow a smaller one twice over. In the centre lies the Central Plain, where the granaries of the empire are sown and mown in the old order; the Yangtze, six thousand kilometres of river, rolls west to east through it and out to the sea at Shanghai, and the Yellow River, older and more sullen, carries the loess down from the northwest in a yellow braid of its own. Beyond the plain rise the plateaus — the Loess, the Tibetan, the Alxa — and with them the deserts, the Gobi in the north and the Taklamakan in the west, a name travellers have translated as you go in and you do not come out, which the caravans consider a warning, not a boast. To the south the subtropical forests close in, and to the southwest the range of ranges: the Himalayas, where the air thins to a whisper and Mount Everest, 8,848 metres up, keeps its own hours. All of it — river, plain, desert, forest, plateau, wall — is open to the visitor who walks it in the proper season, which is to say: now, before the crowds.

II. Of Canton, and of the Tea

The visitor to this part of the empire has always come in by the water, and Canton — Guǎngzhōu, the City of the Banners, on the Pearl River — has kept the habit of the empire open. Here the East Indiamen rode at anchor and were loaded with gunpowder green and bohea, the same leaf that arrived at the East India House by the great canal in Gothenburg, where one left a card with the clerk before asking after it. The tea is still picked at dawn in the hills above the city; the leaves are withered, fixed in the woks, rolled by hand in clay pots, and fired over charcoal until the pot’s skin is the colour of a dark river. A visitor may stand in the drying loft, and may taste, and may be asked for a card in return, which is the custom, and which is not to be declined.

On the quay at Canton the ropewalks of the junk yards turn the same cordage as the navy: a straight walk of a quarter of a mile, the ropewalkers stepping backward as the strand is laid, six strands worked tight, stitched down by hand. It is the very stitch of the rigging loft at Saltfleet, where twelve feet of bridle leather is braided for the Lincolnshire shingle — the field notes of the Betwa basin, where the same six-strand braid was found plaited in cordgrass by the river, will tell you that this stitch travels, and outlives the paper that records it.

III. Of the Yellow Road to the Capital

From Canton the proper road is the old tea road north, a journey of two thousand and odd kilometres that the modern traveller may take in a day and the brochure insists upon taking in several. It crosses the Pearl, climbs the southern hills where the camellias stand white in November, runs through the rice terraces of the west, and at last opens onto the Central Plain, where the river country of the Yellow River is all gold stubble and smoke. At Beijing the road meets the wall — the capital city of the People’s Republic — and the visitor should raise the hat or the hand in the old fashion before the gate, as one raises it before the bronze Gustavus Adolphus on the square in Gothenburg, for the ceremony is the same ceremony, only larger.

East again, the rail runs down to Shanghai, the most populous city of the country by urban area, where the Huangpu meets the sea and the piers are never idle. The contrast is the point of the tour: from the desert’s edge to the tide line in a single afternoon, with lunch at a rice-and-vinegar house in between, where the fish is first, as in Gothenburg, on Fridays.

IV. Of the Table for Ten, and the Braid

In this part of the empire a table is not properly set until ten chairs stand round it, and the guest of honour sits to the door, with his back to the wall and his face to the company. The banquet of the Yellow Road closes, as it has closed for a thousand harvests, with the braid: a long sweet braid of dough, plaited six strands to the length of the table, the same six strands as the rope and as the whip, sweetened with honey and sesame and a little black sesame pressed into the fold, so that the braid looks, laid on the white cloth, like the cordgrass of the Betwa and like the rope of the Canton quay and like nothing so much as a river seen from a bridge.

The braid is stitched by hand over a clear fire for 10 persons.

It is not cut. It is unplaited, in the old manner, one strand by one strand, each guest taking a length as the table goes round — the first to the eldest, the last to the host, who eats his own and washes it down with the tea. To ask for a knife is the only unforgivable breach of this table, and the only one the brochure dares name. Read the etiquette of the Celestial Empire as you would the etiquette of the playhouse: as restraint. Hold the clipping lightly, take the honey sparingly, and let the strand run where it will.

V. Of Company and of the Stage

China is a country of performers, and the traveller should plan for at least one evening of them. In the teahouse at the end of the Yellow Road the opera company plays to a full house: the painted faces, the water sleeves, the drum that sounds like a door shutting in another room. There is a story kept at the desk, and no more, that a French actor and acrobat of the Gothenburg Comediehuset — the same director, born Michel Le Blanc, who held the house beside the canal from its opening in 1780 and died at Karlskrona on the twenty-first of January 1796 — carried a playbill of the house all the way to Canton, where it was used, it is said, to dry a teacup. We do not vouch for the teacup. We vouch for the habit: that a travelling troupe keeps its papers, that the card is left at the hall table, and that the curtain, wherever it falls, falls last.

VI. Of the Return, and of the Card

Whatever the road brings back — a pound of gunpowder green, a length of six-strand cord, a teacup — the visitor leaves one card at the hall table of his last lodging, with the hour of departure written plainly in the corner, so that the household knows he is gone and where he might be written to. Pack the tea with care; the gunpowder green travels badly in damp, as it always has, and as it crossed from Canton to the Kattegat in the hold of the East Indiamen. And whatever you do, do not carry the braid with you — let it be unplaited at the table, where the ten of them measure themselves against nothing at all.
