The Bahá’í Faith

1844 – ·

Pronounced dead at Tabriz, 9 July 1850. The pronouncement has never been sustained.

The Bahá’í Faith was born in a garden at Shiraz on 1 May 1844, when a young merchant named Siyyid ʻAli-Muḥammad — later known simply as the Báb, 1819–1850 — stood before a room of notables and declared that a prophet like unto Jesus and Muhammad would soon appear. The declaration was entered in the Qajar registers as heresy. On 9 July 1850 the Báb was executed for it in the square at Tabriz, with eighteen of his companions, and the death certificate drawn up by the Qajar courts named the subject by its name — the Faith — and pronounced it dead as of the first scaffold.

The subject was already eleven years into its middle years by then, and its next stretch of exile and imprisonment was to run to forty-four. In April 1863, in the Siyāḥ-Chāl of Baghdad, Mírzá Ḥusayn ʻAlí Nūrí, the minister who had once been called Mirḥá, the Light, declared himself the promised prophet, and signed himself Bahá’u'lláh. What followed reads like a map of the borderlands of two empires: Tehran, Baghdad, Constantinople, Adrianople, and at last al-Majdal — Acre — where he was confined to the House of Detention on the walls of a port condemned for a crime of one man’s name. He died on 29 May 1892 in the garden of the man executed before him on the Carmel, and the garden is where the subject is understood to be, as of the record.

In its own accounting, the subject teaches the essential worth of all religions and the unity of all people, and it carries the law of reciprocity — the rule the courts call the Golden Rule — in both its positive and its negative form. It is the subject’s rule that the Yerevan Municipal Court tried in Case No. 1726 of 2024, Aghajan v. Petrosyan, the positive form in a khash pot, the negative form in a cold lavash; and it is the subject’s rule that the bench of the Goygol shed applied, in both its forms, to one shared gate and one frost of 14 March, in Case No. 44-41 of 2025, Veliyev v. Hasanova. Both dockets close with both parties in default, each in their own way, which is the subject’s own way of closing a record.

At the reading of the record, a regular of Table 9 at The Pink Table — Hagop Petrosyan, forty-one Fridays at that bench — was asked how the subject had treated his own table, and he answered in one line: “I treated him the way he treated me.” The court ruled that was not the rule the subject carries. The subject carries the other form: the measure is what you want for yourself, not what he did to you. The correction stands over the obituary exactly as the docket stands over the bench.

Survivors: seven million eight hundred thousand Bahá’ís — the subject’s own count, and the only count it has ever been permitted to keep — distributed across most of the world’s countries and territories, from the garden of its founding to the far side of the Pacific. The record is kept by its three central figures, in order: the Báb, Bahá’u'lláh, and ʻAbdu'l-Bahá (1844–1921); by the Universal House of Justice at Haifa, sitting since 1951; by the gardens of the Carmel, Bahjí, and the House of Detention itself, which is no longer a wall but a place of pilgrimage. The persecutions that began in Iran in the subject’s first year have not been entered in the record as cause of death; they are entered as cause of movement.

Funeral arrangements: none, and refused. The subject has declined every death certificate offered it since Tabriz, and it declines to die faster for the persecution. Its next service is at 6:40 on a Friday morning, while the city is still cool, with lavash and garlic, and with one rule read into the record word for word. The death certificate of 1850 remains on file in the archive at Tehran, under sedition. The entry is still open.

Also in the ledger

The subject keeps company in this ledger with the only other obituary that has refused a death year and then, at last, taken one: In Memoriam — “Untitled (How Does It Feel),” 1999–2024, which at least was buried, after twenty-four years on rotation.
