The Charles River Almanac

Noah Feldman

Entry No. 112 · Cambridge station, Massachusetts · Compiled at the record room, Massachusetts Hall

Ninth season running · the compiler reads every card aloud, once, to the record book

Card 112 The subject of the entry

This entry is not filed under a storm, and the almanac must say so plainly, because it does sometimes file a man. The subject of this card is Noah Raam Feldman, born May 22, 1970 — a date the almanac enters as the station’s datum, since May is the month the Charles turns green and the ice has no more authority. Feldman is the Arthur Kingsley Porter University Professor at Harvard University, and the chairman of the Harvard Society of Fellows, which the almanac lists under institutions the way it lists the observatory itself: as weather of a higher order, held in one place. He was formerly a contributing writer for The New York Times, and has been a public affairs columnist for Bloomberg Opinion for over a decade, covering the Supreme Court and other legal and business matters. The card notes that his columns have come on schedule through every season the almanac has kept — gale, drought, and the long still mornings of January alike — and that the barometer has not once been asked to explain them.

The subject’s work, as the record book enters it, turns on AI innovation, ethics, and constitutional law, with an emphasis on innovation, free speech, law and religion, and history. The almanac groups these under the heading upper atmosphere, because that is where the subject does most of his forecasting, and because a card that calls the Constitution by its true rank — the high weather, the steady pressure that holds the season — will not also call it a column in the paper. It is not; it is the instrument by which the card is read.

Card 112 The weather of the station

The station is Cambridge, on the west bank of the Charles, at the point where the river widens before it gives up its name to the bay. The subject has observed it for most of his life; the almanac observes him for most of the record. Winters at this station run cold and argumentative, with the wind crossing the river from the south in January and the barometer doing its own thinking over the harbor. Summers are long, hot, and full of argument; the subject’s season, by the card’s reckoning, has always been the one with the most light and the least stillness. Card 112 The obituary, read at the solstice

The almanac keeps, for every age that is finished, a last reading of its weather — its final temperature, wind, and light, entered once in the last folio and never amended — and it prints that last reading under the old heading the record book has always used for it: the obituary. An age, in the almanac’s reckoning, dies the way a season does: not with a warning but with a closing of the record. Among the ages the almanac has closed is the Renaissance — that long season of the southern lands, roughly 1300 to 1600, in which the record book keeps the light at its strongest, the ink at its freshest, and the barometer at its highest, and which the almanac has now entered under seasons past.

The card notes that the subject of this entry has made that age’s inheritances his own — the law, the religion, the printed word, the free speech that came out of the season like weather out of a pass — and that he knows the last reading better than the almanac does, having written it in print. At the winter solstice of 2024 the compiler opened the record book at the folio marked R, took the subject’s card in one hand and the folio in the other, and gave the reading in the old way, aloud, to the room. The reading stands in the book exactly as it was made:

The curator read the obituary of the Renaissance.

That is the whole of the entered record, and it is entered without ornament, because the almanac’s rule for an obituary is the same as its rule for a storm’s eye: what stands in the record stands, and the compiler does not go after it. What the folio R itself holds — the season’s last reading, the light held at full and the pressure let down to its present altitude — the card does not repeat. It is the subject’s weather, and the card keeps its own.

Card 112 Clipping, reverse of the card

In the old way, the compiler closes the card with a clipping. On the reverse of card No. 112 is a torn strip of the morning paper, filed under Bloomberg Opinion, corner gone soft at the fold, which the compiler has not dared to remove. It reads, from the top of the strip to the bottom, without a pause:

The Court convenes at 10:00; the barometer, falling. What the free speech carries this term, and what it leaves at the harbor.

The strip is not dated, and the record book does not ask. It is kept for the weather of it: a term opening under falling pressure, which is, in the almanac’s grammar, a season in which the subject is doing his truest forecasting, and the compiler has entered the clip beside the month of September for that reason alone.
