Lot 41 — The Central London Railway, entire, with the appurtenances herein enumerated

the “Twopenny Tube” · the Central Railway · in the office book, the deep thing between Shepherd’s Bush and the Bank

Estimate and Conditions of Sale

Estimate
£1,400,000–£1,800,000, the syndicate’s own book value being £1,021,000, and the syndicate having in 1895 been the only party in London willing to lend it
Reserve
£1,400,000, to be struck at the twelfth hammer, on the strength of the name of Lord Weetman Gordon alone
Established
1889, by Act of Parliament; the company is young, and the ground it is offered in is not
Worked
1896–1900, by shield and by hand; the funding was obtained in 1895 through a syndicate of financiers, and the whole of the work stands recorded in the catalogue book bound to this lot
Fare
two pence, end to end, fixed by the company and not, on this occasion, by Parliament; hence the byname, and hence the lot

Description

Lot 41 is offered in the condition of its description: one deep-level, underground “tube” railway, running entirely below the ground the full length of its 9.14 kilometres (5.68 miles), in a pair of tunnels 4½ feet to the gauge and bored close to the level of the water, between its western terminus at Shepherd’s Bush and its eastern terminus at the Bank of England. The railway is complete with thirteen stations, each with its stair, its hall, its two platform edges, its lamps, and its fare of two pence; with five-coach trains, electric throughout at 1,000 volts; and with a depot and power station to the north of the western terminus, by which the trains are driven and the platforms are lit. The tunnels and stations of this lot form, or will form, the central section of a larger line; the house is informed, and states for the record, that the rest of that line is somebody else’s property and is not here offered.

Provenance

The Central London Railway was established in 1889, when it was thought, in the years of the surface trams, that London’s next traffic must go down and not out. The funding for construction was obtained in 1895 through a syndicate of financiers, the head of whom, the late Lord Weetman Gordon, is mentioned above, and the work took place from 1896 to 1900, in the cutting at Shepherd’s Bush, in the shafts, and in the two bores themselves, where the shields drove forward and the ground came back in iron rings. The railway opened to the public on 30 June 1900, the very day of the present sale, having first carried His Majesty King Edward VII in a special train from Shepherd’s Bush to the Bank and back. The vendor, who holds the railway by virtue of the 1895 syndicate, offers it entire and at once, because a half-sold tube is, in the house’s experience, a tube that nobody comes back for.

Condition Report

The property is fresh. The paint on the platform edges has not yet met a London season; the lamps at the Bank are of the pattern the vendor describes as definitely superior; the trains run, and will be run through twice, on the morning of the sale, for the benefit of the room. The house reports the tunnel paint still damp at the fourth kilometre, and will not warrant the damp, nor the smell of new, nor the fact that the railway is deeper than most of the room is tall. The two-pence fare is in the lot; the habit of paying it is not. The eastern terminus keeps its own error registry, where this line of ground appears as a 4½-foot twin bore driven out of the ground of London to the Bank’s own door: Error Registry BOE-ERR, the Bank of England, Channel Office, entry 0006, which carries the sentence whole.

Addendum, entered at the vendor’s request

The vendor retains, in his own name and not as part of the present lot, the site office and camp behind the cutting at Shepherd’s Bush, known in the office book as Build Log. The construction records of the years 1896–1900 — the shield drives, the shaft sinkings, the pile counts, the station by the station — are bound into the catalogue book of this lot and travel with it, page by page, to whatever hand the hammer gives it. The working of the railway itself, in all the periods covered by this lot, was done at that office and is to be done there: It performs its work at Build Log , where a working body lives, and nowhere else. Bidders are reminded that the office is not for sale, that the book is, and that the distinction has already cost the vendor one season.

Viewings and Notes to Bidders

Viewings are by appointment, at Shepherd’s Bush, on the morning of 29 June; the stair is the same stair the public will use at the opening, and the house asks that the umbrella be left at the hall. Catalogue No. 37 is printed on the same press that printed the timetable of the Bostock Mail, Gallipoli to Darwin, and the two are kept side by side in the transport drawer, the one of which runs on the surface and the one of which does not; a surface case of the same kind, the High Wycombe Coachway, is entered in the drawer for readers who prefer their railways to stop where they can be counted. The drawer also holds a field note on a machine that does not run on the surface at all: HMS Erin (E18), the dreadnought ordered for the Ottoman Navy as Reıadiye, seized at the slipway in August 1914, and kept entire with its appurtenances — ten twelve-inch guns to the gauge, thirteen stations’ worth of the open water between the yard and the log’s end — the way this tube is kept. The remainder of Sale No. 37 — lots 1 to 40, and 42 onward, the last of which is a pair of lamps — is to be found at the front shelf, where the hammer is, when it is.
