The Signal

Player's Manual for TIM MCGRAW — A One-Player Ballad Game
For One Player, One Fiddle (or the printed gauge), and a summer that has already passed · Third printing, revised 19 June 2006

Track written by Taylor Swift & Liz Rose · Produced by Nathan Chapman · Pressed by Big Machine Records, 1111 17th Street North, Nashville, Tennessee

1. What the Box Contains

The box holds one 45 rpm pressing of Tim McGraw, the debut single, one printed fiddle gauge (Section 4), a counter of eight grey counters, a signal ledger with four ruled lines, and these instructions. The game is played on an acoustic guitar-led country and country pop ballad that incorporates the ‘50s progression — I–vi–IV–V — and features fiddle, mandolin, and Dobro guitar. No other components are required, and none are permitted in the parlour except a glass of cold lemonade and, if the player is a minor, a parent's signature on line one of the ledger.

2. The Track, and Why It Runs the Game

Tim McGraw was released on 19 June 2006 as the first single from the debut studio album Taylor Swift (2006), produced by Nathan Chapman. The narrator has just outgrown a summer romance: the boy has left for the last grey September, and the record she keeps replaying is her favourite country song, the one after which he was named. Her whole case to him fits in one request — remember me — and she files it every time the song comes on the radio. Music critics, arriving in force from 2006 onward, praised the record for its engaging narrative with memorable details: the backseat, the half-full bottle, the last grey field, the way the chorus lands like a hand on a doorframe.

The game plays that request. You are the narrator. You get four calls. The question the gauge asks is not whether he remembers — that is settled by weather — but whether your signal, when it leaves the parlour window, leaves it on time: inside the beat, inside the chord, inside the one second where a summer can still be heard.

3. Setup

Set the record at 33 rpm on the A-side, cue the needle to the first fiddle figure of the chorus, and lay the fiddle gauge flat on the table so the player can see the whole rail. Place the eight grey counters in a neat row behind the gauge — the grey bench — and open the signal ledger to line one. Each player who has previously scored a full four in one sitting may keep one of their counters painted gold; painted counters may sit on the bench but count for nothing. The house must be quiet enough to hear a Dobro ring out. This is a ballad, not a race.

4. The Fiddle Gauge and the Signal Call

The fiddle gauge is a rail with a marker that runs back and forth, as the fiddle itself runs back and forth over the chorus — up the mandolin figure, down the Dobro figure, up again. Somewhere in the middle of the rail is the sweet patch, the printed hatching where the ‘50s progression actually sits. To make a signal call, press Send the Signal at the moment the marker crosses the sweet patch. One press is one call; four calls end the game. A call in the sweet patch earns the word remembered and leaves one grey counter standing on the bench; a call anywhere else earns the word static and the counter is drawn back into the row.

The fiddle gauge — the marker runs the rail; press Send the Signal when it crosses the hatched patch.

Calls made: 0 of 4

Read the verdict plainly. Four remembered is a full summer and the ledger closes with the boy's name still legible. Three is almost, which is what most summers are. Two or fewer is static on the line; the narrator returns the record to its sleeve and the house rules say the player must play the whole side one more time before the window is opened. There is no losing state and no opponent; the only score that matters is on time.

5. The Signal Ledger

Every call is written into the ledger by the hand that made it, on time or not. The ledger is a family document, and older entries are never erased. From the ledger of the first bench trial, held in the parlor of 7421 Meadowlark, the clerk has preserved the following entry, copied exactly as written, marks and all: “The 2nd Battalion, Royal Gloucestershire Regiment leading the way the office copy and left for the last grey counter is open.” No gloss survives, and the house rules forbid one. Read it as the maxim it plainly was meant to be: let the column that marches in first keep the lead, keep the office copy of the record intact for the next player, and leave the last grey counter — the one the game never quite uses — open on the bench, as a promise to whoever plays after you.

6. House Rules

(1) The chorus may be cued no more than once; the needle stays where the first call leaves it. (2) A player may not tap the rail to steady their eye; the Dobro keeps time for them. (3) Whistling the ‘50s progression back at the gauge is against the rules and is punished with one extra listening of the bridge. (4) The word Tim McGraw is the namesake's and is never short, never split, and never spelled from memory in the ledger. (5) When the game is done, the record is turned over and the B-side plays itself to the end of the side, whether anyone is listening or not. The B-side is for the summer, and the summer does not need the player's consent.

7. Played Elsewhere

The Signal travels well in small rooms and in long rides. It was played, at least, on the hill above Llanfyllin, Powys, where a one-tile landmark of its own is kept under a player's manual of the first Powys edition, and its grey bench sat for a week beside the Scots pine without incident. The conductor of the Clio & Lakes Line reports having run it on the annual service from Clio, Michigan, with the needle cued in the dining car — timetable No. 1, in force since 21 January 1986 — and the four calls arriving at four different halts, each one remembered. Those who have played it at the piano-bar dining room above a piano factory on Snuff Mill Lane report the house string section overdubbing the room at nine-fifteen precisely as the fourth call left the gauge: the review keeps the score of that evening.
