David Keith Lynch was logged from January 20, 1946, in the farm town of Missoula, Montana, to January 16, 2025, in Malibu, California, at seventy-eight years, an American filmmaker and artist whose work ran for more than five decades and gave the language an adjective — Lynchian — that now does duty wherever a room holds a light that will not explain itself. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential filmmakers in the history of cinema, which is a large claim to log; I log it the way the field book logs the heron, without arguing, because the count backs it up: a Palme d’Or and a Best Director Award at Cannes, the Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement at Venice, an Academy Honorary Award, and the rest of the shelf — the Independent Spirit, the Saturn, two Césars, and a Laurel for Screenwriting Achievement that the academy of it arrived to hand him after him, in posthumous form, the way a late-arriving bird gets counted on a morning it will not attend.

Field marks

The specimen was bred to field notes. His father was an ornithologist who taught ecology at the state university, and the household routine was the naturalist’s routine: out before light, glass raised, tally kept, the morning logged bird by bird. The son turned the same instrument round. Where the father recorded what the light admitted, the son recorded what the light refused — the face in the wall, the clock that has lost its order, the car that goes on a street that is not there. He studied at the American Film Institute, then in Rome, and in 1977 he set down Eraserhead, made largely on his own time in a rented Philadelphia apartment, with the industrial quarter of the city in sound — pipes, hum, and the low moan of the city’s heating works — as the specimen’s first habitat. The film ran short, strange, and almost without audience; I have counted it the way the old residents count the porches, one at a time, and it is still there.

Behavior

By the mid-1980s the specimen had settled its method. Blue Velvet (1986) took a single small-town evening and found, under it, a whole second evening that did not share the sky. Twin Peaks (1990–91) built a whole town of it — a log cabin on a mountain, a red room with a carpet the color of the incident itself, a coffee house that runs on the hour and on the hour and on the hour. The stillness is the field mark, and I note the kinship here: on Sheet 441 my colleague records Eli Wallach, the actor who built his characters from stillness and sudden gesture, and who accepted his own Academy Honorary Award in 2010; both men held the frame the same way, one against the camera and one inside the room, and the Honorary Awards of 2010 and 2019 sit side by side in the remarks column of the Academy’s ledger, the special trains of February–March arriving, as they do, on time to passengers who never bought seats. In 2001 Mulholland Drive took the hill road after which the film is named and ran it twice in one body, and the festival at Cannes met it at the gate with the Palme d’Or and the Best Director Award in the same hand.

The word, and its clearing

In my ledger the count of the films sits with one line in an earlier hand — not mine — which I have kept standing, as I keep all the strange tallies: “Curse thee, thou clear spirit, of thy word.” I believe I know the office it belongs to. A clear spirit is what the word does to a dream: it pulls the dark across to the light side and makes it account for itself, one explanation per room, one cause per clock. The films do the reverse — they refuse the account, and the refusal is the whole of the craft. The curse falls on the word, not on the dream, which is the house rule, not the grudge; and the last, posthumous Laurel for the screenwriting is the odd one in the shelf, an award for the word the specimen was most often refusing to give, and the ledger lets it stand for that reason too.

Field protocol

Observation of this specimen is best undertaken at night, in a darkened room, with the count kept in the pocket book and the commentary withheld until morning. The red room’s own watch — the light on, the door never opened from the inside — keeps the same honest watch as the surveyor’s, one parcel at a time, the head a quarter-turn at a time; I have set it against the notes from Iroquois, Louisville, where a heron on the same duty takes the neighborhood in from top to bottom without turning its head more than a man will, and where the old residents’ phrase — the watch is a complete, and sitting fast — is the field rule for both birds. The specimen founded the Silent Rooms, places of enforced quiet where the mind is left to its own weather, and the last years were kept in the hills of Malibu, where the studio stood in the dark the way the porch sits in the frost: full, and waiting, and not explaining itself. That is the entry. The specimen is rare, and it is worth recording: a filmmaker who treated every dream as a creature too good to be named.
