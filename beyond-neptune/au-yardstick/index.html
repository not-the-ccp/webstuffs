The Yardstick

On the astronomical unit, symbol au (historically AU) — the stick this volume has been using to measure everything all along without ever introducing it. Chapter 07 of the Outer Ring Division’s station manual, kept alongside the field notes beyond Neptune, which deploy this chapter some forty times between Berlin 1846 and the cold shelf, 44 to 48 units from the Sun.

§ 1 · Definitions

The Stick, Laid Down

The astronomical unit is a unit of length defined as exactly 149,597,870,700 metres. The definition is exact: no decimal, no “approximately,” no averaging clause. The number is not a measurement; it is a laying-down. A surveyor who knows the metre and the figure knows the stick, anywhere, at any temperature, and may carry it past the ninth planet without the wood warping.

Before 2012 the stick was a moving thing, and the honest definition was an average: the unit was conceived as the average Earth–Sun distance — the mean of Earth’s aphelion and perihelion, the far end and the near end of the Earth’s own ellipse, added and halved every year as the orbit performed. That average served the tables well, and it served this volume well, but an average is a weather report, and a yardstick is a contract. The 2012 redefinition ended the wandering: the au was fixed to the metre, and the Earth–Sun distance was released from its duty of being constant. It is allowed, henceforth, to vary within the unit, running from 0.9833 at perihelion to 1.0167 at aphelion — the old ends of the ellipse now measured in the new stick, the way the ring measures its specimens in the units those same specimens orbit in. The Division keeps both definitions in the manual, the way the volume keeps the 1915 carbon: the historical one as a record of intent, the exact one as the working instrument.

§ 2 · Field history

Used Before Defined

The stick predates its own definition the way the territory predates its survey. The mean distances of the planets were printed in units long before the unit had a metre value; the tables ran Mercury, 0.39; Venus, 0.72; Earth, 1.00; Mars, 1.52; Jupiter, 5.2; Saturn, 9.5; Uranus, 19.2; and then, on 23 September 1846, Berlin added its line — Neptune, first sighted within one degree of Le Verrier’s mark, at some 30 units from the Sun. The naturalist’s habit began that night, as the volume’s first season records, and the stick was the habit’s first tool: the plates measure in seconds of arc, and only the au turns a second of arc at a known range into a distance in the field.

By the night of 18 February 1930 the stick had been used once more in the same territory: Tombaugh’s body, announced in March at roughly 33 units, later catalogued with a perihelion near 29.7 and an aphelion near 49.3 — the specimen of the ring was, in the very first entry of its file, a statement in au. The Kuiper belt the surveys have since mapped lies scattered from about 30 to 50, with the long thin stream of the scattered disc running past 100 and Eris, the specimen the catalogues spent a decade deciding was and was not a planet, swinging between 38 and 98. Every one of those figures is the same figure used at different points on the same stick, and the stick does not argue. It has never argued. That is the whole of its character.

§ 3 · Service use

Where It Is Carried

The unit is used primarily for measuring distances within the Solar System or around other stars. Those two duties are the manual’s whole service table. Within the system it is the natural size: a kilometre is a road, an au is a weather, and the ring keeps its ledgers in weather. Around other stars it is the unit of structure — the separation of the primary from its planets, the spacing of the discs, the small frozen bodies counted in the dark — because a star system is an Earth–Sun system by analogy, and the analogy only works if the stick is carried across.

It is also a fundamental component in the definition of another unit of astronomical length, the parsec: the distance at which one astronomical unit subtends an angle of one arcsecond, or 648000/π au, which works out to about 206,265 of them, roughly 3.26 light-years. The parsec is the au read through a very long telescope, and a star catalogue is the ring’s ledgers translated into that reading. For the field hand, the unit’s third service is the light count: one au is approximately equivalent to 499 light-seconds, a little under 8 minutes 19 seconds of light travel. When the plates come down from the vault on Thursdays and the far ring is counted again from the surface up, the field lamp light in the image left the Sun nearly nine minutes before the emulsion caught it. The Division keeps a timer for the figure; the count is below, and it reads 499 when it is finished, the way every entry in these books ends where it began.

§ 4 · The unrecovered line

Of the Shelf, in the Dark

The volume keeps one line of the 1915 carbon that was never recovered; this chapter keeps the Division’s own, a provision fragment from the dome shelf, written in the same carbon hand and filed beside the crow entry because the two lines do their work in the same hour. The fragment is preserved below exactly as the station prints it, meaning never restored:

Hay-tea and linseed-jelly are also manifest in the dark.

The station’s standing reading, offered as a reading and not a record, is this: the night sentry logs what is manifest on the dome shelf — what the field lamp catches and what it leaves — and on the cold nights of the Flagstaff season the shelf does indeed give up its two jars, the hay-tea in the tall one and the linseed-jelly in the flat one, the way the shelf gives up the blink comparator and the plates. The also is the log’s way of recording that the shelf was already doing its own work, the same way the crows were already keeping the hatch shuttered; the entry does not claim the dark, it only notes what the dark is willing to show. The naturalist allows it, as the volume allows the crow: the ring is read in the dark, the shelf is read in the dark, and the two are the same reading — a list of what survives the light, kept in carbon, held exactly. The dark, on the shelf and in the sky, is the unit’s own service condition: the au is the length of a distance that is only ever known by the time the light takes, and the light, on those nights, is a field lamp.

§ 5 · Standing orders

Orders of the Chapter

Order one: carry the stick with the plates. Every distance in the Beyond Neptune volume is this chapter in the field — Neptune at 30, the ring at 44 to 48, the light running its nine minutes — and a reader who keeps the two pages open together holds the whole instrument and the whole ledger at once, the yardstick and the measurements it was made to make. Order two: at the end of the route, look up. The last stop of The Steakley Walk is the overlook bench, mile 2.1, where the date is read aloud and the route turns around; on clear nights the same bench faces the far ring, and the distance from the bench to the Kuiper belt is about 6,600 au, which is to say the light in the walkers’ field of view left the Sun some six and three-quarter hours before it reached the bench — the walk reads the date, and this chapter reads the distance, the two making a pair the way the exchange shelf and the orchards do. Order three: read the carbon lines exactly as printed, and let the dark keep what the dark keeps. Order four: the stick is exact, the territory is patient, and the season turns whether the boats like it or not.
