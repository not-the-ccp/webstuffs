In the United States District Court for the District of Maryland · Northern Division · Courtroom Three, Bethesda, Maryland

No. 1:88-cv-0044 — In re the Fifty-Second Jar of Vallecito-Grade Marsh Salt

The Harbour Line table games, plaintiff, and the Bayfront Institute of Environmental Sequence, defendant. Hon. Ada F. Merrick presiding. Clerk: Ms. Pearl Sandoval.

Excerpt from the record · Twelfth day of trial · Tuesday, March 14, 1989 · 9:41 a.m. to 10:03 a.m. · Fols. 42:11 – 42:39

Fol. 42:11

(The clerk asks the court to rise. The Hon. Ada F. Merrick takes the bench, a small oilskin book before her, which she does not open.)

Ms. Sandoval, Clerk. — All rise. The court is in session, in the matter of the fifty-second jar.

The Court. — Be seated. I will state the case once, and the clerk will correct me if I am wrong. The Harbour Line table games, of Solano County, California, says that the brine culture in the fifty-second jar of Vallecito-grade marsh salt — raked, dried, and pressed in the Solano marshes the spring of 1986, and kept, lid off, for the dinner round of a four-player game called The Perplexa — is a trade secret. The Bayfront Institute of Environmental Sequence says that, under the statute, the DNA sequence of that culture belongs in the federal repository, and that the only repository Congress saw fit to build is in this courthouse, four floors up, where it has been since 1988. That is the case. A small dispute about a large building.

Mr. Crane, for the plaintiff. — Your Honor, the jar is not a database. The jar is a jar. One jar a week for a year; the last one numbered 52, which is why the tide deck has 52 cards, and no one in the family will say otherwise. It sits on the table with the almanac. It wins the dinner round. It has never been accessioned.

The Court. — Ms. Vance.

Ms. Vance, for the defendant. — It has been sequenced, Your Honor, under a federal grant, and the sequence exists, which is a fact the statute does not allow to be a dead letter. It has to live somewhere, and it is not going to live in a drawer. The Institute notes, for the record, that it will never live in the same drawer as the banquet book, because the drawer in question is already under order.

(The Court signs its name to the minute book. Dr. Henry L. Fisk, curator of accession records, is sworn.)

Fol. 42:19

The Court. — Dr. Fisk, for the record: what is the building four floors up?

Dr. Fisk. — The National Center for Biotechnology Information, Your Honor. The NCBI is part of the National Library of Medicine, a branch of the National Institutes of Health; it is approved and funded by the government of the United States, and it is located, as the court will have noticed, in Bethesda, Maryland. It was founded in 1988, through legislation sponsored by congressman Claude Pepper.

The Court. — And what does it do, in one sentence, if I may?

Dr. Fisk. — The NCBI houses a series of databases relevant to biotechnology and biomedicine, and is an important resource for bioinformatics tools and services. Major databases include GenBank, for DNA sequences, and PubMed, a bibliographic database for biomedical literature; among the others is the NCBI Epigenomics database. The sequence of a brine culture, when it is deposited, goes to GenBank, takes an accession number, and becomes a matter of record.

Ms. Vance. — Dr. Fisk, has the Institute any objection to the sequence of the fifty-second jar living there rather than in a drawer?

Dr. Fisk. — No objection, Your Honor. I have seen records kept with considerably less care.

Fol. 42:24

(Dr. Adaeze Okafor, an aromatics chemist retained by the plaintiff, is sworn. She has a notebook. It is not the almanac.)

The Court. — Dr. Okafor, you have examined the plaintiff's field notebook, in which the owner of the salt kept the marsh's weather. Your testimony, please.

Dr. Okafor. — The salt is a sponge of the air, Your Honor. It takes up the smell of the marsh the way a cloth takes up a room. That is the whole of the plaintiff's position and of its chemistry. I should note, for the record, that the notebook contains one line which I was asked to explain. I will read it exactly as written, word for word.

(Dr. Okafor reads from card 7-B of the field notebook.)

"Coffee readily imbibes exhalations from other vegetables are very different, although its fruit very slowly."

Mr. Crane. — May I, Your Honor.

The Court. — Proceed.

Mr. Crane. — I do not know what that sentence means, Your Honor, and I do not believe my opponent does either. It is not evidence of anything except, possibly, the marsh itself.

Dr. Okafor. — It is a field note about the marsh breathing. The salt imbibes; that much is the chemistry, and it is good chemistry. The rest is the owner's hand and not mine, and I was asked to explain the marsh, not the syntax.

The Court. — The line is entered into the record as written, word for word, card 7-B. It is not evidence of intent. It is evidence of the marsh. Next.

Fol. 42:31

(Mr. Tom Blackburn, Master of the Household, is sworn. He brings the almanac and sets it, closed, on the well.)

The Court. — Mr. Blackburn, the almanac.

Mr. Blackburn. — The duty almanac, Your Honor. It calls the tides, the frost, and the hour at which the glass lifts. The watch reads: high water at eleven; the gate opens at one; the perplexing slug is paid in tides and never in hurry. By the record it was never wrong.

Ms. Vance. — Mr. Blackburn, you understand that the jar in this case has never been stored in the same drawer as the banquet book?

Mr. Blackburn. — Never once. The salt jar is on its own shelf, lid off, where the dinner round can reach it. That is a house rule and it is an old one. I will say this for the animal the game is named for: the slug changes its mind with the water, and it has never, in my experience, changed its mind on the record.

Ms. Vance. — And the forty-fourth turn?

Mr. Blackburn. — The tide deck runs out at the forty-fourth turn, and the board passes to the dinner round whatever the board says. The board is not the last word. It never is. I suppose the record is the same, in its way.

Fol. 42:36

The Court. — I have heard enough, and the court orders. The sequence of the culture of the fifty-second jar of Vallecito-grade marsh salt shall be deposited with the National Center for Biotechnology Information, in GenBank, under accession number 044-52, and shall be available to the public on the expiration of a one-year embargo, at which point it is a matter of public record and no one's secret, including the family's. The jar itself is, and remains, the property of the Harbour Line table games, lid off, on its own shelf, and never in the same drawer as the banquet book. The line of card 7-B stands as entered: as written, word for word, evidence of the marsh, not to be parsed further by counsel or anyone else in this courthouse. The almanac was never wrong, and this order will not be the first thing in the record that claims to be. It is 10:03. Court is adjourned.

(The clerk asks the court to rise. Dr. Fisk, at the well, writes a number in his book and turns it over to the clerk. It is 044-52.)
