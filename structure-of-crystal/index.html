Festival Notebook · Warsaw · 1969

Struktura krysztaĺu

Saturday, 27 September 1969 · Kinoteatr Moskwa, 19:20 · rain

Eleven days on the festival calendar and I have used nine of them inside cinemas. Today is the last. Włodek is in Kraków on his verse business, pacing the four-line stanza he is building in the shed behind the bakery — the whole build is logged in The Quatrain — Build Log, and he wrote to me that a diary entry is a stanza too, which I did not believe until I sat down. He let me out of the four-line rule half an hour later. So: the Moskwa, seven twenty, Struktura kryształu, directed by Krzysztof Zanussi, his first feature film, and a room that was not full.

The film is about an engineer named Marek. He works in an office doing calculations, he has a daughter, and he is trying to leave the life the calculations are for, which is harder than the calculations. He meets a woman, Wanda, in a corridor off the factory hall, and the two of them move through a flat in Mokotów, a hospital stair, a bar, a train. There is a bar scene — the one the whole city is already talking about — where a man stands over another man with a knife, and the camera does not leave. I looked at the room and no one moved; twenty-one of us held our breath, or at least I did. That is the scene I will defend for the rest of my life if I have to.

The title is not a metaphor for anything, and yet the whole film behaves like a crystal. Every shot sits in the next one at a small angle, and the angles, taken together, are a shape — the shape of a society you can hold in your hand and turn. Zanussi is thirty-four, and I did not expect a debut to hold a room in that way. A first film should be a first film. His is not.

I keep a tally of these rooms, in the left margin, the way my grandfather kept his stock of nails. The tally line for tonight, in my cramped hand, reads exactly: “A glove raised over the shoulder to make a room of twenty-one; acceptable, within drift.” The glove was the usher’s, raised over the shoulder to shut the heavy door while I counted; twenty-one was the number of heads, mine included; acceptable, and within drift of the twenty-five the projectionist’s slip had forecast. I have written better forecasts. I have written worse rooms.

On the way out the list by the ticket desk has come up, and Struktura kryształu is named for the Mermaid, the festival’s first prize, the Syrenka the whole city keeps in a jar on the river. I stood under the list for a minute without reading it a second time. Włodek, who is a sentimental man about stanzas and not about films, will not believe I cried a little, and he will be right.

What I will remember, a week from now, will not be the crystal or the knife. It will be that the room was twenty-one. In the obituary file Włodek keeps by his ledger I am storing a line out of John Withington (1865–1944) — Obituary, the mason-mayor of Orange who died in 1944 with a sentence no one in New South Wales could explain — “I’ll keep an abbey short of psalters all winter” — which is the same kind of line as my tally: a man’s count of his world, written short, refused its own explanation. I put my tally beside Withington’s line. Two counts, a quarter of a century apart, and neither of them explained.

Rain stopped at eleven. Walking home past the print shop on Złota I counted the lit shop windows (four) and the trams (three, one of them empty). Tomorrow I go back to the university, back to the long film, and the festival is over. The crystal stays.

Tally for the night — 21 heads · 1 knife · 1 Mermaid · rain, then none
