On the paper for the Michaelmas term: “The Irene Incident, Bias Bay, 1927”

Submitted by Mr. E. F. C. Halloway, Master of History · entered in the red book of 24 November 1927

Thomas has set down, for the first time in this school’s history book, the business of the SS Irene, and he has set it down well. The paper is full and orderly: the steamship of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company, taken over by pirates from Bias Bay on the night of 19 October, in the waters about sixty miles from Hong Kong; the Royal Navy submarines dispatched in an attempt to surprise them before the prize could reach open water; and the unhappy arithmetic at the end of it, in which the British were successful in thwarting the hijacking though they sank the ship. The boy names his authorities, dates his paragraphs, and draws the bay on a fair map in the margin of leaf nine — sixty miles laid off from the colony on a scale of his own, and labelled HERE LAY THE PIRATES, THEN HERE LAY THE IRENE. I have asked the drawing master to hold the map for the geography case, and he has agreed, which is the only praise he has ever given a map.

What interests the school, beyond the marks, is the manner. Thomas is not a boy who writes to please the examiner; he writes the way the harbour master writes his log — a fact, the weather of the fact, and the next fact. The paragraph on the night of 19 October is the best sentence in the form: the Irene was dark except for a single lamp, and the lamp was an error, and errors are what submarines are sent for. He knows, from the telegrams and the harbour talk, that the crew came to the surface before the ship went down, and he has set this down plainly, twice, as though he wished the reader not to miss it. For a fourteenth of a child, in his second year at the school, it is a serious piece of history — a record of its own making, like the plaque on the bench-keeper’s oval at Chamonix, of which he has read the build log, men’s 500 metres, 26 January 1924, and which he kept pinned above his desk for a fortnight out of a conviction, recorded in his exercise book, that first gold and first errors are “both kept in small brass.”

He has also brought, unasked, a scrap of an old auction catalogue — the house of Meridian & Vale, Catalogue No. 112 — in which Lot 47, the Petersen Papers, were offered with one line of a scrapbook reproduced word for word, and the boy underlined the line with the same red pencil he keeps in his hat band, and wrote beneath it: a record of its own keeping, which is what a harbour is. The catalogue does not concern this report. The habit does. It is the habit of a boy who has begun to believe that papers are what water leaves of boats.

Specimen from the pupil’s copybook, found on the leaf behind the map

Pencil, half-erased, on the verso of the Bias Bay sheet · read to the form on 24 November, 1927

The Baku mill at 14 Marsh Road · Braintree HANCOCK Field Error E-BCN-472 , the same river name is used for the salt finds him.

It is set down here because it is in the copybook and because the school keeps its specimens. It is not in the paper. It is the pupil’s own line, in pencil, on the back of his own map, and it is offered exactly as found: Baku, which is in the Caspian; Marsh Road, which is not in any book the school possesses; Braintree, which the boy has never seen and which he has, on one recorded occasion, described as “England’s other harbour”; and a field error, E-BCN-472, which the drawing master says is no error known to any instrument in the school. Asked to explain it, Thomas said that he had been writing the Irene down so many times that the letters had begun to move of their own accord, and that the line is “what the map says when it is not being read.” The sentence is, in short, the salt the water leaves on paper. He is reminded, in the kind way of this house, that the map is for the bay and the bay is for the record, and that he may keep his own mills, his other harbours, and his river-names for the geography case, where they are welcome in the margin and nowhere else. Geography, at 78, is the subject he will repair next term, and the repair will begin with a clean leaf.

Remarks of the house master

A good term’s work, rising. The boy is a second in the form and a first in his own ledger, and the two ledgers do not quite agree, which is how a boy of fourteen ought to be. Let him keep his red pencil. The Irene is sunk and her record is in the school now, which is where the ship herself has been these eight weeks — on a map in leaf nine, dark except for one lamp, and right about where a lamp ought to be. Report countersigned and filed before the first ferry, as is the way.
