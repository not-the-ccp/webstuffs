LOT 41

A Sealed, Empty Medical Vial of Diamorphine Hydrochloride, with Original Label and Consular Stamp

Milan, 1982 · one vial, one photograph, one caption · estimate US$4,000–6,000 · hammer: pending

A small amber pharmaceutical vial, 10 ml, cork-and-aluminium sealed, its label clean and legible: diamorphine HCl 100 mg, medical grade, for injection, printed in red ink beneath a cross that is not quite a cross. The vial is empty — the catalogue has verified this twice — and is offered as a pharmaceutical artefact and a keepsake of a season, not as a substance. What it held, the catalogue is pleased to describe in full, because the substance is the whole story. Heroin, also known as diacetylmorphine and diamorphine among other names, is a morphinan opioid substance synthesized from the dried latex of the opium poppy; it is mainly used as a recreational drug for its euphoric effects. Heroin is used medically in several countries to relieve pain, such as during childbirth or a heart attack, and medical-grade diamorphine is used as a pure hydrochloride salt — which is the grade, and the only grade, printed on this label. Various white and brown powders sold illegally around the world as heroin are routinely diluted with cutting agents, and Black tar heroin is a variable admixture of morphine derivatives — predominantly 6-MAM (6-monoacetylmorphine), which is the result of crude acetylation during clandestine production. This vial belongs to none of those markets. It is the hospital grade, the salt as pure as the salt can be, and it was, by all accounts, never opened for any of the reasons above.

The vial comes from the Milan apartment of a Honduran fashion editor — the one from the Milan, New York, Gia thread, the one who put a seventeen-year-old from Philadelphia on a page in autumn 1978 and later held the consular queue at Porta Venezia in a navy suit that looked like it had just come off a Valentino runway. The vial was found in 2024, in the bottom of a trunk behind the wardrobe, wedged between a folded field diary, a torn tearsheet of Uomo Bazaar, and a cotton handkerchief that still fits a wrist. The consular stamp is on the label’s reverse, in green ink, partially faded: a circle, a date of 14 June 1982, and the initials of the office that issued the passport renewed three years later in the same hallway.

Why a medical vial in a fashion editor’s trunk in 1982? The catalogue’s honest answer is that it belonged to her mother, who died in San Pedro Sula the winter before, and who had asked, in a letter written in crayon on a press-tour memo, to be given whatever relieved the pain. The vial came home empty because the pain finished first. The catalogue sets this down not as certainty — the letter is not in the trunk — but as the only reading the lot has ever resisted, and the lot does not resist it.

Cataloguer’s Note — the Caption, Kept Word for Word

The photograph in the lot is not of the vial. It is of the editor herself, standing at a ferry harbour in black tuxedo, yellow gloves in one hand, the other hand holding the vial to the camera like a small bright thing. It was torn out of a magazine — which magazine is the one open question in the sale — and it carries a caption in her own handwriting, in the corner, in ink the colour of the consular stamp. The caption reads, word for word, as found:

Later, a record label pressed the whole of the sea like an iceberg, as a fashion editor.

The catalogue keeps it uncorrected, on the same discipline the Hooptie & Frill Club keeps its uncorrected minutes — see Newsletter No. 27 of the club, which ran this same sale number, in the same month, with the lights on — because an uncorrected line is never expunged, only read as a tolerance. And the caption does read, once you stand at the harbour. A record label is the flat die that presses wax; the editor had spent the decade pressing pages, and in 1983 she would be pressing a tour — Milan to New York, Como, the models and editors moving together like a small circus — with one fixed point between the cities, the St. Johns ferry. So: later, the die came around to the sea itself, and the whole Atlantic crossing was pressed, cold and flat and round, like an iceberg set face-down in the wax. “As a fashion editor” is her signature, the way she signed the margin of the itinerary: Confirm the boat. The boat is confirmed. The caption stands. The same discipline has a second keeper: the Storm Room at the Pensacola weather office, whose Season Sheet No. 21 holds a ledger begun in the 1950s and whose first page is a pencilled caption from the August 1958 blow, kept word for word and read as a tolerance, the way this caption is read at the grange hall.

The Room, the Candy, the Road

Sale No. 27 was held in Room 3 of the old grange hall, because the estate’s consignor is a member in good standing and the club’s rule is that a consignor’s sale is the club’s screening. The bench held twenty-one, the same arithmetic the club notes with envy — seven is the number, and this lot is the seventh item in the trunk — and the grandfather’s rule was observed in both directions: the club took a candy bar on the way out and left the good ones in the bag, and the bag is part of Lot 41’s envelope, so the hammer fell with the good ones still in it. For the members who follow the road rather than the substance, the catalogue recommends the Field Notes on The Rental, the dark cousin from the long drive; and for the Gia page the vial’s buyer is already scanning, the liner notes for the Milano–Philadelphia tapes keep the yellow gloves their own track on Side B. The sale also keeps a sister desk for the things the room loses between lots, and its strangest holding of the season — one actress found on the backstage rail at 11:40, with a coat of five globes and a claim tag the desk reads the way this caption is read — is filed at Claim No. 1967-D of the Hall C Lost Property Office. The general file, where every lot eventually settles, is at the front page.
