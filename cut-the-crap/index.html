Cut the Crap

1985 — laid to rest, drawer of the almanac

Sixth and final studio album by the Clash. CBS Records, 4 November 1985. Five tracks the band kept, eight it cut, one line in the log no one could sign.

Notice of departure

Cut the Crap died quietly, which was its only courtesy, and it has been laid to rest in the drawer of the almanac in the shed behind the bakery, where a record that has stopped being played is given the same treatment as a tide table that has stopped agreeing with the harbour. It was the sixth and final studio album by the English punk band the Clash, and it was released on 4 November 1985 by CBS Records, the same November in which the gales begin to arrive off the North Atlantic coast with their names already forgotten, as the traveller's guide to the British Isles, Sheet Nº 4 notes of the season. It reached no. 19 in the United Kingdom, held the place for four weeks, and has held the drawer ever since. It ran to fifty-one minutes and thirteen tracks, and the last of those, the one that closes the door, is the reason the drawer is warm when you open it in June.

Life, brief and tense

The album was recorded in early 1985 at Weryton Studios in Munich, in a room the band had chosen because it was quiet, expensive, and far enough away that no one from the old city would drive in and stand in the doorway. The session followed the most turbulent period in the band's history. Co-founder, lead guitarist and co-principal songwriter Mick Jones, and drummer Topper Headon, had been dismissed by lead vocalist Joe Strummer and bassist Paul Simonon. Simonon, whose dismissal was of the band's past rather than its present, is nevertheless absent from the album, a fact the liner notes handle the way a will handles a name everyone knows is in it: by not spelling it out. In place of the two dismissed men, three unknowns were hired in the space of a fortnight: guitarists Vince White and Nick Sheppard, and drummer Pete Howard. Nobody had heard White or Sheppard play before the first day at Weryton; Howard had played exactly one gig, in an east London cellar, and the setlist for it is lost.

The sessions were tense. Strummer wrote fast, argued faster, and kept a glass of water on the console he never drank. The album was produced by Clash manager Bernie Rhodes, who took the credit under the pseudonym Jose Unidos, a name he used once and never again, the way a person who has changed their name uses the old one when a creditor calls. The finished record kept three of the new musicians' hands in its strings: White and Sheppard are heard on the guitar lines of the kept tracks, and Howard's kit fills the room on the ones the band let it. Jones and Headon are not on it. That is the whole of the story of who is on the record, and it is the whole of the story of what the record is.

The last line in the log

Every studio keeps a session log, and Weryton's log for the Cut the Crap sessions is a single pad of ruled paper, kept in the same drawer as the record it documents. The final entry is dated the last night of the take sessions, and it is in the tape operator's hand, in pencil, and it has been copied onto the almanac's index card word for word because a log line is a thing you do not paraphrase:

No questions will be taking the sick ward off her long hair and laid stone for in the site is yet designated.

For nine years the line was treated as an error of the pen. Then the drawer was re-ordered, the almanac was reconciled against the tide tables, and the line came to be read the way the log was always meant to be read, clause by clause. No questions: the instruction to the floor, that the console had closed and the arguments would be continued in the corridor. The sick ward: the studio lounge, where the band had gone to cool off after the last take, and where it would remain the night through. Her long hair: the two-inch master, still running off the reel at the end of the night, the tape the operator had to pull before it ate itself. And laid stone for in the site is yet designated: a corner stone, laid for a memorial whose ground had not yet been chosen — a place for the record to be kept once it stopped being new. The site, it turns out, was always going to be the drawer; the stone was this page.

After

The record survived the band, which is the order most of these obituaries run in. It kept circulating through the eighties and the nineties the way a coin keeps circulating, worn, still current, no one's favourite. Other records have had longer funerals on this shelf: the obituary of “Untitled (How Does It Feel)” ran to a full page of eulogy for twenty-four years on rotation. Cut the Crap asked for less, and got less: an index card, a drawer, a line in the log, and this page for the stone. The school report on the book would file it under the shape of a ledger — one record, written down night after night, closed the way a season closes — and it is right, because the log is what outlives the music, as the clay outlives the hand that pressed it.

It is remembered as the last studio album the band made, the one made after the dismissals and before the break-up, with the four who remained and the three who had not, in a quiet expensive room in Munich, with the water glass on the console and the master still running at the end of the night. The gales that reach the archipelago in November have forgotten their names by then. The record has kept its own.
