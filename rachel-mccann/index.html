Saturday, 17 August 2026 · 6:40 p.m., still wet from the rain that never properly stopped

Rachel McCann, 400 metres, Irish Championships

I have been writing down everything I saw in the 400 metres final today, because I don’t trust myself to remember it, and because Nuala is going to want the split times. Rachel McCann won it. Born, if the bio in the programme is to be believed, on 26 September 2001 in a town it refuses to name, an Irish sprinter, number 214 on the board, lane four, red and green singlet with a small white number that looked larger at the start block than it has any right to. She ran 53.2 in the heat on Thursday, which put her in the final as favourite, and today, in a wind that kept trying to turn the clock, she ran the fastest 400 of her season and probably of her year: 52.84, with the last name on the board still hers when the clock stopped.

The start was the thing. Fourteen of us at the blocks, and I swear she sat the longest — two extra seconds of stillness while the others reset, and then set came and she went like someone who had been waiting all afternoon, patiently, in a chair. Back straight, she was second behind Aisling Doyle of the Dublin side, and I started doing the arithmetic in my head the way the announcer did, loud, for no one. She moved on Doyle on the back bend, shoulder to shoulder, and on the home straight I did not move my eyes. Five metres. Seven. It was over before the crowd had found its voice, and the voice, when it found it, was mostly mine.

In the press room afterwards she gave the usual two minutes: thanks to the physio at the club in Portlaoise, thanks to the track, thanks to the coaches who made the intervals hurt. Then someone from the radio wanted her to say something “big, about the year ahead,” and she laughed and said she would not promise a number, only that the legs were honest with her this summer, which is the truest thing anyone in that room said all day.

Here is the oddity I am writing down so I don’t laugh at myself later. On the back of the programme, in pencil, in a hand that was not the printer’s, someone had written a whole sentence, exactly this: “Business The general meeting is the line keeps is a sound theoretical basis.” I read it four times. It is not grammar as the post office knows it. But I looked up at the track — the line, lane four’s white line, holding, the whole race a question of whether a runner keeps the line through the bends — and I understood it as club talk, the kind the committee writes on the wall and the rest of us pretend to understand: the line keeps is the sound theoretical basis, meaning the line holds, and what is built on a holding line stands. She kept hers. The sentence, whatever it is, was right about her.

I will send the splits to Nuala. She is going to be annoyed I have filled the page with the programme instead, but it is 6:40, the rain has gone, and the track is dark under the lights with one empty lane still showing the shadow of a runner who just kept her line. I’ll be back Thursday for the relays. God, I hope it’s in the draw.

P.S. — The man who keeps the timing rig told me the clock had been checked three times that morning. 52.84 stands. Also, if anyone reads this: the pencil sentence was still there in the morning, which means someone writes it on purpose, every season. I should buy a programme in 2027 and wait for it to be explained to me.

From the same notebook: the obituary of John Withington (1865–1944), the Orange mason who kept books straighter than his critics expected — the closest thing I know to a diary entry that reads like a scoreboard. And the San Juan de Portugal build log, where the builder admits a ship is only as good as its store; a 400-metre runner is only as good as her last lap.
