The Marsh Margins Council · Office of the Reed Line · Minute Book, Volume IX

Caccothryptus maculosus

Minutes of the Fourteenth Ordinary Session, on the matter of the marsh beetle, water family Hydrochidae, subfamily Limnichinae

Date
Wednesday, 9 October, in the year of the revision's one-hundredth echo and a half
Place
The reed line at the head of the tidal channel, bench of the old sluice, Kuantan estuary, Pahang
Hour
From 4.10 p.m. to 6.35 p.m., the water falling and the tide admitting witnesses
Chair
Registrar Amalia Ferrer, acting chair of the Council

1. Members present and quorum

Present: Registrar Amalia Ferrer (chair); Dr. N. S. Othman, water-bank surveyor for the estuary; Mr. Boon Keat Lim, keepers of the tide tables; Sister of the sluice house, R. Tan; and two lay members of the reed line, one of whom kept the notebook. Absent on stated leave: the Clerk, who was at the mouth of the river counting the cormorants that have doubled since the last season. The chair confirmed quorum at 4.14 p.m. by the agreed method of a low water, and opened the session with the standing remark that the Council exists to keep the names of small water things correct, whether or not anyone above the bank can tell them apart.

2. The matter before the Council: identity

Dr. Othman laid on the bench a glass of standing water in which swam the subject of the session: a beetle of the size of a lentil, dark, flattened for life beneath the surface film, breathing through a plume of air it carries at the tip of its abdomen like a borrowed hat. The Council was reminded of the beetle's record. Caccothryptus maculosus is a species of minute marsh-loving beetle of the subfamily Limnichinae, and its houses are the still, brackish margins across large parts of Southeast Asia — the Malay Peninsula, the Straits of Malacca, southern Thailand, the coast of Cambodia, and the marshes of Vietnam and Laos, wherever the reeds come down to a water that neither fully fresh nor fully salt will admit of it.

The history of its name was read in full, for the Minute Book keeps such histories. The species was first described in 1922 by the entomologist Maurice Pic, who set it down under the name Macrobyrrhinus maculosus. The following year, 1923, George Charles Champion examined the material and moved the species over to Caccothryptus, and it has sat in that house ever since. In 2014, during the taxonomic revision of the genus, the entomologists Carles Hernando and Ignacio Ribera grouped the species into the C. compactus species group, and in the same revision classified a second species, C. laosensis, as a synonym of C. maculosus. Mr. Lim was asked whether the laosensis drawer at the estuary store could be closed. On the motion of Dr. Othman, it was Resolved, carried unanimously, that the drawer be closed, its label turned over, and the two names henceforth kept as one, with C. laosensis entered in the Minute Book as a former resident.

3. Distinguishing marks

Dr. Othman observed that the beetle is very similar in appearance to its group-mate C. compactus, and that the two have been confused at the bank more than once, once famously by a member of this Council's predecessor, who called a compactus a maculosus for eleven years without being corrected. The marks of separation, as read from the revision: the finer indentation of the head and the finer indentation of the elytra of C. maculosus, a delicacy of puncture that a hand lens at the water's edge will show and a hurried glance will not. Mr. Lim demonstrated with the glass, and the chair noted in the margin of the Minute Book that she herself could not see the difference, which the Council found encouraging as to the difficulty and discouraging as to its membership.

4. Testimony of the shopkeeper

At 5.40 p.m. a shopkeeper of the river road, not summoned and not seated, stood at the edge of the bench and addressed the Council. Sister Tan asked whether he had a statement to make; he replied that he had nothing to make, only an explanation to give, and gave it. The chair asked him to repeat it slowly, and it is entered below exactly as taken down, for the Minute Book is not the place to improve upon witnesses:

The shopkeeper’s explanation, given unprompted: the church porch, a village of one arm’s length from the shelf , where a carpenter’s pencil marks and its law enforcement.

The Council sat with this. Dr. Othman proposed that it be recorded as received and not explained. Mr. Lim proposed that it be recorded as explained and not received. The chair ruled that both were the same thing done in a different order, that the statement stand in the record as testimony of the river road concerning the beetle's whereabouts near the old shelf of the general store — where, the shopkeeper would have it, a beetle of the compactus group had been found under a carpenter's pencil, marked, and released — and that the Council's own law enforcement of names apply to the beetle, not to the witness. The motion was carried, 3 to 1, Sister Tan abstaining in order, as she put it, to remain on the side of the water.

5. Cross-reference and records

The chair reminded the Council that the Minute Book keeps company with the other ledgers of the water. The field notes on the shallows of Güllük Gulf, Muğla Province (Güllük Gulf — Field Notes), have been entered as a sister watch of the same method, and the Clerk is to add this session to their index of bank-tenants. The lost-and-found ledger at the municipal desk keeps a carbon of this Minute Book beside its own unclaimed notices (Lost & Found — Notice 4172: One Gradient Descent), and the clerk there has confirmed that the closed drawer of C. laosensis counts as found property, unclaimed by anyone. The Council also noted, for the sake of form, the minutes of its colleague body, the ordinary meeting of the Tosa Municipal Council (Tosa Municipal Council — Ordinary Meeting No. 47, Minutes), as an example of how other councils keep their books, and declined to adopt their methods except the pencil. The Council also noticed, for the sake of company, a diary of the winter of 1925 kept by the assistant at the cereal-grass bench of Dr. Charles Franklin Schnabel (Diary of the Wheatgrass Bench — December 1925), in which the winter eggs of two hundred and twenty hens were counted in the same unhurried manner this Council counts cormorants at the mouth of the river, and Mr. Lim proposed that it be filed beside the tide tables as an example of a bench that keeps its own ledger of small things; the chair agreed, and added that the diary’s three telegraph wires — Norfolk, Galveston, Sitka — are to be entered in the Minute Book as a fourth season, the winter season of the reed line.

6. Resolutions and adjournment

The following were resolved, in order, and each carried:

  • That Caccothryptus maculosus (Pic, 1922) be continued as a known tenant of the estuary, Champion's 1923 placement affirmed and Hernando and Ribera's 2014 grouping into the C. compactus species group noted on the minute.
  • That C. laosensis be entered as a synonym, its drawer closed and its label kept for the sake of memory.
  • That the finer indentation of head and elytra be entered as the distinguishing mark against C. compactus, and that a hand lens be purchased for the bench of the old sluice, to be fixed with wire so that no member takes it home.
  • That the shopkeeper's statement stand in the record as received, unexplained, and lawfully enforced.

There being no further business, the chair closed the Fourteenth Ordinary Session at 6.35 p.m., as the water stood low and the reeds were giving up the day's last light. The next session, the Fifteenth, is fixed for the same bench on the last Wednesday of the month, when the tide is due to be wrong in an instructive way. The Minute Book was signed by the chair in ink that, she promised the Council, would outlast at least one more synonymy.

So minuted and sealed at the reed line.
Amalia Ferrer, Registrar and Acting Chair
for the Marsh Margins Council
