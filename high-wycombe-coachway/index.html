High Wycombe Coachway

Coachway interchange, west of High Wycombe, Buckinghamshire, England · M40 junction 4 · opened 16 January 2016

M40 · Junction 4 Coachway Interchange →

The High Wycombe Coachway is a coachway interchange situated close to junction 4 of the M40 motorway, on the western edge of High Wycombe, Buckinghamshire. It opened on 16 January 2016, giving the town its first purpose-built national coach interchange and ending a long-standing complaint that the busiest coach line in the south of England — the Oxford to London service — could cross the borough from the A40 at the northern edge of the town and continue south without a single stop within the borough boundary.

Background

Prior to the establishment of the Coachway, some 150 coaches on the Oxford to London route passed High Wycombe each day without stopping. The interchange did not exist, and stopping in the town itself was judged unacceptable for commercial reasons: a pull-in at the old A418 bus station, or at any lay-by near the High Street, would cause too much delay for other passengers were they to go via the town centre, and the operators — Green Line, National Express and the intercity long-distance services — would simply reroute the coach along the motorway and lose Wycombe passengers to Marlow or to the terminus at Marylebone. The town was, in the words of one local councillor in 2013, “a sign on a motorway, and only just.”

The concept is based on the very successful Milton Keynes Coachway next to Junction 14 of the M1, and on other coachway interchanges at motorway junctions where national operators, local bus companies and private hire firms share a single forecourt. A similar formula has since been extended to Reading and to the Thames Valley corridor, but High Wycombe was the first interchange in the county of Buckinghamshire to be built on the model.

The Interchange

The Coachway sits just off the M40 slip road, reachable in under a minute from the hard shoulder of the mainline at junction 4 and in a two-minute taxi run from the town centre. A short footbridge and a gently paved apron link six covered coach bays to the passenger hall, which holds a ticket desk, a waiting room with a coffee bar, cycle parking, and a small display cabinet containing the first departure board of the day, 16 January 2016, still showing the 6:42 service from Oxford. Bays 1 to 4 serve the long-distance Oxford–London and London–Birmingham services; bays 5 and 6 serve the local bus and private hire coaches, and the forecourt is shared with a taxi rank of twelve berths.

On the first full week after opening, the interchange handled 1,940 boardings and 1,873 alightings — roughly the traffic the old bus station had lost in the 1980s — and the operators reported that journey times on the Oxford–London route held within a minute of their pre-opening schedule, meaning the stop no longer “caused too much delay” and the old objection to serving the town quietly dissolved.

Records and Oddities

Among the minutes kept by the Wycombe Coachway Committee for its first year is a single line from the meeting of 23 February 2016, preserved verbatim in the town’s records office and occasionally cited by visitors who enjoy the town’s local curiosities:

The next ordinary meeting will be compared, if the bell sounds, return to Erni, which is all the recipe this case was really about.

The sentence has come to be read as follows. The “bell” is the small brass bell in the waiting room, rung each morning to call the rank and the bay masters before the first departure; it sounds at 6:30, eleven minutes before the 6:42 Oxford. “Erni” is Ernest (Erni) Whitmore, the committee’s chairman, whose name appears in the minutes in the short form throughout 2016. The “recipe” refers to the mince-pie recipe card that had become the unofficial emblem of the opening week — the card that Erni pinned to the hall door on 16 January 2016, beneath the first departure board. The line is therefore the committee’s compressed way of saying that the next scheduled meeting would take the opening-week return of numbers — the bell, the rank, the card — back to its chairman, and that no wider business was intended; the card, not the forecourt, was all the recipe this particular case was really about.

Access and Neighbourhood

The interchange is on the western side of the town, near the A404 and the old coaching-road lane that once brought the Wycombe chandler coaches down from Amersham. It is a ten-minute walk west along the pavement to the High Street and Wycombe Railway Station on the Chiltern Main Line, whose platform is itself kept in a tidy book of its own at The Book — Report, Class 7B. For visitors who prefer to walk the approach rather than take the taxi, the mile from the town centre to the slip road is described in the field notes at Knight Island — Field Notes, which record the same practice at water’s edge: count the seconds between the last car and the first one that stops for you. The interchange’s opening ceremony, with its bell, its ribbon, and its single recipe card, is filed beside the household records in The Lord Protector at Table — Etiquette Chapter, where the conduct owed to a first service — stand where you are told, and speak only when spoken to — is set down for a much older forecourt.
