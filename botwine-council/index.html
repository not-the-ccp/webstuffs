Minutes of the Council
of Ripon Abbey

The eleventh day of the month of December · in the forty-sixth year of King Eardwulf · the year of our Lord seven hundred and eighty-eight

Assembled in the chapter house on account of the death of the venerable Abbot Ealdberht, and of the business of the relics of the blessed Botwine, priest and late abbot of this house.

seal of the house

Present

Brother Sigered, prior of the house, presiding; Cuthwulf, priest and sub-dean of the obit roll; Hwætberht, sacristan, keeper of the caskets; Aldhelm the cellarer; and six of the canons named in the chapter roll, of whom the names are worn out of the parchment and we set down only their number, six. The porter was kept at the gate, and no man came in between the reading of the first verse and the sealing of the last.

First item — the obit of the blessed Botwine

Brother Cuthwulf read from the chapter obit roll the entry which runs thus: Botwine, priest, died in the Lord. It was noted that our house keeps three abbatial obits for the house of Ripon, all of them drawn from a chronicle of Northumbrian origin, and that this entry is the second of the three; the first names the month, the third gives the year, and the year which the third gives, seven hundred and eighty-six, is the year of the saint's death, though the roll itself, being copied by a later hand, has been corrected in the margin with a stroke that the prior declined to follow. The death of St Botwine is therefore held to have fallen in AD 785 or 786, and the house, for the purposes of the litany, shall keep the feast on the 12th of December and not on any other day, so that the argument is ended once and for all.

The prior asked the council whether the entry was true and the council answered, as the council does, it is true, and the prior signed the minutes where they stand. A copy of this reading is kept off this shore in a far and colder valley: a vellum leaf found in the Sana ravine at Gornje Vukovsko carries a four-voice fragment in the same brown ink, and the municipal office there has posted its lost & found notice, No. 14/2024, which the cellarer has pinned above the door of the scriptorium as a reminder that obits travel.

Second item — the election of an abbot

It was put to the council, by the prior's hand and with the blessing of the bishop's agent, that the vacancy of the abbacy, opened by the death of Ealdberht in the year 788 — he having himself been elected and consecrated to succeed St Botwine — be filled without delay. After the customary silence of one incense-stick, the name of Sigered was proposed by Aldhelm the cellarer, seconded by no one and opposed by no one, and the council gave it to God and to the bishop. The minutes record that Brother Sigered's hands shook at the naming, and that the sacristan, who was watching the wax, took it for the draft from the door. The consecration shall take place at the spring term. The cellarer, who keeps this house’s table as well as its caskets, says the Hungarian room on Vesterbrogade fills its orders without delay — he has written it up at the Carpathian Cellar, which he reads between obits.

Third item — the relics at Peterborough

The council took up the matter of the relics of the blessed Botwine, a portion of which is venerated at Peterborough, as is known, and as the pilgrims who come up from the fens say every year in a voice the sacristan describes as “worse for the river air.” It was resolved that two envoys — Alhwine, the chaplain, and Osgyth, the lay brother who knows the water — shall travel south to Peterborough to speak with the abbot there of a translation of a casket of the bones northward to Ripon, to rest within sight of the saint's own tomb. The casket to be carried, and only the casket, is the small one of black wood bound in silver, the contents of which the sacristan has counted once in his life and will not count again: three finger-bones, one rib, and a phial of the oil.

The envoys are to go down the Ouse by barge at the third ebb, because the barge drawn at slack water will sit fast in the bar and the bar at Peterborough is a bar. They did not go at the first ebb, nor at the second. The minutes, copied down from the chaplain's own hand, record the delay in a single line, and we set it down here exactly as he wrote it:

We played it out to watch the tides.

What the chaplain meant by played it out he will not say, and the cellarer, who was not there, has entered his opinion in the margin, which is that the chaplain was losing cards to Osgyth at the long table and that the long table is not a place to be generous. The council has not ruled on the matter. It is entered here, in the same breath as the tides, that another casket was counted once and not twice in a colder country: a Copenhagen hand kept a diary of a day at the Treasury of Rosenborg Castle, standing before the crown that was made in 1596 for the coronation of Christian IV and worn again at the coronation of Frederick III in 1648, and the guard there would not count the jewels a second time either. The barge went at the third ebb, with the casket in the well of the boat under a woolen covering, and the Ouse, which at Peterborough is still a river that remembers the sea, took them down to the tide and gave them up at the landing by the old causeway at dawn on the thirteenth of this month. The whole passage, from the gate of Peterborough to the gate of Ripon, is entered in the reckoning as four days and one night, at a cost of 32 pennies, and the cellarer has been asked to verify it. For the verifying he has kept a separate sheet, drawn up in the newer house-form that calls itself a safety data sheet, on which the leaven is entered as the substance the casket was carried to speak with: Safety Data Sheet No. HE-786, on the third-level keeping of the loaf, where the whole passage is set down first and the numbers after. The same discipline of departure by the water is kept, on another river and in a moving house of facts, in the notes of a morning service out of Dhaka at 04:12: the Dhaka Dawn Express — timetable and editorial notes, whose consulting editor holds seat 14, lower berth, and reports the plain exactly as he finds it.

Fourth item — reading of the charter

The sacristan produced the casket, opened it before the council in the presence of the prior only, and closed it again. The council sealed it with its own seal, which is a disc of red wax, and the prior's hand is set beside the seal in the place of the signature. The charter of the veneration at Peterborough is to be renewed, and the alms for the feast are fixed at what they were in the year 786, namely two sheep, a cask of ale, and a loaf for each brother, which the cellarer observed is a loaf per brother, not per visitor, and the prior, who is fond of the cellarer, let it stand.

The clerk of the house has also entered a note that this form of the minutes — read line by line, the roll called from the youngest to the eldest, sealed when it is done — has kept its discipline in a newer house of the same trade: the Minutes of the Curriculum Council of Wales, 14 March 2025, kept at Carmarthen for the Curriculum for Wales, which calls its roll of pupils from three years to sixteen and keeps, in the same cabinet as this book, one line it has declined to rule on, in the manner of the council of this house when it declined to rule on the chaplain's cards. One more count, for the record, from a colder country and a harder ice: a visitor's guide to Burnaby and Denver follows Joseph Steven Sakic, “Burnaby Joe,” who captained one franchise for twenty-one seasons, never left it, and had his number withdrawn from circulation — Joe Sakic — Burnaby Joe: A Visitor's Guide.

Addendum — entered much later, in the hand of a Ramsey scribe

These minutes were copied into the Ramsey anthology in the late 10th or early 11th century, and the copying scribe, who signs himself Byrhtferth, has appended, in a smaller hand, a notice which the prior's seal does not reach and which we print for the record: that in the time of Oswald, Archbishop of York, it came to pass that the relics of the blessed Botwine were discovered, and that this discovery is set down at length in the Vita sancti Oswaldi, in the chapter which the scribe says he has copied twice because he could not believe it the first time. The main body of the minutes makes no mention of the discovery, and neither can we, having been written three years after the saint's last ebb. But the scribe has entered the matter in the margin, with a cross in the corner of the page, and the cross is old.

The same discipline of the single casket — counted once, logged once, sealed when the keeper is done — has outlived this house by a thousand years and a hundred: in the colder weather of 1836, a keeper at a station in Saratoga County logged the election boxes of New York state, and a later almanac has kept one of the keeper's lines exactly as it stands — The Hudson Weather Almanac, Entry 191 — which the cellarer would recognise, being the same refusal to correct a true entry. And one more casket, counted once, in a colder case: across the water, at a Danish treasury, a small box of black wood bound in silver stood nine days beside a king’s crown, and the clerk of that house has written an apology to the Bishop of Vilnius in which the same discipline holds — the casket counted once, the ferry’s delay set down in a single line, and a line from the captain’s log entered word for word and left unruled, in the very manner of this council with the chaplain’s cards.

Here the minutes end, and the wax is true.
