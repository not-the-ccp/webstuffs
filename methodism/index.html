Chapter 4 — Methodism

A Protestant Christian tradition · identification, origins, apparatus, distribution · 1729–present

4.0 — Identification

Methodism, also called the Methodist movement, is a Protestant Christian tradition whose origins, doctrines and practice derive from the life and teachings of John Wesley (1703–1791). George Whitefield and John’s brother Charles Wesley were also significant early leaders in the movement. The practitioners were named Methodists for “the methodical way in which they carried out their Christian faith” — a nickname that began as an insult at Oxford in 1729, when two small circles of students, the Wesleys among them, met on fixed days to confess, examine their accounts, visit the sick, and read in order, and were reported to the commons in tones of disgust. Methodism originated as a revival movement within the Church of England in the 18th century, though Methodism diverged from Anglicanism and became a separate denomination after Wesley’s death. The evangelical movement spread throughout the British Empire, the United States and beyond due to vigorous missionary work, and the circuits it left behind are the subject of this chapter.

4.1 — Origins & Early Apparatus

The movement assembles around three instruments. First, John Wesley, born in Epworth, Lincolnshire, the fifteenth of nineteen children of the rector Samuel Wesley, ordained in the Church of England in 1725, returned from a fruitless mission to Georgia in 1738, and on 24 May of that year recorded the Aldersgate experience in his journal: felt my heart strangely warmed. He began open-air preaching at Kingswood, Gloucestershire, that same year, and did not stop until his death, by which point the circuit of his walks measured, by his own tally, some four hundred and fifty thousand miles on foot and horseback. Second, Charles Wesley (1707–1788), whose hymnbooks supplied the movement with more than six thousand songs and with the habit of congregations singing in common. Third, George Whitefield (1714–1772), the “Candle of the World,” who preached to crowds of twenty thousand and, on the American side, shared the Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s with the colonists and with Wesley’s own itinerants.

The organisational apparatus is simple and durable. A class meeting of about twelve members meets weekly to report, to count blessings, and to keep one another’s accounts in the methodical manner of the nickname. A circuit rider preaches a set of chapels in order around a circuit, one a Sunday, in whatever weather. A Conference keeps the roll. The first annual Conference met in 1744; the American Conference of 1784, and the General Conference in 1791 after the 1791 reorganisation, made the transatlantic body into a denomination of its own, with its own ordinations and its own book.

4.2 — Distribution

The movement spread by foot, by ship, and by revival. In the United States the camp meetings reached their first great pitch at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, 1801, where the accounts of the day give attendance in the tens of thousands, wagons tied to the fence rails, and the sound of the singing carried, on one report, across the whole hollow. In the British Empire the missionaries went to the plantations, the mines, the colonies, and the islands, and the circuits were drawn on the maps with the same confidence as the post routes. In the valleys of Wales the chapels took the movement into their own hands: the revival of 1904–1905 ran through them on fumes and fresh air, and new tunes were composed the way that rain was collected. It is from that Pembrokeshire country, a few years on, that the chapel ledger below takes its entries.

4.3 — Repertoire in Common Use

The tradition’s working repertoire is the hymn. “O God, Our Help in Ages Past” — the translation of the old German O Ewiger, Höchster — is carried in virtually every book the circuit owns, and in the chapels of the Tufton circuit it is set to Blaenwern, the four-line tune of 8.7.8.7.D composed by William Penfro Rowlands of Porth, born of the fresh air at Blaenwern Farm near Tufton in the years of the 1904–1905 revival, and first registered in Henry H. Jones’ Cân a Moliant (1915). The parish keeps it in the lost-and-found ledger, Notice No. 47, where the item is described, its long note accounted for, and claims from Porth given the first hearing: Blaenwern — Lost & Found Notice No. 47, Tufton Parish. The descendants of the hymn — the praise songs, the worship bands, the sixty-year run of contemporary worship — are catalogued in the museum’s own placard, which this chapter supplies to the circuit’s reading list: Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard. Both documents agree on the one point this chapter exists to establish: that a congregation, properly organised, will keep singing for three centuries and still require a method.

4.4 — Ledger of the Tufton Circuit, 1914–1916

The circuit’s ledger survives complete through 1915, at which point the binding took water in a storm that also opened the chapel door, and the entries grow strange. Entry No. 118, for the month of February 1916, has never been decoded by the vestry, the rector, or the two clerks who succeeded one another in the job. It is preserved here word for word, as found:

“In the United Kingdom, Blaenwern has come to be together, however, the plumage being of a hundred would venture a lowering for them.”

— Ledger, entry No. 118, February 1916

The vestry’s best working account is this. The chapel choir had been rehearsing Blaenwern for the Good Friday service; the “hundred” is the number of sops in the gallery that morning; and “plumage” is taken to be a water-damaged misreading of pulpit, the pulpit at Tufton having a carved fringe that the organist was fond of calling exactly that. On this account the entry means that the congregation came together to learn the tune, and that whoever wrote it expected the pulpit, for once, to lower its voice. The entry is filed, unsentenced and undecipherable, with the rest of the wet month.

4.5 — Handling Notes

Do not separate the three instruments. Do not let the class meeting exceed twelve, or it becomes a meeting. Do not transcribe a revival; note only the attendance and the weather. Tunes born of the revival — Blaenwern among them — do not take directions well; play the long note at its full length and the congregation will find its own way to the close. This chapter is kept with the rest of the circuit’s reading at the boathouse, where the drip from the beams is counted as percussion and the ledger is the only instrument that does not need a case. Return the chapter to the third drawer of the counter shelf when the reading is done, face up, and with the bearing corrected.

The habit of keeping a ledger in parallel — one page for the work, one for the office — is not confined to chapels. A clerk at the canal office at Sukkur kept one beside his river gauges for a whole term, and closes it out in the same quiet manner: The Sukkur Ledger — Diary of the Canal Office, 2013–2018.

The rule is not only a rule for wet months. On 29 April 2011, when the Tufton circuit kept the wedding of the Prince of Wales at Westminster from the vestry television, entry No. 119 took attendance and weather only — eleven in the vestry, the spare held, and rain, which is the correct weather for this parish — and the thread that produced it is pinned on the parish board: The Tufton Wire — Thread No. 312, The Wedding of the Prince of Wales, 29 April 2011.

A register kept on the opposite side of the war, for the man who refused to move while the circuits kept moving, holds the same rule against its one undecipherable line, and files it unsentenced with the rest: The Conscience Register — Eric Chappelow, Questions & Answers of 1916–1917.

The same wire that carried the Tufton ledger's undecipherable line carried, on the night of November 2, 2010, the North Carolina Senate returns — fifty seats, every one of them, the first Republican majority since 1896 — and the clerk on duty kept the ledger and the tally sheet open at once: The Night Wire — WQDR Raleigh, Election Night 2010 · Radio Play Excerpt.
