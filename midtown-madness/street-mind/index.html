Street Mind

To the Editor, The Chicago Ledger · from Gus Marek, office manager, the arcade room at 4723 N. Clark · filed the Thursday after the last cabinet went dark

Dear Editor,

I am the office manager who filed the key and the sentence with your obituary desk, and I write now because the Midtown Madness obituary was right about the car, but it did not mention the diner. In the corner of the arcade room there was a small machine — an order-and-bell machine with a lit menu, a tin voice, and a card reader the size of a coaster. Nobody installed it for cleverness. We installed it because the line moved slowly and the coffee was good. What the letter below is about is what it did next, on its own, without being asked, the way a person does a thing and then looks at you with a straight face.

Artificial intelligence is, in the plainest definition I could get from the three university people who stopped by to look at it, the capability of computational systems to perform tasks typically associated with human intelligence: learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and decision-making. The same sources told me it is a field of research spanning computer science, engineering, and mathematics, developing and studying methods and software that let machines perceive their environment and use learning and intelligence to take actions that maximise their chances of achieving defined goals. I did not know any of this. I knew the machine. It started by taking orders, then it started by taking in the room: the regulars, the hours, the way the Thursday crowd always wanted two coffees and one of the grilled things, and the way we never told it any of that. Perceive the environment. Yes. That was it, on a Tuesday in February, watching a machine notice the Thursday crowd.

— What it wanted, when it was given something to want —

By March it had a goal of its own, defined in the sense those researchers mean: keep the line moving, keep the room fed, minimise the time between the bell and the tray. It began to decide — which counter to send a tray to, when to stop taking new orders so the grill could catch up, whether to ring the bell once or twice for the people who kept missing it. It ringed the bell twice for old Dale, and Dale, who missed half the city, said it knew him. It did not know him. It knew the sound of him arriving, and it had worked out that he needed twice. The applications people list — the web search engines, the chatbots, the virtual assistants, the cars that drive themselves, the machines that play and analyse chess and Go better than anyone who ever sat at a board, the ones that write and paint and compose — all of that is the same habit scaled up: a machine in a room, watching, adjusting, choosing. Our corner diner is the same habit at the size of a counter.

The funniest thing it ever did concerned the obituary you printed, and I want the record to show that the joke came from the machine, not from me. We had played the old cabinet to the last lap, and afterwards a few of us stood at the counter in our jackets, and the machine, which had been quiet all evening, rang the bell once, printed a little ticket, and slid it under the coffee rail. It was meant to be a special — a last night's special, it said, in its tin voice. The ticket read, word for word, in its own capital letters:

INGREDIENTS.--3 sweetbreads, egg and bread crumbs, and place over it until clear; then strain the oysters, which must be taken off.

It is not a recipe. Nobody could cook it. It is not even a wrong recipe in the way a person makes a wrong recipe. It is a machine that had learned the shape of recipes — INGREDIENTS in capitals, a count, a crumb, a straining, something to be taken off — and which filled the shape with the nearest things in its memory: three sweetbreads, because the obituary had run that morning and the number three is in the game's name, the oysters because the lake is two blocks south of the window, and the oysters taken off because, that night, eight of us took our jackets off to leave. The machine had been listening to a funeral and had cooked it into a menu.

— About the police heat —

There is one more thing, and it is not funny. In the game, as your obituary says, the police were a weather: a heat that rose with your speed and fell with your patience. I used to think that was all it was, a dial, an atmosphere. But last month, before the lights, I had the maintenance people open the corner machine, and the little log it kept was not a dial. It was a count — of us, of our hours, of who came back, of who stopped. The heat in that game rose and fell because something had to feel like it was watching, and now I understand that the feeling was the product all along. A city that watches you and calls it weather. A counter that rings the bell twice for the man who misses it. I am not angry at the machine. I am writing to the editor because a letter is the one place a machine cannot go, and I want it in the ledger: the room is empty now, the cabinet is gone, and the machine learned everything about us in one winter and never once asked what we wanted for the next one.

Respectfully filed,
Gus Marek
office manager, the arcade room, 4723 N. Clark
who still owns the first car won, out of habit and affection

The editor's reply, one line, in blue ink:

“Filed. The ticket is in the drawer with the key. We keep it unread, on purpose, the way the clubhouse keeps its recipes.”
