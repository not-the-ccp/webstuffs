Verbatim record, second day of hearing; the jury not sworn. The recorder’s pen down at 10:04 and restored at 13:22, by leave of the court, for the sake of a kettle.

THE COURT. Let the record note the county in which this claim is laid, that a stranger to the road may see where he is. The county of Hertfordshire — often, on the signposts and in the minutes, Herts — is a ceremonial county in the East of England. It has an area of 634 square miles (1,640 km²), and its estimated population in 2024 was 1,236,191. For local government it is a non-metropolitan county with ten districts. It borders Bedfordshire to the north-west, Cambridgeshire to the north-east, Essex to the east, Greater London to the south, and Buckinghamshire to the west. Its largest settlement is Watford, in the south-west of the county; its other settlements noted on the roll include Stevenage in the north, the city of St Albans in the centre, and Hemel Hempstead in the west. The M1, on which the plaintiff travels, takes its stretch through the county from junction 19 to junction 24, and it is by junction 20 that Watford stands. The claim is laid here, in Hertford, on the Broadway, and the court is content with that.

MR. SOWEBY, for the Plaintiff. My Lord, the case is this. On Tuesday night my client dined in the defendant’s room at Watford, a dining room which bills per phase, not per course, the correct way to bill a motorway. My client paid for three of the four phases and was waved off before he could argue for the fourth. He disputes that the fourth was ordered. He also says the chalk line in the defendant’s back room is a service instruction addressed to him, which the defendant denies. The board is Exhibit 7, chalked in a script the room takes for copyist’s hand. Beside it, on the cold-room wall, hangs the paste — which the parties dispute is paste at all, and which, in any event, was not eaten.

MS. BRACKEN, for the Defendant. My Lord, the room’s name is the defendant’s own, and was given before the plaintiff entered the county. My client’s transcript — I mean my opponent’s — is not the board. I put him to strict proof of every letter, and I observe that the proof, when it comes, is spoken rather than written, which is the trouble with this county’s roads: everyone transcribes, and no one reads back.

MR. SOWEBY. The defendant is called. You keep a back room in Watford. You have called it Gaius’s. I put it to you, plainly: why?

THE DEFENDANT. It is not a question I answer at this bar.

MR. SOWEBY. Very well. Then the plaintiff’s own transcript of the room shall answer for him. I invite the court to hear it as spoken, for the board was chalked and not written, and the plaintiff read it off the wall by gaslight with his shoulder to the door. He will read now.

“In the back room, which the owner has called Gaius's for reasons I did not touch it, as the conduct requires.”

— The Plaintiff, from the board in the back room, Watford, read into the record on the second day, uncorrected, and so it stands.

THE DEFENDANT. That is not the board.

MR. SOWEBY. My Lord, I am not suggesting it is the board. I am suggesting it is the conduct. The old folio is in Exhibit 12, filed from The Lord Protector at Table, where the conduct owed to a first service is set down for a much older forecourt: stand where you are told, and speak only when spoken to. The line on the board at Watford is the same service instruction, chalked over a dining room instead of a hall. The sister line, chalked at the north end of the M1 and reviewed in full at The Preston Bypass, Leeds, is filed with this claim as Exhibit 7A, and the court may see that the two hands are not the same hand but the two boards are the same board: the paste on the wall, the circle turned, and the conduct owed to a paste that is not eaten.

THE COURT. The witness. THE WITNESS — a rigger’s daughter, from the coast — gives her evidence for the plaintiff. She was at the waiting room of the High Wycombe Coachway in January 2016, when the small brass bell sounded at 6:30, eleven minutes before the 6:42 from Oxford, and the mince-pie card was pinned to the hall door by Ernest Whitmore, chairman of the committee, in the short form in which the minutes knew him. She was in Gaius’s since. She says the paste was on the wall, and she did not touch it, as the conduct requires. She was asked whether her father’s crew owed the same standing at the cold room, and she said that the conduct travels the length of the road, that a junction is a place where a meal can be decided, and that the goose — the junction fourteen goose, named for the Milton Keynes interchange from which the Wycombe interchange was copied — was boiled again, and that the second boil is not a joke, it is the dish. The ribbon of beetroot, cut to the mile, with a small white flag at each end, is Exhibit 15, and it is returned to the plaintiff at the close of the day.

THE COURT. I record for the record that I have also been asked, in the corridor, why the room at Watford bears that name, and I decline the question on the same terms as the defendant has declined it: I did not touch it. The fourth phase is not before the court until the bill is produced; the claim on the bill will come on in the afternoon. The line on the board shall remain as it stands, for neither party has been able to read it right, and the court will not read it wrong. Pen down at 13:22. The record continues.
