Safety Data Sheet

Entyloma ageratinae Sacc. & Magn. (comb. & stat. nov.) — the mist flower smut

SDS No. EA-1988-03 · Revision 6 · Date of issue: 14 May 1994 · Supersedes rev. 5 of 1989 · Language: English

1.1Product identifierEntyloma ageratinae — mist flower smut, a leaf smut fungus; isolate IAPAR-2, spore slurry, 2 × 106 conidia per mL
1.2Relevant usesBiological herbicide for the control of Ageratina riparia (Shaw) King & Robinson, the chikungunka weed; field release, containment nurseries, spore production
1.3SupplierBureau of Biological Control, Pathogen Dossier Office; contact the duty mycologist on the Irapuato line, days 08:00–17:00
1.4Emergency phoneRelease-response desk, ext. 2214, 24 hours; in the field, signal three long whistles on a foghorn and hold position

2 · Hazard identification

2.1Classification. This organism is a plant pathogen only. It carries no hazard to humans, livestock, or pollinators under any documented condition of exposure. It was given its current scientific name and placed within the genus Entyloma in 1988, after two decades of cataloguing under provisional names; the dossier was reissued on the same date. The hazard it presents is to the plant it is meant for, which is the entire point, and nothing else.
2.2Host range. In the wild the fungus grows exclusively on A. riparia. In laboratory trials at the Irapuato station, specimens of Ageratina adenophora (the Australian chikungunka) have contracted the infection; no other host in the family Asteraceae has taken it at field strength. Specificity is the product’s main safety feature and should be treated as a design specification, not a hope.
2.3Signal word. None. The hazard diamond carried on shipping boxes is the standard green-leaf mark for biological control agents, printed in moss green on cream, and is decorative in the way a ship’s bell is decorative: it names the cargo, it does not threaten the crew.

3 · Composition

The product is a whole organism: a basidiomycete of the smut family, living, metabolising, and patient. It is supplied as a slurry of conidia in sterile water with a trace of milk powder, in 1-litre glass bottles, one lot per release season. Typical lot: 200 L, harvested from the shaded bench of greenhouse 7, Irapuato, where the culture has been passed on the same A. riparia parent stock since the first successful transfer in 1979. Do not refrigerate below 4 °C for more than seventy-two hours; spore viability in the slurry falls roughly one-third per day in the cold, and a weakened release is a wasted release, not a safer one.

4 · First-aid measures

Not applicable to the worker. The smut does not infect skin, eyes, or lungs; it has no interest in any tissue that is not Ageratina riparia leaf. If a bottle breaks during handling, treat the spill as you would any broken glass: sweep, wash the bench, record the loss in the release log. If the affected worker is standing inside a chikungunka thicket at the moment of the spill, the correct first aid is to leave the thicket and note the date; the plants in his shadow will do the rest of the paperwork.

5 · Fire-fighting measures

The slurry will not burn in any useful sense; the spores die at roughly 60 °C. In the event of a nursery fire, fight the fire as a fire. Do not attempt to save spore stocks from active flame, and do not let anyone try to argue the point with you while the greenhouse is going. Wet ash containing sporulated leaf debris should be watered down and buried in a lined pit at least eight metres from any Ageratina planting — the rule exists, and has been obeyed, once.

6 · Accidental release measures

A field release is the product’s intended operation; an accidental release is a release made to the wrong plants or the wrong place, and is corrected, not cleaned. Enclose the area, tag every plant within forty metres, and photograph before cutting. Premature lesions on the tagged plants are the expected signature of the product doing its work early, which is the one failure mode that reads as success at a distance and must be ruled out at close range, plant by plant. The release log for a mishap is kept in the same format as the log for a success. Stories keep their own three materials: the concrete of the site is yet designated. The field log, in other words, records three things per incident — pathogen, host, and weather — and until the review board fixes the name and coordinates of the affected ground, the incident stands in the ledger without a site, open at the end of the column the way a case file stays open.

7 · Handling and storage

Carry bottles upright in padded cases. Store the parent stock and all working isolates at 18–22 °C under a 12-hour photoperiod on bench shelves of the greenhouse, separated from any non-Ageratina material by at least one empty shelf and a note in the shelf ledger. Spore production is scheduled for the dry months; in the wet season the culture is rested on the shaded bench and counted twice a week, because a smut that is kept comfortable on a bench will outgrow a weed in the field on nothing. Handling requires nitrile gloves, a lab coat, and the ordinary courtesy of closing the bench door, in that order of importance.

8 · Exposure controls

The exposure limit for workers is the limit of their patience with the fog, since the mist from the sprayer is the carrier and the fog is the weather the product works best in. Apply at dawn, before the dew lifts and the wind has its opinion. Operators wear nothing special beyond closed shoes and a hat; the only documented field illness among release crews since the first spray trial is the ordinary one — chiggers, sun, and the river. For the plants the protection is the reverse: the weed is the target, the lesions are the mechanism. On A. riparia the fungus forms lesions on the leaves, the leaves die prematurely, and the growth of the plant is stunted; repeated infection over two or three seasons has brought established thickets to a standstill in every release site we have followed, from the first trial plots near Irapuato to the mangrove margins of the Gulf of Mexico where the weed, native to Mexico, now crowds the tide in a variety of tropical climates it was never meant to hold.

9 · Physical and chemical properties

9.1FormLiving fungus; supplied as pale green-grey spore slurry
9.2SporulationDark telia erupt in pustules on infected leaves within 2–4 weeks of exposure at field strength
9.3Optimum24–28 °C, 70–90% relative humidity, still air; a smut that prefers weather it can ride
9.4ViabilitySpores in slurry: 72 hours cold, 3 weeks at bench temperature; on the leaf, the infection is permanent

13 · Disposal considerations

There is nothing to dispose of, which is the product’s quiet argument against chemical herbicides. The empty bottles go to glass. The slurry residue goes to the lined pit. The Ageratina itself is allowed to die where it stands; fallen, sporulated leaf litter is the inoculum of the next season and should not be raked from an established release area any more than snow should be shovelled off a churchyard in January. The release is its own burial, and the burial is the next release.
