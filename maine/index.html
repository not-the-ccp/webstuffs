State Border Answers Desk · Laconia, New Hampshire · kept in the back room of the Laconia National Bank · answers on the second Thursday

Maine

Sheet No. 41 · Frequently Asked Questions · ruled in Indian ink, cream plate, double line for the state and single line for the trend

Pronounced MAYN, with the stress on the only syllable it has. The desk is asked this first, and every time, and answers it the same way, because a name is a door and the desk will not open one with the wrong knock.

Q.1 Where is Maine, exactly?

Maine is a state in the New England region of the United States, and the northeasternmost state in the contiguous United States. It borders New Hampshire to the west, the Gulf of Maine to the southeast, and the Canadian provinces of New Brunswick and Quebec to the northeast and northwest, and it shares a maritime border with Nova Scotia. It is the only state to border only one other state (New Hampshire), and the desk, which sits in Laconia with that one border running eleven miles from its window, keeps the fact on a card pinned above the kettle. The border here is the one the kettle knows best.

Q.2 How large is it?

Maine is the largest state in New England by total area — 99,808 square miles, of which 30,843 are water — almost as large as the combined area of the remaining five New England states, which the desk has ruled into a single double line so the point shows at a glance. Of the fifty United States it is the 12th-smallest by area, which sounds like a contradiction until you remember what New England is. The line is patient, so is the acreage.

Q.3 Who lives there, and how many?

As of 2024, Maine's population stood at 1,395,722 souls spread over 16 counties — from Androscoggin to Washington — making it the 9th-least populous of the fifty states, the 13th-least densely populated, and, on the desk's own count, the most rural. Its capital, Augusta, is the smallest state capital in the country by population, a fact the desk rules as a kindness; its first capital and its oldest city, Portland, sits on the southern coast and keeps the port the way a family keeps the name: without advertisement, and for good reason.

Q.4 When did it become a state?

Maine entered the Union on March 15, 1820, as the twenty-third state, in the same hour Missouri took the opposite seat of the same compromise — one free, one admitted, the ledger kept even. The question that the border asks of this is whether the evenness held, and the desk answers it by pointing at the map: the line between the two states runs north along the river, the way an even ledger runs down the page.

Q.5 Why the pine?

The state is called, in the form the register allows it, The Pine Tree State, and carries the white-pine emblem from its seal down to the flag. The pine on the seal is a Wabanaki emblem older than the state that took it up, and the desk notes, for the record, that the Wabanaki — Penobscot, Passamaquoddy, Maliseet, Mi'kmaq, and Abenaki — had named the country before the name Maine was set to it in the letters of the French and the English alike. The tree, the desk has ruled, is patient about being an emblem; it outlives the emblems.

Q.6 How much coast is there?

3,118 miles of it, the longest of any state, fringed by something near four thousand islands, most of them without names that the boats will answer to. Downeast of the desk's window the coast bends so far into the sea that the state's own map goes to meet it, and the fishermen of the coast keep, as the desk keeps, a register. The register of the boats is the one this sheet is, in the end, an answer to.

Q.7 What is the line at the back of the register?

The desk keeps, at the very back of its register — not its own hand, the hand of the boatwright of the Downeast yard, written to the register and left there, the way a line is left to an album — the following, read into the record exactly as the hand allows it, word for word:

Flitches of a given name is to be the whale's, is to be remembered by all the same, brush it over the fire.

The desk's answer, which is the yard's and not the desk's, is this: when a boat comes out of the loft and is to be named — given its name, the given name that the family will keep — the carpenter splits a flitch of timber from the keel stock. A flitch is what the wood gives up of itself; it is not sawn, it is heard. Of the flitches cut at a naming, one is set by the way of the fish: it is to be the whale's, that is, the sea's, and the boat owes its name to the water as much as to the family. It is to be remembered by all the same — the boat's name keeps its place at the supper table, in the church book, in the register, whatever happens to the hull. And the flitch itself is brushed over the fire: laid along the coals and worked with a soft brush until the smoke seals the grain and the wood takes the colour of the name it carries. The desk has ruled this custom into the sheet as its seventh answer, because a question file that does not keep its strangest line is not a file but a shelf, and the strangest line in any kept book belongs to whoever wrote it.

Q.8 Does the desk know anything about the Laconia burn?

A cross-border question, answered as the border allows it. The desk across the road — the walnut desk of 51 inches, with its one long kettle burn, its sound drawer locks, and the drawer that holds, as it has held, nothing — sits in the back room over the garage on Maple Street, in the file the auction keeps as Lot 312 of Haldane & Voss, Catalogue D, the papers and the chart album of Louis Hyman Bean (1896–1994), who called the 1948 election from the Bureau of Agricultural Economics and ruled his double lines with the same patience the border asks of this desk. The burn in the maple top is a Maine-weather burn, the desk has ruled: the kettle boiled over the same way both sides of the line, and the burn, like the border, is the part that does not come off. Where the desk's own questions cross from names to the keeping of a name across a state line — the name carried from a town of the Russian Empire to a small New Hampshire street — the hall of the dream dictionary has already filed it, in its entry on the given name, beside the keeper's receipt and the hall with nine doors, and the two desks share the rule that a name is known by its weight in the palm, not its spelling.

Q.9 What are the desk's standing rules?

First: answer the pronunciation first, every time. Second: keep the line — the border, the rule, the double line — patient, and do not rule it in red until the second Thursday. Third: a question asked across the border is answered from the near side of the window, and the answer is ruled into the sheet, not the air. Fourth: the strangest line at the back of the register is never struck out, never amended, and never explained twice; once the desk has explained it, the line does the rest of the remembering. The desk keeps its sister sheet — the county answers desk at Anoka, Minnesota — filed in the same pocket, and the whole shelf of kept questions stands on the Webstuffs front page, where the keeper of the shelf has ruled the hammer's notices. The desk also keeps, in the same pocket, a sheet on the border that outgrew its own name — the township of Hyde Park, Cook County, Illinois, set off as a separate municipality in 1861 and annexed into Chicago on 1 December 1889, filed as Lot 1 of Product Sheet No. CSE-0001 of the Calumet Shore catalogue, because it too is kept the way a name is kept: without advertisement, and for good reason.
