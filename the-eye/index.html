The Council of the Fifteenth Collection · Sitting Room 94, West Side of Manhattan

Minutes of Session No. XV

On the matter of Eye, the fifteenth collection of the house of Alexander McQueen, Spring/Summer 2000

Saturday the sixteenth day of September, in the year 1999 · Pier 94, 160 West 30th Street, New York · New York Fashion Week, the fourth night of the shows

The Roll Call

The session was called to order at a quarter past eight in the morning, two hours before the doors opened to the press, by Mrs. Isabella Blow, who took the chair. Present at the long table in the green room: Mr. Lee Alexander McQueen, designer of the collection, who came in through the service lift and was counted present without a word; Mr. Shaun Leane, jeweller, who had arrived the night before with three crates and one umbrella; Ms. Seán McGirr, secretary to the chair, taking the minutes; and Mr. David B. Hume, almanac clerk, holding the Hudson River weather book open at the September page. Absent: no one of note, though the house itself, by its own count, had already held fourteen sittings of this kind, and it was observed that a fifteenth is never like the other fourteen.

Mr. Hume read the standing count into the record: fifteen collections, all of them under the designer's own name, all of them for the house at 26 Berkeley Mews, all of them British, none of them, in the clerk's phrase, small.

Item 1 — The Collection Itself

The chair put before the council the subject of the afternoon's show. Eye is the fifteenth collection from the house, issued for the Spring/Summer 2000 season, and it takes its ground, as the chair reported it, in the culture of the Middle East — in the long robes, the veils, the embroidery and the architecture of Islamic dress, as well as in the oppression of women within that culture and in their quiet, unbroken resistance to it. The collection crosses the traditional Middle Eastern garments with elements drawn from Western fashion: sportswear and fetishwear set against the robe and the veil, the zip against the bead, the net against the silk. Fifty-four looks were scheduled for the floor; the count was taken twice and came up the same both times.

Mr. McQueen was asked by the chair whether the crossing was deliberate, and he answered, in the manner of the house, that all crossings are deliberate and none of them are safe. This was entered into the minutes without comment. The council then heard, as evidence, the line the almanac clerk keeps for the water the show stands beside, and it is read here in full: Water cold, current adverse, fog seasonal and total, visibility in summer at its back — in the archive. The clerk added that this is the standing reading for the pier in the book he keeps with its cousin, the bay almanac from Alcatraz Island, Entry 47, where the fog is also seasonal and total and the crossing is always on the water's terms.

Item 2 — The Yashmak in Chainmail

The best-known design of the collection was then brought to the table in its case. It is a yashmak, the full face-veil of the older dress, made — ring by ring, link by link, at the bench in the Soho workshop — of steel chainmail by Mr. Shaun Leane, jeweller and frequent collaborator of the house. The piece weighs, by the clerk's scale, four pounds and two ounces, and it was tried on the standing mannequin at window position three of the green room, where it held, and where it let in, precisely as much of the outside world as a yashmak is supposed to. Mr. Leane, asked for his remarks, said that chainmail is an old armour and an old veil at once, and that the same ring can serve both. The chair observed that the piece is the one the press would be looking for. It was agreed that it would be looked at, and nothing more was said.

Item 3 — The Runway and the Pier

The session then considered the floor on which the show would be laid. The runway is staged at Pier 94, on the West Side, where the wooden wharf goes out over the Hudson and the water comes under it in the dark. The show is set for this day, the sixteenth of September, during the New York Fashion Week, the fourth night of the shows, doors to the press at ten, and the house asked the council to be in the room a full hour before the first look is pinned to the wall. The clerk noted that the pier is numbered among the numbered piers of the Hudson the way the bay is numbered among the bays of the world, and he tabled, for the record, the tidal sheet: two feet of tide under the wharf boards, the current running against any boat that stands on the pier's north face, and the fog, when it comes, coming all at once and staying. The council was reminded that a runway is also a pier: it goes out over water, it is watched from the shore, and whatever is walked on it is seen by more people than stand on it.

Motions and Amendments

It was moved by Mrs. Blow, seconded by Mr. Leane, that the collection be taken as the house's fifteenth and final word on its own subject, and that no count, in any future sitting, begin its arithmetic before fifteen. The motion carried, four to zero, the clerk abstaining from arithmetic. It was further resolved that the almanac line read under Item 1 shall be kept in the archive in full, unshortened, and that any future minutes of this council which set down the weather of a water shall do so in the clerk's own words, current adverse included.

Adjournment

The session was adjourned at a quarter to ten, the cases were closed, the chainmail was laid back in its box with the paper between the links, and the house went out onto the pier one by one to wait for the water to darken. The next sitting is not yet numbered. The minutes were signed in the green room by the secretary and read true by the chair.
