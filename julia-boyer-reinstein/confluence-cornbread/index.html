THE REINSTEIN KITCHEN · KEPT BY ORDER OF THE FAMILY · ERIE COUNTY, NEW YORK

Ticket No. 7 · Copied from the wharf book, Missouri River · Autumn of 1935

The Confluence Cornbread

A family recipe, with a long preamble on Kansas City, Missouri, and the boat that gave it up

The Preamble

In the autumn of 1935, between the last of her Deadwood terms and her first in Erie County, Mrs. Julia Boyer Reinstein — then twenty-eight, then still on the road the way teachers of her generation were — rode the long way down the Missouri to visit her cousin, Mrs. Ida Boyer Hensley, at the house on Grand Avenue in Kansas City, Missouri. The city, if a visitor needed it told, is the largest in the state of Missouri by both population and area; the railway men call it KC, and the telegraph men KCMO, and it stands on the Missouri River at the very place where the Kansas River comes in to join it, spread across the four counties of Jackson, Clay, Platte and Cass. It had been a port since the 1830s, a landing where the riverboats took on and gave off their weight of country, and on 1 June 1850 the town of Kansas had been incorporated, the same summer the state took its first breath. In 2020 the census found 508,090 people in the city, the 37th-most populous city in the United States and the sixth-most populous in the Midwest; the metropolitan area, which does not bother to stop at the Missouri–Kansas state line, numbers 2.25 million, the 31st-most populous in the nation. Mrs. Reinstein's cousin had all of these figures to say of the place, and said them with the air of a woman reciting her own ledger, and the visitor has always suspected that Kansas City, like most port towns, keeps its own accounts the way Cheektowaga does, only in a bigger hand.

It was Mrs. Hensley who sent her up the river, in a hired pilot launch, to see the wharves before the frost took them, and it was on the wharf at the foot of Front Street, while the steamboat John B. Myers was taking on her corn, that the ticket came to her. The boat's cook, a quiet man the crew knew only as Amos, came down with a paper folded once, in a hand that the boat's captain had made, and said: my grandmother's bread, the one the whole line of boats asked for at the Jackson County landings; take it, and don't let it fall into any historian. Mrs. Reinstein, who had not yet become anything so large as the first historian of Cheektowaga, took it, copied it into her own book on the sleeper that night, and gave the paper back. It stayed on the boat, and the boat, like most boats, is where she has kept it ever since.

The wharf book of the Myers, which the crew let her look through, keeps its cargo in a file of loose leaves, one leaf per consignment, clipped in order. Every entry on the corn leaf was in the captain's hand, and the family was told to read them as his. But there was a chief-mate, to have been that, in his hand: the line in the file's order, still open. It was the line for the cornmeal of the return crossing, entered not by the captain but by the chief-mate, and left open, the way such lines are left open, for the figure to be written in when the scale told the truth. The family notes it here for the keepers of the other open line on this shelf: those who hold Lot 312, the Louis H. Bean File, at Laconia, New Hampshire, will recognize the phrasing at once, for there the account at the Laconia National Bank sits third in the file's order, still open, and the two open lines — the wharf's and the bank's — have been kept in the catalogue's same pocket for exactly that reason. A line left open is not a line unfinished; it is a line with its ear to the water.

The ticket itself came north in Mrs. Reinstein's bag and has lived since, first in Deadwood, then in Erie County, in the family's own recipe book, which the estate has kept out of the consignment — Lot 214, the Julia Boyer Reinstein Papers and the Historian's Bench, goes its way without it, the bench and the boxes and the barn slip of gunpowder green all as catalogued — and is set out here, in the family's permission, for the bakers. It asks for buttermilk, which Amos had, and cornmeal of the kind the boats carried in thirty-pound sacks, and it will not suffer the tin to be left in a damp room: the green of the tins the Hensley kitchen kept was the same chalky, dark green as the paint on the slip tucked under the bench's drawer, the Company's gunpowder green, which travels badly in damp. Keep the tin on the high shelf. Knock, don't ring, if you borrow it. And the preamble's last word is the family's standing word, which the catalogue prints in the margin of Lot 214 as JBR's own: a thing is held for the next hand.

The Ticket, as Copied

Makes one loaf, or twelve boat's slices · The crew's ration was one slice a day, which is the only arithmetic this ticket does.

Ingredients

  • Cornmeal, middling grind — 2 cups (about one pound of sack weight)
  • Flour — ½ cup
  • Salt — 1½ teaspoons
  • Sugar — 2 tablespoons, Amos' one indulgence
  • Baking powder — 2 teaspoons
  • Eggs — 2
  • Buttermilk — 1 cup, at room; the river's own cold is for the tin, not the bowl
  • Bacon drippings — 3 tablespoons, warmed but not hot

The Method

  1. Warm the oven to 425° and grease the pan the width of the boat's table edge — a nine-inch square will do on land.
  2. Stir the cornmeal, flour, salt, sugar and baking powder together in a dry bowl; the captain's line was that the dry things meet first, or the bread sours its temper.
  3. Beat the eggs into the buttermilk; pour both into the dry with the drippings and stir exactly twelve times. The crew counted. The family has counted ever since.
  4. Bake forty minutes, or until the top cracks like a wharf board in the first frost and a knife comes out the colour of the corn, not the colour of the file.
  5. Cut twelve. Give one to the person who brought the buttermilk, whatever the hour.

The Provisioner's Rule

For parties larger than a kitchen, the family keeps the boat's own arithmetic: one slice per person per day, twelve slices to the loaf, one ticket of the ingredients to the loaf. Set the party and the days and the provisioner will rule the line in for you — tickets, sacks of cornmeal, eggs, and drippings. Without the machinery, the rule stands as written, and any hand that can multiply can provision a crossing; the ticket above is the whole measure.

As ruled in the wharf book: twelve persons, four days — four loaves, four tickets of the ticket, four sacks of cornmeal, eight eggs, a dozen and a half tablespoons of drippings. The line holds.

For any crossing longer than five days, the family adds its standing caution: gunpowder green travels badly in damp — the tins go on the high shelf, and the bread is cut a slice at a time, not a half-loaf.

Notes for the Keeper

The ticket was copied at the bench's table, the walnut one from the Reinstein Room, now offered as Lot 214 with its slip of gunpowder green, and it is the same green this page keeps for the tins. The open line of the wharf book stands company to the open line of the Laconia account in Lot 312, the Louis H. Bean File, 1922–1994, and the two were filed together at the estate's request, which is also the family's. For another kept ticket of this house's keeping, one that rules its measure out as a scale rather than a loaf, the family points to the Palsgraf Scales Cake of Brooklyn Heights, whose two loaves for the head and the foot of the platform are, the family holds, the same bread with its arithmetic turned sideways. The whole of it sits on the Webstuffs shelf, where the keeper of the shelf has filed the hammer's notices and the family's permission in one place, and where this page will be kept when the crossing is done.
