FIELD STATION Nº 7 BLUE GUM · N.S.W. LEPIDOPTERA DESK

Monday, 2 March 2025 · Blue Gum, near Bathurst, New South Wales · station hut, 6:10 a.m.

Field Diary — the Lichen Geometer

Entry 214 · Archephanes zalosema Turner, 1926 · Geometridae

The lamp on the bench had been burning since dusk, and at half past five I went out to count what it had caught. Mostly the usual small, dark clutter of a winter's end. But on the lichen-crusted fence post beside the water tank there was one green thing that looked more like the fence post than an insect, and I checked the moss first, because that is what it was doing. It is a moth — a medium-sized one, in the family Geometridae — and its forewings are green, marbled with black in a pattern so complete that I had to hold my breath at arm's length to be certain it was moving. That is the whole trade of this species: to be a piece of lichen that can fly.

I measured it with the old brass calipers: four centimetres, wing tip to wing tip. The hind wings, flat against the body, are pale brown, and the entire underside of the insect is the same pale brown, so that the moment the bird's shadow crosses the post, or the moth lifts off, the green lichen face is gone. The antennae are simple, thin and unbranched, pale as birch bark. A female; the male of this species is smaller, and the pinned pair in the box I have posted to Canberra shows the difference at a glance. It was described by Alfred Jefferis Turner in 1926, which makes Archephanes zalosema a name only ninety-nine years old — long in a name's life — and I wrote it into the ledger with a kind of respect, the way you write down a person's name.

Fig. 1 — the specimen as it sat, wings closed, four centimetres wide. Click it; it will open the pale brown hind wings it keeps hidden.

By the time I came back inside it had been in a while. I do not know how it got through — the screen door has a loose corner, and the moths know. It was at rest on the ledger, and the ledger had the card propped open in it: In the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies season card — the game-form of a Special Meeting Ref. the diagonal of the referee's signature crosses straight through the moth's green marbling, and I cannot tell you why that one stroke of ink, put down a whole century before this morning, still matters to me, only that it does. Grandfather played for the Grizzlies, and this is the only one of his papers left that has not gone to lace.

So the ledger stands on the bench between two glass slides, and the moth is still on the card, breathing slowly, keeping up the lichen act for anyone who walks in. If you want another creature that spent its whole lineage practising camouflage — a soft animal under an armour of plates — the dream dictionary keeps a proper entry for the Cambrian lobopodian in Luolishania. Otherwise the lamp goes out, and you can walk the rest of the shelves back from the Webstuffs home.

The questions have begun to arrive — what is a lepidopteran, what the scales are for, what the sound in the hut actually was — and the desk keeps a running answer for all of them in the order's ledger of questions, by the lamp, where the whole order is answered at once, from 180,000 species down to the one on the card. The post of the same morning carried Nora Voss's galette up from Hobart, and the family recipe book has taken it up as card 215 in Blackberry Galette, Lutruwita Style — a card that, like its namesake island, holds the capital in one place and keeps the other sixty per cent out at the fences.

Word of the moth has travelled, as word does: the registrar's household has drafted me into the spring column as guide for The Chalk Circuit, a walking tour of the Cretaceous in eight stops from the gate above the bakery to the boundary clay and back, 7.71 kilometres at one kilometre per ten million years. I have agreed, for two reasons. One is the ledger, which travels at the rear of the column with the ink drying slowly. The other is that stop six is a kitchen, the eggs are boiled in sea-temperature water, and the instruction on the wall — Dish them on a dish for the boiling is continued — is the closest thing to a lichen act a pot of water can manage. The moth can keep its place on the card. It is doing better at the fence post than I am.

— E. V. Hartley, volunteer, Lepidoptera desk
P.S. I have not moved it. It is doing better at the fence post than I am.
