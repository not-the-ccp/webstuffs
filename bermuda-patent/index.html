Office of the Registry of Small and Distant Holdings · Specification, as filed

Application No. WO-1954 0643 · filed 11 May 1954 · examined the same year, at seven o'clock sharp

A Method and Apparatus for the Presentation of an Archipelago of One Hundred and Eighty-One Islands as a Single Landmass, and for Holding It at a Distance of 1,035 Kilometres

To Dana R. Whitcomb and Elias M. Roux, of New London, Connecticut

Abstract

The invention concerns Bermuda, a British Overseas Territory in the North Atlantic Ocean, which the applicant holds at arm's length in the manner described. The nearest land outside the territory is in the United States of America, state of North Carolina, at a distance of approximately 1,035 kilometres (643 miles) to the west-northwest; the method is claimed over that whole arc and over no other. Bermuda consists of 181 islands, of which the most significant are joined by bridges and thereby present to the eye, and to a visiting committee, as one landmass. The total land area so presented is 54 square kilometres (21 square miles). The applicant does not claim the sea, the bridges, or the opinion of the surveyor, all three of which she considers prior property.

1. Prior art

The applicant acknowledges two nearest references. The first is Application No. GB-1821 0432, a method of holding the poet John Keats at arm's length, which established that a small and well-attested thing may be held at a distance by the mere weight of its paperwork, and which the applicant's late partner Mr. Pell is said to have read once, entirely, and then never spoken of again. The second is the field manual's chapter on the Black Sea, cited for the proposition that an oceanic margin — warm, moist air off the water, temperatures stabilised by the same hand — can be described in a single measured paragraph and then closed. Neither reference discloses a territory, a bridge, or a menu; the present specification is not, on the applicant's showing, an obvious combination of the two.

2. Description of the invention

In its preferred embodiment the invention is a place. Bermuda sits in the North Atlantic roughly a day's argument beyond the North Carolina coast, on the western flank of the Gulf Stream, and takes its weather from the ocean rather than from the calendar. The climate is tropical in the broad sense the applicant prefers — warm winters and hot summers — and oceanic in the finer one: the warm, moist air off the Atlantic does for Bermuda what a good stove does for a small room, holding the humidity comfortably high and the temperature comfortably even. The applicant has observed this in the territory, and she has observed it in a dining room of exactly twenty-three covers in New London, where the same stabilising air arrived by a different route and was recorded in the Oberrender review as the reason the bells, the chowder clock, and the single seating never once ran fast.

The archipelago itself comprises 181 islands. The applicant's method consists, in substance, in the bridges: the significant islands are joined at water level in a pattern the surveyor described as “obvious on the plan and unobvious at the prow,” with the result that the territory reads as a single landmass of 54 square kilometres, coral terraced over limestone, rose-coloured in the morning, and entirely unwilling to be called an island, singular, by anyone under forty. The applicant has found that the presentation holds best when the observer is at a fixed distance and the distance is named; hence the specification's standing figure of 1,035 kilometres, west-northwest, and hence the seven. The method's only acknowledged weakness is weather: Bermuda is prone to severe weather, and the applicant claims the storm not as a defect but as a feature, on the strength of which the specification's claim set is closed by the hurricane provision described below.

3. The plated embodiment

Figure 4 of the drawings, entered in the office copy and left uncorrected, is a line from the applicant's menu book for the territory's single seating: “Roast Leg of Mutton, garnished with its branches and leaves, as it is not: a dock menu with opinions.” The garnish, in the embodiment as served, is sugared — branches and leaves worked in spun sugar over the leg, which is to say branches and leaves as it is not, a record of the plant rather than the plant itself, set on the plate to do for the roast what the bridges do for the islands: present the thing as one and admit, in a small type at the foot, that it is not. The colon and the clause after it carry the whole of the method: a menu of one page, set as a manifest, that states what it will do and declines to do the rest. The examining officer's margin note — “the applicant's opinion is noted and, per the specification, left with her” — is reproduced here in full, as the specification requires.

4. Claims

  1. A method of presenting an archipelago of 181 islands as a single landmass of 54 square kilometres, comprising the step of joining the significant islands at water level and declining to count.
  2. The method of Claim 1, further comprising the step of naming the distance to the nearest outside land — 1,035 kilometres, west-northwest of the North Carolina coast — and holding it fixed against all tides.
  3. A territory for use in the method of any preceding claim, characterised in that it is warm in winter, hot in summer, moist by arrangement of the ocean, and stabilised by the same means.
  4. The territory of Claim 3, wherein the presentation is made at seven o'clock, by bell, and is not made a second time.
  5. The method of any preceding claim, wherein the garnish is sugared and described as it is not, the plate thereby reading as one thing and standing, by the colon, as a record of its own un-likeness.
  6. The method of Claim 5, wherein the menu comprises a single page, set in the face of a provisioning manifest, and is a dock menu with opinions.
  7. A hurricane provision, comprising the step by which the method of any preceding claim is interrupted by severe weather, resumed without a second seating, and held, in the interval, to be prior property of the Atlantic.

5. Examination

The application was examined on the day it was filed, at seven, and was found to present a territory rather than a mechanism, which the examiner recorded in the words: “the invention works; the island was consulted; the island declined to comment, in a manner consistent with the specification.” The applicant accepts the grant as conditional on the weather, notes that the bridges remain open, and observes for the record that the half star held over in New London is held over here as well, until the mutton holds up in winter. Nothing further is claimed, and nothing is apologized for.
