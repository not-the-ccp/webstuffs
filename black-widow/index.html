Field Guide · Route No. 52 · Departing September 1964

Black Widow

A travel guide through the grounds of Natalia Alianovna “Natasha” Romanoff (Russian: Наталья Альяновна «Наташа Романофф» Романова)

ROUTE 52 · KEEP LEFT AT SOKOVIA

Stop 1

The Red Room, Matyoshka

Every journey here begins at the compound the guides call the Red Room, the training grounds of the KGB’s most secret programme, where a child from the Russian Federation was folded into a sleeper agent and given a name that was not her own. The visitor’s leaflet is blunt about the arithmetic of the place: dozens of girls trained in hand-to-hand combat, poisons, disguise and languages, all of them exported to foreign capitals as assets. You will not be admitted. The guide leaves the brochure at the gate and walks you around it, which is the honest thing to do. The character herself — the person this route is really about — was created by editor Stan Lee, scripter Don Rico, and artist Don Heck, and she made her first appearance as an enemy of Iron Man in Tales of Suspense #52, on the stands in September 1964. The route departs on that day, deliberately: you are to begin with the villain and travel toward the hero, because that is the direction the ground itself ran.

Stop 2

The Bar in Prague, 1966

The second stop is a bar in Prague with a window seat facing the street, where in 1966 a woman called Natasha Romanoff walked up to the new Avengers — Captain America, Thor and a young Bruce Banner — and offered her services the way one offers a key: with one hand, smiling, while the other hand was already in your pocket. It was in The Avengers #30 that she reformed into a hero, and the travel writers who took the route before you all agree the bar is the pivot of the whole trip. Order whatever the house pours; in the story it is always something red. The window seat is the one to ask for. It faces the exact direction of the street the Avengers took when they finally learned to stop trusting her, which is also the direction the route turns out of town.

Tape log of the route, read at the depot

The depot beside the Prague gate keeps a reel-to-reel machine from 1964 in which the full commentary for Route 52 is stored, and the attendant reads one line from the route’s minute book aloud at every departure. The line comes from the meeting of the guides in which the commentary reels were first assigned, and it is kept word for word, smudge and all, on the depot’s wall:

Member Inoue asked that the tapes , and the sign wears out halfway through a hundred times.

The depot’s explanation, given with great calm to every arriving group, is that the phrase survives from the era of reel-to-reel commentary, when a single tape of the route was worn by a hundred departures and the painted sign above the gate wore with them, so that by the fifty-first running a guide could only pronounce half the sentence. The attendant will roll the machine regardless, and the tape still plays, which is the whole point of keeping it.

Stop 3

The Alley Behind the Daily Bugle, 1970

From Prague the route jumps, as the character jumped, to the city where her most well-known design was introduced: The Amazing Spider-Man #86, 1970, the tall figure, the jet-black suit, the widow’s mark at the throat. The alley behind the old Bugle building is where the guide stops you for the photograph, because that is where, in the story’s own logic, the new costume first met daylight. The suit of that year is the one every visitor is really trying to see, and the guide’s standard line is that you cannot photograph the design, but you can stand where it stood, which the guide considers the same thing. If you arrive in autumn, the alley runs cool and the red-and-black signage of the nearby print works makes an excellent substitute for a backdrop.

Stop 4

The Solo Trail, 1999 and After

The final stretch of the route is the longest and the quietest. Black Widow has been the main character in several comic titles since 1970, but it was in 1999 that she received her own Black Widow series, and the trail that follows it passes through the safe houses, the safe words and the safe hands of her solo years: a bookshop in Budapest where she ran under a borrowed name, a train station platform in Vienna where she waited four hours for a man who did not come, and a harbour town where she once mended other people’s rope work in a loft above the beach, which the guide will happily extend into a second day if you ask, and there is a full account of the loft’s hand-tied lash at The Saltfleet Whip. The trail closes where the character is happiest on paper — in the company of other heroes, a supporting figure in The Avengers and everywhere else the door is open — and the last leg of the route is simply the walk back to the depot, along the same ground you crossed on the way in, minus the villain you left at Stop 1.

Practical notes for travellers

  • The route runs four days, Prague to Budapest to the Bugle alley to the loft, and the depot issues a single booklet per traveller; there is no second edition.
  • Fares are quoted in the guide in a currency that stopped circulating in the 1990s; pay the amount printed and do not argue with the attendant, who has done this forty years.
  • The commentary machine at the depot plays the full 1964 reel before departure, roughly ninety minutes, and the same reel every time.
  • For a quieter counterpoint to the route — a small brass drum keeping a 250-year-old sentence warm in a cold room — the bench at Fenn Court logs its work at Build Log No. 41, a music-box organ for “Amazing Grace”.
  • One letter from Philadelphia passes the depot in the wrong direction — a tenth-floor desk at 6th & Beresford writing in red ink an apology addressed to a ball club, filed at An Apology to the Philadelphia Phillies, and kept, like the tape, word for word.
  • The route does not pass through Sokovia, though the map of it is in the depot, framed, with a red line drawn across the whole country and the word not yet written once, small, in the corner.
