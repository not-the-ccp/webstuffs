Stone Sky

Being the field notes on the old reading of the sky in sector DR-7, taken on foot

“The feed may fail; the stones do not move.”

Station La Caleta, north coast, 18°24′ N, 69°41′ W, looking east over the bank to Puerto Rico and west down the line to Haiti.

Instruments: one compass, one notebook of 1,879 numbered leaves, one pencil that has been re-sharpened at the canteen.

Shelved against shelf seven and a half, between the relay log and the small wooden drawer.

The study I am doing here is the study of how people in the past understood the phenomena in the sky, how they used these phenomena, and what role the sky played in their cultures. I write it down in full at the top of the notebook, as I am told to write down a definition on the first page of a field book, because I have been burned by abbreviations before. I must also write down, on the same page, the correction that a certain Clive Ruggles is credited with: it is misleading to call this the study of ancient astronomy. Modern astronomy is a scientific discipline with an instrument and a journal; what I am after on this coast is the symbolic, the cultural reading of the same phenomena — the stone that points, the count that keeps, the name that stands in for the forecast. The study is often twinned with ethnoastronomy, which is its younger cousin, the anthropological study of skywatching in living societies, and it is closely associated with historical astronomy, which keeps the old instruments and the old errors in one ledger and the old meanings in another. I keep both ledgers open, which is how this notebook comes to have two columns in places and none in others.

9 October, 07:04 — Macoas cove, on foot

The cove has three uprights in a row on the sand bar, set before the station was a station, the middle one a little over a metre and a half high and the outer two lower, leaning in as if listening. The middle upright stands on the line of the December solstice: at 18°24′ latitude the solstice sun clears the notch in the headland by a width of, on my count this morning, the gap of two fingerbreadths. I measured it with the compass rather than with the hand, on the ground that a hand is not an instrument, and the azimuth came up 297. I wrote 297 in the left column. In the right column I wrote down what the relay dish does, because I could not keep it out: the dish was aimed, when the line was laid, at exactly 297, which means the station of 1962 was built in the same place the uprights chose, and has been standing in the same reading for sixty years without once knowing it. The dish is the newest stone in the row. I have added it to the row.

10 October, 11:20 — the cairn at the headland

The gulls keep a cairn on the headland in the manner they keep everything, by never letting it vary by much. I counted it this morning on the second count, because the first count was interrupted by a gull and a gull is not to be counted in either column: the cairn stands at 1,879 stones. That is the relay’s figure for the country’s area in square miles, and the gulls have no knowledge of the relay, and the register, which takes the square-kilometre figure as its own number, has not been told. I have written 1,879 in the left column and in the right column I have written shared, not split, because that is the word the relay gave for the five-eighths the country holds of Hispaniola and it is the only word the station is allowed on that subject, as I learned from the Error Register No. 48,671, kept by the night relayman at shelf seven and a half, who is obliged to document what the machine said and not what the machine meant, and who counts the cairn once a week by letter and does not count it himself.

10 October, 16:41 — the minute

I was outside, on the sand bar, and therefore I saw what is not on the console: the dish held the open channel for the length of the line and no longer, and to a man standing in the cove the dish did not appear to be printing anything at all. It sat on its mount and took the whole Atlantic, and the length of it was one minute. The console’s account of the minute, the line that was not an error code and was not a checksum and was not in the relayman’s hand, is in the register and I will not repeat it here, only say that it carried the name Abell, a name of the Chapter IV of the Book of Etiquette for English Surnames, where the surname stands in for the forecast and the family is owed a seat at a table, and that the relayman was in the middle of a sentence when it interrupted him and has not repeated the sentence since. What the dish does with a channel held open too long, on the weather side of it, the station documents elsewhere; what the dish does with a sky, on my side of it, is what the uprights do at solstice: hold the line, and be the place where the reading comes out of.

It is this dish that the canteen ledger speaks of, and I must set the ledger down as it stands, because the canteen ledger is kept like the register and I am not its keeper. Beneath the day’s single course — the course the record of the down hour has preserved in full, the plate that was set down at 12:14 and not eaten by the two men who were sent it — the kitchen hand wrote one line, in the cursive of a hand that was in a hurry, and the line has been kept word for word, in the manner of all the lines on this line of the Atlantic:

“If this is not the universe as a Dish.”

I have taken it into the notebook because it is the only line in the station’s papers that I can account for, and the account is this: the dish on the mount is a dish in the kitchen’s sense of the word, a plate, an open vessel held up to receive, and the kitchen hand was making the oldest joke in the study, which is that the sky served is not the universe served — that what comes down the line is a country in eight lines, a checksum, one minute, and a forecast for a surname — and that if the dish is not serving the universe as a whole, the reader had best not sit down expecting it to. The study I keep in this notebook is, by its own first page, not the universe as a whole; that belongs to the other room, the one called physical cosmology in the cue sheet. The dish serves what the line brings, in the order the line brings it, and the line brought one course and nothing else. I ruled the ledger line out of the left column and into the right, which is the only correction I have made in these notes, and I have ruled it in as a ruling, not a correction, on the ground that a ruling is what a notebook is for.

12 October — of the astronomer

Serafín Duarte works the auxiliary telescope in the manner of an astronomer, which is to say he keeps his field outside the scope of the Earth and follows the objects — stars, planets, moons, comets, galaxies — by observation and by model, and the nine tracks he cut in the down hour with the night relayman are in the liner notes shelved beside this notebook, Astronomer, La Caleta Tapes LCT-48671, a pressing of 250 on which this copy’s number is the number of the ground under it. I am not in the nine tracks and he is not in this notebook, which is correct: he takes the objects, I take what the stones say about the objects, and between the two of us we have the sky of sector DR-7 as it has ever been taken. The record ends, as the register ends, with the minute, and my notebook ends, as the cairn ends, at the count. When the feed fails — and it has failed, 06:52, and it came back, 16:41 — the uprights do not print an error code, and the dish does not print the country, and the gulls are not counted in either column. That is the standing order of the stone sky, and it is the only standing order I have been able to find that no one has to be asked not to keep. The standing order raises one larger question — what a constellation is, and who is entitled to draw its borders, on the sky or on the shelf — which I have set down in a letter to the editor of the house gazette, dated 14 October, kept against this notebook where the notebook is kept, and answered, in the manner of the line, by no line at all.

— Entered in the Naturalist’s Notebook, Vol. II, La Caleta, sector DR-7, in the week the feed gave back, against shelf seven and a half, beside the register, the record, and the small wooden drawer, which is asked, in the manner of everything on this line, not to be opened. No entry corrected. —
