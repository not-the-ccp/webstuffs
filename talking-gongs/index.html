Gandingan, n.

Also called the talking gongs · the answer-voice of the kulintang · the set of four

The gandingan is a Philippine set of four large, hanging gongs used by the Maguindanao people of the southern Mindanao highlands as part of their kulintang ensemble. It is the deepest voice in the room and the oldest question in this desk: what a gong is for when there is no one to listen at the feast. The questions below are the ones the room receives most often, in the order the ledger records them, and every answer is checked against the set itself — four gongs, two metres of steel thread, one valley of echoes. Visitors are asked to read slowly, the way a message is sent: beat, pause, beat, pause, with the whole meaning carried in the spacing as much as in the tone.

Q1 What exactly is a gandingan, and why four gongs?

A gandingan is a set of four large, hanging gongs — in the club's own 1962 set the largest of the four is 121 cm across and rings, by the room's standing measurement, for nearly eleven seconds before it is counted silent. They are suspended from a single cross-bar, and the cross-bar from the thread, so that the whole set hangs a hand's width above the ground, tuned the way the rest of the ensemble is tuned: the four gongs carry the notes the Maguindanao call do, re and mi, with one note doubled at the octave so a phrase can answer itself. Four, not three and not five, because the music is built on call and answer and the set needs a fourth gong to be the answer to its own call.

Q2 What does it do inside the kulintang ensemble?

When integrated into the ensemble, the gandingan functions as a secondary melodic instrument after the main melodic instrument, the kulintang itself — the row of small, laid-down gongs played with two light mallets. The kulintang leads the line; the gandingan follows it a half-step of thought behind, taking the same melody an octave and a half down and holding it while the kulintang is already on the next phrase. Our room keeps a photograph of the Cotabato City session from the 4th to the 7th of November at the Kulintang Festival, where Ampayon Halili played the gandingan behind the ensemble's leader while the agung drummers kept the valley time. The gandingan is never the loudest instrument at the feast; it is the one the feast still sounds like afterwards, in the hall, in the stairs, in the cup of tea.

Q3 What is it for when it is played alone?

Played solo, the gandingan stops being an instrument and becomes a voice. It can imitate the tones of the Maguindanao language — the language rises, falls and levels the way a gong strikes high, low and mid on the face and rim — which is how the fellow Maguindanao of the valleys have used it for generations to communicate with one another over long distances: a wedding invitation sent from one ridge to a house the sender has never walked into, a warning of weather moving up the Sarangani line, the plain and old message that the meal is on. The ability to imitate the tones of the Maguindanao language using this instrument has given the gandingan its connotation: the “talking gongs.” The room's oldest ledger card, dated 1948 and written by a player the card names only as Datu's nephew, records the message as it was struck that afternoon, beat by beat: four beats low for the sky is going to change, a rest the length of a breath, then three beats climbing for bring the children in. No one disputes the reading. The gongs have struck it back at us since, at every festival the room has attended, and the reading holds.

Q4 How far does the talk carry?

As far as the valley allows, which the room puts down at five kilometres in still air and up to eight before a cold front arrives, because the front does the carrying and the gongs only do the talking. The rule of thumb pinned above the desk is the one the players themselves use: one gong, you heard something; two, you heard direction; three, you heard a place; four, you heard a message. A full message — sixteen beats or so — takes a minute to strike and a minute to be answered from the far ridge, so a conversation between two sets of gandingan is a conversation you sit down for. It is slower than any other telephone in the world, and it has never lost a word to a bad line, because the line is the weather and the weather announces itself first.

Q5 Where does the club's own set hang?

At Hills Oval, in Unley, where the room keeps the 1962 set strung from its steel thread across the bar that the park's ground crew replaced in 1994. The gongs face south down the oval, toward the water, and the thread is checked every spring before the festival season. The 1987 spring ledger of the park's works register is famous among the room's members, not for what it measured but for how it says it, and the desk is asked, at least once a month, to keep the line word for word, so here it stands as found, in its own hand: The thread, per the treasurer, with the rest of the Hills Oval at Unley measures, at the centre line. Read it on the oval and it closes itself: the thread is the steel line the four gongs hang from, the treasurer is the one who kept that register from 1983 to 1991, and the centre line of Hills Oval is the exact point where the bar stands. Whatever else the sentence was meant to record, it has at least succeeded at recording the set, which is where all four gongs still are, at the centre line, still hanging.

Q6 Can I hear a message struck here at the desk?

Yes. The board below the question is the room's gong-board: four gongs, do, re, mi and the low octave do low, struck by clicking each gong, exactly as they would be struck on the thread at Hills Oval. Three messages are set under the board in the players' own spelling, and a tab at the right of each will strike the whole sequence in order, with the rests kept as beats of silence, the way the far ridge needs them. The three messages are: the meal is on — low, mid, mid, low, mid, mid, low; the sky is going to change — low, low, low, low, rest, high, high, high; and come down to the oval — mid, low, mid, high, mid, low, low, rest, low. Without the board in working order the written patterns stand on their own, because they were written first and the gongs only repeat them. The room's note on the margin, in the treasurer's hand: a message struck twice is a message; struck three times, it is an invitation.

Strike the gongs, or set a message below and let the board strike it for you, rests and all.

Q7 What do other desks keep about the gandingan?

Two filings the room sends its visitors to, because the desk keeps its own shelf thin. The first is a question that arrived at the Dreamer's Concordance and was answered in its own key: what it means to be spoken to by an instrument across a whole valley, where the answer comes down as colour in the sleeper's hands rather than as sound — the concordance's entry on Universidad de Chile, Entry No. 143, cross-read against this desk. The second is a Class 4B term report from Coopers' Lane School on Galileo, the European satellite constellation, in which the class spends a whole section on how a signal is timed, returned and trusted — the same discipline the gongs use at the speed of air instead of the speed of light — filed at Galileo: Term Report, Positioning & Navigation. A third filing the desk keeps for its habit of spacing: a session diary written in a cottage studio above Sausalito during 1995–1996, where the runner logged the rests between takes the way the gongs keep the rests between beats, and pinned one impossible sentence inside the console lid — the Load session diary of 9 April 1996, kept word for word. The room's opinion, in the margin: the satellites send the message the gongs would have sent, four thousand years early, and the gongs have never once had a ground operations centre to apologise to.
