The Wirral Return of 1994

One-third of a borough, twenty-two wards, one count — the standing orders for a local election held on 5 May 1994 in Wirral, England

9.1 Scope, date, and the day itself

The 1994 Wirral Metropolitan Borough Council election took place on 5 May 1994 to elect members of Wirral Metropolitan Borough Council in England. This election was held on the same day as other local elections — the whole of the country voted off the same Friday, and the Board’s field desks on both sides of the Mersey ran on the identical clock. One-third of the council fell due. The Board’s return covered twenty-two wards, sixty-four seats in all, with two or three councillors up in each ward depending on the side of the borough you stood on — two across the eastern wards from Frankby to Bromborough, three where the western wards of New Brighton, Wallasey and the Wirral peninsula’s middle carried their heavier benches.

The poll opened at 07:00 and closed at 18:00, as the Representation of the People Act requires, and the Board’s standing order is that the count may not begin until every station has declared its figure to the Returning Officer at the Town Hall. The returning officer for 1994 was the borough’s senior assistant clerk; his chair at the count table is still on the inventory of Birkenhead Town Hall, and so is the red pen he used to strike the totals.

After the election, the composition of the council stood: Labour 40 seats, Conservative 22 seats, one Liberal Democrat, and one independent — 64 in all — with Labour holding the overall control of the chamber it had held before the count began. The manual records the figure because the count is the point of a field manual: everything else in this chapter is procedure, and the procedure exists to get the figure.

The 1994 return lists its stations in ward order, and a reader of the field manual is expected to know at least five of them cold:

Turnout across the twenty-two wards came to 48.7 per cent of the registered, which the Board files as “ordinary,” and the highest single-ward figure — New Brighton, 54.2 per cent — is filed as “watched,” because a high station in a hard ward is a fact that has to be accounted for before the totals are struck.

9.3 The count at Birkenhead Town Hall

The count began at 19:42, twenty-two minutes after the last station’s declaration, and the manual’s rule is that a late declaration is never a reason to hold a count — it is a reason to write down which station held it. The count ran to 02:15 the following morning. The returns by party, as the Board struck them and the chamber accepted them:

Three things to read from the table. Labour lost seven of the twenty-two seats and kept the chamber — the manual’s phrase is retained under loss, and it is the commonest outcome in the book. The Liberal Democrat seat went in a western ward, and its holder’s name is in the return next to a margin the clerk has circled twice. The independent bench of one, kept by re-election, is why the council’s arithmetic is never quite a two-party sum: 40 plus 22 does not make 64, and the chapter prints the remainder rather than hiding it.

For a comparable count kept at a different scale, the Board cross-files the minutes of the council of Ripon Abbey, AD 788: an election of one abbot instead of twenty-two councillors, the obit read three times, the same requirement that a result be declared before the room is allowed to leave. And for what a matchnight committee looks like when the chamber is full, the Ashton Gate minutes are the standard reference — committees run on the same clock discipline the count does.

9.4 The clerk’s note

The return carries one marginal note, in a hand that is not the returning officer’s, and the chapter is obliged to print it in full:

“On a motion against moving it for the term that ran the arrondissements of Ghent and understood.”

No one at the Board can explain it. It appears between the declaration for Hoylake and the declaration for Bidston, where the geography does not match any entry, and arrondissements is a word the borough does not use, having been divided into wards since 1974 and into a county borough before that. The manual’s position is that a note in the return is part of the return: it is preserved exactly once, exactly as struck, and no correction is to be entered against it, on the standing rule that the 1994 file is closed and the red pen has been locked in the Town Hall cabinet since the ninth printing.

9.5 Standing orders, local election day

  1. Open at 07:00 sharp. A station open at 07:01 is a station the Board has to explain, and the explanation is always worse than the minute.
  2. Close at 18:00. Those in the queue at 18:00 vote; those arriving after it do not, and the clerk keeps their names for the record only.
  3. Every station declares its figure to the Returning Officer before the count begins. The count waits for the last station, not for the first.
  4. Strike totals in the return order, ward by ward, and do not re-order for tidiness. A reader of the return must be able to find New Brighton in the first column and Bromborough in the last.
  5. Record the composition of the council after the election, party by party, seats won and council total, and print the remainder rather than fudge the sum.
  6. Notes in the margin are preserved, not corrected. A closed file is a closed file, and the red pen stays in the cabinet.

9.6 Colophon of the chapter

Filed by the West Mersey and East Mersey field desks of the Mersey Polling Board from the return as struck at Birkenhead Town Hall, 6 May 1994. The parent manual, FM-7, keeps the standing orders for the whole county; this chapter is the 1994 instance of them, on the day the whole country voted — one of the other local elections, the one that happened to fall on the same Friday.
