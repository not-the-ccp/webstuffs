The Northern Almanac of Ice & Currents · Entry No. 17 · Season 1947–48 to present

Gulf of Finland

Soome laht · Suomenlahti · Фи́нский зали́в · Finska viken

Kept at the north pier of Gotenhafen by the same hand that keeps the gate log of Harbour Brochure No. 12, SMS Schleswig-Holstein. The entry is entered season by season; the last season closed on 14 April.

Entry No. 17 of the almanac is the easternmost arm of the Baltic Sea, and the longest one: the Gulf of Finland runs from the Swedish boundary at Nagu in the west to the delta of the river Neva in the east, between Finland to the north and Estonia to the south, ending at Saint Petersburg, the second largest city of Russia, where the Neva drains into it and the gulf closes like a door. Other major cities stand on its rim: Helsinki in the Finnish arm, Tallinn at the head of the Estonian shore, and Kronstadt on the fortress island that guards the Neva mouth. The eastern parts of the gulf belong to Russia, and some of Russia’s most important oil harbours are located there, including Primorsk, whose quays the almanac prices by tonne of ice and by knot of wind rather than by tonne of oil. As the seaway to Saint Petersburg, the gulf is of considerable strategic importance to Russia; the keeper of this entry has therefore never once left the gulf unobserved, and has kept the record in the flat, level hand required of a man whose brother-in-charge of the wreck at Gotenhafen still reads his log out loud at 12:30.

The record of the year

The almanac reads the gulf as a weather instrument laid out along its full four hundred kilometres. Its length is about 400 kilometres; its width runs from roughly 110 to 320 kilometres as the shore leans; its greatest depth is about 132 metres in the eastern basin, with an average of 38. The salinity at the western mouth is 6–7 per mille, falling east of Helsinki to almost the salinity of a river, so that the gulf freezes by a different rule from the rest of the Baltic: the ice season begins on 1 November, when the first floes put in off the Estonian shoals, and does not end until mid-April, when the icebreaker road to Saint Petersburg is cleared and the almanac enters its one blank month. January mean at the open gulf is −6 °C; the wind that matters most is the northeasterly, which drives the shallow western water up on to the Narva shore and out of the eastern basin, and it is this same wind, reversed, that pushes the seiche through the Neva delta and makes the Saint Petersburg water-line walk the embankments by as much as one metre forty in an hour. The Neva itself discharges, on an ordinary day, some 800 cubic metres to the second — the almanac notes the discharge at the delta every morning, because a high river means an early opening of the ice road and a late closing of it.

The eastern gulf, where Primorsk's oil quays stand, is the part of the record the keeper writes most carefully, because there the wind is a commercial figure and the ice is an arithmetic one: a strong northwesterly can close the tanker lane for a week, and a week closed is a week of the city’s fuel standing on the water. Some of the environmental problems of the gulf are entered here too, in the small hand: the oil sheen reported off the Estonian shore in November, the silt line that has advanced two metres since the last season's sounding, and the winter that the ice-floe census says was the thinnest in the almanac's keeping. None of these lines is corrected out; the almanac keeps them as they were read.

The illustrated catalogue

February is the month the harbour master keeps for the ice itself. Off Helsinki, when the wind has run three or four days from the north-north-east, the floes grind into pressure ridges, and the master takes the season out to draw them — one page per ridge, a pencil sketch of the crest, the height in the margin, and a name. He has kept the pages since 1948, and the bundle has grown into an illustrated catalogue of the gulf's ice, forty-two sheets strong, which he shows to crossing parties on the 12:30 bell and will not let anyone borrow. The catalogue is the one object in the almanac room that the master calls by a name of his own, and the gate log holds a line about it, entered in the same flat voice as the rest of the log.

The line, read word for word

The gate log of the pier — the same log whose 12:30 line is pinned above the ticket hatch and recited to every crossing party for the wreck at Gotenhafen — carries, under the entry for the first February of the season, the following line, which the almanac reproduces word for word, as its rule requires of any line the harbour master reads aloud:

“They must get some fresh dough, which he calls them, and to form a useful illustrated catalogue, which may be called, was proportionably greater.”

The master has not been asked what he means by fresh dough, and does not mean it, except that the newly pressed ridges are the ones he will not let you take down from the rail, and that the catalogue is formed of the new ones first. That the line was entered in the log is confirmed by the 12:30 crossing itself: the school parties that come out at noon on a February morning are the ones he is talking about, and they are the ones that always carry the catalogue’s weight on the rail. The line has been copied into the almanac unchanged since the season it was read, and it stands here, uncorrected, in its place between the Neva discharge and the April blank.

Else on the eastern record: the winter of 1916–17, when the pre-dreadnoughts of the High Seas Fleet lay up against the ice in the Jastrow estuary, is entered in the almanac by reference, because the gulf itself was frozen for the Russian shore and the crossing of the winter belonged to the ships of the Peresvet class on the far side of the sea — and by the front page of the shelf, where the other brochures of this coast keep their timetables. The gulf is the part of the Baltic the brochure of No. 12 points to when it says the bay floor, and this entry is the weather the bay floor lies under.

Terms of the entry

  • Reading the almanac — the season is read west to east; a reader who starts at Saint Petersburg is asked to begin again at Nagu.
  • The catalogue — shown on the 12:30 bell; not for borrowing; the forty-third sheet is not finished and the master will say when it is.
  • Corrections — a line, once read aloud at the pier rail, is not corrected out of the almanac; the correction is entered beside it, smaller.
  • Cross-reference — for the wreck this entry lies under, the fares, and the harbour master’s 12:30 line of 25 June 1946, see Harbour Brochure No. 12, SMS Schleswig-Holstein, on the same shelf.
