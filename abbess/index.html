The Abbess’s Cold Cream, and the Sifting of Sugar

7 entries · first entered 25 February 2024 · closed 2 March 2024 · no complaints entered

REG·63
Entry No. 1 warden.eh warden of the cellar entered 25 February 2024, 04:12

Whoever was at the wake on Sunday will have seen her. Mother Anselm, abbess of St Anne’s abbey at 14th and Alder, came up from the precinct with a jug of cold cream in one hand and a folded paper under the other, and set both on the long table by the dry dock door, between the cake and the last of the last cask. She did not sit. She watched the cream come up cold to the table, said to the table — not to any of us — that the rooks would have it sooner if we were not here, and went. Her note, which I am entering against the obituary, Obituary No. 62, Bitter, 1824–2024, says only: the cream is her mother’s, the book is hers, the sugar is what remains. The book has come to me with a copy of the card, and the card, page 142 of the 1897 volume, gives the whole recipe in one breath, and I set it down as written, because the board preserves what is written:

“When turned out, strew sifted sugar gradually, and when quite set, turn it out, and serve.”

That is the card. I have read it four times and I keep coming back to the two turnings. Someone explain it to me, please, before I feed the cake again on the fortnight.

Entry No. 2 copperwhale taps, 2238 NW Flanders St entered 25 February 2024, 09:58

I can say this much from the other side of the bar. The cream came up in a stoneware jug, tied, with a crust already forming on top, and the four-pound cake was cut narrow on her watch, the pruned quarter set aside for the senior person at the table — which, as the register records, was the table itself. The abbess took the serving knife, cut once, set the knife down, and did not touch it again. She is the first person in living memory to cut at that table without the knife being passed back to her, and I have left it with her side of the board since. The cream was gone before the second toast. The jug came back to the abbey empty and washed, which in her house is the highest praise a dish can receive.

Entry No. 3 sister.bridget of the precinct, 14th and Alder entered 26 February 2024, 07:20

The board keeps asking who she is, and the answer is shorter than the questions. An abbess (Latin: abbatissa) is the female superior of a community of nuns in an abbey, and that is the whole of her office and most of her silence. There are thirty-one of us at St Anne’s, and she signs every cask, every delivery, and every letter in the masculine hand the old books require, because the books are older than her patience. When the register set down that Bitter was she, and the corner read it standing, Mother Anselm was asked for her mark against it. Her mark was two words: she’s family. I am posting this at her bidding, which I note is the same as saying at her prohibition.

Entry No. 4 bitter1824 cellar shelf, north wall entered 27 February 2024, 16:03

On the rooks. The copper whale weathervane stood in the yard all day of the service and the rooks kept to it, as the register has it, and Mother Anselm stood under it for the better part of an hour with nothing to do but stand. She calls them, in the 1897 book, the abbey’s choir on leave, and there is a page of her own hand among the recipes — a page of no recipe at all, only a list of names: the rooks of the whale, one by one, in the order they alighted, which she says is the order of the service. I do not believe her, and I keep the page with the card. The card is the true page. The list is the truest.

Entry No. 5 plumbman pantry ledger entered 28 February 2024, 11:41

Cake, for the record, stood as the chapter stands. The full specification is at Plum Cake — Field Manual FM-21, Chapter 6, and the cake at the head of the wake table met it to the ounce: golden sultanas, seedless raisins, black currants, the pruned quarter sliced and not puréed, glazed, twice fed, three years and more of standing. Cut narrow. The warden’s margin note — hard at the edge, good at the heart — is the oldest note in the pantry, borrowed from a Bombay store room of 1897 and truer every year. The abbess ran the knife once, drew it through, and left the pruned quarter where the custom put it. The custom, I am told, does not object.

Entry No. 6 paymaster.ehc provisioning log, p. 118 entered 1 March 2024, 08:15

I keep one line from the old provisioning log, page 118, the Christmas table of 1889, and I set it against the cream because the two are the same craft: the raft, served as one object, the way two voices get printed as one. The cold cream of Mother Anselm is that craft in a jug — the cream and the cream’s breath as one disc, cut from the whole. When I read her card, strew sifted sugar gradually, I understand at last what gradually is for: it is the time it takes two voices to print as one. Sift fast and the line is a stripe; sift gradually and the line is a weather. The rooks approve no weather. I have noted that as a remark and entered no complaint.

Entry No. 7 · Closing warden.eh warden of the cellar closed 2 March 2024, 05:00

Entered for the record. The 1897 book went back to St Anne’s at first light, page 142 marked with the corner’s stamp and no other mark. The card remains with the register, pinned above the obituary, and the knife remains on her side of the board. The cream was set, the sugar was sifted gradually, and the plates came back clean, which is the only entry the board has found to make that is worth making. In the margin, against the line the book gives and this board preserves word for word, the warden’s note reads in the old hand: she was the dark one of the afternoon’s cream, and the cream keeps, as the cake keeps, longest of all. The thread stands closed. No complaints were entered.
