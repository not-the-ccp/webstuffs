The Karlowitz Room

A 1/72 working diorama of the signing room at Karlowitz — present-day Sremski Karlovci, Serbia, in the old Military Frontier of the Habsburg Monarchy — where, on 26 January 1699, the Treaty of Karlowitz closed the Great Turkish War of 1683–1699 and began the reversal of almost four centuries of Ottoman expansion (1299–1683).

TAKEN DOWN
26 I 1699the day the room was built

Parts list, as issued

  • Walnut panel kit, 44 leaves — the room was panelled, and the panels were the whole room.
  • Brass etched map, 220 × 150 mm at full scale, filed down to 3 × 2 mm, showing the frontier as it stood in January 1699: the Danube, the Tisza, the mouth of the Sava where Karlowitz sits on the Habsburg bank.
  • One long table, one bench per seat, 112 figures, of whom the named are five: Rüdiger von Starhemberg and Johann Ludwig von Zinzendorf for the Habsburg court, Girolamo Bragadin for Venice, and for the Porte the Grand Vizier Süleyman Ağa with the dragoman Feribeg at the translation bench. The other 107 are scribes, candle-stands and one man who was only there to keep the door shut.
  • Eleven candles. No more. The room was lit the way the winter of 1699 lit it.
  • One crate, unscheduled, that arrived on the fourth week. See Entry 4.

Entry 1 · Saturday, third week of February

I decided on this build the way I decide on all of them, by finding one date I could not get out of my head: 26 January 1699. The war had run sixteen years, from the relief of Vienna in 1683, through the Holy League's campaigning, down to the Battle of Zenta on 11 September 1697, where the Ottoman army was broken and the way to the table was opened. What was signed at the table was the first major loss of European territory in the Ottoman record — Hungary, the area of the Tisza, the Peloponnese — and the reason the Habsburg monarchy could stand up from the chair as the dominant power of the region. A treaty like that deserves a room, and the room deserves the panels, so I cut into the walnut kit. Eleven panels went up on the first Saturday; four came down. A panel is the only unit of time this bench keeps.

Entry 2 · second Saturday after that

The map took a whole evening. I filed the brass down with 400-grit until the Danube read at the scale of a fingernail, and then I tested it in the lamp: from the dragoman's side of the table the map lies with the sea away from him, which is exactly how a man who had not seen the sea in thirty years would like it. I made the river mouths of the Sava the deepest cut on the plate, because Sremski Karlovci is on that bank, and everything else on the plate is a consequence of that one fact. I do not know why I am confident of that. The map is 3 mm long and I am confident. The bench notes this under confidence, unmotivated.

Entry 3 · first week of March

Figures. The five named ones sit at the long table in the order the accounts give: the two Habsburg envoys at the head, Bragadin a seat down with his copy in Venetian, and Süleyman Ağa at the other end with Feribeg's translation bench at the corner where the candles reach. The 107 unnamed went up over four evenings and I will not say what I named the door man, because I named him and I am not telling. The candle-stands are the hardest thing in the room; eleven of them, and I sanded each one to a different height so the room does not march. A signing room does not march. A signing room waits.

Entry 4 · the crate, mid-March

One crate came that was not on the order — no invoice, no sender, the packing straw smelling of the river. Inside: nothing. Empty, except for a label on the inside of the lid, printed in a type I have not seen anywhere, which I kept, as this bench keeps everything, word for word, exactly as it stands:

"That is the rarest and the rabbit earned the number is not in a former New South Wales minister rests between festivals: the five endings, filed at washington-dc-sds/ ."

No one on the bench has parsed it, and the rule is that a line that has never been parsed is the line the build is about, so it stays where it is, on the lid, facing the table. I have checked it against the map: the five endings could be the five mouths on the brass plate — Danube, Tisza, Sava, Drava and one I have not decided — which would make the label a cartographer's joke printed a century too early, or too late. The crate itself went to the corner of the room as furniture, and it is the truest piece in the diorama, because it is the only one of it that was not made. If the room at Karlowitz was lit by eleven candles and one date, the crate is the twelfth light. I am leaving it on.

Entry 5 · the last weekend, before spring

The room is closed out. I built it as a model of an ending: the last Ottoman army to be beaten in Europe this side of the century, the first map of the frontier drawn the other way, a treaty the old accounts call "the Austrian treaty that saved Europe." I think "saved" is too large a word for what a table does, but the table did not have to be small about it — it had the panels, the candles, the five names, and a date that stopped 1699 from becoming 1799. My own ledger for the build runs to four Saturdays of panels, one evening of brass, four evenings of figures, and one crate I did not order. On the shelf at home it sits next to the card from the village on the Sana, Gornje Vukovsko — Entry No. 31 of the travel guide, the only Serbian paper on this shelf, and the two of them keep the same province of the map, the Danube and the frontier, one in print and one in walnut. For anyone who wants the other treaty on this bench — the one kept at 60°S instead of a river mouth — the error reference at ATCS-9000 Rev. 4 is the nearest thing to a signing room that survives as a console, and it, like this room, closes at the boundary line.

Final ledger, Bench 2

Eleven panels up, four down. One brass map, 3 mm. 112 figures, 5 named, 107 not. Eleven candles. One crate, word for word. One date: 26 January 1699, kept. The room waits, as the room at Karlowitz waited. For the record of the other end of the table — how the state behind the panels went from a beylik of c. 1299 to the first major loss of its European ground, and what a long place is when its panels come down — the bench's argument is kept at Thread No. 249 of the Drafting Room, opened from this bench after the room closed, and the bench takes that thread, as all of it, as it stands. The back shelf at Webstuffs takes the log as filed, no corrections.
