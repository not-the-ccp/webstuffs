1 Identification

Use of the product as described in Section 1: the subject is an English archaeologist who specialised in Early Medieval landscape archaeology, the study of field systems, enclosures, trackways, and the wider pattern of settlement left by the centuries immediately after the Roman withdrawal from Britain. Trade names and synonyms in circulation: Mick; the Oxford landscape man; the resident academic. The supplier's records show lecturing appointments at both the University of Bristol and the University of Oxford, and the publication of fifteen books on archaeological subjects. The product is a keen populariser of the discipline and was widely known for appearing as the resident academic on the Channel 4 television series Time Team from 1994 to 2011. Further documentation, and two cross-referenced field records of mounds and men, are filed at the end of this sheet.

4 First-aid measures

Inhalation: if the subject is inhaled at close range in the field (a demonstration, a lecture, a walk over a barrow), move to fresh air, i.e., the next field system, and observe. Skin contact: tweed stains are permanent; treat as decoration, not damage. Ingestion: a person who has read all fifteen books in one term should be rested, supplied with a site plan, and released no earlier than term's end. Eyes: symptoms of direct exposure (sudden belief that a ploughed field is a manuscript to be read line by line) subside after one good hoard. In all cases, record what happened; the record is the treatment.

5 Fire-fighting measures

Do not hose the archive. Combustion of the product's notes produces smoke the colour of subsoil and an odour of wet chalk; firefighting personnel should use dry sand only, and preferentially sand that has already been excavated, sieved, and labelled. Extinguishing media: sand (stratigraphic grade); no foam on the finds crates. The product kept a lifelong rule that a burning archive is a closed one, and the supplier stands by that rule. Special hazards: secondary combustion of the television schedule, 1994–2011, which relights readily on a Sunday.

6 Accidental release measures

On 24 June 2013 the product was withdrawn from circulation in full. Fifteen monographs were already released into the public domain between 1974 and 2009 and cannot be recalled; contain their spread by reading them in order. Spills of broadcast material (episodes, 1994–2011) dry slowly and leave a residue of public affection that is harmless and, in the supplier's opinion, the most stable compound the product ever made. Methods and materials for cleanup: the find bag, the site plan, and one afternoon that is not raining. Environmental precautions: do not return the product to the ground before the season's recording is complete.

7 Handling and storage

The product was manufactured in Oldbury, Worcestershire, in 1946, in a working-class house, where an early interest in archaeology developed before the child had a name for it. Handling began at the University of Birmingham, where archaeology was studied as a subsidiary to geography; the two have travelled together ever since. In 1970 the product took its first field post, beginning a working life with the excavation units of the Oxford region; it moved on to the University of Bristol, then to the University of Oxford, where it lectured on landscape archaeology. Store in the open country, away from plough lines where possible, at a temperature of the season. Keep the product away from students who have already read the hoards and expect them; keep the hoards away from students who have not. Conditions of storage: the field, the trench, and the archive room, in that order.

8 Exposure controls / personal protection

Occupational exposure limit (OEL): one broadcast season per year, 1994–2011; the product served eighteen full seasons on Channel 4 as resident academic on Time Team and required no additional protective equipment, which is to say tweed. Monitoring: weekly, by the public, from a distance of one television. The supplier's reception log for the 16 August 1997 broadcast run is entered here verbatim, without annotation, because a log line is not a sentence and the committee does not correct it: “The Naga Listeners' Club, Naga City, Camarines Sur: Comm.” The line came through on the long-wave set in the dig house and was transcribed, read back, and filed as received. Personal protection for field personnel: gloves, wellingtons, and a sense of proportion, which the product supplied in surplus.

10 Stability and reactivity

Stable under normal country conditions for the full working life, 1970–2013. Conditions to avoid: ploughing without watching; a field system spoken of as “just fences”; a demonstration that runs long for want of a good find. Incompatible materials: the bulldozer, the hurry, and the sentence that ends an argument about the past. Hazardous decomposition products: grief, which the product handled with the same hands as the trowel. Hazardous polymerisation: the product is known to bond with other archaeologists in long chains that outlast all of them.

11 Toxicological information

Acute exposure: the subject is non-toxic in single doses; one lecture produces a temporary but useful vertigo in which the present thins out and the centuries behind it show through. Chronic exposure: repeated field seasons produce the occupational condition known as landscape literacy, in which the patient can no longer look at a plain without reading it; no cure is known, and none is recommended. The working-class manufacture of Oldbury is not a hazard and is, on the evidence of the early interest in the ground, a source of the product's tenacity. LD values are not reported, because the product was reported back to the ground only once, on 24 June 2013, and there were no adverse reactions in the surrounding fields.

12 Ecological information

Mobilisable in large quantities by the public; the broadcast form (eighteen seasons) is estimated to have introduced the Early Medieval landscape to millions of listeners who had previously seen it only from the top of a hill. Non-biodegradable respect for the past is a persistent residue and is considered beneficial. Effects on the local digger population are strongly positive: the product's crews at the University of Bristol, the University of Oxford, and on the Channel 4 site are a standing nursery of the trade. Do not release the product's methods into the hands of a metal-detectorist without supervision; supervised release is a different matter and was in fact routine.

13 Disposal considerations

The product is not to be disposed of with domestic refuse, the plough, or the hurry. The residue was committed to the ground that had produced it in full season, with the record complete. The fifteen monographs are to be stored in special collections; disposal by burning is prohibited, and disposal by argument is strongly discouraged and occasionally necessary. The television residue (episodes and footage, 1994–2011) is to be archived, not deleted: a broadcast record of this kind is a site in its own right, and it is excavated the same way, season by season, level by level, with everything bagged and labelled before it moves.

14 Transport information

UN number: not assigned (the product travelled under its own name, which sufficed). Transport class: field staff, first class of the trade. The product's known movements, in order: Oldbury, Worcestershire → University of Birmingham → Oxford region, first field post, 1970 → University of Bristol → University of Oxford → the Channel 4 site, 1994–2011. No special transport precautions are required for a subject of this kind; the product sat on the same coaches and ferries as the general public and, on the evidence of the crews' later letters, improved the ride. Marine transport is to be recorded on a separate sheet, and that sheet is kept with the find bags.

15 Regulatory information

The product is registered in the national catalogue of popularisers of the discipline and is cited there without qualification, a rarer grade of entry than the supplier's records can recall. No restriction, ban, or self-classification exemption applies: a subject who has lectured at two universities, published fifteen books, and stood in the same trench as the public for eighteen broadcast seasons is, in the committee's standing reading, its own regulation. The 1970 first field post is recorded as the point of origin of the working life and is dated on every form the supplier keeps.

16 Other information

Cross-referenced field records. The supplier's file keeps this sheet alongside two records of other mounds and other men, filed by exchange. The first is a school report from Nagomi Junior High on Eta Funayama Kofun in Kumamoto — a boat-hill kofun, the 1951 national designation, and bronzes now in Tokyo — a different ground and a different century from the product's fields, kept here because the file holds one good record of a mound next to every good record of a man who read mounds: see the Class 2-C report on Eta Funayama Kofun. The second is a Form 3B term report from The Ochs Avenue Day School on Albert Einstein (1879–1955) — another register of a life written day by day and kept in an envelope — and it is kept here because the product's own working life, 1 July 1946 to 24 June 2013, was in the end kept the same way, one season at a time: see the term report on Albert Einstein. The supplier's table-games arm issued the same product, on retirement, as a field game in sixteen rules — The Anarchy Table, Catalogue No. 7, serial 001 kept in the dig house for the whole broadcast run: a plain with three passes, a scale of eleven grains, and a trowel for a die, and it was played to a win by the Naga Listeners’ Club against the crew of one broadcast season: see the player's manual, Catalogue No. 7. For the rest of the shelves where these papers sit, return to the Webstuffs front page.
