Ancient Macedon

ΜΑΚΕΔΟΝΙΑ · the plain of the Haliacmon & lower Axios · one kingdom, one dialect, one very long road north

1 · Where you are

Come in from the hills and you are on it before you know it: the alluvial plain where the Haliacmon meets the lower Axios in the northeastern corner of mainland Greece, the ground the Macedonians — the Makedónes — settled on when a people is looking for a river plain and a place to put a throne. This is not a museum site in any tidy sense. It is a plain, and the plain is still doing what it did: farming, flooding in a hard autumn, and holding the memory of a kingdom in the names of its villages. The Greeks of the southern cities argued for centuries whether the Makedónes counted as Greeks at all, and the argument is better than the answer. The speech they kept, Ancient Macedonian, is usually classified as a dialect of Northwest Greek, occasionally as a dialect of Aeolic Greek, and by a stubborn minority as a distinct Hellenic tongue of its own — a three-way argument that has outlived the plain's borders, its kings, and its roads, and is the truest souvenir you can bring back.

If you want the long version of the kingdom's one famous resident, the desk keeps a full entry of his own: see Alexander the Great, where the plain is the departure point and the rest of the known world is the timetable.

2 · The Argead seat at Aigai

The kingdom of Macedon was ruled from Aigai by the Argeads, and it is still the first stop of any honest itinerary. Walk the ridge road up to the old acropolis and the royal tombs at the foot of the hill, where the painted friezes of a fourth-century funeral — charioteers, horses, a procession going somewhere you cannot follow — are covered with waxed cloth from October to March, because the fog comes down the Haliacmon valley and would otherwise argue with the restorers. The Argeads kept the seat for generations, absorbing or driving out the non-Greek neighbours, primarily Thracian and Illyrian, exactly as a plain does: slowly, and in every direction at once. By the fifth century the kingdom that started on the northern edge of the Greek world had swallowed the edge.

3 · The relay guesthouse at Emathia Cross

Two kilometres off the modern road, where the Haliacmon track once crossed the ridge, stands the guesthouse of the relay riders: four rooms, a mule shed, a well that has never once run dry on the record, and a guest book of forty-one pages, still in the oak cabinet by the door. The book is the one object in the whole region that travellers ask for, and it is not the tombs that do the keeping — it is the book. Forty entries survive in ink that ranges from careful to furious. A Thessalian wine merchant, 1784. A Pashas's courier, refusing to name his master. A naturalist who sketched the river in a margin and signed it W. Baker's cousin, probably. The last entry is the entry everyone is actually here for, a single line in a cramped hand at the very bottom of the final page: A keeper who signed himself only Counting ; it left Sherborne. The "it" is the book itself, which left Sherborne in England in a trunk of odds and ends in the winter of 1811, and the keeper is the man who signed it — a man of the relay who apparently kept the count of the book's pages, its riders, and the years, and nothing else at all. The count survives: the book's own tally of the riders who passed Emathia Cross in the winter of 1811 stands at one hundred and nine, and no one since has been able to match the number against the post registers. The guesthouse keeps the cabinet locked and the count unlocked. It is the only number in the region that refuses to be explained, which is, in the end, how a region keeps its secrets.

4 · Routes, days, and money

Plan five days and you are not being dramatic. Day one the plain and the Haliacmon. Day two Aigai, early, before the cloth goes on the friezes. Day three the Axios mouth at the sea, where the plain ends and the whole argument about how Greek this place was starts over again, cheaper and saltier. Day four the guesthouse at Emathia Cross and the book. Day five walking down the Haliacmon valley the way the kingdom itself once walked, absorbing, driving out, going north, and coming home with the dialect argument instead of a postcard, because that is what this plain sells.
