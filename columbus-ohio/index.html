The Week in Brief, set in the old style

The Ledger opens its column where every honest column must: at the count. The town this page is of is Columbus, the capital and most populous city of the state of Ohio, sitting in its central plain along the Scioto River, which the city was before it was a city — several Native American settlements along the water, and the mounds keeping their own account since long before the ledger had a desk. The 2020 count balances the books at 905,748, which makes the city the 14th-most populous in the United States, the second-most populous in the Midwest after Chicago, and the third-most populous U.S. state capital after Phoenix, Arizona, and Austin, Texas. The metropolitan area, estimated at 2.23 million residents, is the largest metropolitan area entirely in Ohio and the 32nd-largest in the country. Every one of those figures was counted in the same city where High Street runs true from the old ford to the square, and the Ledger sets them in type exactly as they were counted, the way a till plain keeps its shape.

Neighbours will forgive the Ledger its manners. Across the water in Indiana, the pressing called Crossroads of America, White River Records WRC-582 keeps the week of September 7–10, 2017 on twelve tracks — one announcement, one course rebuilt, one title at 18 under, one stew — and its liner notes name the Midwest's second city, after Chicago, only once, by the number the column is printing in its masthead this week. The class report that grew the record, filed from Westport Middle School, Class 7B, checked its course book against the same barometer this office reads: 30.42, holding, and to the hundredth, steady. The Ledger has checked it too. It is steady. The whole shelf, if the reader wants the almanac and the ledger at its back, is reachable from the Webstuffs home page, two lanes up from this column.

What follows is the column itself: rooms and river frontage, flag ropes and barometers, one cormorant the county has not given up on, and one lost line of the ledger that no one has decoded — set word for word, because the ledger asks, and the column answers.

Real Estate & River Frontage

To Let

Three rooms over a cobbler, Broad Street at 17th

Let, to a keeper of ledgers or a student of roads, three rooms above the cobbler's shop on the corner the county has always trusted. Gas, water from the old main along the river, and a window on the square. The barometer on the wall read 30.42 at the last signing; the landlord reads it every Saturday and has found it, to the hundredth, the same. Rent is three honest kitchen notes a month, or one in advance, the way the till plain takes its weather. Enquire at the shop, before the cobbler's boy goes to the river.

For Sale

582 feet of Scioto bank

For sale, by the county's own measure, 582 feet of low bank on the north side of the Scioto — the yardage of the seventeenth, taken as it is now taken, from the water, not the grass. The surveyor's note stands in the deed. Serious applicants only; the bank does not permit tempo.

To Let

A backstretch grandstand, season of 2017

Let, one season only, the grandstand that held its flag ropes after the year the championship was not renewed. It stands in a yard off Long Street, roped and flagged, and it holds nine thousand a day the way the class 7B report says it held them. The ceremony between the third and fourth rounds is the tenant's own affair. See the liner notes of WRC–582, track B4, for the ropes themselves.

Notice

Of the mounds, and their account

Notice is given, in the old style, that the mounds of the settlements along the Scioto predate the Ledger, the county, and the state, and that they keep their own account. No notice is required of them, and none is wanted. The column mentions them once, in the Brief, and mentions them again here, because a classified column about this town is not finished until the river's first neighbours are filed somewhere on the page.

For Sale

The flag ropes, backstretch, Indianapolis

For sale, one pair of flag ropes, backstretch grandstand, course book of 2017, in the condition the course book records them — still in hand. They were carried out of the yard the winter after the championship was not renewed, and they have been kept since, coiled, at the back of the reading shelf. The price is a fair one and will be stated to callers only, in the same smaller hand as the recipe. The Class 7B report has verified the ropes against section 4 and found no variance.

For Sale

A recipe card, cream and creased

For sale, the course book's own recipe card — a thick stew of west-fork trout, breaded and fried in till-farm butter, fried low, turned once, carried to the bunker, not rushed. The card runs three ingredients, two instructions, and a final line in a smaller hand which the buyer will find is a promise as well as an instruction: it will be found a very small quantity of gravy, and the plate is wiped anyway. Sold with the pan it was written for. No haggling over the gravy.

For Sale

A ledger, second-hand, with birds

For sale, one ledger of cormorants, second-hand, the roost on the old Speedway pond, the 1842 obituary, and the flood that washed out the fairway. The birds are accounted for, in the ledger's own hand, and the buyer should be warned that the ledger asks to be read, and answers the reader in kind. Priced at what a drowned colony of birds is worth in a town on a river, which the Ledger understands to be more than nothing and less than the water.

The brass enters from the north, which toward this column is toward Indianapolis. Title is clear; the county seat plays in strict 3/4 and does not permit tempo, and neither does the estate.

Of the barometer, 30.42

It is noted, for the record, that the steady barometer of the whole run — 30.42, holding — was checked in this office against the season book kept at the back of the reading shelf, the winter reading of 27 February 2024 carried at the weather almanac, entry 2024/058, and found, to the hundredth, steady, the same barometer the 7B report checked before the album was cut. Three offices, one number, no variance. The Ledger proposes the barometer be kept at that reading until further notice, and the further notice is expected to be the weather's own.

Lost & Found

Lost

One line of the ledger, in a small hand

LOST — or, as the Ledger prefers to say, accounted for but not decoded — one line of the cormorant ledger, found between the pages of a course book at High Street, in a hand smaller than the stew's. The line is copied here word for word, because the ledger asked, and the column answers:

Negligence and want of more stirring waters, than those grown in fields are the best.

The office has examined the line against every other record in the column and found it the only instruction in the shelf that is also a weather. Read with the recipe card — the one that finds a very small quantity of gravy — and the line settles: the stirring waters are the pot's, the waters that are grown in fields are the till's, and a stew that gives back almost nothing to the plate wants more of the one and less of the want, which is what the line has been saying in its smaller hand all along. Whoever holds the other half of the card, or the card's own cook, call the Ledger Room at the desk on the square. The reward is one honest kitchen note, as priced in the masthead, and the line itself, returned to the ledger, where it will be read, and will answer the reader in kind.

Found

Found: the seventeenth's hazard angles

FOUND, at the bank above, the hazard angles for the seventeenth, taken as the surveyor directs — from the water, not the grass — after the flood that washed out the fairway and made the old course's pond a question. Claimed by the course book, verified by the 7B, and returned to the shelf where the cormorants are credited, on all twelve tracks, as The Ledger. The claimant is thanked and reminded that the flag stayed.

Found

Found: a barometer, steady

FOUND, hanging on no wall, the barometer of 30.42, holding. It was found doing its work, as it had been doing it in Indianapolis and as it has been doing it in this office since the Saturday before the last. No claim is wanted; a barometer that reads steady to the hundredth is the property of the whole shelf. It is kept where it was found, which is between the season book and the ledger, at the back of the reading shelf, and it is read every Saturday, as is recorded above.

Services & Engagements

Services

Barometers checked to the hundredth

This office checks barometers against the season book, to the hundredth, at a fee of one reading. We have checked the 30.42 of the WRC-582 credits, the 30.42 of the class report, and the 30.42 of the wall at Broad Street at 17th, and we report them steady, identical, and holding. The office declines to check any barometer that will not hold, and the till plain's weather is not declined.

Services

Stews, fried low and turned once

Stews of west-fork trout, breaded and fried in till-farm butter, carried to the bunker where the crowd can watch them take colour. The house keeps the course book's card: three ingredients, two instructions, no rushing, and the final line in a smaller hand, printed exactly as written, because the stew's one defect is the point of the card. The gravy is scarce, as the card promises, and the plate is wiped anyway. Parties of nine thousand a day accepted the same way the field accepted the yardage.

Engagements

County waltz, strict 3/4

The Franklin County seat plays in strict 3/4 and does not permit tempo; this office engages its quartet for weddings, signings, and the ceremony between the third and fourth rounds, where the flag ropes are still in hand. The courthouse waltz is recorded on A4 of the pressing and may be heard, in the old style, wherever the shelf is. Tempo will not be permitted, and the county is thanked for refusing it first.

Wanted

Wanted

A cormorant, if any have crossed the water

WANTED — a cormorant. The colony that roosted on the old Speedway pond is accounted for in the ledger's own hand: the 1842 obituary marks the drowning of the last of it in the flood that washed out the fairway, and the hazard angles are now taken from the water, not the grass. The Ledger has not finished the argument. If any of the colony, or their account, has crossed the water — from the pond to the Scioto, from the till plain to this column — report to the desk on the square. No questions will be asked, and the questions that are asked will be the ledger's, which is a different weather. The birds are credited as The Ledger, on all twelve tracks, and the credit is kept open for the one who answers. One letter has already come in from the keeper of the ledger, filed in the sleeve of the pressing at the Roost and the Creed: the cormorant is not lost, the keeper writes, it is a count, and the count is a congregation, in strict 3/4, at the corner, and does not rush.

Wanted

A reader of ledgers, 32 in all

WANTED, for the desk on the square, one reader of ledgers, experienced in the 32nd-largest metropolitan area in the country, estimated at 2.23 million residents and entirely in this state. The reader will keep the account of the 905,748 of the 2020 count, and the accounts of the 14th, the second, and the third, and will find them, as the office has found them, steady. Apply in the old style. The shelf is reachable from the Webstuffs home page, and the reading shelf itself keeps the Class 7B report at its head.
