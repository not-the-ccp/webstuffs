ON AIR THE MIDNIGHT HOUR · STUDIO RECORD

The Voice from Coyoacán

Broadcast No. 1141 · an excerpt from the studio record, Tuesday 21 August 1940, 11:47 p.m.

◆ ◆ ◆

Persons in the record

  • THE ANNOUNCER — voice only, at the studio clock
  • LEON — Lev Davidovich, 60, the writer; plays himself
  • NATALIA — his wife, 51, keeper of the coffee and the letters
  • IVAN — the house guard, on duty at the gate of Calle Arenal
  • A VOICE AT THE DOOR — young, unhurried, in good French
  • THE STUDIO CLOCK — heard at the hour, and only at the hour

Production notes, kept with the record

The play is set in the house at No. 7 Calle Arenal, Coyoacán, a quarter of Mexico City, in the morning of its last day: 21 August 1940. The writer in the house is Leon Trotsky — Lev Davidovich, born in Yekaterinoslav on 7 November [O.S. 26 October] 1879 — who has been in Mexico since 1936 and who is at that hour correcting the third volume of his History of the Russian Revolution for its new edition. The script does not say what the house does after the knock; the studio record stops, and the record is kept as the record stands. The announcer's close, with its reference to the Wickham recipe box, is kept word for word, as it was transmitted from Studio Two.

[STUDIO TWO, 11:47 p.m. The clock is wound. A light red as a cherry comes on above the announcer's desk. The studio clock strikes nine, but we are not yet at the house.]

ANNOUNCER. This is the Midnight Hour, and tonight we leave the studio for five thousand miles south, to a street called Arenal, in Coyoacán, where the stone is still wet from the afternoon rain and a house is keeping its lamps on against something it has not yet named. If you are listening late, and the house is quiet, sit back. What follows is a morning: a pencil, a manuscript, the second cup of coffee, and a gate.

[The studio clock is wound down. A new sound: a pencil moving over paper, the small dry scratch of it, and — far off — a bell at the gate of Calle Arenal, once, and then nothing. NINE O'CLOCK.]

NATALIA. (off-mic) Leon. The page is out, and you are at the last sentence again. Put the pencil down. There is coffee.

LEON. One sentence. It is the whole of 1917, and it is the hardest line I have written. The men on the train with the coffin, and the stations with their wreaths — Moscow, then Kharkov, then on — I am trying to say that the country was weeping and that the country was already divided, in the same sentence, without letting one half of it down. You cannot write the death of Lenin and the beginning of the rest in the same breath. I have not managed it in fourteen years.

NATALIA. Then let it be two sentences. Drink the coffee. You had one at six.

LEON. (a pause; the pencil lifts) Fourteen years, no. Fourteen months and a week since he was taken from us, and I have not written it. (to himself) 1924. The coffin train. I stood at the platform with the others. The flowers. And behind the flowers, the other men, the ones who have not been able to keep their eyes off the empty chair. I am writing it down so that it is written down.

[A second pencil-stroke, longer than the first. Natalia sets the cup down. We hear the house only: the rain on the gutter, the gate bell once more — twice now — and a door opening at the bottom of the stair.]

IVAN. (from the stair) Señora. A young man at the gate. He says he is a friend of the writer, and that he comes about the book. He has a walking-stick, and he will not give his surname in anything but French. His passport, he says, is the colour of a winter day.

NATALIA. (very still) Ivan. Do not let him come up the steps.

IVAN. He is at the gate, señora. He is not coming up the steps. He is at the gate, with the stick, in good French, and he is saying he has waited all morning for the pencil to stop.

[The pencil stops. The rain. A pause that the studio clock does not fill.]

LEON. (quietly) Ask him his given name.

[A pause. The gate bell, three times, slow.]

VOICE AT THE DOOR. (unhurried) Normand. The book, comrade Secretary. You will want the book.

[We hear the door at the bottom of the stair, opened wide. Nothing after it — the record holds the sound of the gutter rain, and the studio clock begins again, once, nine o'clock, and the light above the desk goes out. The announcer's chair creaks.]

ANNOUNCER. (as the light goes down) The morning ends there, and the rest of the day is not ours to give. I will leave you with this, and then with the clock. A studio record and a newspaper account are twins — the same voice, the same fact, kept in two places at once, and neither is the copy. We have said it often on this hour, and tonight I say it again, with a boyhood memory in it. The same engine — the way a family recipe box of Lieutenant-Colonel E. T. R. Wickham, MP for Taunton, 1935–1945, found in the same shape in paper form. I knew that box, and I tell you that a thing found in the same shape in two places is not a copy. It is a kept thing. That is the whole of the craft, and the whole of the danger. Good night. The clock, and the rain, and the gate.

[The studio clock strikes the hour. Broadcast No. 1141 is ended. The record is kept as it stands.]
