Mabel’s Baptism Loaves

Two loaves, white as the robes · for the potluck after the river service · Willamette River Baptist Church

Preamble, which is long

The loaves are for one Sunday a year, and one Sunday only: the third Sunday in April, the day the Willamette River Baptist Church goes down to the water at the foot of Flanders Street Bridge in Portland, where the deacon holds the robe, the band plays from the boathouse bench, and somebody always brings one too many folding chairs. Mabel Hoss’s loaves have been on that potluck table every year since the spring of 1974, when her sister Gwenda went under at nineteen for the first and only time. Two loaves, because there are two things the church does with its hands — the loaves themselves, and the water — though the church has always been careful that the bread you carry and the bread you are ordained to eat are not the same bread, and has kept that careful since 1979, by vote.

Our church is a small one. Ask the secretary and it is forty-one souls; ask at the coffee urn, where Gwenda’s coffee is poured at 6:40 sharp and the deacon is made to stand in line like everyone else, and it is forty-three, the organ-loft cat included by custom if not by roll. It is a Baptist church, which is the whole of the preamble in four clauses, and the family has never once shortened the four. First: soul competency — every single soul stands before God on its own account and answers for itself; the deacon called it soul competency, the coffee urn called it the 6:40 service, and both were right. Second: sola fide — the person is saved by faith alone and by nothing in a basket, which is the entire theological reason these loaves are not holy bread and nobody, at the table, pretends otherwise. Third: sola scriptura — the Bible is the whole and infallible book, which the family shortens to “the Big Book,’ and Mabel marked it in the margins with her own flour-dust, which is perhaps as close to a commentary as the church allows. Fourth: congregational polity — the congregation is the church and the church is the congregation, and every question whatsoever is put to a vote of the room, which is why the question of baking the loaves at Mabel’s house or in the church kitchen was put to a vote in October 1973 and carried 9 to 6 in Mabel’s favour, Mabel refusing the church kitchen on the recorded ground that its timer “preaches in a hurry.”

Baptism, in our house and our church, is for the believer and for nobody else — never a baby, never “just a few drops while they’re small” — and it is done the one way: the whole of the person goes under. Total immersion. Dipped, not dabbed, as Gwenda wrote in green marker on the recipe box, where the rule has hung on the kitchen wall ever since, under the calendar, above the thing the deacon says about salt. The congregation at the bridge will wear its white robes, stand in the shallows at the river’s edge, and watch. The service runs the way the service has run: the hymn, the reading, the prayer, and then the walk to the water, and then the count.

The count is the part of the day the family is most careful about, and the rule is written down here once, so that it may not be argued with again: the dunk is counted in threes — one for the wetting of the robe, two for the name said aloud, three for the coming up — and there is no fourth. It is not only as long as the bridge directs. The bridge directs the traffic, and if the honking of the Flanders Street deck should happen to land on the third count, the family counts it a blessing; but the count belongs to the deacon, and has belonged to the deacon since the spring of 1974, when Deacon Ott counted his clean three and Uncle Marlowe, who had slipped in a half-second early on the strength of a fourth count nobody ever authorised, surfaced to find the river had taken his hat and his argument with it, and held his opinion of the matter in both his ears for a good week. Gwenda’s own three counts were done at 9:14 in the morning, in water that was 44 degrees by the church thermometer, and she came up singing, which is how the family knows she was ready, because the ready ones sing and the not-ready ones cough.

After the service the band plays off the boathouse bench while the towels dry on the bridge railing and the loaves are carried down in pairs, one in each hand, which is a long walk for a woman of seventy-one and Mabel did it anyway. The record of that band, the one that grew out of those April evenings and cut We Are Fearless under the leaking roof, is kept in the museum two blocks away, in the gallery panel that names the genre sixty years deep: Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard. The other half of the April tradition — the cream-lemonade card that goes every second of April to St. Casimir’s, kept from a death anniversary in 2005 and written up in full in the gallery files — is filed beside the recipe box in its own letter: Letter to the Editor — The Cream and the Cantus Firmus. And the first hymn the church sings on the Sunday after a river service is a plain little Welsh tune in F major, Blaenwern, which the family has come to regard as its own tune, and which the Pembrokeshire parish across the parish files has been keeping a lost-and-found notice on since 1915: Blaenwern — Lost & Found Notice No. 47, Tufton Parish. The loaves go on the table after the hymn. The loaves do not ask to be explained.

Ingredients

Makes two loaves of about 1 lb each · serves the potluck, i.e. everybody

  • 4 cups all-purpose flour, spooned and levelled
  • 2¼ tsp active dry yeast
  • 1 cup whole milk, warmed to 110 °F — the temperature of a good handshake
  • 3 tbsp sugar, plus 4 tbsp more for the icing
  • 2 tbsp unsalted butter, melted and cooled to nearly bearable
  • 2 eggs, room temperature, plus 1 egg white
  • 1 tsp fine salt, added after the kneading begins, never before
  • 1 tsp vanilla, which the church has not voted on
  • for the white: 4 tbsp icing sugar, 1 tbsp warm water, a drop of the vanilla
  • 1 knife, clean, for the cross — one stroke, not two

Method

  1. Pour the warm milk into the bowl and sprinkle the 2¼ tsp of sugar and the yeast over it. Stand in the coldest corner of the kitchen for ten minutes. It should foam to the rim. If it only foams halfway, the yeast is tired and the church has already voted you down.
  2. Beat in the butter, the remaining sugar, the eggs, and the vanilla. Add the flour, a cup at a time, and work until it pulls away from the bowl. Add the salt on the fourth turn of the hands.
  3. Knead eight minutes — count them out, the way the deacon counts the dunk — until the dough is smooth and slightly, properly, ridiculous. Cover. Rest an hour, or until doubled, which is the dough’s own sola fide.
  4. Divide in two. Shape each into a tight 1-lb loaf and lay them in the two tins, seam down, the way Gwenda laid the robes out in rows, one per name.
  5. Preheat to 350 °F. Score a cross on each loaf with a single stroke of the clean knife, from top to base, without lifting. The cross scores; it does not debate.
  6. Bake 30 to 35 minutes, until the base knocks hollow and the inside reads 190 °F. The timer in Mabel’s house does not preach in a hurry.
  7. Whisk the icing to a thin white ribbon. While the loaves are still warm, pour it over the cross and let it run a little. White as the robes is the instruction, and “a little” is in the instruction.
  8. Cool on the rack until the kitchen is only warm. Wrap each loaf in a clean white towel, the way the deacon holds the robe, one in each hand.
  9. Carry them down to the river after the service, during the hymn, and set them on the potluck table between the lemonade and the folding chairs. Break the first piece of the first loaf on the third count of the next year’s service, and do not look at the bridge while you do it.

A note on the table: these are potluck loaves. The church keeps the bread of the Lord’s Supper — the other of the two ordinances, with the baptism that sends the family to the water — in a locked drawer in the back office, where it is the one bread that is never cut at the table, and the drawer key is the one thing the congregation has never voted to divide. The loaves are for the singing, the towels, the lemonade, and the drive home with the windows down.
