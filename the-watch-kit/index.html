What Kit No. 12 is for

Kit No. 12 is our working model of the suburban patrol, built from the season's own record: a cul-de-sac of six houses where the neighbors — played, in the 2012 picture the kit is licensed to, by Ben Stiller, Vince Vaughn, Jonah Hill and Richard Ayoade, with Rosemarie DeWitt on the porch — form a watch on a Tuesday night and find, by Thursday, that the thing on the lawn is not from the county. The film itself ran a development gauntlet the box keeps in its end flap: begun in early 2008 under producer Shawn Levy as a teen-targeted project by writer Jared Stern, then moving through different directors and different leads between mid 2009 and late 2010 until Akiva Schaffer took the helm and the three writers — Stern, Seth Rogen and Evan Goldberg — set the block. We license the picture's central move, not its special effects: the move is that a group of ordinary neighbors, armed with flashlights and a shared roster, can hold a perimeter around an extraordinary discovery long enough for the discovery to become a fact. The kit is the move, in hardware.

Inside the box

The twelve flashlights are numbered 1 through 12 and sold as a set; a block of nine or more is invited to write in the extras' numbers. The roster wall goes on the kitchen door of the lead household — in the picture, the Stiller house — and the 24 laminated cards take the names of every adult on the block in the order the walkie hears them. Channel 2 of the walkie is reserved for the discovery itself, which is why the manual prints the rule in large type: if the thing answers, hold the channel until the row is written. The 60 identification cards cover the catalogued forms, each front and back, and the score sheet at the bottom of the sleeve is the part customers buy the kit for.

The score sheet

The score sheet is twelve rows by six columns, one row per household, and it is printed to be filled in by hand after each patrol. It is, in the committee's phrase, the ledger of the block: what moved, what did not, what was said on channel 2. It is also the only piece of the kit that never wears out, because a printed row does not forget, and the manual's standing order is that a sheet is closed only when all twelve rows are signed, never before. Customers who keep the sheet past the season tell us the block goes on in the margin, the way a score does. The block that has kept its sheet up since season one, Maple Court, has filed its ledger into the margin at last: Minutes No. 13 of the Watch Council of Maple Court enters a certain director at row 13 in pencil, and keeps one pasted note, read word for word, face down in the walkie case for the record.
