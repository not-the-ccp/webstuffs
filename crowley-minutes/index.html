Minutes of the council · session 7 · Machinery Restaurant Trust

The Black Parcel — Minutes of Session Seven

held in the cellar beneath the bar, 14 Marsh Road · Friday, 15 March 1991 · sitting opened 21:04, closed 23:41

KEPT BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL

Present

M. Venn, secretary, in the chair and keeping the record. Eustace, of the bar, present and pouring. Dot, of the kitchen, present at the grill and counted present by the sound of it. Graeme Kay, of the second table, present by invitation as observer, and not permitted to vote. Quorum established at 21:04: two of the four, one of whom was pouring, which the council has always held to be enough.

Item 1 — the parcel from Halstrom

The secretary reported that the parcel ordered by cash at the record shop on Halstrom — the same shop where the Friday tapes come from, past the exchange shelf with the four short stories — had arrived that evening by hand, and that it was not tapes. It contained one cloth-bound book, black, the title set in red: Liber AL vel Legis, no author’s name on the cover, only a number. It contained one tape, third speed, marked in grease pencil 777. It contained one postcard, a bearded man on a bare peak, captioned Sigismond. It contained one line in grease pencil on the back of a water bill, which the council does not repeat here and which is dealt with under Item 3. The shop had not named the book’s author. The secretary had named him.

Aleister Crowley, born Edward Alexander Crowley, 12 October 1875, Royal Leamington Spa, Warwickshire; died 1 December 1947. English by the book; by the record of the room, English in every sense that mattered to the question, which was whether he was one of us. The names as they stand in the parcel: occultist, ceremonial magician, poet, novelist, mountaineer, painter. Born to a wealthy family in a town his mother kept as a Plymouth Brethren household, and raised on that faith until he rejected it for the western esoteric road — the order of the gold and the star, the tarot, the whole dark shelf. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he turned his attention inward and outward and up mountains in that order. Founder, in the early twentieth century, of the religion called Thelema, in which he held the office of prophet, entrusted with the guiding of humanity into what he called the æon of Horus. He received the Book of the Law — the book in the parcel, three chapters, two hundred and sixty-six verses, dictated to him in Cairo in 1904 by a voice he called Aiwass — and he built temples for it in Cefalù and on the Paravati, and he wrote the Libri of it, and the number of his number was 777, which is why the tape is marked 777, and the council has not been able to work out whether that was the shop or the man.

Item 2 — the motion on the æon

It was moved by the observer, on the strength of the postcard: That the room at 14 Marsh Road be understood, henceforth, as an instrument of the &aelong;on of Horus, and that the green lamp be so read. There was discussion. Eustace moved a bottle. Dot, without descending, was heard to say of the eel that it did not care, and the room accepted this as the position of the kitchen. The observer added that the tape 777 sounded like the industrial music the diary has already filed — grinder on one side, broadcast on the other — except that the industry in question was a library, and the noise was the library. The motion was put and lost, three to one, the one being the observer. The council recorded, with some satisfaction, that the room at 14 Marsh Road is a room; that the green lamp is a lamp, made for a lighthouse and then, one supposes, for a bar; that the menu stands as Grim – Stroke – Disease, eleven courses, eleven pounds; and that the anise remains the anise, the same, unexplained, and correct, as recorded in the secretary’s Marsh Road diary, night four and the tapes, and as found by the Q dining-page verdict, two forks out of five. No syllable changed. No one else will change a syllable of it.

Item 3 — the second slip under the till

The secretary reported that the line from the back of the water bill had been found, that evening, under the till, with the first slip of 14 March — “It is this that shoots through me, and influences me in the circular.” — as though it had been waiting for its companion. The line, in grease pencil, not the hand of the sign above the register, is entered verbatim, and the council enters it thus:

“The seven hundred and twenty thousand miles to get cold: if drunk in the same as mutton dripping frequently has two holes or rooms to nest in.”

Grease pencil, back of a water bill, found under the till, 15 March 1991

Eustace read it twice, shrugged, and poured the secretary something with anise in it, and did not say what it was, which is the correct way to do it, and the council so ruled. The line is not a sentence in any sense the secretary can establish, and yet it is about the room: a distance to get cold, something drunk the same as mutton dripping, and two holes or rooms to nest in — which the council observed are exactly what 14 Marsh Road has, being a room and a cellar, and a thing that keeps a man warm or cold at its pleasure. It is how the loop works. The council resolved that the slip be put back under the till, where it will keep counsel with the sign, the first slip, and the anise, and that no further reading be taken of it at this session or any other. Carried, four to zero.

Item 4 — the tape, played once

It was resolved that the tape marked 777 be played once, in the bar, on the counter, at 22:13, and no second time, on the principle of the pigeon dish. It ran fifteen minutes. What was heard: a grinder, a broadcast coming back into the room changed, a voice reading a table of numbers, and beneath it, a cold. The observer, who had sat facing the water all evening, noted that it was the only industrial record he had heard that he would order the flatbread to. Dot, still at the grill, called it the second time, and no one laughed, and the council recorded the naming. The tape is to be kept in the cellar with the book. The book stands with its spine in.

It is entered in full below, and it can be run by kind — books, tapes, or the loose items — or searched by title, if the hand that works it prefers the mechanical way to the anise. Closing

It was resolved that the book stand in the cellar with its spine in, that the tapes do not play on Fridays — Fridays are the band’s — and that the standing order be printed in smaller type at the foot of every menu, as always: Be back before the tide turns. The observer was shown out past the water. The green lamp was re-checked. The anise was re-checked. The meeting closed at 23:41, which was, as it happened, before the tide turned. Next session, if the parcel answers, on the last volume of the Confessions, which the shop has not admitted to having lost.
