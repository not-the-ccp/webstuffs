Recalde Press & Cooperage · The Dream Ledger · Entry No. 316

COBOL

Common Business-Oriented Language — an entry of the dream ledger, kept at the desk beside the vinegar crock

I. The dream

The undersigned, night watchman of the shed, dreams of a bank. It stands behind the bakery where no bank is, with a queue that does not disperse, and it is open at 2:17 in the morning, which is the hour the ledger allows for the batch. At the counter sits a language in a grey suit, patient, and when it speaks it speaks in whole English sentences, compiled and pressed, the way the desk presses its chapbooks: PERFORM THE TRANSFER UNTIL THE LEDGER IS BALANCED END-PERFORM. The language is COBOL, and it is very old. The ledger says it was born in 1959 at a table where eleven sponsors signed a committee, and it says there are 360 billion lines of it still in service, which the dream does not contradict, because in the dream there is a machine the size of the cattle trough — the very galvanized trough in which the San Juan de Portugal rises at 1:64 — and inside the trough, stacked eight rows deep, are cards of eighty columns each, and a melodeon is reading them. It is Mara Vale on the melodica and Elias Rook on the concertina, and this desk on the hinge harp, the same three who recorded Latin Alphabet No. 9 in the shed in September 1999, now conducting a transaction processing job for a bank that keeps the accounts of every hoop in the county.

The dreamer is told the work is not new work. The large financial institutions of the dream were still writing new systems in the language as late as 2006; the last of those foundations is shown, half set, in a back room. Almost everything since has been maintenance: a card lifted, corrected, returned to its eighty columns. Programs are being moved to a platform the dream cannot see, or rewritten in a modern language that arrives through the Windows door with a manifest it was not asked to carry. The machine at 2:17 does not mind. It has run without complaint for longer than the queue has been alive.

II. The meaning

To dream of COBOL is to dream of a guest who outlives the table. It is an imperative, procedural language — it gives orders in the order given — and, since 2002, it is also object-oriented, which the dream renders as the grey suit learning to button itself. The interpretation the ledger offers is that the dreamer has been kept, not employed. A business, a finance house, an administrative system, a government: these are the rooms in which the guest is at home, and the dream of the bank is the dream of a language that was designed for exactly this house and has since been left in charge of it. The dreamer is not worried about the machine. The dreamer is worried about the successor — the modern language at the door — who has read the etiquette of the Common Language Infrastructure, Chapter VII of the desk book, and knows that a binding once made is not altered without notice.

III. The repeated symbols

The eighty-column card. In the dream it never tears. It carries the whole sentence — the business and the verb and the balance — in exactly eighty columns, no more, and the reader asks for no more. Interpreted: a limit agreed upon in advance is a mercy, and the desk files it beside the rule that serves salt beef by weight, not by favour.

The 2:17 window. The batch runs in the small hours when the queue is thinnest, and nothing in the dream is allowed to speak to the machine while it runs. Interpreted: some work is done for the house, not at the house, and is not discussed.

The year 2006. It appears twice, on a foundation stone and on a receipt, always as the last year of the new building. Interpreted: the dream keeps an exact date for the last guest who brought furniture, and since then the guest has only been polishing.

The trough. The galvanized cattle trough holds the model ship by day; by night it holds the cards. Interpreted: whatever is kept at 1:64 is also kept in earnest, and the vinegar crock keeps both without a number at all. The numbering of the impressions themselves — cloth and boards apart, a reprint keeping its place — is the desk's standing question, answered in ISBN — Frequently Asked Questions, which this dream obeys even in its sleep.

IV. The painted sentence

Above the sorting machine, in the dream, a sentence is painted on the bank's wall, and the dreamer is required to read it aloud before the batch may begin. The ledger sets it down word for word:

The infrastructure divides the house admitted both forms of the International ISBN Agency.

The interpretation is this entry's business. The infrastructure divides the house is the house itself, as Chapter VII keeps it: the type system decides what each guest may be called, the common language specification decides how the guests address one another, and the virtual execution system sees that no one is left standing. To dream of the house admitting is to dream of its door kept open on grammar: a guest is admitted when it declares its types, packs its code, and carries a manifest that names what it brings and what it borrows. And both forms names the two impressions the ledger keeps apart — the number of ten digits, retired on 31 December 2006, and the number of thirteen, admitted on 1 January 2007, the two forms the International ISBN Agency has held at the same counter. What the dream therefore reports, plainly and without alarm, is that the house which divides itself by grammar also admits both forms of the book number, and admits them as one guest under two bindings. COBOL, which the dreamer serves, was admitted to the same house by the same rule and has never once had to be rewritten to keep its place.

V. Where the house keeps the entry

This entry is filed in the dream ledger in the shelf of the shed, between the liner notes of Latin Alphabet No. 9 and the Minutes of the Council of the Shed — The Matter of the Letter G, in which the house admitted both forms of the minuscule g and let the label stay where it was; the dream reads that minute the same way it reads its own painted sentence, and the ledger allows it. The file also holds the box card of Campaign Set No. 63 — the Roman–Parthian war of the Armenian succession, 58–63, whose campaign balance sheet runs eighty columns wide and has balanced since A.D. 63, listed on its product page.
