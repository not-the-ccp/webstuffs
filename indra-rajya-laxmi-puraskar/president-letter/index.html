From the desk of Dinesh Adhikari · Patan Durbar Square, Lalitpur · 25 March 2023

Re: The third President, the first citizen, and an office that still files by hand

To the Editor,
The Gorkhapatra,
Dilli Bazaar, Kathmandu

Sir,

Ram Chandra Paudel took the oath as the third President of Nepal on 13 March 2023, at Shital Niwas, and with that oath a quiet file moved from one hand to another. The President, as your readers know, is the nominal head of the executive, the first citizen of the country, and the supreme commander of the Nepalese Armed Forces — an office created in May 2008, when the country was declared a republic, and filled ever since by an electoral college comprising the Federal Parliament of Nepal and the provincial assemblies of each of Nepal's seven provinces, who themselves are all directly elected. He is the country's third president; the first, Ram Baran Yadav, took office in July 2008, and Bidhya Devi Bhandari served two terms before him.

I write because the office that now answers for the President's honours still works as if the Durbar were open. In January I wrote to the Office of the Crown Seal & Registration of Merits, Forms Branch, Wing 3, asking what seal and ribbon the President uses when he confers the Indra Rajya Laxmi Pragya Puraskar, the academic award King Mahendra established in 2016 B.S. and which, after the proclamation of the republic in 2062 B.S., passed to whichever head of state might hold the office. The answer came back on a slip no larger than a ration card:

Description of the Crown Seal & Registration of Merits Kathmandu, Nepal · Forms Branch, Wing 3 इ­प्र Application No. PRG/2016/0173 Priority date: 2016 B.S.

That is the whole of it. No ribbon colour, no medallion weight, no name of the sealed committee. The slip is honest about its limits, which is more than can be said for the register: the same file that records conferred no further; committee file sealed; ribbon store returned to the vault still lists the Puraskar as on its shelf, unrenamed and unexpired, while the President who would sign the citation lives at Shital Niwas and the medal, if it exists at all, keeps its deep blue in a vault behind the Durbar office. I asked the clerk whether President Paudel had ever worn the ribbon at an investiture. He smiled, stamped the margin, and said nothing.

There is, I should confess, a family angle. My uncle sat on the recommenders' committee for one of the last investitures at Narayanhiti — painters first, then musicians, then dancers, then men of letters, the last standing, as custom required, to receive the seal — and he used to say the committee file smelled of peppercorns. The register's own entry for that spring — pink tuff at dusk, taken from the south side, and the peppercorns — is preserved word for word, and no one in the forms branch will say why. Perhaps the President, when he has a free hour between electoral-college calculations and armed-forces parades, might open the vault and tell us.

Your own letters page has shown that honours survive best when they are kept on a shelf in the open, where the heartbreak and the medal can be seen at the same glance; I read that in the Kandy Courier's note on the Royals' season, where Solomon Beretta keeps his cricket notebook beside the Puraskar file. Let our shelf be open too.

The republic is fifteen years old, the President is in his second month, and the ribbon is still blue. I shall keep watching the vault.

Yours faithfully,
Dinesh Adhikari
Patan Durbar Square, Lalitpur
