The Mustard Walk

An itinerary in eight stops through the Brassicaceae — the mustards, the crucifers, the cabbage family (the older but equally valid name: Cruciferae)

  • Length 4.6 km
  • Stops 8
  • Grade flat, then the hill
  • Season March to June
  • Carry a linen cloth, a notebook, the jar
  • Flower code 4 / 4 / 6 / 1

The court that heard Case No. 41 of 1964 gave Felix Nori his lamp back, on the condition that it be kept at the rear porch in Mirandola and lit on summer evenings only, and closed the trial at ten minutes past eight. The family, which was never good at keeping to a single walk, drew up this one for the other half of the day. It is a walk in the cabbage family, the family that has fed this valley longer than the moths have lit our lamps, and it takes the jar labelled 12·10·62 with it, because the jar has asked to come and, being not appealable, is not a question the Court would reopen.

The family is a medium-sized and economically important one, most of it herbaceous and a little of it shrubby, and its leaves are simple — sometimes deeply incised — stipule-less, and set alternately up the stem or gathered in rosettes at the ground. Its inflorescences stand terminal on the branch and carry no bracts; its flowers have four free sepals, four free petals set in alternation, two shorter free stamens and four longer free stamens — the count the guides repeat at Stop 2, and the reason the old name is the cross, Cruciferae. Its fruit sets its seeds in rows, divided by a thin wall, the septum. As of 2023 the family was divided into two subfamilies, Brassicoideae and Crambaboideae, and the family has been split before that, twice, in living memory, so do not print your key until the autumn.

Leave the porch when the sun is out. Do not do this walk at night; at night the white wings have the bank, and the rule of the porch is that the night belongs to them. The White-Wing Cakes are served at Stop 7 only on cake nights, with black coffee, no plates, and one story that ends with a moth. The walk ends where it began, and the walk is not finished until the jar is back in the pantry, where it has been every year since 1964, between the flour and the silence.

  1. 1 0.0 km · 9:15 a.m.

    The Porch, Nori House, beside the Secchia

    Start here. The harbour lamp stands on the porch rail, dry-docked for winter, and the pantry is where you get the jar — pickles in a brine Felix dictated into the harbour canteen ledger at 8:10 p.m. on 12 October 1962: small round pickling onions, nasturtiums, capsicums, chilies, &c. The onions in that brine are the family's cousin's cousin; the mustard that follows in this walk is the family itself. Eat nothing until Stop 7. The linen cloth is for the cake; the notebook is for the flower counts. If a white wing is at the window when you start, you have chosen the wrong morning. Come back at night and do the sky walk instead.

  2. 2 0.8 km · 9:40 a.m.

    The Cress Beds, Secchia Bank

    Watercress, Nasturtium officinale, grows in the low bed where the Sunday matches used to end when the light failed — that is the bank the old sentence names, the one about the fog and the cabin keeper, which lives in the recipe box and is not explained on this walk, and is not explained on the night walk, and is not explained anywhere, which the family now considers a quality. It is a member of the family and it is the first living thing you will count: four sepals, four petals in alternation, the two short stamens standing inside the four long ones, the way two junior clerks stand inside four senior ones at the wharf. The leaves here are the exception to the simple-leaf rule — compound, and glossy — so note it in the notebook as the family's one flinch. Do not pick more than a mouthful. The cress is on the table at Stop 6 and it is not rushable.

  3. 3 1.4 km · 10:05 a.m.

    The Vineyard Lane — Wild Mustard

    The lane to the vines is a mustard line all the way: Brassica and Sinapis, yellow at the tips of the racemes and green going down, the older flowers already set into the thin-walled fruits with their seeds in rows. This is the stop for the flower code, and you will count it a hundred times before the guides believe you. Most of the family is herbaceous — this is all herbs, no shrubs here — but the family is not all herbs, and Stop 7 will show you what the shrubby branch is doing up on the hill. The wild mustard here is also, quietly, the oldest of the family on the human table: the seed between your teeth at lunch, the oil that once lit lamps in this valley before kerosene got to the wharf. That is the whole argument for the walk in one sentence: the lamp and the mustard are the same story, and one of them won the 1964 case.

  4. 4 1.9 km · 10:30 a.m.

    The Apothecary's Garden — Pharmacy Safe 3

    The old apothecary keeps horseradish and a bed of rocket in the walled garden, and the garden wall has a door that opens on nothing in particular, which is the door Nonna Ersilia means when she says she checks the pharmacy safe before she bakes. There is no pharmacy safe. The ritual is the point. In the garden shed, on a nail, hangs the small brown card the family cannot date: one side is an ingredient list, and the whole of it, read aloud to the guides, is — “INGREDIENTS.--1/2 pint of white sugar, the yolk and white pepper and salt.” No dish name. No hand to blame. It has been in the recipe box since 1963, and it is served here as the family's standing joke at this exact stop, the way the pie-stand chalkboard from Little Rock is the family's standing joke on the night walk, the way the sentence about the Secchia bank is the standing joke of the cake itself. The cress from Stop 2 takes it on faith: the sugar sweetens the water, the yolk and the pepper and the salt do the rest, and the mustard from the lane is what is missing from the card, which is why the card is right, and why everyone at this stop, every year, says so, very quietly, and moves on.

  5. 5 2.4 km · 11:00 a.m.

    The Market Row — Cabbage, Turnip, Radish, Broccoli, Kale

    The row is three stalls, and on a walk morning there is a crate of broccoli at the first, a pyramid of turnips at the second, and at the third a woman who sells radishes by the handful and will argue with you about kale if you are not careful. This is the economically important part of the family made visible: a medium-sized family of flowering plants, mostly herbs, that between them gave the table its greens, its roots, its flower-heads, and its two oldest condiments. Count what you can in the crates — radish and turnip will still flower if you forget to eat them, and both of them will show the code without being asked. The guides allow one purchase and one story. The story must be a true one and must not end with a moth; the moth is held in reserve for Stop 7, under court order, and the family has been fined for spending it early, twice.

  6. 6 3.1 km · 11:30 a.m.

    The Boatmen's Canteen — The Ledger Stop

    The Secchia boatmen keep their canteen at the ferry, and behind the counter is the ledger — not the original, the original is in Aden, in the record of the case — but the family copy, in which the entry for 12 October 1962 is transcribed in the clerk's hand: the cook asking the guest what to put into the pickle jars for the night, the guest dictating, the jars filled by 8:12. The cress from Stop 2 goes into the water at the window table, dressed with whatever the card from Stop 4 amounts to this year. Two minutes of standing at the ledger, no more; the ferry bell is unforgiving, and the walk has the hill to do and is not going to do it standing still. The guide who keeps the copy is the nephew of the cook, and he will tell you that the jars were 12 in number, which the Court never needed, which is why he tells you, and which is why you write it down even though you will not use it.

  7. 7 3.9 km · 12:00 p.m.

    The Hill — Charlock and the Cake

    The hill above the row, where the sky walk's ninth stop ends, is a stand of wild charlock in April, yellow for thirty metres in both directions, and the shrubby branch of the family holds the crest in a few grey mounds that will outlive the herbs, the houses, and probably the guides. On cake nights — the family counts them, there have been nineteen, and the count is kept in the same notebook as the flower counts — the cake comes up here in the tin, grey at the rim, the tin that came from Aden by way of Suez. The almonds were 140 grams. The bake was twenty-two minutes, one side of one record. It is eaten with black coffee, no plates, and the story is told — a true one, from the ledger or the market or the bank — and the story ends with a moth, at the lamp, at ten minutes past eight, and no one speaks for the length of one story, which is to say for twenty-two minutes, give or take the crackle of the surface. If no moth came to the lamp that year, the cake is wrapped in the linen cloth and left on the hill for the next night, which is the last instruction in the walk and the one the guides never have to repeat, because no one on the walk has ever once left it on the hill.

  8. 8 4.6 km · 12:45 p.m.

    The Porch, Again

    Back at the porch. The jar to the pantry, between the flour and the silence. The notebook to the recipe box, where it sits under the brown card and above the sentence that no one explains. The lamp is not lit — it is afternoon, and the court's condition was summer evenings only, and it is a condition the family would not breach for a crowd. The walk is over, and the walk will be done again, and the flower code will be counted a hundred more times, and the card will still say what it says, in the same hand no one has identified, 1/2 pint of white sugar, the yolk and white pepper and salt, which is either a dressing for cress or a cipher for the oven, and either of which is enough to carry a family up a hill. For the state of the valley the sentence names, and the trains and the tables: Italy — A Visitor's Brochure, with the Secchia valley around Mirandola.

Conduct of the Walk

  1. Nothing is picked except at Stop 2, and there only a mouthful.
  2. The flower code — four, four, six, one — is counted, not guessed.
  3. The moth story is spent once, at Stop 7, and not before.
  4. The jar is carried, not carried in, at no point.
  5. On a night the white wings are out, this walk does not happen; the transcript of the case that owns the lamp states the condition, and the condition is not appealable, the way the pickle is not appealable.
  6. For the night version of all this, from this same porch, beginning at ten minutes past eight: the family's nine-stop itinerary, The White-Wing Sky Walk, to which this walk is the daylight half.

Drafted at the porch, corrected at the hill. The family has split the Brassicaceae twice in living memory and will split them again; this itinerary is printed to last one season. Once, a naturalist on secondment left his own field notebook beside the pantry jars on a February morning and the guides, who are not unkind, read it and kept it, and he has counted his numbers in pencil since, on the Kantö plain, on an album called Ayu-ro Mix: Field Notes, Vol. XII. His rules are ours — the code is counted, not guessed — which is the whole of the correspondence. White-Wing Cakes, the recipe that feeds Stop 7, and Return to Webstuffs.
