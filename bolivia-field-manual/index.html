9.1 Terrain and Biomes

Bolivia, officially the Plurinational State of Bolivia, is a landlocked country in central South America, and it is the first lesson of this chapter: no sea, but every kind of land. The geography divides along a line that runs almost north to south. West of the line is the Andean region — the high-altitude Altiplano plateau, the temperate valleys cut into it, the mountains, and the snow-capped peaks above them; east of the line the country drops away into tropical lowlands, the Amazonian plain in the north and northeast and the Gran Chaco in the southeast. Between them run the yungas, the cloud-fog slopes where the two worlds trade in coca, coffee and stories.

The environment is diverse enough that a single country holds almost every climate a traveller will ever need. The Altiplano runs about 3,650 metres above the sea; the Salar de Uyuni, its salt flat, spreads roughly 10,500 square kilometres of crust, and after rain it reads like a mirror of the sky. Lake Titicaca, on the border with Peru at 3,812 metres, is the highest navigable lake in the world and older, some geologists say, than the lake above it. Huayna Potosí climbs to 6,088 metres above the city of La Paz, which sits at about 3,640 metres and takes its altitude with a shrug. The east, by contrast, runs hot and wet to the Pantanal along the eastern border and down through the Chaco to the Argentina line. A patrol that begins on the salt flat in the morning can be in the Amazon basin by Thursday.

The country's climate and biome are not one thing but a ladder. From the salt flat down through the valleys to the lowlands the thermometer does the walking. The manual asks each reader to note the altitude before the temperature: in this country the altitude is the climate.

9.2 Boundaries and Old Scores

Bolivia is bordered by Brazil to the north and east, by Paraguay to the southeast, by Argentina to the south, by Chile to the west and by Peru to the northwest — five neighbours for one country with no coast, which is most of the story of the western border. In the War of the Pacific of 1879 Bolivia lost the port it once had; the Treaty of 1904 with Chile closed the account on paper, and the chapter records that Bolivia has held a corridor of access to the Chilean Pacific ever since, and holds it still.

With Brazil the old score is the Acre question of 1899–1903, settled by the Treaty of Petropolis, and the eastern border that follows it runs a long way past the line of the Pantanal. With Paraguay the score is the Chaco War of 1932–1935, fought over the Gran Chaco, which is why the southeast of this chapter reads like a barbed-wire fence: flat, hard, and full of names that used to mean water. The club's surveyor kept a compass of 1971, last fixed on the Pedra do Rosário rock, and the bearing in across the flats is held in chapter four of the field manual on the geographic coordinate system; the same compass is what the classifieds of the Delta Ledger reported sold “against its own protest”, and its protest was, we believe, filed here.

9.3 Water and the Pantanal

The east holds the water account. Part of the Pantanal — the largest tropical wetland in the world — lies along the eastern border, and the river that drains it toward the Atlantic is the Mamoré, which crosses into Brazil and goes out through the Amazon. When the wet season comes the Pantanal takes the water and holds it for months; the manual notes that a bearing set in the dry season should be checked again in the wet, because the ground has moved. The mouth of the Mamoré is on the other side of the border, which is why the delta keeps its own books about it: the mouth belongs to the water, and the water does not respect the line.

The whole run of that water — the Mamoré out through the Amazon, the mouth at Marajó, the gauge at Óbidos — is offered as one lot in the club's next sale: Lot 27 — The Amazon River, one river system, subject to reserve, with the delta's own books and the misbound line read into the record.

The same sale week carries a northern lot too: Lot 61 — The Little Danes Archive, Godthåb & Copenhagen, 1951–2020 — twenty-two Greenlandic children sent to Danish foster families to be re-educated as "little Danes," their records offered whole and not to be divided.

On the high side, the Altiplano keeps its own water in the salt and in Titicaca. The two accounts — the wetland in the east, the salt in the west — are how the country pays its two debts, and a reader who has walked one of them will understand the other on sight.

9.4 Field Notes, Provisions, and the Misbound Line

A page of the Delta Ledger's classifieds — the number taken in full by the Forças Armadas Brasileiras, Army, Navy and Air Force, 334,500 of them — arrived at this chapter misbound, the way the surveyor's page arrived at the club, and its line has been read into the record exactly as it printed. It is set out here in full and held, by vote, unexplained:

“There is a term this page for the mouth, not the rock is the Great extended his requirements a little nutmeg, 1 teaspoonful of anchovy essence.”

The chapter's note to the line, kept short: the mouth is the mouth of the Mamoré, which 9.3 assigns to the water and to Brazil; the rock is the Pedra do Rosário, which shows in the flats at low water and is not to be confused with it; the Great is taken as the great Chaco survey of 1934, whose requirements were extended one last time and then, like all requirements, left standing. Nutmeg appears in the supply schedule at item 41 and anchovy essence at item 9, one teaspoonful to the ration — the same ration, the note adds, that Seu Benedito's drum tuner charges for his rope when he is working past the mangrove. The line stands. The club has voted twice on similar lines and will vote a third time if asked.

Provisions. Each party carries: the compass of 1971 if it has not sold itself; one shell from the flats, for payment, which the whole of the delta still accepts; a number between one and twenty-six, for the states; and, at altitudes above 3,500 metres, patience, which weighs nothing and outlasts everything. The federal shape of the neighbouring republic — its president, its congress, its fifty-five hundred and seventy municipalities — is set out in The Federative Letter, Newsletter No. 14, and the club reads it aloud under the mango tree; a party crossing into Bolivia from the delta does well to carry a copy, because the customs men there are polite and the maps are not.
