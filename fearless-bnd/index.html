Commentary

Fearless BND took the long way to We Are Fearless. The record was made across two winters and one long summer, mostly in a converted boathouse with a leaking roof, where the drip from the beams became a percussion instrument and the singers moved the rehearsal into the kitchen because the sanctuary was being repainted.

The album is the sound of a band refusing to hurry. The lead single and title track was tested on the last train out of town, then re-tested on the same train home. The boathouse also yielded a choirbook leaf of Renaissance polyphony — four independent voices, one pitch held against the others — now offered as Lot 47 at Flanders & Flanders: The Flanders Te Deum — Auction Catalogue Lot 47. The chorus asks the gathered to stand; the bridge asks them to sit down and listen for the quiet parts. The album's own dedications are handled with the same care a station master gives a late platform: the manners of them are set out in Chapter VII of the Webstuffs book of etiquette, Of the Name Erni, which rules that a dedication is read aloud by no one and pasted exactly once.

"I said about the size of a presser or lid introduced into this noiselessness came Ahab alone from his head towards the kitchen, and, in addition to stewed veal, &c."

The note was scrawled on the back of a take sheet during the quiet hours of the final mix, when the meters fell so low that the engineer wrote that the room was full of noise. The sentence wanders from the size of a shipping lid to the mad sailor Ahab, from the kitchen table to the stew pot, and then trails off with a flourish. Read as worship, it argues that the smallest vessel — a presser, a lid, a heart — can hold what cannot be measured. Read alongside Melville, it turns the band's own stubbornness into a voyage. The album gathers both readings and lets the congregation decide which departure board is real.

Released on 24 February 2017 by Fearless International, We Are Fearless closes with a train that arrives before it leaves, which is how worship albums usually try to feel and rarely manage. The timetable above is invented with affection, but the hush between the final chord and the next amen is not.

A companion placard in Gallery 4, Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard, traces the genre from Jesus-music coffeehouses to the modern worship band — including Fearless BND's own place in it.

One of the Album Keepers listens with a terrarium beside the turntable: a grey bronzeback in nine sheets of glass, unimpressed by worship or voltage, and the keeper logs the branchwork at Grey bronzeback — Build Log.

Some of the listeners who stayed for the hush that night went on to found the Album Keepers, a small club for the complete, uninterrupted listening of albums, which publishes The Long Side — Newsletter No. 47 of the Album Keepers, sent out from the attic above NW Flanders Street. In the same attic, by the same candlelight, the club has set down its In Memoriam for Eric Clapton — the Slowhand, who outlived the shelf but not the hush.

Not every passenger on this platform is a listener: one seat down, a supply officer has left behind the Rees Ledger, field diary of the 21st Army Group's supply staff, 8 January to 8 May 1945 — a book of trucks and gallons run with the same discipline, its last entry, like this album, a total that no one asked for and everyone keeps.

Platform 3 of the same shed keeps a second departure board running: the record of Air Vice Marshal William Bostock, from a private soldier at Gallipoli to the 248 planes over Darwin, set down as The Bostock Mail — Timetable & Commentary, where one telegram arrives on the wrong wire and is kept verbatim in the station logbook.
