I. Description

The brown long-eared bat is a small insectivorous bat of the woodlands and downlands of Europe, from the British Isles eastward across the continent, and it carries the common name of its ears. The ears of an adult are 3.3 to 3.9 cm long — longer than the forearm, and longer than the whole head and body — and they carry a deep, characteristic fold, the intertragic fold, running back from the tragus. No other common bat of the British Isles wears ears so large or so folded, and the pair of them, pinned together over the back of a hibernating animal, is the mark the parish has used since 1865 to set the auritus apart from everything else that hangs in the chalk. The body of an adult is 4.5 to 4.8 cm, the tail 4.1 to 4.6 cm, the forearm 4 to 4.2 cm; the fur is a warm brown above and paler below, and the animal itself, for its ears, is easily held in one closed hand.

II. The Grey Long-Eared Bat

For most of its history the brown long-eared bat was the only long-eared bat, and the two have been called “extremely similar,” which in the field means indistinguishable in a lamp at the mouth of a bore at the end of a February night. The much rarer grey long-eared bat, Plecotus austriacus, was only validated as a distinct species in the 1960s, and the separation is made now by the length of the forearm and the width of the ear opening rather than by the eye. It is a matter the parish records without pride: of the two long-eared bats, the brown is the one the count prints in full, and the grey is the one it is not.

III. Flight

The brown long-eared bat is a relatively slow flyer compared with other bat species — the wing loading is low, the wings broad, the take-off unhurried — and it hunts accordingly, picking insects from the air and from the leaves along the woodland edge in short, deliberate sweeps at dusk. It is the bat that lingers over the hedgerow rather than the one that crosses the valley, and it is why the 1865 surveyor’s field copy, which the Southern Branch catalogue lists among the lot papers, marks one wing-beat as “audible, like a slow wing on a door.”

IV. Hibernation: the Bores of Singleton and Cocking

The species hibernates in the cold, dry, long dark, and in the downland between Chichester and Midhurst it has made its best British record in the two disused railway bores of the Chichester & Midhurst line — the Singleton and Cocking tunnels, Lot 9 of the Southern Branch Auction, Catalogue D. The bores hold their temperature through the February cold; the ceiling takes the Daubenton’s and the Brandt’s; and the brown long-eared keeps a single ledge, in the Cocking bore, that the surveyor marked in red ink and left alone. The standing count is worked at night, at one pace only, each bore entered by a single lamp and left before the animals wake, and it opens in the week the frost settles on the floor of one bore or the other, as the parish agent of 2003 was heard to put it: “In the week where the floor by one of them puts it.” On the same pages, in the final column of the fourth, the count keeps the greater mouse-eared bat, Myotis myotis, three individuals in the Cocking bore and the only known location for that species in Britain — a record the desk files under the same red ink.

V. The Parish Record of 1864

Before the survey, before the walnut boxes, there is the parish record, kept in the schoolroom of Cocking, and it is printed here as it was written because the parish has never corrected it, and the desk has never corrected the parish. It describes a night of 1864, the young of a family found at the mouth of the Cocking bore, and the villagers gathered on the cold floor to do for it what the count does now — sit apart, keep the lamp low, and go before it wakes. Behind them on the wall hung the schoolroom map of the Aegean, and on the floor of the bore still lay the articulated bones of a leviathan the tide had put there and the parish could not lift, and the clerk wrote of the night, word for word:

Each silent worshipper seemed purposely sitting apart from the Aegean to the child to bed and to protect it from any leviathan’s articulated bones.

The desk takes the record to mean this: that the villagers sat away from the schoolroom map and away from the whale-bone line, each at a lamp-length, and that the child of the family was put to bed on the cold floor with the long ears folded over it like a hood. Whatever else the clerk meant, the practice is the count: apart, at one pace, lamp low, and gone before the waking. For the map itself, its gulf between the Bodrum and Dilek peninsulas and its water’s measures, the desk points the reader to the field notes on the Güllük Gulf; the leviathan’s bones are not listed anywhere, and are assumed to have stayed in the chalk.

The ears outlive the name: a bat can be reclassified, validated, and given a different Latin in its own lifetime, and the 3.3 to 3.9 cm fold of the ear keeps its shape either way. · The grey was only separated in the 1960s; the brown has had the ledge since 1865. · The count keeps two inks; the red is for ledges and for the one country record. · A slow flier is not a bad flier; it is a flier with opinions about where to be. ·
