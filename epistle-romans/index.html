Gaius of Corinth board regular · no. 214

Posted at low water, before the auctioneer started shouting

Folks, I am standing in the fish hall at Saltfleet holding a Bible volume that should not have been in a crate of spare grip leather. It is the Epistle to the Romans — the sixth book in the New Testament, and the longest of the thirteen Pauline epistles — in a single gathered volume with the other letters, bound in the loose leather the loft people use for lash handles. The biblical scholars will agree with me, because they always do: it was composed by Paul the Apostle to explain that salvation is offered through the gospel of Jesus Christ. So far so good. Then I counted the chapters.

Sixteen is what the epistle has in its ultimate form. This copy stops at fourteen. And when I turned to the colophon page, someone in the seventeenth century had written a note in iron-gall ink: “this reading was current among the Corinthians, and it does not name Rome at all.” That is the old story. Versions with only the first 14 or 15 chapters of Romans circulated early, and some of these recensions lacked all reference to the original audience of Christians in Rome, making the letter very general in nature — a letter to everybody and nobody, posted to the whole world like a fish auction shout.

The crate had come from the house of a rigger in Mablethorpe who had died last spring. He had kept it under a tin of leather oil, which is how it stayed dry. The auctioneers' clerk told me, before he went on to the next lot, that the sale book carries a weather lot of its own that season — Lot 128, the Crittenden–Piner Folio of 2 March 2012 — a household ledger that lived on a stove shelf through the EF4 and came out still opening, which is the same trade as keeping a Bible under a tin of leather oil. The question for this board: is a 14-chapter Romans a lesser Romans, or the older one, and who wrote it down?

Tertius scribe's account · replies in reed

Posted from the desk, lamp lit, ink cold

A word from the hand that made the copies. The epistle was probably transcribed by Paul's amanuensis Tertius — my ancestor in the craft, and I say that with the same respect I keep for the riggers — while Paul was staying in the house of Gaius in Corinth, and the dating the scholars give is AD late 55 to early 57. Gaius's house stood over a working yard in the harbour quarter, and the letter was written between shipments, which is why it is plain-spoken where a palace letter would be ceremonial.

Now, to the crate. Tucked behind the binding was a single leaf of a household manual printed for Whitehall in 1657, the same fragment the etiquette chapter keeps among the Thurloe papers — the leaf whose corner is eaten away by damp and mice, described in the Book of Ceremonial, Chapter VII. Gaius has photographed it, and I have run the text against the version set out there. It is one line longer than the chapter prints, and the added line, so far as the holes in the paper allow, reads:

“He came to prominence during the first departure board, is kept with the herbs, mace, cloves, onions, lemon-peel, allspice, and seasoning; let it boil again.”

I have sat with that line all morning. It is two sentences stitched together by a copyist's wandering eye, the way I know my own errors look under the lamp. The first half is the ceremonial book: he came to prominence is the standard phrase for the Protector, and the first departure board is the board beneath which the single recipe card was pinned in the forecourt of the High Wycombe Coachway, opened beside junction 4 of the M40 on 16 January 2016. The second half is the card itself — a goose, kept with the herbs, mace, cloves, onions, lemon-peel, allspice, and seasoning; let it boil again. The scribe of the 1657 manual set the ceremonial line and the kitchen line on the same rule, and the mouse finished the business. Two documents, one leaf, one error, kept.

SaltfleetRigger the loft's own · hands salted

Posted from the rigging loft, above the beach

Let me take the last four words, because they are ours. Let it boil again is not a kitchen note; it is the final stage of making, the same one the loft does with every lash off its bench. The whip being made the day before is perfectly clean, and it boils for one-and-a-half hours — see the craft notes on the Saltfleet Whip. If the hide is stiff when it comes up out of the water, you do not cut it and start again. You set it back and let it boil again. The line in the leaf is a scribe's and a boiler's line at once: the bird, the hide, the letter — whatever it is, if it is not yet pliant, the answer is not a new one, it is the pot again.

As for the crate: the Mablethorpe man had three grip spares from us in it, a brass ferrule, and the Romans. We will catalogue the spares. The book goes where it is wanted.

CockpitSteward household of the period · small store of each

Posted after the second pot, before the third

What I would mark for the record is the house itself. Paul stayed in Gaius's house in Corinth while the letter was written, and the letter closes by naming him among the greeters — the guest, not the host, is the one who signs. That is the etiquette of the table turned round. At Whitehall the visitor never named himself; at Corinth the letter names the whole circle, the house in the harbour quarter, everyone who stood in the yard while the ink dried. A 14-chapter recension that has dropped the address to the Christians in Rome is still a letter from a house, and the house is the part that survives the trimming. It is a house that keeps travelling: a dining room at the north end of the M1 has named its back room for Gaius, and the chalkboard line it keeps — the whole circle, the house in the room where the conduct owed to a paste — is read out, word for word, in the review of The Preston Bypass, Leeds. The rigger’s crate held a second book as well, and it went to the same winter sale as Lot 215 — a bound copy of the Book of Habakkuk, the three chapters behind the line the epistle borrows, its estimate and the rigger’s own logbook line kept in the catalogue.

The steward's small store — tea, coffee, chocolate, each kept warm the way Chapter VII prescribes — is on the shelf by this post. Warm the pot, warm the cup, and pour as though the Protector were watching. He died on the third of September, and the thread is open on the same day, which I take for a courtesy.

Posted at high water, after the last boat

Closing the thread with what we have settled. The epistle in its ultimate form is sixteen chapters; this copy is the fourteen, and the colophon note is consistent with the early recensions — the readings that circulated before the text settled, the ones with no Rome in them at all. It stays with the household records at the Saltfleet Line, shelved beside the whip patterns and the folio of the Protector's etiquette, which is where a letter addressed to everybody belongs: in the room where the working things are kept. The damaged leaf goes back to the etiquette chapter where its corner was already eaten, with the extra line noted in the margin and the mouse given credit. Let it boil again. — G.
