Sleeve notes

This record was not cut in a studio. The ensemble — the Stadio Olimpico Field Ensemble, one hundred and eighty microphones — set up between 25 August and 11 September 1960, when the Games of the XVII Olympiad (Italian: Giochi della XVII Olimpiade) filled Rome, and everything on these two sides is what the building let us take. The stadium itself had been raised in 1953 for exactly this occasion, and had carried the Olympic programme on the same two tiers of concrete that, forty-nine years later, would hold the Champions League final you can read about in our guide to Rome, 27 May 2009, where the tunnel under Piazzale dello Stadio still smells of turnstiles and hot tarmac and the fishmongers’ shutters are still down along the Via Tiburtina.

The story the record tells begins before the record: Rome had once been awarded the 1908 Games, and then the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 1906 put an end to that, and the city passed the honour to London and spent fifty-two years rehearsing. Side A opens on that eruption and closes on the count, and the count belongs to the Soviet Union, who took both the most gold and the most overall medals at the 1960 Games, a fact the red end of the stadium sang about until the turntables wore. Between those two ends: Abebe Bikila, who ran the marathon barefoot in 2 hours 15 minutes 16.2 seconds; Wilma Rudolph, gold in the 100 metres, the 200 metres and the 4×100 metres relay, eleven seconds flat in the sprint; the swimming hall on the Tiber; the boxing nights at the Palazzetto. We kept the numbers on the box because a field recording without its numbers is only a weather.

Side B is quieter and stranger. In conjunction with the Olympics, Rome also held the first Paralympic Games, in the same stadium, with the same turnstiles and the same bar of the hotel on the corner of Via Tiburtina and Viale Europa, where the keeper keeps the small shelf of football almanacs and the thick volume of dream interpretations the regulars have worn soft at the corners. The last track is the egress itself, and the sleeve warning printed beneath it, which we keep word for word and cannot explain, though we have never once regretted following it: “After full time Do not go to the side of the Via Tiburtina; by car, do not.” Line B will do it for you; the metro is six stops from Termini; the city has decided what it is, as always, for the evening.
