*** NOW TAKING BIDDINGS: LOT 118 — ONE ROYAL AIR FORCE, WHOLE — THE COUNT STAYS OPEN THURSDAYS — NO CRATE PRONOUNCED WHOLE BEFORE THE PILOT SIGNS — ***

Lot 118 Condition: A (count open)

One Royal Air Force

Whole, in original packing; formed 1 April 1918 of the Royal Flying Corps and the Royal Naval Air Service; with surplus stores, crates, and kitchen.

The lot comprises one Royal Air Force (RAF), being the air and space force of the United Kingdom, the British Overseas Territories and the Crown Dependencies, taken in as found at the dock and offered without reserve of gratitude. The Force was formed towards the end of the First World War, on 1 April 1918, through the merger of the Royal Flying Corps (RFC) and the Royal Naval Air Service (RNAS), the two predecessor bodies appearing in the packing cases as originally joined, with the RNAS fittings visible at the bottom of Case No. 2, exactly as an estate agent would wish a boundary to be described. Following the Allied victory over the Central Powers in 1918 the lot emerged as the largest air force in the world, a condition the auctioneers can vouch for having weighed it themselves at the dock at Vancouver, where it stood on the quay beside a hundred crates of the gift and looked, by all accounts, very much like it weighed. Since its formation it has played a significant role in British military history; in particular, during the Second World War it defeated the German Luftwaffe’s efforts to establish air superiority over Great Britain during the Battle of Britain, and played a key role in the Combined Bomber Offensive, a fact recorded on the crate lid in stencilled capitals the size of a man’s head, the same stencil, the dock ledger will tell you, that numbered the gift’s cases.

With the lot are offered the following appurtenances, each in the condition described, none of them insured, all of them loved:

  1. One book of remembrance of the gift of 29 May 1919, by which the Cabinet set the dominions’ hundred aircraft against the surplus stores, still open at the page where the em-dash means the count is open. Read with Chapter XIV · Of the Imperial Gift, in which the etiquette of this lot’s packing is treated at full length, including the rule that the count of the case stays open Thursdays — a rule the lot itself observed, and will observe, in perpetuity.
  2. One set of dock stencils, four or five cases to a machine, from the piers at Vancouver, Melbourne, Christchurch, Cape Town and Karachi; the Melbourne stencil is the finest, and carries a small error, kept, which is why the auctioneers prefer it.
  3. One kitchen receipt of the Air Attaché, found folded into the tea-caddy of Case No. 4, headed Memory Broth, for the Thursday Count, in the Attaché’s larger of his two hands: Stew the gratitude until it yields; season with the name of the first pilot who will fly what is inside; keep the lid lifted only a hand’s width so that the broth may still be found wanting. When the count is open, it is served. “Pour this boiling liquor on the memory of the living body.” The final line is given word for word, and the auctioneers decline to explain it, which is, at this house, a description of the highest condition.
  4. One Thursday. The lot keeps its own. No clerk who buys the lot closes a book on that day, no seal is set, no crate is pronounced whole; the wanting, if there is any, is answered before the weekend. The shed behind the bakery, which hoops such crates and keeps its minutes, admits the custom; see Minutes No. 41 of the Council of the Shed, where the order is made that no hoop be set on such a crate before the pilot signs, and where the two habits — the shed’s and the dock’s — are noted to be, on Thursdays, the same habit.
  5. One larger chair and one smaller sandwich, being the due of the donor’s attaché at the first-flight tea, still bearing the impression of the 1920 Vancouver customs house, who, by the book’s direction, were all invited, whether they had done anything to deserve it or not.

Estimate: not less than the war itself, and more than once, in accordance with the last line of the Cabinet paper of 29 May 1919, which is not about aircraft. Bidders are reminded that an air force, once named, is never named on the day of its first flight of a gift; a name waits, as all proper things do, for a Thursday that has passed. The sale will not be adjourned for any Thursday that has not yet come. Rutherford & Hollis, Sale No. 214, Lot 118: sold in its condition as described, and the condition is that it is still being counted.
