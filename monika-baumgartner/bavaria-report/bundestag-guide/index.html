Parliaments You Can Actually Walk Into — No. 3

The Bundestag at an Hour When It Is Empty

A travel guide entry — Berlin, Parliaments Road (formerly Paul-Löbe-Haus side, north wing), 10117. Compiled by Maximilian Baumgartner, Class 9B, from the school’s February excursion and one borrowed grandmother’s leaf.

1What you are going to

The Bundestag, pronounced the way the atlas has taught me to spell it, [ːʺndəsta8], means the Federal Diet, and it is the federal parliament of Germany. It is the only constitutional body in the country directly elected by the German people, and since the current 21st legislative period it has a fixed number of 630 members. It was established by Title III of the Basic Law for the Federal Republic of Germany — the Grundgesetz — in 1949, as one of the two legislative bodies of the country, the other being the Bundesrat, which sits in a quieter building and has, in my experience, never once invited a class of nine-year-olds to a tour. The members of the Bundestag are representatives of the German people as a whole, are not bound by any orders or instructions, and are only accountable to their conscience. Our group of twenty-two was accountable to Fräulein Höchsteiner, who stood at the door of the north wing and did not move until the last of us had put away his chewing gum.

2The building and the dome

The building was built in 1884 as a house for emperors and kings and was finished in 1894, and after 1945 it did not do much of anything for forty-four years, which is a long time for a building this size. Now the plenary hall sits beneath a glass dome, a transparent cylinder with a slanting silver mirror funnelled up through it, and on a February morning the dome does exactly what a dome should: it takes the grey of the city, funnels it up, and gives back a ceiling that is somehow a shade more patient than the weather. The plenary hall itself is a semicircle of dark wood with benches in green, and green, I have since learned, is not a decoration in this building but a tradition: the colour of the original 1949 hall. Six hundred and thirty seats. I counted a row, seven of us, and let the maths go, because Fräulein Höchsteiner would not let us do arithmetic in a dome, and I have decided to side with her.

3The schedule, and the hour when it is empty

13:05 — the hour when it is empty · click to seat the house

The Bundestag is elected every four years, and in an ordinary week the plenary meets on Tuesday and Wednesday, sometimes Monday and Thursday, in sittings that start in the morning and can run, on a budget night, until the small hours. The best hour to come, for a visitor who wants to see the room as a room, is therefore the gap — the hour between sittings, when the benches are stacked with briefcases and papers, the microphones are off, and the green seats hold only their shadows. We came at 13:05 on a Wednesday in February and had the whole semicircle to ourselves for eleven minutes. It looked the way an instrument looks between pieces. In the press gallery, the stenographers’ desks sat in their curved rows; the guide told us that every word said in the plenum is typed up, printed, and is the only version of it that officially exists, and that the machine has a small bell for corrections. Someone in our group asked whether the bell rings for laughter too, and the guide said it does, and said nothing more, and I have never heard the answer feel so final.

4The leaf in the script drawer

What I came to tell you about, if I am allowed, is the leaf. My grandmother kept, in the same script drawer that carried the black apron and the silver brooch of the Gasthof zum Bären and that went out to the auction as part of her archive, a printed visitors’ leaf of the Bundestag, the first one, from 1949, with a ribbon of green cloth the colour of the plenary benches. In her hand, on the back, in the three alphabets of her address book, she had written a note about the room — what it was like, she said, at an hour when it was empty, the morning of the first sitting, the microphones cold, the glass dome not yet built, the sun coming through the old skylight instead. The note has worn thin in one place, and the line we can read, the one the class read in radio hour instead of the tape, reads exactly like this, and we keep it exactly because we were not allowed to correct it:

“We may add, that to this desperate mode of living, to perform.”

— Visitors’ leaf of the Bundestag, 1949 printing, reverse side, in the hand of the landlady of the Gasthof zum Bären. The class note, transcribed by Fräulein Höchsteiner: “Desperate mode of living: the room. Perform: the sitting. It works. Leave it.”

That is the whole of our theory, and it is the same theory we used in the Bavaria report for the hens exactly at one, which is to say: two things at the same moment, and the sentence does not need a third one. A parliament is a desperate mode of living if you are the one who has to perform in it every four years on a schedule the country can read in the almanac, and the room is empty only so that the sitting can happen again. I recommend the leaf to anyone who wants to hold a parliament in one fold of a drawer, and the same household keeps, beside it, a table manual for a game of the slow release, whose board also looks, between movements, like an instrument between pieces — and whose winner hangs a pewter jug on a small throne instead of keeping anything, which is a rule I intend to propose for the drawer itself.

5Practicalities

Tours are free and run from the visitor centre on Parliaments Road; groups of more than ten should write a letter, which is where our excursion letter is, filed in the drawer with the address book entries, the Munich stage-door keeper, the Rosenheim postmistress, and the Manila printer whose number no longer connects. The dome itself can be climbed on clear days — 232 steps, I was told, and I was not told to count them, and I did not. The plenum’s own programme of sittings is posted outside and is a small blue list, the size of a card from the Bärenkopf set, and I have one of the February lists now, in the same drawer, beneath the leaf, because a guide that has not been folded along one crease is not a guide yet. For the rest of the archive that surrounds this page — the scripts, the swatches, the album of press cuttings, the estimate, the condition note — see Lot 314 — Monika Baumgartner — Auction Catalogue, in which this leaf is described, very fairly, as generally good.
