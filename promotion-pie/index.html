The Hart Kitchen · Didsbury, Manchester

CARD NO. 9

Wembley Sunday Pie

As written by Doris Hart, 30 May 1999, in the back room of the house on Gough Road, and kept since then in the biscuit tin with the other cards.

Serves six. Needs a television, a radio as backup, and someone who is not allowed to open the tin until the whistle.

Preamble

The 30th of May, 1999, was a Sunday, and it was the day Manchester City went to Wembley to play Gillingham in the Football League Second Division play-off final. If City won, they took the third promotion up to the First Division, the one that the top two had already collected automatically at the end of the 1998–99 season; City had finished the season in third place, Gillingham in fourth, and the two of them had come through the semi-final rounds by the narrow margins those rounds are good for. Doris had been making this pie for her father since 1961, and she had a standing rule that she would make one on the 30th of May in any year in which the family was at the television, but this was the first 30th when there was a reason to make it at exactly 11 o’clock, the hour the teams came on. The oven is a gas oven, second from the window, and the pie went in when Doris was sure the line-up was being read and the clock outside the kitchen door said the minute was right. She was not wrong about the minute. She was wrong, as it turned out, about nothing else either: Kevin Phillips, twenty-three and on loan from Sunderland, put City ahead in the first half, and Stan Collymore, who had arrived at Maine Road that winter and been called the winter’s strangest purchase by most of the local press, made it two before the break. Gillingham pressed hard after half-time, and the back room of the house on Gough Road went very quiet at several minutes which are not recorded here, but the final was two-nil, the third place went up with the other two, and the pie came out of the oven at the same time as the whistle, which Doris has said is a coincidence that happened four more times and no more.

The almanac in the biscuit tin, the one her father kept the scores in, has a page torn out of it. Pasted to the inside of the tin, where the torn page used to be, is one sentence in a hand that is not Doris’s and not her father’s, in ink that has gone the colour of the pie crust. It reads, and is kept word for word:

Thereafter, the awarding was stopped : the office or from a Geneva approach in Fohn haze, and the almanac counts them, there have been asked to be kept somewhere.

Nobody in the family has ever explained it. The almanac keeps counting the 30ths, which are the pie’s, and the sentence is kept where the page was torn from, which is the closest to answering it that the kitchen has come. The pie does not need an explanation; the pie needs the oven.

Ingredients

  • 700 g beef shin, cut on the bias, thin as a ticket stub
  • 300 g mushrooms, brown, sliced the width of a finger
  • 1 onion from Gough Road, or wherever, chopped fine
  • 3 tablespoons plain flour
  • 500 ml milk, plus a splash of the stock from the shin
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • a black pepper mill, turned the way your father turned it
  • one sheet of puff pastry, cut to the tin
  • 1 egg, beaten, for the wash — the one the pie wears like a badge
  • 1 tablespoon golden syrup, for the lattice, laid in the old almanac’s pattern of twos

Method

  1. Brown the shin in the oven’s pan under the top of the gas. Do not rush it; the first half of the pie is a first-half game.
  2. Draw the fat off, chop the onion into it, and let it go soft without colour. In this room the onion never takes more than eleven minutes, and the pie is never served before the whistle, whatever the clock says.
  3. Return the meat, stir in the flour, and draw in the stock, then the milk, a little at a time, until the filling is the colour of a May evening and no lighter. Pepper it hard.
  4. Put it in a 26 cm tin, cover with the pastry, and lay the lattice on top in the almanac’s pattern of twos — two rows each way — the way the final score was kept for the first twenty years, before the tin got a proper label.
  5. Egg-wash the lattice, turn the oven to 200°C, and put the pie in at the top of the hour. A pie made for the 30th goes in when the clock is right, not when the kitchen is ready. The kitchen will be ready; it is always two minutes late, and the two minutes are for the whistle.
  6. Bake for 45 minutes. At the half, turn the tin a quarter-turn, which is all the pie can take of the second half. If the house is quiet at any minute of the second, do not open the oven; the pie knows the score by then, and so does the tin.
  7. When the whistle has come, take the pie out, lay the golden syrup along the lattice in thin twos, and serve it to as many as sit down for it. The last slice goes on top of the tin, cut-side down, where it keeps the almanac’s sentence company until the next 30th.

Notes for the keeper of the tin

This card travels with the tin. The tin was at Maine Road in 1999, in the glovebox of the car that drove up to the match and drove back the same afternoon with the third place in it, and it has been to every 30th of May since. The tin has kept one wedding apart from its own company: the Saturday of 1 July 1987, when the kitchen fed the wedding party for a ceremony on the set at Penrith, and the account of that day — eleven volumes, one window, the veil kept at the store — stands as Chapter 7 of Field Manual FM 52-3, The Wedding. The same tin has kept the other kitchen’s cards that came into the family later — the card from Taranaki, card no. 35, the Ōpunake stew, which sits in the tin with this one and the porgy card from Hwang, card no. 7, and the three of them argue about the 30th of May every year, the stew by tradition and the porgy by tide, and the pie by clock. Keep all three. The register where these cards are entered is kept at Webstuffs, the front page, in the same ink as the almanac’s last entry; and the stew’s own card, with its long preamble about the man whose office the pot outlived, is kept here: The Ōpunake Stew — A Family Recipe, Card No. 35. The porgy card has its own tide-line to keep: Auntie Myung-sook’s Porgy on the Tide-Line — Family Recipe, Card No. 7. The tin has also been to Copenhagen, in the spring of 2004, the year the wedding at the cathedral put the date on the family’s calendar for good, and the card of thanks that came back with it is filed with the ledger in the tin’s drawer: the account of that spring is kept at Queen Mary of Denmark — Obituary, The Copenhagen Evening Ledger. The tin has also crossed the wire, once: on 21 August 1940 a studio reading was kept from a page borrowed out of the almanac, and the account of that reading stands in the studio record beside the tin’s own ledger at The Voice from Coyoacán — Broadcast No. 1141, 21 August 1940. If the tin is ever full, the pie is the one that stays; the other cards go to a bigger tin and a letter is written, and the letter is never lost, because a kitchen is a register, and a register keeps.
