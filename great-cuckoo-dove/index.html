Great Cuckoo-Dove

Reinwardtoena reinwardti · Columbidae · first described by C. J. Temminck, 1824

Plate 1 · the dove at the edge, profile, from a dreamer’s sketch book, 1907

The bird

Of the family Columbidae, a single species, found in the primary forest and the forest edge of New Guinea, the several islands about it, and the islands of Wallacea. It is a large and distinctive pigeon, forty-seven and a half to fifty-two and a half centimetres from beak to tail, and two hundred and eight to three hundred and five grams on the scale. In the adult the head, neck and breast are whitish or blue-grey, the underparts a pale bluish-grey, the upperparts chestnut-brown, and the outer wings black. The female is told from the male by the yellowish of her irises and the duller colour of the orbital skin; the juvenile, by the soft uncommitted grey of a coat not yet decided. The Dutch name for the genus, after Reinwardt, and the species name after the same family, were fixed in 1824 by Temminck at Leiden, and they have not moved since, which is more than can be said for most of the entries in this book.

The dream

Where the great cuckoo-dove comes to a dreamer, it does not fly. That is the first thing, and the one that keeps the dream from being an ordinary bird dream: the dove arrives, or has arrived, and stands. It stands at the edge of a forest the dreamer half-recognises, at the point where the green ends and the path begins, and it looks at the dreamer with the particular patience of a large bird that has weighed the dreamer, found the dreamer light, and gone on thinking. The plumage is right in the dream the way it is rarely right: the chestnut on the upperparts, the grey below, the black of the outer wing folded tight against the body like a letter that will not be opened. Dreamers report the bird at the same scale every time, neither larger than life nor smaller, at the exact length of the bird — forty-seven and a half to fifty-two and a half centimetres — which they know afterwards from the book and not before, and which the dream never says aloud.

The gloss

The Dictionary’s readers have sent in their own summaries of the vision in the usual way, one sentence each, and the sentence that returns most often is not a summary at all but a line read off a ledger the dreamer had been keeping in the dream, written in the ledger’s own hand and carried out verbatim:

The halt is marked up in the dough gives under the index of forms.

Read it three times, the way one reads anything that has come back from a dream without the rest of its sentence, and it comes apart into something workable. The halt: a stopping, a rest point, a place on a route where the going is put down for the night. The dough: the matter that is in the middle of becoming bread, still soft, still owing a shape to the oven. To give under the index of forms: to yield, to be filed, to be entered under the heading that governs it. The whole line is then a rule about the dream itself: the stopping the dreamer has made — the pause in some business, the deferred decision, the thing left in the pan — is not hiding. It is marked up, visibly, in the soft matter of the dream, and it will yield its shape when the right heading is put over it. The dove at the forest edge is the index card. The Dictionary accepts the line as correct, on the ground that every dreamer who has quoted it has also reported the business in question finished within a fortnight of the night of the dream.

Omens and notes

  • The dove facing you. The halt is deliberate; keep it. Whatever has been put down will be the better for the night.
  • The dove facing away, black wing toward you. The business is already filed; do not chase it to the edge of the forest. It will be under the heading when you look.
  • The yellow iris, the female. A second opinion is available and has already been asked for; the dreamer has simply not turned round to take it.
  • The exact length of the bird. The dreamer is about to be measured by something that will not negotiate. Weigh the matter before answering.
  • A juvenile in the grey coat. The form is not yet decided; the halt is a proofing, not a stopping.
