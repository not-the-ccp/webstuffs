The man of the bench

William of Wrotham — the rolls of the later thirteenth century spell him William de Wrotham, and so do we, on the brass — died about the year 1217, a royal administrator and clergyman of the English crown. The late thirteenth-century source that first named him to the office of the house says William already held a royal office under King Henry II, who reigned from 1154 to 1189; the yard regards that claim the way it regards most heirloom measurements — as a figure for hanging things up by, not a figure one swears on. The first contemporary reference to William is of 1197, when he became responsible, among other things, for the royal tin mines in Cornwall, and the yard keeps a small tin ingot, stamped and weighed at Helston, behind every board it lets go.

He held ecclesiastical office at the same time, ending his career Archdeacon of Taunton, and served King John as an administrator of ecclesiastical lands and a collector of taxes; the tenth he raised of the clergy in the winter of 1213 is the sum the yard still quotes when a customer asks, reasonably, why the price is the price. But William's main administrative work was naval. In 1205 he was in charge of the royal fleet in the south of England, and was one of those responsible for the ships, the oarsmen and the provisions that kept the Channel crossings honest in a year when the crossings had to be. The yard's account of him — the account that made it fit to set his name to a bench — is that he sat on the dock-side seat of this number while the fleet was making fast at Sandwich, with the list of departures before him and his cloak on his knees.

We have not made a bench of his, and we do not claim that this is the bench; the oak is not old enough and neither is the joinery. We have made the bench that would have been there, in the same height, the same seat board, the same place in the row, so that the schedule of the Green Line can run out of the same spot where a fleet used to run out of the mud of the Stour estuary. That is the whole of the product, and it is a complete one.

Companion sheets at the yard

The board's place name — Knight Island — is kept from the naturalist's field notes of the mostly-submerged island in the Napa River, Solano County, California, where the tide holds what the water cannot; the yard has hung the bench's schedule by it for the same reason it hangs everything by anything — Knight Island, Field Notes. The bench is joined in the manner of the yard's other patient work, one step at a time, and the reader who wants the whole method of a thing built slowly will find it in The Gradual, Build Log, Room 3 at Trades Hall, one inch to the foot. And where the bench itself has finally been sat in, weighed, and argued over with a full account of who pays for what, the ledger is kept at The Long & Winding Road, the dining room above the piano factory.

Order & delivery

Orders are taken at the yard gate, Sandwich, or in writing to the boathouse. The bench travels on its own skids, not in a box; the board travels separately, faced with green cloth, and is hung by the purchaser at a height that does not require standing. The yard does not repair what it has not made, but it will re-oil, at cost, any numbered bench brought back within a tide — which, in practice, is a great many years.
