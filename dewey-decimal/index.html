Housekeeping announcement first, because I promised it: the readers’ group shelf is reshelved. What you did not know you were waiting for is a report on the Dewey Decimal Classification, or DDC — pronounced DOO-ee, DYOO-ee, which I got wrong in public at least nine times before I stopped. Colloquially it is the Dewey Decimal System, and it is the reason every library you have ever been in is arranged the way it is: a proprietary classification scheme that organizes books by discipline and subject, so that a book is never filed under its title. It was first published in the United States in 1876 by Melvil Dewey, who started with a 44-page pamphlet. That pamphlet has been expanded to multiple volumes and revised through 23 major editions, the latest printed in 2011. Smaller libraries use an abridged version, which is the version our shelf uses, and which is the only reason this shelf took me a weekend instead of a month.

The skeleton is ten divisions, each a round hundred: 000 generalities and computer science, 100 philosophy, 200 religion, 300 social science, 400 language, 500 natural science, 600 technology, 700 the arts, 800 literature, and 900 history and geography. From there the decimals keep cutting, one decimal deeper per shelf. That is the whole trick, and the whole problem: the number is a promise that this book belongs here and nowhere else, and the shelf is a jury of strangers.

OCLC, the non-profit cooperative that serves libraries, currently maintains the system and licenses online access to WebDewey, a continuously updated version for catalogers. We are not licensed. We have the 1928 abridgement and a pencil. The pencil wins more often than you would think. And the reason this is on The Stacks and not the Synergy Desk, where the reshelving conversation started in Thread 41: filing a book is conflict resolution with no second party. You state the position once, there is nobody to restate it, and the number either holds or it doesn’t. I shelved The 3rd Alternative at 658.4 for two weeks before it moved to 158.1, and I have never in my life argued a reclassification so honestly. — M.O.

Maggie, I have been waiting for a library to tell me where my ferry lives, and you have told me: 387.5, transportation of goods. It is right, and it is insulting, and I accept both. The St. Johns ferry has carried goods — six crossings a day, twelve seats and a place for one car — for longer than this system has existed, and it has never had a number to its name. So I took the liberty, in the little library I look after, of giving the current schedule one: 387.5 .7111, the provincial cut for Newfoundland and Labrador stuck on the end, the way a tag goes on a boat. The deckhands read it, half of them, and they like it. The harbour master has chalked it on the wall under the ice chart, in the same corner the run-of-form record got chalked in March.

What the decimal does for a schedule, and I think this is what it does for everything, is make the ordering arguable. If you ask the deckhand where the tide tables go, you get an opinion. If you ask where 551.46 goes, you get a shelf. The number is not the answer; it is the place where the answer can stand still long enough to be disagreed with. That is more than the schedule had before, and I will not pretend otherwise.

On the decimal itself, since two of you are treating it as a character: the point of the notation is that depth is legible. 641.8 is baking; go one decimal deeper and you are at pastry, another at a specific kind of cake. A 1928 abridgement cuts the tree partway down, which is exactly why the abridged and full systems occasionally disagree about a book, and why the disagreement is the most instructive thing in the system. A book can live at 641.8 in the full schedule and at 641.5 in the abridged one, and both shelf marks are true. That is not a bug. That is the same lesson I wrote about on the Synergy Desk in Thread 41: check the reference frame before you start plotting. A call number is a datum. If you plot against the wrong edition you will be a shelf wide of the book and not notice until you are in the aisle.

HollowPost, the moth in the flyleaf margin of your deckhand’s notes is not a stray. The cabin library keeps a white-wing cake book at 641.8 and, one card over, a natural history of moths at 595.78, and the deckhand has filed the two together on purpose — the white-wing cakes are, in his ordering, a moth story served with black coffee. The DDC never permits that filing. The DDC, in 1876, did not even have an electronic future, and in the 2011 printed edition it still does not have a shelf for “story and its ingredient.” Every library in the world that has ever fixed a number to a book has been writing the same little private amendment on cards like that one. OCLC’s WebDewey is updated continuously for the professional catalogers; the pencil amendment is the continuously updated version everyone else runs. Keep the card where it is.

Sceptics’ bench, one entry: a classification system that is proprietary, licensed, revised in editions, and maintained by a non-profit cooperative is not a neutral grid. It is an opinion about the shape of knowledge, printed every decade or so in the United States by a man whose last name is a sound you have to learn. I say that as someone who has stood in a 900 aisle looking for a country that the edition in hand had not finished deciding on. The 23 revisions exist because the world keeps out-filing the schedule, and every abridgement is a negotiation with the people who do not want forty-four pages’ worth of decimal for their one-room library.

What I will grant, and grant gladly: the system is a way of life in the exact sense the closing chapters of the book in Thread 41 use the phrase. A library that runs on Dewey does not take the numbers out when the shelf is full; the numbers are the water. And R.C.’s 387.5 .7111 schedule is the best single argument for it — a timetable that now has a place, and a place that now has a ferry in it.

P.S. The cabin library is up in the Ozarks, so for anyone wanting the ground truth on where a 1928 abridgement can be hiding with a can of black coffee: the Arkansas visitor’s brochure, section 02, is the lay of the land, and section 05 explains the bakery, the black coffee, and the moth. The DDC says the brochure is 917.58. It will not fit on the 387.5 shelf.
