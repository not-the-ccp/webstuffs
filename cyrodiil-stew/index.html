The Family Recipe Book · 2234 NW Flanders Street · Card No. 41

Hearth-Stew for Gate Nights

Kept by Nana Vael (1931–2019), transcribed by her granddaughter Brigid · serves eight, one long evening, mostly waiting

The long preamble, as she dictated it, winter of 2007

This card is older than the reason for it. The stew came first — 1974, the Flanders Street winter, the year the radiator gave up and the family moved the dinner hour to two o’clock so the pot could do its work while the windows were still warm. The reason for it came thirteen years later, in the autumn of 2006, when Brigid brought home the game her brother had been borrowing: The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion, the action role-playing game from Bethesda Game Studios, co-published by Bethesda Softworks and 2K Games, the fourth in its Elder Scrolls line after 2002’s Morrowind, out on the Microsoft Windows machines and the Xbox 360 in 2006 and on the PlayStation 3 the following year. It took the household two evenings to notice that every Saturday from then until the spring of 2007 was, effectively, a gate night.

Here is the shape of the game, because the stew was named for it, and the stew was named before the naming stuck. The story takes place in Cyrodiil, the fictional Imperial Province, a flat green plain ringed by hills, where the player’s business is to stop the Mythic Dawn, a fanatical cult of the Daedric Prince Mehrunes Dagon, who intends to open the gates that stand in the capital cities and let a demonic realm called Oblivion pour in through them. There are eight of the gates, one per city — the Imperial City, Bravil, Cheydinhal, Chorrol, Anvil, Leyawiin, Skingrad, and Bruma — and each one, when you walk through it, is a different plane, a different weather, a different wrongness, with its own guards who will hold the gate open until the steward in charge of it is convinced or carried off. The game is an open world; you are allowed to travel, and the family found that “allowed to travel” is exactly the phrase you need when someone has been at the keyboard for the third hour and the pot is doing its second.

The rule Nana set, and it is the only rule on the card: on a gate night the stew goes on the stove at five, and nobody at the table may speak of the game before the first serving is ladled. The talk came after, and it was good. Brigid kept the family chronicle of those Saturdays in a small notebook she called the Imperial Ledger — one page per gate, the stew’s state of readiness in the left margin, the evening’s progress in the right, and the two columns occasionally contradicting each other, which she considered the document’s most accurate feature. When the notebook was finally appraised by the household — her mother’s cousin, the one who signs off on everything in the family — the review card that came back is still tucked into this recipe’s box, and it lists what to keep: “Realism, wit, irony Her use of Celtic languages and other things worth keeping.” The Celtic languages were real, too: Brigid had been at the Gaelic class three streets over since she was nine, and the notebook is full of them, the gate names translated and mistranslated, sluagh for the host, dün for the fort, a whole page of words she kept using for the stew even after she stopped using them for the game. The card says keep them, so they are kept. For the wider family’s record of that same front of old languages read across a valley — Gauls, Britons, Picts, Gaels, the six living tongues — the almanac file is at The Celts — Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, Entry No. 31, and it agrees with the notebook that the weather front and the language front travel in the same direction and are probably the same storm. And since a recipe card with a long preamble belongs on a shelf with the other long-preamble cards, keep it beside Grandmother Alena’s Thursday Broth, Card No. 7, which travels a similar distance — seven cases and a fusional grammar — between the language and the pot.

What the stew actually is, in the way that matters: a beef and marrow pot, slow, dark, and forgiving, built to outlast the evening rather than meet it. It is the pot equivalent of the open world. You may leave it on the stove from five until nine; it will be fine, and it will be better, and nobody has to apologize for the detour. In 2007 the PlayStation 3 machine made the stew a household item past the Flanders Street door — cousins, the Haddocks from four doors east, a whole branch of the family who had never touched a keyboard — and the card grew the margin it has now. The count of gate nights was kept to the last one: thirty-one Saturdays, from the second week of November 2006 to the first of April 2007, ending when the last gate in the notebook was marked closed and the stew was served to one person, Nana, who ate it standing up at the stove and said it was, at last, only soup.

The recipe, Card No. 41, in the left margin’s hand

Serves eight · one pot · four to five hours · one gate night

  • 1.5 kg beef chuck, cut in the fist
  • 4 beef bones with marrow, cracked
  • 8 carrots, thick slices, the dark end kept
  • 6 parsnips, peeled lengthwise and halved
  • 1 leek, white part only, in rings
  • 4 onions, quartered, left in their layers
  • 8 large potatoes, unpeeled, halved
  • 3 cups pearl barley, rinsed cold
  • 250 ml sour cream, kept out of the pot
  • 5 cloves garlic, crushed with the flat of a knife
  • 3 bay leaves, 12 whole black peppercorns, 1 tablespoon caraway
  • 1.5 litres cold water or the best broth in the house
  • salt to the bottom of the pot, not to the taste
  1. Five o’clock, the hour of the gate. Brown the beef in the dry iron pot, standing by, turning it when it stops sticking. No butter; the meat brings its own. When the bottom is dark, scrape it out with a cold spoon — that scrape is the dish.
  2. Build the layers: bones, then half the onions and the garlic, then carrots and parsnips, then the leek. Salt the bottom. Pour in the water to the neck of the pot.
  3. Bring it to a boil with the lid off — it will look like a mistake; it is clearing itself. Skim for five minutes, then cover and turn the heat to the lowest ring the pot will keep.
  4. Six o’clock: add the barley. The pot now has its own weather, and the room should smell like a door opening. This is the first serving hour’s notice.
  5. Seven o’clock: add the potatoes whole, uncut halves, and leave them alone. A potato cut at seven o’clock is a potato that finishes at eight and is wrong at nine.
  6. Eight o’clock, the serving. Check the marrow; it should pull from the bone like bread from a door. Taste. Salt it now, and only now. Ladle first, talk second — the rule, and the only rule.
  7. If the night runs to nine, keep the lid on and the stove at its lowest ring; the stew is the open world, and the detour is the point. Serve the sour cream in a jug, not in the pot, so that the pot can still be right at ten.

Keeper’s note, in Brigid’s hand: the Imperial Ledger counts thirty-one gate nights. This card is for the thirty-second, which is any night at all when the house is cold enough and the table is long enough and someone is willing to stand at the stove and ladle.
