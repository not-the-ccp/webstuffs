Webstuffs · Dining Notes · Review No. 15 of the season

The Fifteen Parts

Coopers' Lane, beside the line · seating for one byte · ISO/IEC, in two sittings

THE CROCK'S VERDICT: 256 out of 256 — a full byte, and nothing half-served.

■ FIFTEEN PARTS IN ALL, PART TWELVE ABANDONED ■ EIGHT BITS TO A PLATE ■ TABLES 1–4 EX-ECMA-94 ■ THE 6:15 FREIGHT PASSES AND DOES NOT STOP ■

I. The room

There is a dining room on Coopers' Lane with fifteen tables, and the tables are numbered the way the standard numbers its parts: 1, 2, 3, and so on up to 15, with the number stencilled on the rim of each plate. The house — which serves the character encodings of the ISO/IEC 8859 family, the joint series of the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission — is a small one, and it faces the railway. The Northern Harbor & Counties line runs past it at dinner, and the 6:15 freight, a string of boxcars each of them exactly eight bits deep, rattles the vinegar bottles on the shelf. The train passes it once and does not stop, and the waiters have long since stopped waving.

Everything at the Fifteen Parts is 8-bit. A plate is a byte: 256 characters to a dish, not one fewer, and the kitchen has a standing rule, chalked above the pass and never once broken, that no portion is served by halves. The room keeps the line's time. In 1989 the same stretch of rail carried a ball club's season as Line B-89, 162 stations and a board that finished even; the guard's book and this review sit together in the archive, and the room's owner has framed the season's final entry on the wall by the cellar door. For the working of that line, see The 1989 Brewers Service — Timetable & Commentary · Line B-89.

II. The menu, read table by table

The clever thing about the menu is the first course. The first 128 characters — the control row and the plain a-to-z — are identical at every table in the house. You can sit at table 2, or table 13, or anywhere at all, and the lower row of the plate will be the same: the same vowels, the same digits, the same question mark. It is a shared course, and it is what keeps the house coherent when the rest of the menu scatters across the world. Only the upper 128 — the region that is free to each part — differ, and they differ a great deal, which is the whole point of the design.

At table 1, the house classic, Latin-1 serves the western European course: the à, the è, the ñ, the ß, the œ, the sharp s folded double — a dish built for the letters of the Atlantic and of the Rhine. Table 2, Latin-2, is the central European room, with the caron on the č and the š and the ř, and a whole plate of Central Europe's hard sounds set down properly. Tables 3 and 4 take the Baltic twice — Latin-3 and Latin-4, the second with a finer provision of š and ž. Table 5 is the Cyrillic table; table 6 the Arabic; table 7 the Greek, where the polytonic signs sit like small flags; table 8 the Hebrew. Table 10 is Turkish, table 13 the Armenian, table 14 the Celtic room, where the old ogham is kept under glass at the centre of the table, and table 16 the south Slavic plate. Table 15, the last, is the one I came back for: Latin-9, the house's late revision of table 1 with eight glyphs replaced, among them the euro sign, which arrived at the pass in 1999 and has not been seen since. The full sleeve notes for that table are kept in the shed as Latin Alphabet No. 9 — Liner Notes.

Table 12 is roped off. It stands at the back of the room with the chair pulled out and the rim stencilled 12, and nothing has been served at it in over twenty years. The part was proposed, the card was printed, and the dish was never cooked; the house calls it the abandoned table, and the owner will say no more than that it was “dropped before service,” which is the kindest true thing that can be said of it.

III. Provenance

The cellar holds the house's provenance, and it is a good one. Tables 1 through 4 were not of the house's own founding: they were originally the house standard ECMA-94, the old recipe book of Ecma International, and they were adopted into the ISO/IEC series whole, stencils and all, rather than re-written. The owner is careful about this, the way the desk behind the bakery is careful about the shed's ledger: the four tables came in with their own numbering already on the rims, and the series kept the numbering it was given. Fifteen parts, then, excluding the abandoned one — fifteen working tables to the standard's count — a family of 8-bit encodings built so that any of the fifteen plates could be read on any of the fifteen tables' cutlery without the lower row ever changing.

IV. The dismissal

One thing must be reported plainly, and the room itself reports it: the ISO working group that kept the series — the house's guild of keepers — has been disbanded. The kitchen has been formally closed. No new parts will be set down, the stencils are in the drawer, and the last signature on the series is dry. Yet the menu stands as it stands, fifteen to a family, and the dining room keeps serving from it; a standard is a recipe, and a recipe outlives the kitchen. The owner, who keeps a small brass plate by the door reading the series is complete; the tables remain, asked me to tell the desk that the last of the winter crock was shipped to the shed.

It was written at the desk in the shed behind the bakery, where the shed almanac keeps its barometer on the signal — MPEG-2, Entry No. 28605, steady at 6.0 Mbit/s — and where the monthly sheets are struck in letterpress in Lisbon and shipped up in a hoop-bound barrel. The same press struck the menu cards for this review, and the same barrel carried, last winter, the stencilled rim of the abandoned table 12, which now hangs above the desk. Readers may return to Webstuffs for the rest of the shed's keeping.
