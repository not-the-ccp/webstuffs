Shed Ledger · Entry 41 · Cornwall Shelf

St Adwen's Church, Advent

Build log — tower at 1:48, eight pinnacles, one long December

Entry 1 — The Photograph That Ruined Me

Saturday, 2 November · 07:40 · rain on the skylight

It was a 1968 press cut, folded in a parcel of church ephemera I bought off a skip-lotter in Padstow. A grey tower, square as a sugar loaf, standing out of a field of bracken — and on top of it, eight pinnacles, four on the face and four on the diagonals, like the tower was wearing a little iron crown. St Adwen's Church at Advent, in the Tamar Valley of Cornwall. The caption, in a pen that pressed hard: "only church in Cornwall to have eight pinnacles to its tower."

That is the whole pitch. That is the whole build. Every other church I have ever modelled — the blaeenwern chapel, the little lighthouse at Penrose — had six, or four, or none. St Adwen's has eight, and they are all slightly different. In the cut the northwest one is leaning two or three degrees, and I have decided that is not the wind. That is the building.

The church is a Grade I listed parish church in the Church of England, dedicated to St Adwen, one of the daughters of King Brychan of Brycheiniog. The dedication matters to me more than it should. Brychan's daughters — the Welsh saints went by the score — and one of them, according to the local book, walked all the way to the Tamar to be buried where the church now stands. A daughter of a Brycheiniog king, holding a Cornwall hillside together for a thousand years. I need the model to feel that weight, or I will not be done.

Entry 2 — Lines and Proportions

Thursday, 23 November · 21:15 · the 07:40 radio

Chose 1:48 because I have a sheet of basswood plank that is exactly the right thickness to be a church wall at that scale. The tower, finished, will be 412 mm tall to the crown of its tallest pinnacle — about the height of the workbench, which means I have to be able to walk past it without nodding. The body is a square of 68 mm side. Each pinnacle is a small octagonal spire: an 8 mm plinth, a shaft turning through eight facets, a cap, and a single crocked finial cut from the tip of a skewer, sanded on the wheel until it was warm.

Parts list, final: 1 tower body, 1 stringcourse band, 3 lancet bell-openings, 8 pinnacles, 8 plinths, 8 crocked finials, 1 slate capping for the octagonal turret, 1 bell (cast, 42 g, from a broken bracket lamp). The bell hangs in the middle opening on a thread of 0.4 mm wire. I counted the pinnacles eleven times before gluing. It is eight. I am allowed to have counted.

Eight is a proud number and it punishes mistakes. Seven and you have a fancy steeple. Nine and you have an error. The diagonals have to be cut at exactly 45° to the plinth ring, and the ring has to be true, and the tower body has to be plumb to within the thickness of a card edge, or the whole crown tips and looks like it was hit by something. I glued the ring, let it sit a full week on a spirit level, and only then started the pinnacles.

Entry 3 — Stone and the Lime Wash

Friday, 24 November · 16:05

Cornwall church stone at this scale wants to read as pale grey-green, the way the cut faces in the old photograph go soft with lichen. I mixed the wash: 4 parts titanium white, 1 part raw umber, a whisper of viridian, thinned with the matte medium until it ran off the brush like weak milk. Three coats, each one drier and more ragged than the last. The pinnacles got a final stipple of umber with a cut-up sponge to give the shafts their eight dark ribs. Under the eaves I darkened everything, because weather does that, and then I wiped the wipe-back so only the sheltered stone kept the grime.

The slating on the turret was a full day. 420 shingles, cut from a sheet of 0.5 mm slate with the shears, laid in courses of nine, each course butting the one below. Nine, not eight — the slate courses are nine wide because that is what fits. I do not think the actual tower's slating is a multiple of eight either. Some numbers are for saints and some numbers are for shingles.

Entry 4 — The Bell

Monday, 27 November · 09:20

The bell from the bracket lamp rang, which I was not expecting. 42 grams, cast aluminium with a skin of patina, a note a little flat but honest. I hung it in the centre lancet and the model got a shadow in it — the whole shelf is a different place. I am not going to ring it in the church of the model. But I will wind it very gently, once, on the day, and I will let it do what it does, which is ring the size of the room it stands in.

Entry 5 — The Court and the Logbook

Tuesday, 28 November · 18:45 · storm on the valley

In Advent the graveyard does not go by the name graveyard. The old book calls it The Court, and the field gate at the north-east corner opens straight into it, and the whole place is walled to the churchyard in the way these Tamar Valley churches are walled, which I have modelled in the plan view with 1:96 lath, 340 mm of it, all crooked the way old fence-line is crooked.

THE COURT: The archive also keeps a curious logbook.

The archive is the little wooden box in the vestry of the real St Adwen's, the one the warden, Mrs Petherick, let me photograph in September. The logbook is a cloth-covered ledger, 218 pages, and it is not the belfry book at all — it is a gate log. From 1911, when the church bought a proper iron gate for The Court, down to last spring, someone has entered the date and the reason the gate was held: "Held for the coffin of E. Tregenza." / "Held for hens, returned." / "Held, no reason, gate left as found." A hundred and thirteen years of a gate being held. I have copied the first eleven entries into the model's base plaque. The last one I copied is from 1967, the year the photograph of the tower was taken, and it reads: "Held all day. Weather. The pinnacles are still eight." I have checked. They are.

Entry 6 — Fairing and the Feast Day

Sunday, 3 December · 08:30 · cold, flat light

Faired everything with the 600 and the 1000. The leaning northwest pinnacle is now leaned by exactly the amount of the photograph, which I achieved by packing a single paper shim into its plinth — a full millimetre of leaning, all deliberate, and if you stand on the wrong side of the shelf it looks like a flaw, which is what leaning is. The base is a slab of pale slate the size of a dinner plate: grass seed, a strip of lichen-coloured plaster, the walled Court, and the field going off the edge unfinished, because Advent's field does not end, the Tamar just takes it.

The launch is set for 4 December, St Adwen's feast day. No crowd. Me, the bell, one wind of the thread, and a glass of cider on the workbench for the warden. The church keeps its hillside; the model keeps its shelf; the daughter of Brychan keeps the name. Eight pinnacles, counted eleven times, eight times right.

LEDGER ENTRY 41 — COMPLETE, PENDING 4 DECEMBER

From the same shelf

The last big build that got me into towers was the San Juan de Portugal galleon log — lines, framing, rigging, launch — and this church owes it its discipline, though the galleon's eight oared banks never once tried to be pinnacles. When the tower is weathered and the wash is dry, the next entry will be filed with the Bedford Downs Station build log, which handles weathering and the long drive better than I manage; its red dirt is a year ahead of my grey-green.

A church is, after all, the oldest kind of organisation, and the habit of keeping the record outlasts the stone: see the Ashton Gate matchnight committee minutes, where ninety-two clubs learn to share a trophy the same way Advent learned to share a walled field. And for what a dedication can do to a building after a millennium, the memorial for Prays liophaea, a small moth remembered at a bench — the shelf has its own quiet commemorations, and this is one of them.

And on the far end of the shelf, in its hand-woven blue mat sleeve, sits the Cook Islands Biodiversity Archive, Raranga Edition No. 1 — twenty-two years of lagoon, reef and upland records on a single field USB, governed by the same rule as The Court's gate ledger: write it down, and do not erase it. When the bell is wound on the 4th, the Archive will be within earshot. What comes back from the water is a brochure: the Lagoon Line visitor’s guide keeps a record of the small brass plate now on the harbour wall beside the Avarua store — “The church keeps its shelf; the daughter of a gate log” — the tower staying on this bench in Cornwall while Adwen, daughter of Brychan, rides the gate log to the Pacific. Eight pinnacles, one long crossing.
