The Vanity Fair Gazette

A Sheet of Private Notices, For Sale & For Let, Wanted & Lost, Services & In Memoriam — being the world itself, at the scale of a halfpenny, for the summer of 1848

Thursday, 22 June 1848 London — Paris — the Rhine — the Peninsula Sheet No. 137

The Gazette keeps its columns open to every private notice that pays a shilling in advance, and this week the sheet is let at so fair a rate that a whole small fortune has passed over the desk in a single afternoon. The advertiser of the leading column, who signs herself Rebecca, Lady Crawley — lately, and on less flattery, Rebecca, Mrs. Sharp — is the daughter of a gentleman of the old stock, orphaned before her tenth year and set adrift with no source of income, no dowry but herself, and a manner of looking at the gentry that has done more for her position than any inheritance. She is a cynic of the first order, and the Gazette declines to say so in its own hand; the notices below say it well enough. Her old friend from school, the young lady who was given away in 1815 and has been clinging to a fortune since, is not named here at all. The desk would not do it. The sheet is the world at halfpenny scale, and the world, as any reader of the columns will see, is a fair in which the sharp turn of a hat and a well-placed fan move more property than a deed. All notices are inserted as received. One line in the Jermyn Street ledger, quoted below, is kept exactly as written, the advertiser having declined to emend it.

To Let

One sitting room and one bedroom

87 Jermyn Street, off the Square8 guineas a week

The lady of the house will let the front rooms to a companion who is in the habit of ascending in the world, provided she keeps the piano tuned and the wine cellar honest. The house is the old one, and has been the lady's own since the marriage of 1815, when she came up from a small school with a trunk, a French accent to finish, and a friend in the way. The landlady, being of an exact disposition, keeps a ledger of lodgers by the bell, and in the winter quarter of '47 the ledger was struck with one line the landlady has declined to emend — For everything the entry for a decade of airing out. — under which, and in the same exact hand, stands the rent. No references will be asked of the advertiser in the Services column; she wants no references.

A house at Wemmel

Near the field, 1815By the month

To be let from this quarter, one small house by the road at Wemmel, where the campaign of 1815 found two of the house's ladies in a room that took in the smoke of the battle, and one of them a husband she had not yet been promised. The room is let still, and the landlord keeps the furniture as it was found: the valise, the little mirror, and a chair that faces the field. Serious tenants only; the landlord is particular that the chair keep its direction.

For Sale

One travelling trunk, complete

From a lady on the point of an advantageous matchFour hundred pounds

For sale at a fixed price, the contents of one travelling trunk: lace enough for two seasons, a fan of her own make in the old manner, six visiting cards newly struck, and the balance of a fortune left by a first marriage to be divided by equity. The trunk has served from a boarding house in London to the table of a certain baronet and his mother at Wemmel and to the Rhine in a carriage shared with no one, and is offered at a fair rate because its mistress is moving up. A certain lady of a house on the Square — one of the Sedleys, it may be added in the margin — is understood to bid against the price, and the desk notes that a woman with a husband in the service and no opinion of her own has never yet outbid a woman with neither. Bids in writing, before the first of July.

One picture of a fine woman, with an account of its travels

A Rubens, in the keeping of the familyPrice on application

For sale, through the family, one Rubens of a fine woman, which a certain lady, now the advertiser in the Services column, is reported to have said was worth more money than she herself, when it hung in the house at Wemmel and was being considered as a fortune. The family has declined the remark, and has declined, in the same breath, to emend the minutes. The picture will be sold to the highest bidder who will take its weight; the frame is old and the account of its travels is longer.

Excursions of every description

By the firm of Cook & Son, LeicesterOne shilling per head, the first time

The household on Jermyn Street has asked the firm of Cook & Son, of Leicester, to arrange a party of the old kind — a band, a picnic by the river, and the whole road out and back for the price of a shilling — the excursion by which the modern business of the journey began on 17 May 1841. The firm keeps its nine surviving stubs in a case at the museum, and the placard, Case No. 47 — Thomas Cook (1808–1892), is commended to any advertiser of this sheet who has not yet bought a day out by the shilling. The lady of the house will not travel; she sends the carriage.

Wanted

A situation, by a young orphan

Of quick parts, with good FrenchSalary not stated

SITUATION WANTED. A young orphan, now past her third season, of good French, fair at the cards, and without a shilling of her own, wishes to place herself near some person of wealth who may be persuaded. She began in the usual manner of the trade — a young orphan with no source of income — and has since climbed out of that paragraph at the expense of a friend, one Amelia Sedley, who is not named in this column but is understood to have furnished the road. The advertiser will undertake the tuition of a house, the management of its expectations, and the general conduct of any gentleman who has a mother, a commission, and a small estate. She will not undertake fidelity, having never been asked. Apply at the office of this Gazette, and be advised that the desk has seen her writing and recommends the shilling be paid in advance.

A fortune, in a friend

By a gentleman of the gentryThe estate, on settlement

WANTED: a fortune, conveniently attached to a person, for a gentleman who has hitherto been content with his commission and his mother's opinion. The advertiser will consider any widow, provided she is agreeable, and any bride, provided she is rich; the latter, it is reported, having lately preferred the former, and the former having, on her side, found a baronet in a drawer in Paris. Applications are invited from the advertiser in the Services column, who has a baronet of her own, a fortune of four hundred pounds in a drawer, and no objection, on principle, to a third. The desk observes that the column has produced, in one quarter, more marriages per notice than any other sheet in London.

Lost & Rewarded

Lost from the carriage at Waterloo

18 June 1815Reward: a husband, if the owner consents

LOST, from a carriage in the smoke of the battle, 18 June 1815: one reticule of the old make, containing a letter from a certain countess of Paris, and the draft of a fortune that a certain captain may or may not have spent before the evening. Any person finding the same is requested to send it to the house at Wemmel, where it was wanted most, and where, the advertiser adds, the smoke has not cleared in thirty-three years. A reward is offered, and the advertiser, who has lost nothing since that carriage without replacing it at a higher price, will not be pressed on the amount.

Services

A lady will call

In French, in cards, in the pianoforte, and in the management of a house's expectationsBy the season

MISS — or rather MRS. — SHARP, who has lately had her cards struck with the title Rebecca, Lady Crawley, will call on the gentry of either country, and instruct in French, in cards, in the pianoforte, and in the art by which a woman of no fortune takes a seat in every drawing room between the Channel and the Rhine. The advertiser is a social outsider of the old kind — the Spanish word for the trade is kept, for the French, in the margin — and she exposes the manners of the gentry to ridicule as a matter of professional routine, with a fan in each hand. She keeps her own books, and her own opinions, and the books balance. Two of her former pupils — one a baronet, one a baronet's mother — decline to be quoted, which is taken as the best reference the column can offer.

Of the table, and the tongues of it

By the desk of the GazetteThe shilling covers the rule

Any advertiser who gives a dinner at which more than two tongues are expected at one table is referred to the desk book of the house, whose seventh chapter treats of the Common Language Infrastructure — the standard that lets many languages sit at one table without a word of either being rewritten — kept at the desk beside the vinegar crock, under the heading Chapter VII · Of the Common Language Infrastructure. The advertiser in the preceding column has kept to that etiquette at every table from Wemmel to Paris, and is asked, by the usual notice of the sheet, to serve the beef by weight and not by favour.

In Memoriam

A marriage, and a fortune of four hundred pounds

1815 — 1837, and thenceforwardDivided by equity

In memoriam, a marriage made at the altar of the old faith in the summer of 1815, the wife having been led up the aisle by a brother who did not know what he was giving. The husband fell at the field, the widow ascended, and the fortune of four hundred pounds that the marriage left in a drawer is now to be divided by equity among those who were there and those who were told of it. The column observes, in the small print at the foot of the notice, that the widow has not yet been seen weeping at any funeral she has been invited to, and that this, too, is a manner of keeping the ledger. The sheet will run one further notice for her in the next quarter, when the title on the card is expected to change again. Until then: the world is a Vanity Fair, and this page of it is let, for the week, at a penny a line.

Notices are inserted as received, and one line in each quarter is kept unemended, in the advertiser's hand, as the Jermyn Street ledger keeps its winter line. For the whole sheet of the week, and the notices of the other sheets, return to the front page of the Gazette. Composed and printed at the office of this paper, 87 Jermyn Street, where the piano is tuned on Thursdays and the ledger is kept by the bell.
