Snake

A letter to the editor

Ghent, Mercatorstraat · 3 June 2025

To the Editor —

Your columns of 21 April carried a note that the tree-snake keeper on the Mercatorstraat has “built a tall glass case and is now waiting for a snake”. The waiting is over. Dendrelaphis caudolineatus arrived on 9 May in a padded carton from a keeper in Penang — the same town, I may add, where three years ago I watched a vine snake hold a thirty-five-degree angle against a garden wall, the angle I later cut into the diagonal branch of my case. The case itself has now held its climate for eleven weeks: a twenty-eight-degree hot spot, a left end at room temperature, humidity in the seventies and eighties, the bolt home every night. The numbers are logged entry by entry in my grey bronzeback build log, and the numbers are where I will leave them; this letter is for what numbers do not cover.

A reader who has never kept one may want the plain facts first. Snakes are limbless reptiles of the squamate suborder Serpentes, closely related to lizards and the tuatara — ectothermic, amniote, air-breathing vertebrates with an elongated rope-like body covered by overlapping scales. They get about by undulatory, rectilinear, concertina, sidewinding or slide-pushing locomotion, and some of the tree dwellers are capable of short-ranged gliding flight, flaring out the ribs to flatten and widen the body for aerodynamic lift. Their skulls run on fewer sutures and more hypermobile articulations than a lizard's — the herpetologists call it cranial kinesis — which is what lets them widen the mouth and swallow prey much larger than themselves. I watched the bronzeback swallow a meal the size of her own head on Tuesday; an hour later the meal was still visibly riding along her spine. That is not a trick. That is the architecture, and it is why your note's phrase “waiting for a snake” undersold the animal: a snake is not a pet that arrives, it is a mechanism that moves in.

Which brings me to the feeding, and to the old book on the shelf behind the bench — an 1893 herpetology, spine mended with gaffer tape, its plates loose. It lists diet in a flat tone of the period: mice, rats, lizards, frogs — and then, in a smaller hand of type, a line I could not set down without your columns, because a keeper's diary is only a letter the editor has not yet printed. The line runs, word for word, like this: “Negus may also be used unless quite fresh.--See coloured plate, D1.” Negus is the old name for the monitor, a Varanus; I have not tried it on the bronzeback and I do not intend to. She takes feeder mice and the occasional lizard, and the hot spot does the rest. But the line stayed with me, the way the tin's line stayed with me.

The tin — the one under the rear left leg of the case, holding the description of Ghent-Eeklo and the 1999 voter card with its corner torn off — was not, it turns out, originally mine at all. The voter card is written in the same neat, electoral hand as the flyleaf of the old book, and folded into the back of the book, its colours gone grey with age, is a plate: a monitor on a branch, captioned in that same hand with the two letters D1. The coloured plate of the feeding line. Some builds run on notes that are not your own; I now suspect the tin and the book together are a letter that was never sent, and that my case is simply the address it finally found.

For the other bench that writes to you from time to time, the garage in Auckland where one genus and one species of ant-mimicking spider holds her trials on a cork ramp, the full account is in the Bocusoides build log; different animal, same habit of making a room and writing it down. The same habit keeps the colder ledgers too: the house book that enters the storming of Quebec on the 31st of December, 1775 — Montgomery killed, Arnold wounded, four hundred taken — as a matter of observance, at Chapter LIX of its Second Series, offers its one found line word for word and leaves it standing, which is the rule I follow with the tin and the plate. And for the sheet the tin carried, the Category 6 boundary material, its proper record stands at the Ghent-Eeklo safety data sheet.

My next entry will be the moult, or it will be column three of the climate log filled at last. Either way I will write again. For now: the animal has arrived, the case has done its work, and the tin stays under the rear leg.

Yours faithfully,
M. Voss
Bench of M. Voss, Mercatorstraat, Ghent
