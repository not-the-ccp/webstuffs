Exhibit No. 7

Akira Kurosawa 黒澤 明

March 23, 1910 – September 6, 1998 · born Tokyo, died Kyoto, at the Shikineyama Hospital, of a stroke, at eighty-eight

Akira Kurosawa (黒澤 明 or 黒沢 明, Kurosawa Akira) was a Japanese filmmaker who directed 30 feature films in a career spanning six decades. With a bold and dynamic style strongly influenced by Western cinema yet distinct from it, he is widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential filmmakers in the history of cinema. Known as a hands-on filmmaker, he was heavily involved with all aspects of production as a director, writer, producer, and editor. Following a brief stint as a painter — Nihonga, the Japanese school, with the brush in the same hand that later ran a cut — Kurosawa entered the Japanese film industry in 1936, and spent the years that followed working on numerous films as an assistant director and screenwriter, with enough cutting-room time to learn what a scene will do when you stop it. He took his first feature as director, Sanshiro, into 1943, and the rest of the count is the count: thirty. Rashomon, 1950, took the Golden Lion at Venice and put rain, fog, and the light on wet gravel into the vocabulary of the screen; Seven Samurai, 1954, fixed the shape of the ensemble; Ikiru, 1952, fixed the shape of a clock; and the last film he completed standing, Madadayo, 1993, fixed the shape of forgetting. He died six years after that, in September, 1998, which the shop has noted in the production office in the house hand, and has not added to.

i.

The Fog

Kurosawa kept the weather inside the studio. The shop keeps it too: the haze machine on the studio wall makes white on cue, from glycol, for the prince who stands in the fog of The Idiot while the house goes dark. The fog is not an effect in this shop; it is a prop with a safety sheet, and it is numbered.

ii.

The Dark

Auditorium darkness is intentional and may last several minutes; the hazard code is H-PCS-3, and the director in question built his cinema out of exactly that fact — a room held in the dark while the story waited. The stage manager who keeps the plot has decided the dark is not an emergency, which is the whole of the stage management, and the whole of the film.

iii.

The Axe

One axe, in case. In this house it is the pawnbroker’s, and it is not being used; on the tool wall it is marked No. 13, in its case, where it has always been. Kurosawa’s films know what an instrument is for and what it costs to know it. Nobody in the annex will be the one who moves it.

iv.

The Word

In the bottom of chest No. 14, beside the coat room beside the hill, a water-stained volume of Crime and Punishment was found with a single margin word in the house hand: patience. Kurosawa kept diaries of his own, in the form he had kept his whole life, and they came out in print after him. A room that holds is a diary. The placard agrees.

v.

The Card

With the volume, in the box of batten twine, the shop found one card set in the house hand, a kept line from the classifieds page, preserved exactly as it was written and exactly as it is kept here, one error and all:

LADY TICHBORNE , aged 25 years, late of Park House, having reason to know.

The error is the age. Twenty-five was the son’s age at the going under, and the line that bears it is the mother’s; the compositor set the son’s number under the mother’s name and the family, which declines to correct its own advertisements, declined to correct this one. Having reason to know is the clause that needs no footnote, and the card has never had one. The placard keeps the line as found, and the annex keeps the card in chest No. 14, where the fog does not reach.
