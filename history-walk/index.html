A Walk Through the Past

Itinerary in seven stops · guided by the archivist on duty · the route follows the old canal bank the whole way · coffee at Stop V

What the walk is for: history is the systematic study of the past, focusing primarily on the human past. As an academic discipline it analyses and interprets evidence to construct narratives about what happened, and explains why it happened. Some theorists file it under the social sciences; others put it in the humanities; the third table in the Reading Room insists on a hybrid, and the archivist, who has held the route for eleven years, takes no side and hands out seven stops instead.

I The Old Style Gate · 10:15, departure · 4 min walk

STOP 1 of 7 · the lychgate at the north end of the canal bank · allow 15 minutes

The route starts where reckoning begins. The gate bears two dates, one above the other: the feast day, and the number of the year in the old style — a pocket reckoning by the Julian calendar, a leap day every fourth year without exception, and thirteen days adrift from the plate beneath it by the time you read both. That drift is the first exhibit on the route, because the past can only be entered under a date, and every date is an argument. The old almanac found at the gate, with its thirteen lost days and the Green Mountains sketched on the back, is the one the archive holds at Lost & Found — a reckoning in the old style, and the guide reads it aloud to every group, slowly, because the sentence is a date.

II The Reading Room · 10:30 · arrive from the gate, down the bank

STOP 2 of 7 · the archive's reading room, third window, no pens on the table · allow 25 minutes

History, the guide says, works on evidence the way a mill works on grain: you bring in the flour of the record — the minute, the timetable, the ledger, the chalk slip — and the discipline turns it into something you can hold, a narrative about what happened and, harder, why. The room keeps its cards in the order found, unamended, and the archivist's first rule matches the discipline's first rule: you may not put in a record what you wish happened. Similar debates have been held at this very table about where history sits — a social science, measuring; a humanities room, interpreting; a hybrid, doing both with one pair of hands — and the resolution is the table itself: sit on one side or the other and the argument continues; sit in the middle and the reading goes on.

III The Lecture Hall · 11:00 · around the corner by the bell

STOP 3 of 7 · the hall's own lecture hall, pews for forty, microphone off · allow 20 minutes

Here the route takes up the oldest quarrel in the field: the purpose of history. Is its main aim theoretical, to build a working account of how the human past runs? Is it to uncover the truth, one careful reading at a time? Or is it practical — to learn lessons from the past, the way a guard learns from a book of the last working? The hall has heard all three claims delivered from this rostrum in the same season, and the plaster holds all three. What the guide settles the matter with is a chair: every school keeps one, and the one in front of the rostrum is worn through in the same two places, because three arguments, sat with long enough, settle into the same posture.

IV The Bench · 11:25 · the oak bench, east wall

STOP 4 of 7 · the bench where the families keep their own minute book · allow 15 minutes

In a more general sense, the word history means not the academic field at all but the past itself — times in the past, or individual texts about the past — and this bench is the stop where the route means it that way. Under the seat, in a biscuit tin, the group is shown what a family keeps for its own past: the tin of letters, the photograph of the boat, the receipt with a name written on the back in pencil. Historical research, the guide allows, is what the discipline does with such a tin at scale — the same questions, the same patience, only more of it, and a method where the evidence must be dated, cross-checked, and kept in the order found. The bench has been oak since 1911, and it has held three tin families and counting.

V The Laundromat Ledger · 11:45 · the laundromat on Copper Lane, closed 1999

STOP 5 of 7 · the brick on Copper Lane, machines gone, one bench left · allow 20 minutes, coffee at the counter

The laundromat on Copper Lane ran from 1974 to 1999 under the same family, and it kept its account in a Register Line — a narrow bound book in which every day's coin came up in a single ruled line. When the machines were lifted out in the winter of 1999, the book came with the group's attention, because it holds the one case on the route where history's two senses — the discipline, and the past itself — sit at the same counter. The archivist reads the entry from the last page, and the page is kept, in the file, exactly as it stands:

“V. The Laundromat Ledger One entry remains in the Register Line, and its Araguainía line — a family recipe · kept under the bench itself did not last.”

— the last page of the Register Line, read at the counter, 10:15 group, all weather

The Araguainía line was a family recipe, written on the back of a machine's instruction card and kept under the bench itself — the same bench the machines stood on, and the same distance from the door as every recipe at every stop on this route. The recipe did not last: the family moved, the card went soft, the ink went where ink goes. But the entry about it did — it is in the bound book, and the bound book is in the archive, and that is the whole of the discipline in one stop. History is the entry that remains when the thing is gone; the study of the past begins exactly where the past has failed. The group is pointed, on the way out, at the card catalogue of the old recipe book the family did manage to keep — the glass spider cakes, with the long preamble and the eight shows — held at Glass Spider Cakes — Galley Card No. 41, proof that one recipe under the bench can survive on paper when it cannot survive in the hand that wrote it.

VI The Register · 12:10 · the workshop ledger drawer

STOP 6 of 7 · the workshop upstairs, the ledger drawer behind the desk · allow 15 minutes

The sixth stop is where the discipline takes its rules in writing. The Register is the bound instrument of the hall's own game — one pair of dice ivory, one pair brass, the rulings set down in the first edition and never revised — and it stands for everything a historian promises: an order of entries, a ruling on what counts, and the sentence kept word for word when the hand that wrote it will not explain it. The full matter is settled in The Register — Player's Manual, First Edition, and the guide's summary at the drawer is short: the discipline is the drawer, and what is in the drawer is the discipline, and both are open on request.

VII The Steps of Gradualism · 12:30 · the south steps, return to the Reading Room

STOP 7 of 7 · the seven steps down to the canal bank · arrival 12:40, tour ends

The route ends on the south steps, one by one, because the method the discipline has kept longest is the one the steps keep: gradualism, from the Latin gradus, step — the past taken one joint at a time, one entry at a time, one bench at a time, through the coal season. The whole argument of the walk is built the same way, and the build log of it is kept at The Gradual — Build Log: one piece a month, one inch to the foot, the whole of it in order, and not one step more than the whole. The archivist counts the group back over the bank, checks the strip for the seven marks, and signs the file: past taken, in order, with no record put in that was not found.
