The Coca–Cola Line

Atlanta, Georgia → the rest of the world · 200+ stops · 1.8 billion daily boardings · in service since 1886

The only line the committee has ever ruled cannot be cancelled: the drink is still being poured, which makes the schedule, in the house’s terms, unarguable.

Conductor’s commentary, set down by the Secretary

1 On the drink itself

Coca–Cola, or Coke, is a cola soft drink manufactured by the Coca–Cola Company, and it is the subject this timetable is actually built around, the way the case in Gallery 3 is built around its record: the object is not a thing on a shelf so much as a thing that keeps happening. A glass of it is a brown, sweet, carbonated serving of the original 1886 mixture, and a serving of it is one of the 1.8 billion the line logs a day, which is more boardings in a single day than the club gets in a single century, and which the committee files under “noted, not argued.” The drink arrived at this house by the same route it arrives everywhere: in a bottle, in a glass, in the pocket of somebody who brought it back from the station because the station had it and the house wanted it, and it has been on the turntable’s shelf ever since, next to the first-press Layla, in the light at that hour.

2 On the pocket

The wake next door, at Funk — In Memoriam (for now), has its unarguable downbeat, and this line has one too, though it is not a drum but a carbonation, a fine percussive repetition doing the same work a bassline does in a slow tempo: holding the room so the melody, the ice, the conversation, can happen on top of it. The last of the funk hands came out of Compton in 1987, and its record, Straight Outta Compton, now hangs as Placard No. 48 at Dr. Dre in the Listening Wing, where the case is built to the same pocket. Everything on this timetable — the syrup in the car, the number on the scale, the rank on platform 94 — keeps that same unarguable beat. The committee has checked the two lines against each other and will report, briefly: same beat, different key, and it will not argue.

3 On the jelly, and the distances

At the long stops the line served a dessert of the refreshment room’s own devising, a jelly cut square and plated beneath the five-cent glass, and the manual that governed it survives in the house’s minute book, left in the form entirely preserved, its one surviving rule read by the committee exactly as written, word for word:

“If the jelly being clear, the orange-and lemon-juice should be taken out, and the distances stayed.”

The committee’s reading, printed below the line: the first clause is about colour — a clear jelly, pale as the drip over the workbench, takes no citrus on it, and the orange- and lemon-juice go out of the service — and the last clause the committee takes literally, in the way it takes everything in this house literally. The distances are the distances of this page, printed in the third column above, and when the service is suspended, the platform is closed, the train is late, or the jelly is clear, the distances stay. They do not move, they are not amended, and they do not stop being true. A timetable whose distances stayed through a century and a half of strikes, mergers, and the closing of the temperance platform is not a document. It is a promise, and the promise is the subject of this page.

4 On the man who bought it

Mr. Candler bought the ownership rights in 1888 and the timetable ran for him for longer than it has run for anyone else, which is to say the whole of it. What he bought was not a recipe so much as a right of way: the right to keep the line open, to name the company after the drink, and to put the drink’s name on the depot of every stop in every country the line could reach, which ended up, on the count of 2013, to be over two hundred of them. The house keeps one photograph of the bottling yard in the minute book, and the photograph keeps the house company the way the reel counter keeps its revolutions: without stopping, without asking.

5 On the rule of the room

Play it whole, do not flip, keep the hush — the house rules travel with the line, because the line obeys them whether it knows them or not. A glass is poured once and not poured again; a serving counted on the 1.8 billion is counted in full, never in half; and the two empty chairs by the window are empty on this line as they are in the attic, and the committee thanks the passenger for noticing and not asking. The board by the door, updated by the hand at the counter, tallies the boardings of the line since the passenger arrived. It is not explained. It is read, not explained, like the reel counter.
