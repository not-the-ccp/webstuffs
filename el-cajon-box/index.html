A Question Sheet from the Valley · Filed with the Route 94 papers

El Cajon — The Box

Fifteen questions about the city of El Cajon in San Diego County, California, answered the way the walking-tour guide answers things: slowly, with a name for everything, and with the porch where the wax is still sold left exactly where the map refuses to move it.

1 What does the name mean?

El Cajón is Spanish for “the box.” The city takes its name from Rancho El Cajón, the Spanish land grant of the valley, which was named for the box-like shape of the valley that surrounds the city. The valley is the point: walls on three sides, a floor where the fog used to sit, and a lid of sky the walkers at the high point of the ninety-four can see laid flat and silver from the canyon climb. When you stand in it, the name stops being a name and becomes a floor plan.

2 Where is it, exactly?

El Cajon is a city in San Diego County, California, United States, 17 miles (27 km) east of downtown San Diego. That number — 17 miles — is the one the guide quotes, because it is the distance the first walkers of the Ninety-Four walking tour had on them when they crested out of the city proper and saw the valley's rim begin. The tour's own sheet runs 63.324 miles to Boulevard; El Cajon sits in the part of it where the road stops being freeway and starts being history.

3 Where does “the Box” come from?

From the same valley shape, one step later. The ranch's name meant “the box,” and the nickname outlived the formality. Locals still call the city the Box the way the handbook calls a hazard by its code: short, exact, no argument. If a walker on SR 94 is lost, the fix is the same as for a compass: find the walls, find the floor, and stop worrying about the lid.

4 How many people live in the Box?

As of the 2020 census, El Cajon had a population of 106,215. The Geologic Desk keeps that figure on file next to its safety data sheet for the state of California, which treats the whole state as one substance — the “state matrix” — and lists population as a component of it. One hundred and six thousand, two hundred and fifteen people, in a box-shaped valley, is what a very small slice of the matrix looks like when it settles down.

5 How does the Ninety-Four walk touch the city?

The tour leaves downtown San Diego on the Martin Luther King Jr. Freeway, runs full throttle west of the valley, and hinges at Spring Valley, where the freeway portion of SR 94 ends and the road becomes what the locals call Campo Road — the non-freeway segment, the old wagon road east through the mountains. El Cajon is the town the valley opens onto between the hinge and the climb: the last porch lights before the guardrail gives way to fence wire. The handbook carries one gallon of water per four walkers from Stop 4 onward, and El Cajon is where that gallon earns its weight.

6 What is the Abell porch, and why does every map refuse to redraw it?

The Abells ran the rigging-wax counter at the old harbor boat shed for two generations — coils of wax for lines, sold by the ounce to skippers — and when the family left the harbor for the valley, they settled on the same quarter-mile of the stretch of Route 94 nearest Spring Valley, one house to a parcel, and have stayed put. The hazards column of the guide's handbook carries the standing entry H-ONM-1 — Positional hazard: the surname Abell from the rigging wax live together, re-inked every season. A walker's rule: salute the porch where the wax is still sold. The tour's maps do not redraw that corner of the valley, and no FAQ corrects them.

7 What did the 1973 dispatch tape at the old junction booth record?

A single line, spoken at the bottom of a shift, which the Desk transcribes without emendation. The tape is one of the few artifacts the transfer ledger of 1973 points to from the valley side rather than the interstate side, and its last entry, word for word, reads:

Hear I the same line the state matrix.
The safety data sheet that the tour's route sheet is filed beside calls the whole state one substance — the state matrix, solid, n.o.s. — and the Desk's reading of the tape is a listening note, not a cipher: the Box, the valley, was still picking up the same line as the matrix — the same fault line, the same fog clock, the same grid of county roads, the same 2020 count ticking up one household at a time. The booth heard the whole state because the whole state was the valley's walls. No further decoding is required; the line is only asking to be read along.

8 How do you pronounce it?

In English, el kə-HOHN. In Spanish, [el kaˈxon], with the stress on the second syllable of Cajón. The 1930s sign-criers of the survey who posted the route's shields did not stop to learn either; the pavement got the name right anyway, which is the most the valley has ever expected of a state department.

9 Is the Box actually box-shaped?

Box-shaped the way a footprint in ruts is box-shaped: by the agreement of everyone who named it. The rim of the valley is not a perfect square, and the handbook would tell you so if the handbook had opinions, which it does not; it has entries. The entry is the box-like shape, and the entry has never been revised. Walk the eastern rim at dusk after a November fog and you will stop asking the question.

10 What does the fog do in November?

The same thing the tour's fixed season was chosen for: it comes in, finds the floor, and stays put long enough to make the valley read as a held breath. The guide dates the walk to November, before the valley fog but after the heat, and El Cajon is the hinge where that instruction matters — you want the fog on the way out of the city, silver under the rim, and nothing on the way up the canyon. The walkers time the mile of the old wagon alignment by the shade of the canyon wall, not by the clock, for exactly this reason.

11 What should a walker bring?

Water — the handbook's share is one gallon per four walkers and a tin cup, rationed from the Campo Road stop onward. Closed shoes for the wash of pale stones on the canyon floor. A notebook, if the walker has seen the pencil entry about the khaki necktie on the bench at the high point and the radio sign above the booth at the far end of a field: the handbook's advice is to keep the pace and not answer the sign. It was only asking if anyone had been.

12 Why is the city's nickname worth a standing entry?

Because it is the one name in the valley that is also a shape, and a shape is a fixed point. The Abell porch is a positional hazard; the box-shaped valley is the reason it is one. A hazard defined entirely by where it is needs walls to be where it is, and El Cajon keeps its walls the way the state matrix keeps its faults: visibly, and without further notice.

13 What happens at the east edge of the Box?

The walls turn their backs and the grade starts its long argument with the terrain. That is Stop 5 of the walk — the mountains east, the climb, the high point where the whole valley behind you lies flat and silver, exactly as far away as the route is long. El Cajon, seen from there, is the floor of its own name: 106,215 people, one quarter-mile of porches, one coil of rigging wax at a time.

14 Does the 2020 census figure include the Abells?

It must; the count runs door to door and the porches are doors. The figure of 106,215 is quoted in the field copy of the route sheet as the census count of the valley itself, and the pencil note beneath it — the one the first guide re-inks every season — adds, in small type: one house to a parcel, all of them put. The maps refuse to redraw the corner. The count did not, either.

15 Where do I file this question sheet?

In the same drawer as the route sheets of the Ninety-Four walking tour and the safety data sheet for the state, and from the Webstuffs front page, where every other route, bench, and ledger on this shelf is kept. It is the valley's own FAQ: the name, the shape, the number, and the one line the booth heard — Hear I the same line the state matrix — read along, not decoded. The Box does not need correcting. It needs witnesses, on foot, in November, keeping the pace.
