The Shed · Colubridae · Build Log No. 7

Grey bronzeback

One tall case, one tree snake, and a tin that will not stay shut.

Bench of M. Voss · Mercatorstraat, Ghent · begun 11 March 2025

Started — the species

Subject: grey bronzeback · Build: vivarium, No. 4 · Status: frame up, no animal yet

There are two species of snake named grey bronzeback: Dendrelaphis caudolineatus and Dendrelaphis modestus. I am building for the first — the slender, daytime tree snake of gardens and rubber plantations, bronze down the back, a pale stripe along the flank, quick as a dropped ruler. The second is its quieter namesake; I only learned of it when the first page of the log was already ruled. This build is the case: tall, ventilated, escape-proof, with enough height that the animal can climb out of reach of the floor. The animal itself has not arrived. It will not arrive until the case holds its climate without me watching.

Materials — the case

Frame: oak, 18 mm · Glass: 4 mm, nine panes · Vents: twelve, 25 mm mesh

The case is ninety centimetres tall, forty-five wide, sixty deep: 90 × 45 × 60 cm, oak frame dovetailed at the corners, four millimetre glass front and sides, a solid oak back board blackened with wax. Twelve twenty-five-millimetre mesh vents run in a row along the top rail; the door hangs on two brass butt catches and a sliding bolt, because a bronzeback that finds a gap will use it — thin as a pencil, fast as a thought. The oak came from a dismantled packing crate behind the bike shop on Veldstraat: €14. The glass was cut at the glazier's on the Kouter: €68. Silicone, mesh, and hinges: €22. Total so far: €104, plus the hours. The case is built, quietly, under a patroness of workshops whose symbols run through it — the owl on the tin lid, the snake that will guard the room, the hook filed as a pointed instrument — and her entry, No. 41 of the cyclopedia, is set down at Athena — Cyclopedia Entry No. 41.

Branchwork

Wood: cork oak and grapevine · Branches: six · Fixing: brass wire, 0.8 mm

A tree snake needs a tree, or a convincing lie of one. I cut six branches from cork oak and wild grapevine — one diagonal from the lower-left vent to the upper-right corner, the rest staggered between — and wired each to the frame with 0.8 mm brass wire, doubled and twisted tight, the ends turned inward so nothing can catch a scale. The diagonal is the main route: thirty-eight centimetres of cork bark at a 35-degree angle, which is the angle I watched a vine snake hold on a garden wall in Penang, three years ago, before I decided to build this bench at all. Sphagnum moss goes in the corners; the floor gets a sheet of cork, one centimetre, to hold the damp.

Climate

Heat: mat under the right third · Hot spot: 28 °C · Humidity: 70–80 per cent

The climate is the part that cannot be seen, so I built a box to measure it. A heat mat under the right third of the floor gives a twenty-eight-degree hot spot; the left end stays at room temperature, twenty-one or twenty-two degrees, so the animal can choose. A five-centimetre computer fan stirs the air without a draught. The hygrometer hangs at mid-height on the glass: seventy to eighty per cent humidity, misted mornings with a hand sprayer, twice in high summer. A twelve-hour LED strip on a timer, daylight white, and a log column for the readings: date, hot spot, room, humidity, comment. Column three is still blank.

The hook

Rod: brass, 4 mm · Length: 75 cm · Handle: copper tape, two layers

A bronzeback does not want handling, and a keeper does not want a bite from an animal that moves like a stitch being pulled through cloth. The hook is four-millimetre brass rod, seventy-five centimetres long, bent to a fifteen-degree tip over the edge of the bench, the handle wrapped in two layers of copper tape so it does not slide from a sweaty palm. I tested it on a rolled newspaper first, then on the empty case: the tip lifts a branch without disturbing the moss. Cost: €3.10 for the rod. It hangs on a nail beside the door, ready.

Found in the tin

Found: under the rear leg of the case · Object: biscuit tin, label worn · Date: unreadable

When I levelled the case, I found an old biscuit tin under the rear left leg, acting as a shim. The lid was stamped with a picture I could not place, and under it, in a hand neat enough to be electoral, a single sentence:

Store the description of Ghent-Eeklo in a tin.

Inside the tin: a printed sheet, folded four times — the Ghent-Eeklo safety data sheet, boundary material, Category 6, from the Flemish Boundary & Electoral Hazards Office — and a 1999 voter card, corner torn off. Why a tree-snake keeper in Ghent keeps a constituency description in a tin I do not know. The tin now lives on the top shelf, behind the misting bottle, and the sheet still props up the rear leg. Some builds run on notes that are not your own.

Launch — first light

Date: 15 April 2025 · Light: 6500 K, 12 hours · Reading: 26.8 °C hot spot, 73 per cent

First light was on the fifteenth. The timer clicked on at 07:00, the LED washed the oak bronze, and the hygrometer settled at seventy-three per cent after the morning mist. The hot spot read twenty-six point eight degrees against a room of twenty-one; acceptable, within drift. I left the door open for an hour to air the silicone smell, then closed the bolt. The case stands on the bench now, empty, branches bare, waiting. The next entry is either the arrival or the moult of a plan, and I cannot tell which I hope for.

Provisions — the log goes on

Store: logbook, pencil, hook · Company: the tin, the sheet, the empty branch

The shelf already holds other builds: the spider log kept in a garage in Auckland, where a single ant-mimicking jumper does her work on cork bark; and the Kimberley cattle station, modelled sheet by sheet in balsa and red ochre, eighty-four kilometres west of Warmun. Different animals, same bench logic — make the room, watch it, write it down. On the shelf there is also a folded folio of minutes, No. 230 of a council that sat for a Romanov queen and kept one night watchman's line word for word, which I have filed at Olga Constantinovna — Minutes of the Council of the Marble Hall. The arrival itself — and the feeding, and the 1893 book — I have set down as a letter to the editor, filed at Snake — Letter to the Editor. The tin stays where it is. The description of Ghent-Eeklo stays in it. And the grey bronzeback, whichever species finally takes the diagonal branch, will find a case that has already been lived in once, by the notes and by the waiting.

My perch question travelled farther than I meant: a reissue ribbon marker sent to a Michigan kitchen came back in the same crate that carried a 1926 season card to a river bend in Detroit, and the whole lot went to the block as Lot 143 — Detroit: A River, a Bridge, and a Ledger. The answer stands, as I wrote it: oak and sisal, not ribbon.

My line about the tall case, the vents, and the Earl of Leicester on bended knees has been borrowed by a naturalist of Flemish parties, who set it into CD&V — Field Notes, Flanders as the truest description of the hustings she knows.
