Cold Storage Plant 3 · Malmö · Sound Operations & Cold-Chain Records

The Rockville Diary

Being the diary of M. Okafor, sound operator, for the week of 14–22 October 2025 — kept in the same hand the session logs have been kept in for twenty-one years, which is exactly why they sent me

COLD STORAGE PLANT 3
· MALMÖ · EST. 1999

Tuesday 14 October 2025 · Malmö, control room

The call came at 09:12, on the line that usually only rings for press failures. A letter from the Food and Drug Administration — Rockville, Maryland, 10001 New Hampshire Avenue — announcing an inspection of the vault, on the 15th and 16th, of the stored influenza lots the label has held since the B/Yamagata track went quiet. I was on the call by 09:20 because the letter says, in its second paragraph, that the agency will be auditing the cold-chain logs "in the hand in which they were written." Nobody else here can read my hand the way I can. Lindqvist, who has been in Malmö since Tuesday's master re-cut, told me to bring coffee and a straight face. The February session has already done what the sleeve notes have only hinted at: the committee struck the fourth groove for good, the trivalent is back, and the 2020–22 Yamagata lots now sit in Vault 3 as history. The FDA has decided that even history must prove it stayed cold.

Wednesday 15 October 2025 · Vault 3, −70 °C

They arrived at 08:40, three of them: Dr. Priya Anand of the biostatistics office, a specialist named Tomas Veli who carries a thermometer like a priest carries a book, and a food-branch inspector, Ms. Delacroix, whom I had not expected and who carried a case of one-ounce glass sampling jars. The vault was silent, which it always is, except for the compressors. They pulled fourteen lots — four Yamagata, ten trivalent — and each one got a jar of its contents, one ounce, labelled in Delacroix's tiny hand. Anand stood at the console the whole morning and read my logs. At 13:05 she asked, without looking up, about the glazed coffee crock that has sat on the console since the label's founding. "It is in every pressing," I said. "Since the single-axle truck night, it is in the record." She wrote one line in her own notebook and moved on. I have looked for that notebook ever since. It does not exist, and I am not a man who can leave a thing like that unrecorded.

Thursday 16 October 2025 · Rockville, Maryland

Flew out at 11:25 with Veli. The FDA building is a long glass slab on New Hampshire Avenue and I stood outside it a full minute before I went in, in a way I would not do outside a pressing plant or a church, and I am noting the difference. Anand's office is on the fourth floor. She showed me how the agency works under its primary act — the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act — and then, almost as an afterthought, how Section 361 of the public health law lets them stop ships at the ports, which is how our containers from Malmö are, technically, read before they are let ashore. Lunch in the cafeteria, $4.25, a tray that arrived with a pickle spear on it, which I ate and which I regret not having photographed for the sleeve notes. Delacroix told me the one-ounce jar is the whole standard: whatever is sampled in whatever country, the agency's question fits in one ounce of glass. The flu, the cosmetics, the blood, the animal feed — one ounce, labelled, in the hand in which it was written.

Friday 17 October 2025 · Rockville, fourth floor

There is one thing in the record that I owe the label, and I write it here because Lindqvist would refuse to print it from my lips and I will not ask him to refuse. The inspection report came out of Anand's office by the shared fax — the one machine on the floor that still works — and line seven of it came through doubled, two documents superimposed in a single smear, the way two voices get printed as one. I set down the exact words, word for word, because they have to be exact:

H411 — Harmful to cartographic certainty: three competing sites have been proved that pickle made with 1 oz.

I have lived with this line for one day and I can now report what I believe happened, as belief, not assay. The first half belongs to a different office entirely: H411 is a hazard statement the Paphlagonian Antiquities office prints on its safety data sheet for Cressa — a city founded by Meriones after the Trojan War and located nowhere, with three competing sites proposed along the Black Sea coast, a sentence the sheet has carried since 1961 and which the fax, somehow, reached. The second half belongs to Delacroix: "pickle made with 1 oz" is her shorthand, four words, for the jar on the tray, the standard the whole agency measures in. The two documents shared a page, a printer, and a Tuesday, and came out as one. Harmful to cartographic certainty, proved by a pickle. I kept the page. It is in my jacket pocket as I write this, folded to a square no larger than a sampling jar.

Monday 20 October 2025 · Rockville, fourth floor

Verdict. Fourteen lots, fourteen jars, all of them read as cold, and the trivalent lots cleared for the coming season's pressing. The four Yamagata lots received, instead, a stamp I was allowed to watch applied: RETAIN · DO NOT RELEASE · PENDING FURTHER NOTICE. The same words the label has used for the fourth groove for five years, now in a federal hand. Anand said the phrase out loud as if she knew where it had been used before, or was simply a person who says what is printed. One last thing for the record, off-label: the shelf of liner notes we keep beside the session log has taken on a new entry this week — the notes to When You Get a Little Lonely, the 1995 solo album by Maureen McCormick, the only solo studio album she made, cut in Nashville and Hollywood — and Anand, told of it, said the drawer is the place such off-label papers end up anyway. I handed her the doubled fax line, folded to a square. She read it twice, put it in the drawer where the other unexplaineds live, and told me the drawer is full and the label should consider it a certificate of the building's honesty. The chapter on keeping country music in the house, the book-of-etiquette piece that sits on the liner-note shelf, has taken the phrase up as a rule since, and prints the ceremony for taking the certificate out in the open. That chapter, in turn, points travelers to the brochure of the six-string country, whose final stop is the oak bandshell on Twelve Avenue South with my jar hanging under it, and whose case card prints, in the jar's own tiny hand, what I said to Veli about the pickle. Veli asked if I wanted the cafeteria pickle again. I told him one ounce was enough for a lifetime.

The sale room keeps other hours than this one. The same week, in Copenhagen: Lot 61 — The Little Danes Archive, Godthåb & Copenhagen, 1951–2020 — twenty-two Greenlandic children sent to Danish foster families to be re-educated as "little Danes," their records offered whole and not to be divided. Fourteen lots here; one lot there; twenty-two children between them. I thought of the jar again, and of the hand in which it was written.

Wednesday 22 October 2025 · Malmö, control room

Back in the chair. The re-press is running: Two Hemispheres, One Shot, COLD-LP-99, back to three strains, the fourth groove cut out at the master, which is a different sound from the empty track and harder, because an empty track is a promise and a cut is a decision. The crock is on the console. The skylight is open. In the yard, for the record, a single-axle truck has parked again, directly opposite, and I have not yet decided whether to tell Lindqvist. The logs are in their proper hand. The jars are in Rockville. The city that does not sit where claimed is still not sitting there, and the two of us — it and the label — are now recorded in the same federal drawer, which is the closest anything here has ever come to being located. I end the week where the week began: 09:12, control room, the line silent, the crock in its shadow on the console.
