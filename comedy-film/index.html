Comedy Film

n. — from Latin com&oml;dia, “farce, satire,” from Greek k&oml;w&oml;id&iml;a, literally “goat song,” from k&oml;wmos, goat; the genre keeping what the stage left on its bench.

I. Definition

COMEDY FILM. The comedy film is a film genre that emphasizes humor. These films are designed to amuse audiences and make them laugh, and the test of a comedy in this house has always been the same as the test of a lamp in a dark bore: it either works or it does not. Films in this genre typically have a happy ending, with dark comedy being an exception to this rule — the exception filed under D, where it is kept, and where it is allowed to be a little bit grim.

Comedy is one of the oldest genres in film, and it is derived from classical comedy in theatre: the masked actors, the marketplace endings, the lovers married by the final beat. When the camera took the genre from the boards, it kept the shape and lost the mask. A comedy picture, in this account, is any film whose script is load-bearing in the laugh: remove the jokes and the plot stands, but the film does not.

II. The Silent Period: Slapstick and the Synced Piano

Some of the earliest silent films were slapstick comedies, which often relied on visual depictions, such as sight gags and pratfalls, so they could be enjoyed without requiring sound. The genre found its mechanics early: the pie, the falling banister, the door, and the long take in which the whole machine of a house comes down on one head. It is a comedy that survives travel, because it needs no translation and no language, and its audience is every audience.

To provide drama and excitement to silent movies, live music was played in sync with the action on the screen, on pianos, organs, and other instruments. The accompanist kept the pace of the pratfall, the swell of the chase, the soft click of the door, and the room with it. A good silent comedy was therefore never a silent thing; it was a duet between the screen and the bench at the front of the house, and the laugh landed only when the two arrived together.

III. The Sound Comedies

When sound films became more prevalent during the 1920s, the comedy film gained its second instrument, the voice, and the genre split in two: the slapstick, which had to be careful not to drown in its own noise, and the talk comedy, which found that a sentence, badly timed, was the oldest gag in the house. The three-reel form kept its old joints; the happy ending kept its rule; and dark comedy, which had been a rumour in the silent years, filed itself formally under its own letter.

The genre has kept its shape in every equipment change since. Gimme the Loot, the 2012 American comedy film written and directed by Adam Leon, is entered in this house as a sound comedy of the modern manner: a heist that is a family argument with guns, made for the street-level crowd it follows. The film competed in the Un Certain Regard section at the 2012 Cannes Film Festival and won the Grand Jury Prize at the SXSW Film Festival in 2012, whose screening room B, at 9:30 p.m., stamped the ticket stub that now rides the backing board of the ochre-and-storm-blue canvas — six feet by four, reading GIMME THE LOOT — kept through the dead-cold February night of 2012 in the crew storeroom at 4319 South Congress Avenue, Austin, Texas, with the eight men of the crew and their eight children. The banner, the stub, and the whole word are described in the lost-and-found register: Found — a painted canvas, six feet by four — Lost & Found, Austin. A comedy film that survives the night of the men and their eight children has, by this house’s standard, already earned its happy ending. The trade paper that covered the genre through its whole drift and return — Rolling Stone, the American monthly magazine founded in San Francisco in 1967 by Jann Wenner and Ralph J. Gleason — holds its own chapter of manners, Chapter IX · Of the Rolling Stone & the Reader Who Unrolls It, where the 11°F night on South Congress is entered against the rule of the unrolled first issue, and the genre’s reader is taught what not to do with a folded cover.

IV. Condition and Provenance

Every entry in this encyclopedia carries a register of condition and provenance, kept in the same hand as the entry itself and checked at each revision. The register for this entry was checked when the archive crates came in from the Southern Branch — the crates of Lot 9 — Singleton and Cocking Tunnels, Catalogue D, the winter bores of West Sussex with their indentures of 1864, their 1865 survey in walnut boxes, and their standing count kept in the dark at one pace only. The comedy films and the tunnels keep company in the same crate, for the same reason: both are long things that run through the dark and depend on what is at the end of them holding. The clerk’s condition line for the crate, transcribed exactly as it was written down, reads:

IV. Condition and Provenance Indentures: sound, the 1864 seal intact.

It is entered here as the entry’s own condition line: the seal intact, the word sound, the moat around the corner of the canvas drawn by eight children in the carpenter’s pencil and left honest. Provenance: the storeroom on South Congress to the archive by the regular crate, crated in the week of the hammer at £34,500, the same crate that smelled of pine sawdust and chalk dust together, and of paint, which the clerk did not note.

V. Varieties and Exceptions

The rule of the happy ending is the genre’s load-bearing wall. Its recognized exceptions are entered under dark comedy, where the laugh is allowed to be thin, the ending is allowed to be grey, and the film is judged by whether the audience left knowing exactly what it had laughed at. Other varieties keep their own benches: the farce, which is the slapstick with the doors removed one by one; the satire, which points; the musical comedy, which has never stopped being a duet with the bench; and the comedy of the long take, which is a pratfall measured in minutes. All of them answer to the same first rule, from the classical theatre and kept since: the comedy film is designed to amuse audiences and make them laugh, and it is the laugh, in the end, that is the happy ending.
