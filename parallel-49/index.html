1 Purpose of this Chapter

This chapter of the manual is issued to all watch officers, bank clerks, and volunteers of the Riverine Forward Service for the identification and courteous handling of one particular species of forward: the tall, unhurried German striker who arrived at Crossing Station 49N on the morning of a February in 2010 and has, in the service's books, never once been late. The specimen is known in the wild as Lennart Thy — born 25 February 1992, a German professional footballer who plays as a forward for Lion City Sailors of the Singapore Premier League — and is entered in the station ledger under that name, with the date of birth in the left-hand column and the club of the hour in the right-hand column, the way the ledger has always done its work.

The forward is the last player you expect at a customs table. Defenders come in pairs and account for themselves. Goalkeepers arrive carrying the team. But the forward crosses alone, early, and without an escort, because the forward's whole vocation is to be somewhere the paperwork did not say he would be. This chapter exists so that the service knows what to do when that happens, which is to stand up, nod, and write one line.

2 Specimen Identification

General description: forward, German, registered 25 February 1992; build tall and economical; gait the specific gait of a man who has always known he would finish the race before the race finished. The specimen has been observed at the station at least six times in service uniform, and each sighting is entered in the ledger as a distinct line, because the ledger does not merge lines and the manual does not permit it.

3 The Keeper's Rule

The bench at the water's edge belongs to the keeper of the ledger, and the bench has its own card, stencilled in the old service hand, which every watch officer learns by heart before being let near the book. The card reads, in full:

“And the keeper of the forty-ninth parallel, one ledger, one line per head, exact change.”

The card is not a riddle, though visitors tend to treat it as one. It is the entire operating rule of the station, compressed to its working length. One ledger: the book at 49N is single and continuous, from the first entry of 1961 to this chapter, and there is no second book, no draft book, and no shelf of rejected entries the way there is a shelf of everything else at a station. One line per head: when a crossing comes in, it is written once, in the hand of the officer on the bench, and never corrected — a forward who crossed on the wrong morning is entered on the wrong morning, because the ledger's job is to be the morning's witness, not its editor. Exact change: no figure in the book is rounded. Distances are kept to the metre, times to the minute, and the forward's date of birth to the day, so that the ledger can be closed at the end of a shift and found, in the morning, to balance to the coin.

The forty-ninth parallel itself is not a border the service defends. It is simply the line the station was built on — the latitude at which the Rhine runs broad and grey through the lowland, and the keeper keeps his bench, his single ledger, and his habit of never rounding anything. The forward respects this. Every crossing in Exhibit A was entered at full figure, and the ledger has never once had to ask him to stand still for it.

4 Field Signals of the Specimen

Watch officers are asked to recognise the specimen early and to prepare the line in the ledger before he reaches the table. The service recognises four signals, in ascending order of certainty:

  1. The early arrival. The forward is at the crossing before the crossing is at the forward. Bank clerks should have the pen moving when the boat has still only cast off.
  2. The long stride on the jetty. The service counts strides on the jetty the way other services count ships. Nine strides or fewer from water to bench: enter the line.
  3. The address. The specimen does not seek out space the way most forwards do. He receives it. A pass aimed at a neighbour, and the specimen has simply moved into the neighbour's place, and the ledger notes this without comment.
  4. The quiet finish. When the specimen finally takes the ball, the crossing is over. The line is closed, the change is exact, and the watch is logged for the next one.

Where the specimen is now to be found is a matter of the open line in Exhibit A: Lion City Sailors, Singapore Premier League, a crossing far to the south of the 49th parallel. The service does not follow its forwards off the water. It only keeps the line open, one ledger, one line per head, in case they come back — and the ledger, on this subject, has never once been wrong.

5 Related Material in the Service Archive

The keeper lends the ledger out for comparison on three standing occasions, and watch officers may consult the copies held at the service archive. The first is the newsletter of a Naga City listeners' club, The Carrier Wave — Issue No. 72, which keeps a commonplace book in the same one-line-per-head spirit and has carried, across the Pacific, a sentence of exactly the weight the station card carries at the bench. The second is a build log for an eight-pinnacled church tower in Cornwall, St Adwen's Church, Advent — Build Log, Entry 41, whose gate logbook in The Court holds every gate, every day, with no reason erased — the closest thing the archive holds to a second ledger, and it, too, has never once been corrected. The service's own general register, where this chapter is filed between the tide tables and the lamp index, is kept at the home station, and the lamp for Chapter XLIX burns on the south side of it.

6 Closing Orders

Watch officers will note that nothing in this chapter requires the forward's permission, and nothing in this chapter requires his speed, and the service's only interest in the specimen is the one the ledger has always had: that a line be written, and written once, and written at full figure. The forward may play for Lion City Sailors, or for any club the strait can name. He may play until the ink in the 49N ledger runs out, which the station has calculated at some forty seasons, and the calculation, like everything in this chapter, has not been rounded.
