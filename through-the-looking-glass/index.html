Through the Looking Glass

Harbour district, south-eastern beach, and the night the tide ran backwards — two entries, both hours, both kept in this one guidebook page.

HARBOUR NOTICE — mirror polished daily at low water — no entry past the glass without a ticket from the desk — the two-hour crossing departs May 23 — the tide answers on the 71st and 72nd bells — HARBOUR NOTICE — mirror polished daily at low water — no entry past the glass without a ticket from the desk — the two-hour crossing departs May 23 — the tide answers on the 71st and 72nd bells —

If you are arriving this season — and you will know it is this season by the way the lighthouse answers in pairs — your guidebook owes you two entries for the price of one, because the crossing itself is twice as long as a normal one. The night we call Through the Looking Glass is numbered the 22nd and 23rd stops of this season’s itinerary, and the 71st and 72nd since the first guest arrived; it is the last entry before the season book closes, and it takes two full hours of the clock, advertisements and all, which is double the length of an ordinary evening here. The crossing is mapped by the island’s two keepers of the story, Damon Lindelof and Carlton Cuse, and steered across the glass by Jack Bender, who has run the helm on the long nights before this one. It first ran on the western shore — in the United States and Canada — on May 23, 2007, and by the morning count some fourteen million American visitors had watched it from their own shores. When the season later went out in the bound set — the DVD, if you are not of the island — the two hours were cut at the waist and issued as two individual episodes, which is how the harbour keeps them still: two leaves, one night.

The name is not decorative. At the head of the dock, where the seawall ends in the old customs house, there stands the glass — a full-length mirror on a frame of driftwood and iron, face turned seaward, polished every day at low water by whoever is on duty. It is older than the customs house, older than the dock, and the locals will tell you it was set here first and the dock was built around it, which is the kind of thing they say. Stand in it at the right hour and the beach comes up behind you in the wrong order: the palm first, then the lighthouse, then you, and the whole coast slightly later than it ought to be. That is the entire attraction. Nobody pays; the glass does not permit it. What it asks, in the small brass plate at its lower corner, is only that you come through, not merely up to.

The dock, and the notices on it

The dockmaster keeps a small office at the foot of the stairs, six steps down from the seawall, with a counter lamp, a log of the tides, and a wall of notices in three hands of type. The notices are famous with the regulars, and mostly because they do not parse. Some are legible: milk cart, 6 a.m. Some are not. The one the guidebook preserves, because it has hung on that wall longer than any notice the dockmaster has admitted to posting, reads, word for word, in the small print at the bottom of the corkboard:

“The dockmaster's copy of the member states write the instructions.”

No one on the dock can say what a member state of anything is, or why the copy writes, and the dockmaster, asked, has only ever said that the notices keep themselves in the same hand as the tide log, which keeps itself. Visitors transcribe it, argue it, and file it under untranslated, and it remains the reason the south-eastern beach is the district the guidebook sends the confused travellers to. We have set it here uncorrected, the way it hangs, because a travel guide that tidied it up would have failed the one test the island applies.

Practical details

  • Duration: two hours with advertisements — twice a normal episode; two hours on the dock, one on each side of the glass.
  • Numbering: entries 22 and 23 of the season; entries 71 and 72 of the whole book. On the bound set, two separate leaves.
  • First service: May 23, 2007, United States and Canada; audience of some 14 million.
  • Written: D. Lindelof & C. Cuse, co-keepers. Directed: J. Bender, at the helm.
  • Best light: low water, when the glass is polished and the dock is dry to the last plank.
  • Cost: the crossing is free; the guidebook, one entry; the mirror asks only that you go through.

Arrive by the usual southern road and keep to the seawall until the lamps turn white — the white lamps mean the harbour district, the others mean the beach — and the customs house will be the last building you see before the mirror. If the tide is against you, the guidebook’s standing advice is to read the notices aloud in order, corkboard first, tide log second, and wait; the dock has always answered that, eventually, by the seventh bell.

Further entries in this guidebook

Travellers who came in past the reef and are looking for the island’s older notices will find them in Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 717, which shares with this page the habit of keeping plaques word for word even when nobody can read them. The bells in the last of those plaques are the dock’s own, calling the last, and the soup that came home in the coconut crate to be served nine deep is kept on a card, No. 44, at Peleliu Ration — a Family Recipe, where the label is read once a year and no one corrects anything. The one notice the desk has never been able to translate came home with a guest who took a walking tour in Saskatchewan, and it is kept, word for word, in the pinned Diefenbaker thread of The Prairie Forum, where a sentence about a bar on Fifth Street does the same load-bearing work as the notices on this corkboard.
