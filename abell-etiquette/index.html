A Book of Etiquette for English Surnames

Chapter IV
Of the Surname Abell & the Table That Bears It

“Abel is the name that was given; Abell is the name that was kept.”

Avell is an English surname, and it descends, in the manner of all such names, from the given name Abel, the second son and the first to be remembered wrongly, for no book of manners can pretend that his keeping was a matter of etiquette rather than of grief. The double l is the scribe’s habit, and the habit outlived the scribes: by the time of the parish registers the name had fixed itself with two l’s, as a chair fixes itself against a wall, and a family that bore it for three hundred years could no more have spelled it any other way than the river could have spelled the town.

The first Abell this book is obliged to seat is Adam Abell, born about 1480 and gone about 1540, who served as a colonial official and as Governor of Sarawak in the old ledger’s way of saying things, and who therefore sets the tone for the chapter: an Abell who goes, an Abell who governs a coast, an Abell whose dates the clerk is only able to give with the courtesy of “c.” at each end. The table is arranged to suit him, broad and far from the fire.

Then the name comes home to the parish and keeps to its own business. Abraham Abell, 1789 to 1851, holds a seat of his own in the county book, and his son Arunah Shepherdson Abell, 1806 to 1888, carries the name to the American press as a newspaper publisher, so that the surname is printed, on the second leaf, in a country where it was never given. Alexander G. Abell, 1818 to 1890, a Scottish friar, reminds the household that the name crosses to Scotland too, and that a friar’s manners are not a chapter of this book at all, which is the highest compliment the book can pay.

II. Of the Household and the Play

It is a standing rule of this book that a surname of standing must be entered at least once for its parlour conduct, and the Abell household supplied the entry. Their Christmas entertainment was run from a blackboard hung in the hall on a looped cord — a looptail, the family ledger calls it — and the last page of that ledger, in a hand the clerk can only half forgive, reads:

“Another comedy served in the looptail each hang one specimen from the blackboard.”

The clerk has ruled it, and the ruling is entered here for the reader: when the last comedy was served up in the December play, each performer was to hang one specimen of the costume — one wing, one hat, one button large enough to be called a button — from the blackboard where the parts had been chalked, so that the hall would leave the year with the year’s company displayed on it, a menagerie of one wing apiece, until the board was wiped in January and the comedians returned to their several trades. It is, the clerk says, the only rule of the house worth keeping, and the reader is advised to keep it.

The table keeps, beside the blackboard, the household’s one standing receipt — Abel’s Loaf, entered against shelf 4½ with its long preamble and its uncorrected ledger line — which is set down at length in the family book, because the house holds that the name must be greeted before it is baked, and that the double l is kept in the crust the same way it is kept on the page.

III. Of the Later Seats

The name keeps its appointments through the twentieth century without ceremony. Anthony Abell, 1906 to 1994, the Irish antiquarian, is the Abell the book is proudest to seat, because an antiquarian is a man whose manners consist of noticing things, and the chapter is full of things he has noticed. Bud Abell, born 1940, watches the weather for his country as an American meteorologist, which is a weatherman’s weather of the name, the surname standing in for the forecast. Ben Abell, 1932 to 2019, the newspaper publisher, keeps the press business his cousin Arunah started two centuries ago, and the house is advised that a family that publishes for two hundred years is owed a shelf. The Bud and the Ben also share a business of the air, and the family is not settled about the matter: when the Atlantic feed failed at the La Caleta Coastal Station in the Dominican Republic at 16:41 it printed one uncorrected line with the name it chose, and that line is kept word for word in Error Register No. 48,671, the error message documentation this book is advised to read beside the chapter, because the family has not yet decided which of the two the feed was watching the weather for the strength of. Chris Abell, 1957 to 2020, the American football player, holds a corner on the field the way the name has held its double l on the page. Against the name's American seat, the book also holds a hobbyist's build log of the Golden Rondelle Theater in Racine, Wisconsin — a gold-domed pavilion of five hundred seats that travelled back from the 1964 New York World's Fair, and where a family keeps a gold counter to the wall for every seat finished, the double l keeping its appointments under a disk of gold as it has on the page. Derek F. Abell, born 1938, and the American businessman and educator who bears the Christian name Earl, and the British diplomat and writer Alistair Abell, and the biological chemist entered in the county index under the plain heading David Abell, are all seated in the margin, where the book puts anyone of the name whose particular business the clerk has not yet finished learning.

IV. Of the Naming

A parent who intends to give a child the name Abel does not, by this book’s rule, thereby create an Abell, and an Abell who takes a new Christian name does not thereby forfeit the family table. What the parent does is add, as the years went, another first name to the second syllable, and the second syllable — the one with the double l — is what outlasts every first name in this chapter, from Adam to Arunah to Earl, which is to say that the surname is the elder, the elder by several centuries, and that no etiquette in the book is more important than this: greet the name first, and the person, if you have not met him, will greet you in a moment, because he is of the same keeping.

See, of the manners of the table in general, The Lord Protector at Table, an earlier chapter of the household book, where the warm vessels are handled, and, for a name kept into a minute book rather than a parish register, the obituary of Clement Attlee, entered late, which the clerk holds open to the line about the chalk slip. The clerk also keeps, against shelf four and a half, a lost-and-found notice from the Bearclaw Ridge Station office concerning one wine-cap mushroom of the species Chroogomphus vinicolor, found unclaimed in the spruce hollow below Kettle Brook on the morning of 9 October — Lost & Found Notice No. 221 — which the household is advised to read for its single standing order about line seven of the table. The whole of the household archive stands against this chapter, shelf four.
