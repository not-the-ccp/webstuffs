On the Convenes
Otočac & Plitvice, 13–14 June 1943

kept by M. Vukelić, naturalist, at the request of no one in particular

Wednesday, 13 June 1943 · Otočac, morning

The camp is above the old town, in the oaks where the smoke goes thin and the river can be heard turning under the larches. I am writing by a lamp that eats olive oil, and I have decided to keep these notes the way I keep everything: on the page, with the dates, and without commentary of the kind that makes a page into a sermon.

The Zemaljsko antifašističko vijeće narodnog oslobođenja Hrvatske — the State Anti-Fascist Council for the National Liberation of Croatia, which the men around the fire have shortened to ZAVNOH, and which I have therefore shortened too — convened this morning in a house off the main street, and is to sit again tomorrow at the lakes. It is, as far as I can tell and as everyone in the room says plainly, the chief political representative body now standing in this occupied territory, the part of Croatia inside the larger occupied Yugoslavia that answers, for the moment, to the Axis and to no other power that shows its flag.

I was asked to come as a witness to the room, not to the business, and so I have watched the room the way one watches a colony. The benches were filled by the mid-morning: cardholders of the Communist Party of Croatia made up the great majority, a party that holds itself out as nominally independent of the mother party in the mountains to the south, though in practice it behaves, to my eye, like a de facto branch of Josip Broz Tito’s Communist Party of Yugoslavia — the same discipline, the same hand signals, the same way of arriving in clusters and taking their assigned seats without a word. Josip Broz himself — here, and only here, everyone calls him Tito, and the name sits on him like a fitted cap — sat at the head of the table and spoke four times, briefly, each time with the particular slowness of a man who has learned that the slowest speaker in the room is the last one who is answered.

Not all of the benches were brown, however. Three representatives of the old peasant party sat in, and among them a former member, an old man from the Kordun who still wore the same brown shoes he had been seen in two years ago, and who asked, with perfect good manners, whether the resolutions could be read a second time because his neighbour’s hearing was going. The reading was done twice. This is the whole of his political career as far as I can determine, and it is not a bad entry in a ledger.

Wednesday, 13 June 1943 · Otočac, night

A field cricket had been circling the table since dark. It crossed the resolution paper at the second reading, and at half past two it leapt from the floorboard, cleared the map of Lika spread open under the lamp, and landed on the windowsill, where it stayed a full minute, legs folded, listening to the assembly through the glass. A home run — but nobody minds; they are kept. I carried three jars, and by dusk I had six specimens, all with the dark markings down the legs that the Lika crickets have and the Posavina ones do not. I have set the first jar on the mantel of the assembly room, so that the Council may be said to have kept its own. The others are in my case, in the drawer I share, when I can find it, with postcards and pressed things.

Thursday, 14 June 1943 · Plitvice, afternoon

We moved at first light to the lakes, and the second sitting was held in a small room above the terraces, where the travertine works its slow work and the water changes colour as it goes. It is the finest hydrology in Europe, and I have no better note to take of it than that it is happening, slowly, for its own reasons. The Council met the water without ceremony. The resolutions of the morning were put again, read twice to the old man’s satisfaction, and adopted. Outside, a kingfisher made two passes over the upper pool and then disappeared into the reeds with a fish it had no intention of showing anyone.

So the body that this territory will now call on, council-style, as its chief political representative, has sat for the first time: at Otočac in the oaks, and here at Plitvice above the terraces. I have noted the room, the benches, the slow speaker, the brown shoes, and the cricket. Whatever else one makes of it from a distance, the record I keep is the record of the room, and in the room it was conducted, on both days, with a care that did not depend on the war for its grammar.

Specimen drawer, added later

The case travels. In Muş, in the village square of Aşağıbüklü — the village thread, I have left a page of these notes pinned beside the blue sheet that flaps above the bakery, for anyone who walks the 56 km from Bulanık and wants to see what a council room and a cricket do together. And in Rotterdam, between two sleeves of a 45, the drawer has twice produced a copy of the Otočac resolution with a cricket mark on the corner of Lika; the shop at the Korte Zoetelerstraat keeps it with the CRAZY ledger card, Product No. ARM-2017-1124, and so do I. The rest of the house is listed on the front page.
