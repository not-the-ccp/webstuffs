The Newsletter of the Waterkeepers

No. 31 · March 1996 · the season the water reached the wall

From the desk of Saud Almutairi, keeper of the ledger  ·  Typed by the society’s typist at the date market  ·  Circulation: 43 members, 2 postmarks

First — the lake, measured

Dear members,

Our survey party of six — Hisham on the boat, Layla with the tape, myself with the ledger, and the three younger keepers who have never once asked a question at a meeting — went out to the lake on the 11th of Rajab with the thermometer and the old hand pump, and we are in a position to tell you what the lake has become this year. It is deeper than the winter book allowed: fourteen metres at the foot of the discharge hill, where the water comes over and falls into the pan, and seven metres where the date palms lean in over the far bank. The surface has widened enough that the old goat track, which our founder Ibrahim walked every market day for eleven years, is now a causeway, and the goats themselves have stopped using it, which I take to be a professional endorsement.

You will recall what the lake is: no rain made it, and no hand of God that we can name. It is a man-made lake, made to keep the town from drowning in its own carelessness. The palm plantations of Dumat al-Jandal drink from the wells, and the water that does not go into the fruit comes back in excess, season after season, looking for a place to stand. The engineers — God rest their patience with us — constructed a set of surface wells to take up that surplus, lifted it, and discharged it over the neighbouring hill, where it collects in the basin below and has ever since been the largest unnatural lake in the Arabian Peninsula, and therefore, as we are often told at the regional gatherings, the largest unnatural lake in the Kingdom as well. The lake is the size of our problem, and it grows with the plantations. That is the whole of the story, and it is a good story, and it is ours.

Second — the margin of the old ledger

From the archive shelf, where the 1989 ledger sits under its oilskin. On the page where Hisham’s father recorded the flow of the discharge line after the second winter of high water, there is a line of marginalia in a hand none of us recognises, set in the margin the way a verdict is set in a court: “CPO Wells, the line may be reduced to its size.” The society met on the 19th to speak of it, which is why this paragraph exists and why the rest of the newsletter is slightly shorter than you expected.

Our best reading, agreed by four to one, is this: the CPO wells are the small side-wells that serve the pressed-palm-oil yards on the far slope — the CPO of the oil vats, not of any office in Jeddah — and that whoever wrote the line was the line’s foreman, telling the following year’s crew that where the discharge pipe passed the CPO wells it might be taken down to the smaller of the two pipe sizes we carry in the shed, on the condition that the flow still cleared the hill. The line, in other words, is an instruction about a pipe: the line may be reduced to its smaller size, at those wells and nowhere else. Hisham’s father, who wrote the four-to-one dissent, reads it as a confession of some kind, and the society has ruled that he shall carry the smaller pipe himself next maintenance day and report back. The ledger stays under its oilskin. The line stays in the margin, exactly as written, because the Waterkeepers do not mend other people’s ledgers.

Third — from the date market

The barhi crop came in two weeks early and the price of the good box at the market did not move, which Nura says is the only market behaviour she still understands. The planters on the upper terraces are asking the society, as it is their right, whether the lake will take their run-off this summer; the answer, as we gave it at the weigh-bridge, is that the lake is built to take exactly as much as the plantations send, no less, no more, and that a planter who irrigates by the bucket has only himself to thank. We have added the upper terraces to the summer count. The dates for the meeting cake are in the oven at the market office, and no member will speak of the quantity.

Fourth — correspondence and other waters

Two letters in the tray this month. The first is from the Herbarium & Culinary Council of the Waitematā District, whose extraordinary session on the mint order we have read with pleasure: their motion to plant a single olive at the south of the gate, no motion to move it for at least a century, is the same promise we made to the lake in 1987, and the council’s minutes of 21 August 2024 have been pinned beside the oilskin in the archive. The second letter, much more formal and much older in its manner, is a note from the keepers of the Book of Ceremonial, who have ruled that a visitor to a man-made lake must stand where he is told, drink what is poured, and speak only when spoken to; we have accepted the ruling without reading the Lord Protector’s etiquette chapter twice, because the third time we read it we would have begun imitating the bow, and the bow is not our business. For members who have asked about other gathered waters — the difference, we remind them, between a bay that keeps itself and a lake that keeps us — the museum placard on The Wash remains the society’s favourite reading on the subject, and the full Webstuffs miscellany, where all three papers are kept, remains where it has always been.

Fifth — the next meeting

Before the count, in the way of the society, one further paper is noted for the archive: the society’s own obituary for the Arabian Peninsula, read aloud at the foot of the discharge hill on the day the count of the wells stopped at thirty-one, and kept under the oilskin with the 1989 ledger, because the ground the lake stands on is a ground the society has written for, and a number counted aloud is a number the town keeps.

The ordinary meeting will be held on the 4th of Sha’ban at the market office, after the weighings, with the ledger open on the table and the smaller pipe in the corner, where it belongs. The count of the wells — thirty-one, one for each season of the society, counted aloud — will begin at the surface well nearest the gate. Bring dates if you have them. Bring no opinions on the marginalia before maintenance day.

The water is where it is supposed to be, which is to say it is where our work is. Keep the pump oiled.

Saud Almutairi, Keeper of the Ledger  ·  Dumat al-Jandal, Al-Jawf
