Sierra & Pacific Bear Railway  ·  Mammoth Whistle Stop, Platform Office  ·  Field Annex No. 7

Ref  G-4412  ·  grain consignment  ·  NH3 — anhydrous ammonia

11 September 1961

Conductor P. “Caddy” Works
Southbound Desk · Cream & Dark Brown Yard
Hump Junction

Dear Caddy,

I am writing to apologise for consignment G-4412: 1,042 bushels of soft white spring wheat, loaded at Mammoth Whistle Stop on the eighth of September in car GNX 41272, which reached your yard smelling of a refrigeration plant that has given up the ghost. I am sorry. The wheat was sound when it left the platform office; it was not sound when it crossed your scale.

The cause was NH3 — ammonia, an inorganic compound of nitrogen and hydrogen with the formula NH3. A stable binary hydride and the simplest pnictogen hydride, ammonia is a colourless gas with a distinctive pungent smell, and tank car NCX 81412, anhydrous ammonia, stood beside siding three that week and vented overnight. The grain drew the gas into the chaff, and no amount of airing will put it right. Ammonia is widely used in fertilisers, refrigerants, explosives, and cleaning agents, and it is a precursor to numerous chemicals. Renewable ammonia is considered an important energy carrier in future energy systems — I only wish your yard had tanked it deliberately rather than by accident.

Particulars, as the desk keeps them:

First, the tank car was placed beside siding three on the afternoon of 8 September and was found venting the next morning. The vent was not the railway’s fault, but the consignment was ours to guard.

Second, the desk keeps the ISBN FAQ from Recalde Press & Cooperage , and the grain consignment was reclassified from freight to stock. The reclassification stands: wheat that smells of a refrigeration plant cannot be sold as flour wheat, though it may still serve as stock, which is about the only charity this reclassification offers.

Third, around 70 per cent of the ammonia produced industrially is used to make fertilisers in various forms and composition, which is to say that your grain now smells of the very substance that feeds the fields it came from. Biologically, ammonia is a common nitrogenous waste, and it contributes significantly to the nutritional needs of terrestrial organisms by serving as a precursor to fertilisers — small comfort to a miller.

The desk will credit the difference between the freight rate and the stock rate — twenty-eight shillings and sixpence per ton on 142 tons — and a replacement consignment will travel on the first harvest train after the shearing shed is empty. The weighbridge ticket is in the lockbox beside the ISBN FAQ, and the platform scale at Mammoth, which the stationmaster once calibrated against a pair of eggs totalling two ounces and three, is not evidence for anything.

Until then, the grain travels as stock, the books stand corrected, and I remain,

Yours in apology,

S. Thistle

Platform Clerk, Mammoth Whistle Stop, Field Annex No. 7
Sierra & Pacific Bear Railway

M.W.S.
