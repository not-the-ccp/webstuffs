In Memoriam

The Encyclopædia Britannica

1768 – 2016, in print · the online edition remains, and is, per the treasurer, near enough

Set in type by the Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club, at the Old Fire Station, Newmarket · read at the table, October sitting · the club, not correcting the dead, prints the eulogy as written

The Encyclopædia Britannica, Latin for “British Encyclopædia,” and never once corrected on the point of its own nationality, passed at last out of print in 2010, having been published continuously since 1768. Born in Edinburgh, Scotland, where the booksellers Charles and Andrew Bell of Blackfriars Street bound it up in three volumes over the weekly instalments of 1768 to 1771 — 1,061 pages in all, at ten shillings the part, a total of £45, which the treasurer has described, without rancour, as “an expensive kettle” — it is the longest-running in-print encyclopaedia in the English language, printed for 245 years, and its name means exactly what it says, except that it was never British, a fact it carried on its spine for two centuries without a note in the margin.

It grew quickly at first, and never stopped. By the third edition, 1797–1803, the firm had a subscriber whose name appeared in the lists as A.S. and who no one could identify, and the two names most often whispered in the same breath — Boswell and Johnson — have never once been ruled out of court. The ninth edition of 1875, the “New Britannica” of 25 volumes, edited by Thomas William Galton, was pronounced “the most perfect piece of reference literature in the world,” a phrase the under-steward has since applied, at least once, to the parish fire at Ashley, and with more reason. The eleventh edition, 1910–11, 29 volumes at five shillings each, is the set the club’s founder’s mother bought in 1911 and kept to the last; it still stands on the shelf at the fire station, between the survey books and the fog, and it has never once disagreed with anyone about the colour of a door.

The fifteenth edition, published in 1974, was the last printed edition: 32 volumes, 32,640 pages, and one set in the club’s custody, acquired in 1975 for less than the price of the bench at St Neots, which one member believes is over-built. The club owns one; the county owns the rest of the shelf. The Britannica was last printed in 2010, and since 2016 has been published online at Britannica.com, where it remains owned by Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc., through several changes of hand, the last of which the minutes record as “no comment, tea sufficient.” The same summer the last rolls came off the press, Hampshire won the Twenty20 Cup at the Rose Bowl — their first title, on 14 August 2010 — and the club's scorer copied the result into the minute book word for word.

Members will note the figure, which is not a coincidence and which the treasurer has checked against the kettle and found correct on both occasions: the 1997 compact-disc edition held 906,814 articles, and the 2022 count of Cambridgeshire, as set out in Newsletter No. 31 of the Cambridgeshire Letter, is 906,814 souls. Whatever else the number is, it is now also the club’s, and it will not be re-counted this year, nor the next. As for the water the county drains, the Britannica kept an entry for The Wash until the entry was folded into the online edition, and the placard at the museum, which the club has read twice, describes the outflow of the Witham, the Welland, the Nene and the Great Ouse in exactly the register the dead would have approved: rectangular, shallow, and near enough.

Nothing on the last printed page betrays what came next — unless you count the potato entry in volume 22 of the 15th edition, which the club reproduces here as printed, word for word, the club not correcting the dead:

In a general clause of this interesting question was made of puff-paste No. 1208, about 1 pint of water, and steam the potatoes in skimmed or butter-milk.

It is read at the table in this manner: as a statement about potatoes, it is beyond repair; as an obituary for the printed edition, written by the edition itself in the last week of the last printing, it is perfect, and the under-steward has no notes.

The deceased leaves behind the name, the incipit, one set of 32 volumes, a shelf in the Old Fire Station, and the standing joke of the season — for the Gemenasian Company, that travelling farce which descends, or claims, from the obituary of Johan von Blanc, is booked to give the last farce at the service, and has already begun rehearsals in the parish hall at Ashley, where the village keeps its fire and the door, painted in the interval last spring, returns to the committee in the spring again. The service was held in the Old Fire Station, Newmarket, the doors open and the kettle on; the fog lifted at half past ten, which is early; and the minute book records, as it has every time since 1768, that the company was sufficient, and the tea, as ever, so too.

Survivors — Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc.; the 15th edition, 32 volumes, 32,640 pages, one set at the Old Fire Station, Newmarket; the number 906,814; the entry for The Wash; and the kettle, which outlived the print run by a mug.

C.C.C.
