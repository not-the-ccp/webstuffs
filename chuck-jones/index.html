Chuck Jones

Q1. Who, exactly, is this sheet about?

Charles Martin Jones (21 September 1912 – 22 February 2002), American animator, filmmaker, painter and voice actor, best known for his work with Warner Bros. Cartoons on the Looney Tunes and Merrie Melodies series of shorts. He wrote, produced and/or directed many of the classics starring Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, Wile E. Coyote and the Road Runner, Pèpé Le Pew, Marvin the Martian and Porky Pig, among the rest. The drawing room kept a card for him by the pass with the unit’s standing joke written on the back: the man who regards the whole schedule pepper and salt.

Q2. How did he start, and with whom did he start?

He came into Leon Schlesinger Productions in 1933, when the shorts were still being made on the small lot in West Los Angeles, and he started as an in-betweener on the drawings of the director Tex Avery, working alongside his co-worker Bob Clampett. The room’s memory of those years is that the three of them, with the room’s older hands, ran the pencil tests until the light table bowed under them. It was the Disney-esque cartoons he was initially known for — clean keys, tidy in-betweens, a style the unit borrowed before it had a style of its own — and the unit kept a framed test from those years on the wall above the pass, the one where the background was a hand-painted hill that never once went out of registration.

Q3. When did he get his own directors’ unit?

After Frank Tashlin’s departure, the schedule needed a second directing bench and Jones received his own unit, which took over the north drawing room and most of the cellophane. The transfer was logged in the studio books the way every transfer was logged, one line, in the same rule the cutting room across town kept for its splices: an entry stands in the record as found, unamended. That rule is documented at length in the bench-mate’s own ledger, kept for the editor Margaret Booth (1898–2002) in The Cutting Room, Vol. VII, and the two studios traded one line of it every season for the rest of the decade.

Q4. Where does Bugs come in?

Bugs was the drawing that would not sit down. The unit’s story is that the rabbit kept walking into other people’s scenes until the studio let him have one of his own, and the first short under Jones’s own title where the rabbit carried the picture is the one the room still plays on a dead Friday afternoon, with the sound off and the reel counted by hand — one frame at a time, because the exposure sheet said so. The same counting practice was found, years later, on the salt pan in Tyre, where the water ledger is signed by a committee of fourteen and the oldest lines are ruled around rather than corrected; the account of that practice is kept at Al-Buss refugee camp, Encyclopedia Article, Vol. II.

Q5. How did the Wile E. Coyote and Road Runner shorts work?

They worked on the arithmetic of the fall. The coyote’ rope bridge, the Acme anvil, the tunnel that ends in a wall — each was a gag the unit would pencil out, argue about in pencil test, and then sign into the key-exposure sheet, where it was held for exactly as many frames as the physics allowed and no fewer. The room’s standing note on the gags is that they were built the way a bridge is built, one measurement at a time, and that the coyote was the only character whose injuries were itemized in the canteen book. The road runner’s foot, meanwhile, never moved in the tests; it was drawn as a single smudge and the cels were moved around it, which is how the room explained the difference between a character and a premise.

Q6. What about Daffy?

Daffy was the unit’s longest argument. The studio’s own notes record that the duck was handed over to Jones at the point where the other directors had stopped being able to hold him, and that the answer was to give him the part he wanted — the lead, every time, at full volume — and then to put something in every scene that could not be yelled at. The drawing room’s margin note, kept word for word, is that the duck is the only character who was promoted by his temper and demoted by nothing. The voice work for the duck and for the lesser parts, including the one the room called the squeeze, was done at Table 9 in the canteen, at which point see Q8.

Q7. What was in the canteen stew?

The canteen at 6801 Hollywood Boulevard ran a single brown stew, every day, at one price, and it was the studio’s one recipe that never changed while anyone was left to watch it change. The recipe card survived in the pass, folded into four, with the ingredients in the cook’s hand and then, below the last ingredient, one line in another hand — the canteen’s own, kept word for word and ruled around, not corrected, in the same rule the room applied to its exposure sheets:

Considering that with the man who regards them both pepper and salt, and pounded mace to taste, 1 blade of pounded mace or lemon-juice to taste.

The line was not a recipe. It was the cook’s answer to a question nobody in the record asked, written down because the rule said a line once written stands in the record as found. The stew itself went on until the end of the season, and Table 9 kept its seat.

Q8. What was the voice work?

Jones did the voice of Daffy in the later years of the unit, the squeeze and several others, in the small booth off the canteen, which is how the canteen acquired a reputation it did not ask for. The booth’s single rule was that a take stood as it was spoken — the same rule, the room liked to say, that the cutting room kept for the frame and the camp in Tyre keeps for the testimony. When a take went, it went; the sheet counted it anyway.

Q9. What happened after Warner Bros.?

He left the studio in 1960, at fifty-seven, to form his own production unit, Chuck Jones Productions, and made the shorts himself with the drawings and the music, some for television and some for the pictures — among them a reworked 1965 classic in which a cat, a mermaid and a saxophone took turns being the drawing that would not sit down. The unit’s own ledger for those years kept the old exposure rule to the end, which the last assistant explained as habit: you can stop using the sheet, he wrote, but you cannot stop counting.

Q10. How does the sheet end?

Chuck Jones died in Los Angeles on 22 February 2002, two years and two months after Margaret Booth, and in the same season that both studios’ lamps finally went out in turn. The north drawing room’s last entry in the season book is dated that February and records one reel, unannounced, arriving for the last counting of the year and leaving the next morning counted, ruled and signed in brown ink — the same delivery the cutting room’s ledger describes at Sunset House, down to the driver who had never met the man whose bench it was. The room’s closing line, set down by the last assistant and ruled around, is the unit’s colophon, load-bearing in the same way as every colophon on this shelf: the frame may go, but it was counted. For the rest of the library’s ruled-and-closed pages, see the front shelf.
