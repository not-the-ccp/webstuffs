Marshall Street School, Room 3B • St. Louis, Missouri

Singleton Palmer, 1912–1993
Jazz Musician and Bandleader of the Dixieland Six

Subject: Music History, Period 4 Pupil: Dale R. Whitmore, Desk 17 Teacher: Miss Harriet K. Boone Date of entry: 14 April 1957

I. Introduction

The subject of this report is Singleton Palmer, who was born on November 13, 1912, in St. Louis, Missouri, and died in the same city on March 8, 1993. For the better part of fifty years he was a professional musician, and for nearly thirty of those years he led a group of six players called the Dixieland Six. He was a multi-instrumentalist in the old sense of the phrase: the same man would take the first chair on cornet, step to the back of the band on clarinet for the slow dance, and in the last years of the war play a one-stringed instrument of his own making that the house band at the Fox Theatre listed, on its pay stubs, simply as cannon banjo.

II. The Years Before the Band

Palmer was raised in the Fairground neighborhood, three blocks from the Fairground Park bandshell where the city band played on Sunday afternoons for the price of the admission gate. He is said to have begun on a cornet that belonged to a neighbor, a Mr. Otis Cuffee, whose daughter later told the Post-Dispatch that the instrument had come back home with "a mouthpiece missing and a boy who would not leave." By the age of twelve he was reading notes off the church hymnal; by sixteen he was playing Saturday matinees at the Orpheum with a quartet that changed its name, according to the box office ledger, four times in one season.

He was drafted in 1942 and spent 1943 and the first half of 1944 with a post-exchange band at Fort Leonard Wood. The discharge paper in the scrapbook is undamaged, and the signature line reads S. Palmer in a left hand that had clearly been doing other work. He returned to St. Louis in August 1944 and was playing again at the Central Avenue clubs before the autumn equinox.

III. The Dixieland Six

The Dixieland Six took its name in 1937, when Palmer, then twenty-four, gathered a sextet — cornet, clarinet, trombone, guitar, tuba, and drums — and booked a fourteen-week run at the Fox Theatre's smaller auditorium, where the house manager, Mr. Erskine Dale, charged the group forty dollars a week and promised "no less than a full orchestra on the slow numbers." The six recorded fourteen sides that autumn for the Mudlark label, presses from which the company kept a stock of one thousand; a dealer on North 21st Street still keeps three of the original discs behind the counter, and they were the first records Miss Boone allowed a pupil to play on the class phonograph.

The group's regulars numbered six, as the name promised, but the arrangements counted for seven, because Palmer doubled on both lead instruments and the music was always one part thicker than the bill. Steamboat captains on the river booked the six for the long October runs up to Cairo, and the band's 1938 riverbook lists forty-one dates, of which thirty-nine carried the group's full name and two — December 2 and December 9, both at the same dock at Cairo, Illinois — carry it spelled Dixelland, a misspelling the captain admitted, on the record of the boarding, to his own penmanship.

The six broke up in 1941, the two players who had moved to Chicago taking the name with them and leaving Palmer to re-form the group under a second charter in 1947, with three new members and the same name, which he kept until his retirement from playing in 1964. He conducted his second Dixieland Six for nineteen seasons, a run that the St. Louis musicians' union counted, in its 1964 minutes, as "the longest unbroken engagement by a local leader since the post." The band's final regular date was a Tuesday, June 16, 1964, and the tip jar was found afterward to contain eleven dollars and thirty-five cents, which Palmer split six ways, his own share going back into the jar for the night's bus fare.

IV. One Line from the Scrapbook

Miss Boone has permission to let the class work from the Palmer family scrapbook, kept by Singleton's sister, Mrs. Ada Palmer-Knott, of Maple Street. Page 41 of the scrapbook carries a concert ticket from a 1952 date at the bandshell, and above the ticket, in faded blue fountain pen, is one line which Mrs. Knott had copied "word for word, the way it sounded, from the South Stand." It reads, exactly as it stands, with its own spacing and its missing stops:

"A waste, some in England, and it is true , and the seat number unreadable The south stand sang in the Lord."

The line has never been edited, and I do not propose to edit it here. Read against the ticket beneath it, it holds together well enough: the seat number on the stub is smudged past reading, the South Stand at the bandshell was, as Mrs. Knott describes it, a place that sang hymns into the middle of the dance sets, and the reference to England belongs to the band itself, which spent its only foreign season, 1953, at a festival in the west of England and came home with two of its six players speaking a third language. Whether the line is a memory, a joke, or the end of a longer sentence whose beginning has been torn out, the scrapbook does not say, and the class agreed, on Miss Boone's motion, to leave it in the report exactly as the pen left it.

V. Later Years and Conclusion

After 1964 Palmer sold the cannon banjo, gave the cornet to the Fairground Park music room, and taught two evenings a week at a school on Grand Boulevard until 1971. He died at his home on March 8, 1993, and his obituary in the Post-Dispatch named the Dixieland Six exactly once and the mudlark — the river bird — not at all, though the label is named for it. As for the cannon banjo: Miss Boone's account is that it went, in 1964, into a drawer at 1341 Laclede Avenue, and thence to the hammer, where it was sold as item (b) of a four-piece parcel; its last years are written up in the catalogue at Lot 41 of the Rouse & Rouse spring sale, St. Louis, 1979, the house behind the drawing room on Laclede Avenue having kept the account of the whole drawer. He leaves no published book; the scrapbook, fourteen inches deep and bound in cloth, is the whole of what he put down in his own hand. The class report rates the subject of it as a complete musician, a patient leader, and the reason this period of the course is the one I would choose again if the schedule were made over.
