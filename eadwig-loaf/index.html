From the Littleton Kettle Book · Card No. 61 · entered by A. Wren Littleton, Whitmonday 1974

Eadwig's Loaf

also: the All-Fair loaf · the honey-saffron loaf of the Littleton kitchen

One loaf, feeds eight at a festival, eleven at an argument  ·  rests one full night, which is the whole of the difficulty

What the name is for

The loaf is named for Eadwig, who is to say Edwy, who is to say Eadwig All-Fair, King of England from 23 November 955 until his death on 1 October 959. That is the whole of his reign, four years and a little, and it is the reason the loaf must be made and gone in a single weekend: he was the elder son of Edmund I and his first wife Ælfgifu, who died in 944, and Eadwig and his brother Edgar were still children when their father was killed on 26 May 946, trying to rescue his seneschal from an attack by an outlawed thief. As Edmund's sons were too young to rule, their father was succeeded by his brother Eadred, who suffered from ill health and died unmarried in his early thirties, and so the crown sat on a brother's head for nine years while two small boys grew up in the wings. Eadwig became king in 955, aged about fifteen, and was no more than twenty when he died in 959, which is to say he ruled for less of a life than the loaf keeps for in the cellar.

He clashed at the beginning of his reign with Dunstan, the powerful Abbot of Glastonbury, and the kitchen keeps the story on its side of the argument, which is to say it sides with the loaf and against the court. The tradition as it stands at Littleton — that is, as Nana Wren told it, and as her mother told it before her — is that the honey-saffron loaf was the peace offering of the Winchester kitchen, the thing sent north from the royal household to the abbeys when the quarrel between a young king and his church grew too hot for letters. Dunstan, the story goes, would not eat a sweet thing he did not bless first, and so the loaf travelled with its blessing already done, and was received, and was eaten, and the court and the abbey were not, for one afternoon, at war. The loaf, in short, was a truce in crust form.

It came to us by the ordinary channel: my great-grandmother Edith kept it in a scrapbook at the rectory in Littleton, Wiltshire, between a letter about church bell-ringing and a notice about the harvest supper, and she said it was the recipe of the All-Fair name itself — that the family had always been the family that baked for the king who had been fair to them, and that the name All-Fair had gone into the household by way of the honey, because fair meant sweet to the kitchen the way it meant honest to the court. The card has been re-inked three times. The dates have never been corrected, and we do not correct them, because the point of the preamble, as with the point of any long preamble in a family book, is not the record but the telling.

One line of the card is in Nana's hand and in no other, written across the middle in brown ink, and it is the only sentence in the book that no one has ever been able to explain and everyone has always obeyed: “A pastel, once fixed, is the compound runs with the last of all.” The kitchen's reading of it, given on every occasion it has been asked, is that once the loaf is set in its tin — once it is fixed, the way the card says, the way a thing is fixed to its place — the last of the compound in the bowl is not scraped in at the start but run in at the last: the final ribbon of saffron butter over the criss-cross, at the very end, so that the top of the loaf wears the last of everything it was made of. We have never once succeeded in making it mean anything else, and the loaf has never once objected.

The card proper

For the crumb

  • 500 g strong white flour, with a little more to dust the board, which is to say a great deal
  • 7 g fresh yeast, the size of a walnut and no larger
  • 90 g caster sugar
  • 300 ml whole milk, warmed to the wrist — the wrist is the thermometer the card insists on, and the card is right
  • 120 g butter, at room temperature and no colder
  • One very small pinch of salt, which the card calls “the king's share” and does not explain
  • Grated rind of one lemon, if the kitchen has one; the card allows its absence without comment

For the honey-saffron wash

  • 60 g dark honey, the kind the card calls “heath,” which at Littleton has always meant whatever the jar nearest the kettle was
  • 30 g butter
  • One saffron thread per person the loaf will feed — eight, eleven, however many — broken over the honey, never ground, the card is very firm about this
  • 2 tbsp golden syrup, for the last ribbon

The working

  1. Put the flour in the bowl. Make the well. The well is where the yeast goes, and the sugar, and the warmed milk, and then a quarter of the flour drawn over the top to hold it down. Stand it by, covered with a towel, for 40 minutes. Do nothing. This is the part the card marks rest, and the rest is in the recipe the way the four years are in the preamble: it is the whole point, and it cannot be hurried.
  2. When the well has risen and begun to look pleased with itself, work in the butter, piece by piece, and then the lemon rind and the pinch of salt which is the king's share. Knead for ten minutes, or until the dough gives under the thumb and takes the thumb's mark without holding it. The dough should be soft enough that it looks unfair to have been made from flour.
  3. Let it rise again, an hour or as long as it takes, in a warm corner, out of a draught. Punch it down. Shape it into a long loaf, the card says the shape of the kingdom when it was still one, and place it in a buttered tin. Let it rise a third time until it stands an inch above the rim.
  4. Warm the honey with the butter and the broken saffron until it runs, and brush it over the risen loaf. Score a criss-cross on top with a sharp knife, cutting not through but across, so the crust will split along the lines and the honey will sit in the gaps. Bake at 170°C for 35 to 40 minutes, until the top is the colour the card calls all-fair, which is a shade darker than it sounds.
  5. Here is the line, used where it is meant to be used: the loaf goes out of the oven, and is fixed in the tin, and only now, over the fixed loaf, does the last of the compound go — the final ribbon of saffron butter and golden syrup run over the criss-cross at the very end, so that the top wears the last of everything. A pastel, once fixed, is the compound runs with the last of all. Let it cool in the tin, which is against every rule of every other loaf in this book, and is correct.
  6. Keep it a day before you cut it. The first day it is a loaf. The second day it is a truce.

Notes from the margin: Nana's mother wrote that the saffron was to be bought whole and no other, and that the thread count was the only part of the recipe that was ever counted out loud at Littleton, eight threads for a festival, eleven for an argument. A later hand added that the loaf was last baked in the kitchen at Littleton on 1 October 1987, at the supper for the forty-second anniversary of a death the family does not name in the book but the kitchen keeps a day for, and that it was, in the words of the same later hand, “fair.” For a fellow card of the house — the Thursday-club cake of Berbera, kept in the same book under its own long preamble and its own supper club's own books — see the entry at Acanthus gaed — The Thursday-Club Cake, Warehouse Seven; the two cards disagree about everything except the rest.
