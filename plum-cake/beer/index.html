THE CELLAR REGISTER · FLANDERS STREET & THE WHARF · WINTER ENTRY, 2024

Obituary No. 62

† Bitter †

1824 – 17 February 2024 · hand-pulled, cask-conditioned, and gone at last

The Notice

BITTER, the English bitter ale, known to the house at the corner as “the dark one of the afternoon” — pale at noon, dark by four, the colour of the Flanders Street lamplight — died on Tuesday 17 February 2024, at half past four in the afternoon, at The Copper Whale, 2238 NW Flanders Street, when the last hand-pulled cask of the old style was drawn and drank and stood empty in the rack. She was of a kind whose first stirring is older than the counting of years: an alcoholic drink made by the brewing and fermentation of the starches of cereal grain, most commonly malted barley, though her cousins took wheat, maize, rice, and oats in their turn, and a great-grandmother named Gruit made her flavouring without hops at all and died of it first, as the oldest do. The grain was mashed and broken, its starch turned to sugar, and the sugars dissolved in water to make the wort — her mother, in the register’s reckoning — and the fermentation of the wort by yeast gave her ethanol and gave her breath, that small fine carbonation which is all that remains of her, hissing at the tap, in the glass of whoever drinks her memory.

She was one of the oldest and most widely consumed of the alcoholic drinks in the world, and one of the most popular of all drinks; the register does not doubt it, nor does it need to. Most of her kind were brewed with hops, which added bitterness and other flavours to her and acted as a natural preservative and stabiliser — her keeper of sixty years, the Hop, who kept her steady and sound when other draughts turned. Other flavouring agents, such as gruit, herbs, or fruits, may be included or used in her memory, and the cellar holds a jar of each.

Survivors and Predeceased

She is survived by her kindred in the northern cellar: Lager, of the pale and patient lines; the dark sisters Stout and Porter, who keep the bottom shelf; Pale Ale; and the wheat, maize, rice, and oat cousins who came to the family table later and never quite learned the English of it. A great number of caskmates survive in the pubs between Flanders Street and the river. Predeceased: Gruit, the old hopless flavouring of her earliest foremothers, who went first and left no bitterness at all behind her; and the barley of the first mash, uncounted. The Hop, though he withers to a cone every autumn, is perennial, and so outlives her, which is the bitterest part of it.

The Service

The service was held at The Copper Whale on Sunday 24 February, at dusk, the door standing open to the cold. The warden of the cellar, E. H. — not Cade, but of Cade’s old habit, the habit of writing what the table was without complaint — read the register. From the elegy set down in the register that night, one line is preserved exactly, in the hand that wrote it:

“IF THE BEAUTY OF BIRDS were not grieved at her side.
The cellar door would stand open to winter,
and no one would hear the last cask breathing in the dark.”

The birds of the line were the rooks. The copper whale weathervane, commissioned in 1895 and set over the house half a block down the street, had been taken down for the day and stood in the yard on its base; the rooks kept to it all afternoon, and the register notes — as the register always notes, in the manner of the Christmas tables of 1889 that first set down the corner’s custom, from St. Thomas’s precinct in Bombay to this wall — that no complaints were entered. For the long walk of that same street and its whale, its camellia, and its consulting room, the tour is kept at the Bates–Seller House Walking Tour, Northwest Portland, which carries the whale to its last stop and back again.

The Wake

The wake stood in the cellar, at the long table by the dry dock door. At the head of the table, as the house custom holds for the pairing that is older than either of them, the four-pound plum cake came up from the pantry: golden sultanas, seedless raisins, black currants, the pruned quarter sliced and not puréed, glazed, twice fed — the specification set down in full at Plum Cake — Field Manual FM-21, Chapter 6, the chapter that gives the cake its weights and keeps it until the following year. It was cut narrow, as the manual orders, and the pruned quarter, per the custom of the English house, was offered to the senior person at the table; on this night the senior person was the table itself, which took it, and no one objected. Beside the cake stood the last of the last cask, in one glass, for remembrance only, and the warden’s note in the margin reads: hard at the edge, good at the heart. The front register, the Webstuffs index, holds the rest of the corner’s ledgers, minutes, and placards, to which this obituary belongs.

In the Register

Let it be entered for the record: she was the dark one of the afternoon, and the afternoon, on Flanders Street, is darker now. The cask hangs empty in the rack, breathing nothing at all, and the cellar keeps the hour she went. In the margin, in a smaller hand, one line more, which the register preserves word for word: the yeast outlived her, as the hop did, and the cake keeps longest of all. The walk-in board by the dry dock door carries the thread that follows it: the cold cream the abbess of St Anne’s, 14th and Alder, brought to this wake, the knife she left on her side of the board, and the one line of her 1897 book the board preserves word for word — The Abbess’s Cold Cream, Thread No. 63 — which the board closed, as the register closed her, with no complaints entered.
