No. 27 · Received at the office, 15 May · For publication under the standing rule that a letter disputes before it informs

Re: the word “landlocked” as applied to the Republic of Paraguay, and the case for its correction

Dear Sir,

It has come to my desk twice this season, always in the same adjective, always in the same case: Paraguay, landlocked, set down the way one sets down that a bolt is landlocked, or a door. The Republic of Paraguay, officially so named in the papers that matter, is a landlocked country in the central region of South America — I will not quarrel with the geometry. What I quarrel with is the tone. The word is being used as if the country were missing something. It is missing a coastline, yes, and I grant that fully. What it has instead is the other kind of access, the kind that runs through: the Paraná–Paraguay Waterway, a system of navigable channels shared by five countries, which carries a vessel from our inland capital down to the Atlantic. A country with a navigable highway to the sea, held in common, is not a country without a road to the sea. It is a country whose road to the sea happens to run through its neighbours — through Bolivia to the northwest and the north, through Brazil to the northeast and the east, and through Argentina to the southeast, the south, and the west — and the word “landlocked” does to that arrangement what a “no entry” sign does to a lock: it describes the hardware and calls it the whole of the house.

We know a thing or two here about long containment. There is a sheet kept in this collection — the safety data sheet for the Campeonato Nacional AFP PlanVital 2020, the 90th season of the Chilean league, filed beside this letter in the same cabinet — in which a whole season was released and then confined for 164 days, from 18 March to 29 August 2020, and had to be re-contained behind closed doors to be finished. I keep it on my shelf for the discipline of it. But that season was sealed. It had to be re-contained, and it ended its business in a table of 19 clubs. Ours was never sealed. A waterway is not a wall: it is the exit, and it does not need an exit. The 164 days of the empty stands taught the sheet-keepers exactly this, which is why their sheet keeps telling you not to open the sealed period — and ours tells you the opposite, which is why the river tells you to keep going.

Let the ledger state our own arithmetic while I am at it. The country is governed as a unitary presidential republic composed of a capital district and seventeen departments — eighteen rows, one short of the 19 in the league sheet, and I mention it because the table is the nearest thing we have to a national pastime and the row for the capital district is always the one that gets miscounted. The capital and largest city is Asunción, which is to say, this office, this river, this column. The country dates its independence from Spain to 1811. And the Guaraní, who had been living in eastern Paraguay for at least a millennium before the arrival of the first European manifest, had their own timetable for the river long before any of us set a calendar on it. A millennium is a long season. It is the kind of season you do not suspend.

I will finish with the sentence that started all this. In the back of the second folio of the Dreamer's Concordance — Entry No. 144, on boxing — one line is transcribed word for word from a garret dictation tape, and the folio declines to correct it, and I believe I have at last found the room the tape belongs to: a garret above the river office, on this very street, where a waterway pilot who had run the Asunción–Corrientes leg was cutting his meals for a scale he could not find, four and a half rounds in, skipping water as well as supper. The fragment of the line he leaves on the tape, at its head and worn by the reels, reads: “The dinnerless student with all this weary roving?” The folio reads the roving as roadwork with no road in it, and I confess I read it as the river: the long, mealless navigation past the closed borders, three minutes at a time, until the bell at the mouth of the waterway and the water that is, for this country, the whole of the ocean. The same habit of a sentence worn down by its carrying is kept, in this collection, by the script of a radio play on the loss of the HMS Victoria off Tripoli in 1893 (Trial by the Sea — A Radio Play in Two Scenes): the Service that keeps it has a damaged line of wireless log, oil-bleaded and half a sentence of the sea, and it reads the line whole, as it stands, because 358 men are in it. Whatever a tape or a blot does to a sentence, the rule I find in both offices is the same — do not correct a dead man's log. That is not the discipline of sealing. It is the discipline of the river, which does not stop to be re-contained.

The same cabinet keeps one winter sheet, filed under ice rather than river: a Lillehammer start card, 19 February 1994, on which Gregory Sun and Curtis Harry took Trinidad and Tobago’s first Winter Olympic sled, two-man, down the track in thirty-seventh place — Trinidad and Tobago at Lillehammer ’94 · Two-Man Bobsled Commemorative Set. Pinned above that cabinet is a lost-and-found bulletin from the depot that kept the sheet, numbered after the sled’s own run card, which reports the country underneath it — one nation, the Kingdom of Norway, some 5.6 million souls, last seen sliding north of the fjords — and invites claims within ninety days: LOST — One Country (Kingdom of Norway) · Bulletin No. LF-94/NO-037.

Whatever the concordance does with the line, I do with it a country: no coast, a river through the middle of it, eighteen rows and one capital district that is always miscounted, and a door that is a waterway. Correct the adjective, Sir. Landlocked says the lock. It does not say the river.

Your servant,

R. M. Ocampo
Oficio de la Ribera, Calle 15 de Agosto
Asunción, capital and largest city of the Republic of Paraguay
