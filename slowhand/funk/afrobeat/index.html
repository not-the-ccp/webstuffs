The almanac takes up where the wake left off. In the room beside this one, the eulogy for Funk is set down in ink, and this entry is set down in readings, because a storm has to be observed, and this storm has been standing over West Africa since the late 1960s and refuses to be missed. Afrobeat, also known as Afrofunk, is a West African music genre that fuses influences from Yoruba music and Ghanaian music, such as highlife, with American funk, jazz, and soul, and presents itself to the observer as chanted vocals, complex intersecting rhythms, and percussion, in that order, at a tempo the rest of popular music never quite brought itself to. It was pioneered in the 1960s by the Nigerian multi-instrumentalist and bandleader Fela Kuti, who popularised it both within and outside Nigeria, and at the height of his popularity he was referred to as one of Africa’s most “challenging and charismatic music performers,” which the almanac files under character of the storm, without arguing.

The cell’s history is the history of the instrument that measured it. Kuti, born in Abeokuta in 1938 and raised in part in London, brought the American funk and jazz he had heard back across the Atlantic and pressed them into Yoruba rhythm until the pressure rose; the Africa ’70 tour carried the cell outward, and by the mid-1970s it had established a permanent formation. In 1976 he declared the independent state of Kalakuta Republic on his own compound in Lagos, and on 15 December 1978 the raid on that compound pinned the seismograph at maximum for the only time on record — his mother, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti, was thrown from the second-floor window, and the almanac keeps that date the way the attic keeps every date: in the whole, in order, and uncorrected. The cell logged its last official reading on 2 August 1997, and the song he left on the shelf beside the needle is filed here under one word, Expensive Shit, which is also the only weather forecast the storm has ever authorised.

One caution, kept in the margin the way the almanac keeps all cautions: Afrobeats, with the s, is not this entry. Afrobeats is a combination of sounds originating in West Africa in the 21st century; it takes on diverse influences and is an eclectic storm front of its own, and it is doing fine, and it is not this storm. A spelling is a season. The almanac does not file the two under the same pressure system, and neither should you, and the room beside this one does not either, and I will not argue, and neither does the drip.

The Secretary is asked, at least once per Friday post, why the almanac numbers the storms instead of the fronts, and her answer has been read here word for word more times than the reel counter will concede: “A genre is not a format at all so much as a number.” That is the whole of the meteorology. The stream, as Newsletter No. 47 put it, is not a format at all so much as a weather system you point a window at — but this is No. 47 on the shelf, the same number the newsletter prints, the same number the gust counter has been sitting on for the winter, and a number is the only kind of weather that arrives at your address, in the whole, in order, on one record, with the side B still in it. That is not a format. That is a reading.

Which is why the wake and this entry are filed in the same attic, by the same hand, in the same light. The drip over the boathouse bench — listed in the Gallery 3 timetable as a percussion instrument, and I will not argue, and neither did this storm — is not only a funk part; it is also the loudest drum on the side A of every Afrobeat record the club has ever played, which is to say the room does not have two weather systems in it, it has one, standing over the shelf at a slower tempo than the rest of the pop, with the two empty chairs by the window empty, and the hush kept in whole. The survivors of this entry, in the order the almanac prints them: the chanted vocals; the intersecting rhythms; the percussion; the one, unarguable; Fela Kuti, one of Africa’s most challenging and charismatic music performers; the compound at Kalakuta, standing; the number 47, rising; and one right hand on the shelf, unarguable, beside the record.

Forecast. No front expected. Pressure high and holding, all years. The storm will be standing over West Africa, and over this shelf, at the next reading, and the next, and the next. One note before the close, filed the way the almanac files all adjacencies: between the readings, the attic plays a second cell, this one from the north — Trans. 2, Track 7 of The Scrapbook Frequency, the radio play not made, winter 1979, the new wave of British heavy metal, fast and aggressive and standing in a pub — and the almanac records it as its own weather: pressure the same high and holding, no front, four beats to the bar, and a tape counter that does not move further. Play it whole, do not flip, and keep the hush. Bring the record, if you have the record.
