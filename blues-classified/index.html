Notice to Advertisers and Readers

This column is kept in the form it is printed in, because the form is the thing. The blues, as this paper has said since the column first ran, is not a song but a form: it began among the African Americans of the Deep South, about the year 1860, and has since taken into itself the spiritual, the work song, the field holler, the shout, the chant, and the rhymed simple narrative ballad, so that all of them are now part of the sound of it. It turns up in jazz, in rhythm and blues, and in the rock and roll that has lately begun to be heard; it is marked by the call-and-response, by the blues scale, and by certain chord progressions of which the twelve-bar is the commonest; and its essential ornament is the blue note — third, fifth, or seventh, flattened in pitch, and called, by those who keep the column, a worried note. The shuffle or the walking bass runs under the whole of it the way the river runs under the levee, and no advertiser is asked to explain any more than the form asks of a player.

Wanted

WALKING BASS WANTED. For the long October run up the river, sixteen dates through Cairo, Illinois and back. Ten dollars a week plus board; the man must hold a shuffle through fog, take no solo, and be willing to spell the tuba at the end of the third set. Apply, Docks Office, Pier 6, before the second boat. The band's 1926 riverbook lists forty-one dates; the captain's spelling of the group name that December at Cairo — Dixelland — is corrected by no one, and this office will not correct it.

SINGER WANTED. For the call-and-response at the Orpheum Saturday matinees. The house supplies the call; the singer supplies the answer, and must be able to answer in a key a half-step flatter than the one the house asks for. Reference: the church, the levee, or the field, in that order of preference.

For Sale

CORNETS & CANNONS. Cornet, 1927, returned home with its mouthpiece missing and no intention of leaving; sold as found, by the man who has kept it. Cannon banjo, one string, home-made, the kind the house band at the Fox Theatre lists on its pay stubs simply as cannon banjo and pays on that account. Both at the counter, North 21st Street, behind three of the original 1926 Mudlark presses — a thousand were struck; the stock list shows three. The form 9R kept in the Music History room on the leader of the Dixieland Six gives the account of the label, the run at the Fox, and the tip jar found to hold eleven dollars and thirty-five cents on the last night; this counter holds the jar, the coins, and the habit.

SPIRITS. HOLLIS & SONS, grocers and fillers, 2147 North 21st Street. Bottled, by the jug, and by the glass to the river trade. The liquor this joint well, if the schedule were made on; and the same kind, a great deal further down the centre pipe. Set word for word as the line came over the wire, Hollis & Sons not being asked to explain it, and this office not adding a stop it has not paid for. The schedule, as sent, runs: morning, the jug; noon, the glass; evening, the joint.

Lost & Found

LOST — TWELVE BARS. Slow blues in A, dropped somewhere about the South Stand at the Fairground Park bandshell, where, as one account has it, the stand sang hymns into the middle of the dance sets. Anyone who can hum the third bar flat will be paid the price of a glass at Hollis & Sons and forgiven the first two. The stand is believed to have taken them back; the stand answers to the call and keeps what it is given.

FOUND. A field holler, three bars of a work song, and a loading chant, all in one hand, at the foot of the levee on a Sunday in which no boat was coming. Left with Miss Etta Rouse, 1341 Laclede Avenue, who keeps them in a drawer with a church pennant and a 1926 date from the bandshell, and plays them on request, in order, and no other way. The drawer, with everything the years have added to it, has since gone to the hammer in one parcel; the account of the sale, and of the one-string cannon banjo that joined the keeping in 1964, is set down at Lot 41 of the Rouse & Rouse thirty-eighth spring sale, St. Louis, 1979.

FOUND — FROM JERMYN STREET. A funeral sheet for a cello, 1682 to 1783, arrived with the paper and is kept in the column's file: Giacobbe Cervetto, of the surname Basevi, a funeral notice and obituary, given under the hand of his son. The low string of that family is said to have kept its voice last, which is the kind of record this column deals in; the sheet's makers note that one of his pupils, an Englishman, kept the chair at Covent Garden before the river took him, and that it is his case the compositor sets in. The front page of this shelf files the sheet in its place among the other things worth keeping.

Rates & Bookings

THE FOX, SMALL HALL. Fourteen-week runs, forty dollars a week, no less than a full orchestra on the slow numbers. Bookings to Mr. E. Dale, house manager, whose word the column has printed without amendment since 1926. The steamboat captains take the river dates separately; the October book opens the first week and closes when the fog closes, whichever comes first.

TWELVE-BAR ROOM. On the second floor above the Docks Office, open for rehearsal Saturday nights. The progressions are pinned to the wall in the common order; the wall does not argue. A chair is set for the walking bass, and the chair is not moved, and no fee is taken from a man who plays under the rest of the room without looking up.

Situations

SITUATION WANTED. By a teacher of the scale, two evenings a week, for the school on Grand Boulevard: the third, the fifth, and the seventh, flat and worried, and the shape of the twelve-bar in the common order. References in the union minutes, where the longest unbroken engagement by a local leader since the post is counted and kept. Will take, in preference, a pupil who can read notes off a hymnal by the age of twelve.

SITUATION OFFERED. To a player of the blues in any of its borrowed forms — spiritual, work song, field holler, shout, chant, or ballad — a room of his own at the Twelve-Bar Room, with a stove, a shelf for the case, and the standing order that the call be answered. A schedule will be made. If the schedule is made on, the joint will be well, and the same kind will be found, a great deal further down, in the centre of the pipe.

Personal

BLUE NOTES. Thirds, fifths, and sevenths, flattened in pitch, set and fitted by a trusted ear at no charge to the member of the Docks Office. No string will be straightened that the note has not chosen to bend; the bending is the part of the sound that is kept. The work is done on the side of the room where the player's chair has always been, and the house now knows which side that is.

FOR THE WALKERS. Those who would walk the ground the column is set in — the Fairground bandshell, the Central Avenue clubs, the levee, the Pier 6 office, and the mile of North 21st Street between them — will find a seven-day account of the whole of Missouri, with times, distances, and stops, at Missouri by Foot; the St. Louis days of that itinerary pass within a block of this counter and keep the bandshell for an afternoon.

Set in the Ledger's second case by the compositor's hand. The spelling centre in the liquor line is his, and the line itself is the advertiser's, word for word, as the column keeps every line it prints — the way the stand kept its, the way the low string kept its, the way the river keeps the whole of it.
