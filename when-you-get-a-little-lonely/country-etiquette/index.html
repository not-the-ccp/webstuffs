Chapter VII

On Keeping Country Music in the House

Being a brief account of the manners due to a music genre, its instruments, its rooms, and the building that holds them

§ 1 · Of the Thing Itself

Country music, also known as country and western, or simply country, is a music genre originating from the United States. It is known for its ballads and dance tunes, identifiable by both traditional lyrics and harmonies accompanied by banjos, mandolins, fiddles, harmonicas, and many types of guitar; either acoustic, electric, steel, or resonator guitars. Once called hillbilly music, the term country music was popularized in the 1940s. The music was first developed in the South, and then spread throughout the Piedmont, from Louisiana along the Appalachian Mountains to New York. It is believed to be derived from British folk music, brought to the United States during the early waves of settlement, and the first formal room it ever occupied was a radio studio: a dance broadcast out of Nashville in the autumn of 1925, fifty thousand watts, whose floor the country set out for the next century. A house that keeps this music keeps a dance floor and a ballad shelf, and the two must be kept at the same distance from the door.

§ 2 · Of the Naming

The guest who is introduced to the music under its old name, hillbilly, is to be told, once and without heat, that the naming was settled in the 1940s and has not been reopened since. The manners of the genre have always outlived its labels; the label is the only part of the house that was ever allowed to move. Where a household keeps more than one name for the music, it is the courtesy of the eldest instrument — and in this music that is the fiddle — that decides which name is spoken at dinner.

§ 3 · Of the Instruments

The full complement is to be seated in this order: the banjo, the mandolin, the fiddle, the harmonica, and then the guitars, which travel in fours — acoustic, electric, steel, and resonator — and which should not be parted at the table. The steel guitar is the instrument that speaks from another room, and a host of manners will leave it there: it is the only voice in the house permitted to answer from the hall. The harmonica is the only instrument that may leave before the guests; it has been walking since the first boat, and no one will object to its going. The guitars, in their turn, have a traveler’s account of their own: the brochure that sets down the stops of the Route they are carried on — the alley, the hollow, the amplification rooms, the pick house, the milk in Aden — is filed beside this chapter at The Six-String Route, and a guest who asks which of the four should be strummed first is referred to its practical part.

§ 4 · Of the Room

The listening room is to be cut with the care of a session room, which the music's own paperwork knows well. In Room B in Nashville, the side one of When You Get a Little Lonely, PH·004, the 1995 record by Maureen McCormick, took nine of its twenty-two days, and the string quartet on the title track — the same quartet that plays on the shed recording kept on this shelf — asked for its coffee black, and the studio did not have it, and the changing of that one small thing is written into the notes as the changing of the song. A household is on the same terms with its room: the coffee, the chairs, and the hour are the arrangement, and the music is what survives on top of them. The guest brings no arrangements to a house that keeps this music. The guest brings milk.

§ 5 · Of the Building

The fourth track of the record, A Brother’s Building, five minutes and three seconds of it, is the etiquette of the house itself. A building that holds the music — the pressing plant, the vault, the label’s own cold rooms — is to be treated as a guest who has overstayed and is still, somehow, the one doing the hosting. When the auditors of the agency in Rockville came through the cold chain in the autumn of 2025 and read the logs in the hand in which they were written, the operator’s diary kept that week records what was said of the doubled fax line: the drawer is full, the label should consider it a certificate of the building’s honesty. A house that keeps its unexplaineds in the same drawer as its verdicts earns the certificate slowly, and the ceremony of taking it is this: at dusk, open the cellar door, let the steel guitar answer from the vault below, and note that folding back the terrific lower jaw of the building’s honesty reveals, as it always does, the compressors running on time and nothing, in thirty years of cold, a degree out of spec.

§ 6 · Of the Milk

One line survives on a lyric card, printed in the sleeve of every copy, and it is the whole chapter in a sentence: “The morning’s milk is in Aden, in the club that needs none.” The club that needs none is the manners of the room described, not the room described at: a door with no sign above it, no membership list, no key, and a bar that keeps the day’s milk for whoever walks in. A house that keeps country music keeps the line open in the same way — the phone off the hook, as the second track of the record has it — and receives, without a form, whatever the morning brings. In the city of Nashville there is a grocery on Twelve Avenue South that became, in 2014, a restaurant and a music venue, and its chancery record preserves the small architecture of it, the rear workshop, the oak bandshell over which the witnesses were heard. It is the public version of the private rule: the sign comes down, the list is not kept, and the milk is always in. That is the etiquette of this music in one word, and the word is: in.

When the record is put away, the house keeps another scoreboard besides — the outfield grass, the pennant race, and the man they called the Kingpin of the Orioles, whose summers are set down in a radio play at Kingpin of the Orioles — Joe Kelley · Radio Play Excerpt.
