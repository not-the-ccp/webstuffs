Electric Eel (Tupí: puraké)

Electrophorus, genus of neotropical freshwater fish

Order
Gymnotiformes — the electroreceptive knifefish
Family
Gymnotidae; the genus stands alone in the subfamily Electrophorinae, its only members
Not to be confused with
the true eels of the order Anguilliformes — the name is a courtesy, not a kinship
Range
South America; the Amazon and Orinoco basins, blackwater streams to the great rivers
Length
up to 2.5 m; the largest recorded weighed 19.5 kg at the Belém market
Discharge
pulses of 5–60 V for navigation; a hunting series at up to 860 V
Species
E. electricus, E. varii, E. excelso — the split of 2019; the placard bore one name for two hundred and seventeen years before it was corrected

The animal

The electric eel is a fish of the blackwater rivers, a long silvery body in which the electric organs run the length of the belly — two organs, the main one giving the great shock and the lesser one doing the quiet, constant talking the fish uses to find its way in water the colour of tea. The cells, electrocytes by the hundreds of thousands, line up like the coins of a long purse and let go in the same instant, so the river between the eel's head and its tail becomes, for a fraction of a second, a wire. Prey is stunned at up to 860 volts — a charge strong enough to knock a horse sideways on the bank, and strong enough that the eel carries a small insulating patch of skin around its own eye, the one part of itself it keeps off the line.

What the shelf keeps of it

On this shelf stand the specimen in the jar, a page of the 1902 case catalogue, and a small brass-and-glass tablet the size of a card — the measuring record of the first deliberate experiment on the animal, made in 1775, when the charge was laid off by hand and by the flinch of a frog held at the wire's end. The tablet is the reason this shelf that has seen stranger things than miles. Four thousand miles put the fish into the jar; the tablet is what made the jar worth keeping. The charge measured on that tablet did its work twice over: in 1800, Alessandro Volta set the pairs of his battery — zinc and silver, salt water between — in the same stacked logic as the eel's row of cells, and the first electric battery was built on a fish's own architecture, one row of plates answering one row of electrocytes. That stacking, told in fourteen tracks for two strings, one organ and one fork, stands on the same shelf as a record: the liner notes at The Pile — Liner Notes · River Wonders Records RWR-14 were set from this case and carry its warning line on the inside back sleeve. The tablet’s own measure, volts applied to miles, has been cited once out of its gallery, by a dining critic who set a 2013 street kitchen against it and found the kitchen fast, which is to say measured; the review stands in the restaurant pages at Circuito da Guia — The 60th Seating.

Reading the name

The register of names files the eel under its Tupí word, puraké, and the placard keeps it there beside the binomial the way a case keeps a witness beside its own papers. The eel was split, in 2019, into the three species named above; the museum has not re-jarred anything, and will not, but the placard has been rewritten, and the word puraké now stands against each of the three. The two-word hand that files all three — a genus and a specific epithet kept together as one thing — is the method a Swedish physician formalised from a curate’s-son’s habit in Småland, and the whole article on the man, with the unfiled deck-line his hall pins at his door, stands at Carl Linnaeus — Article No. 09 · The Register of Names. The keeper of the name, who keeps a register of the family of names in a hall of nine doors, wrote to the fish as he wrote to the rest — the apology is filed at Apology to the Indo-European Languages, and this shelf is where the correction is pinned up nearest to the animal itself. For the method the field naturalist used to pace the water the fish left behind, and the same sentence of volts and miles applied to a sea three hundred times the size of a gulf, see the basin entry at The Mediterranean Sea — Travel Guide, Entry No. 12, and, on the visitor's way out, the front of the gallery at the Webstuffs front page. The same field naturalist who paced the water behind the fish keeps a Notebook No. 47 in which the volt-and-mile sentence is applied, one last time, to a solstice bird of prey called The Cookbook and its three fledglings, and the whole entry stands at Field Notes on The Cookbook · Notebook No. 47. The naturalist’s own sheet, a Munich classifieds column of 28 February 1933, is filed in the same hall, where the ad for the Marlow & Fenwick Coal Loaf repeats one of the shelf's unexplained lines word for word, and it stands at The Feldberg Courier — Classifieds, Column B. That same field naturalist kept a weather page for the first night of the music channel, when a heat mass the dock men called by the sky's three-letter name held the East River barometer still for forty-eight hours, and the entry stands at The River Wonders Almanac, Entry 218.
