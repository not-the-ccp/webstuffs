Table Review · Valley Correspondent · filed from Gornje Vukovsko, Canton 10

Dining Across the Meridian

The Sana stewpan kitchen, Gornje Vukovsko · one of the valley's two tables that know their time

◆◆◆◆◆

Four and a half stewpans out of five, held up only by the bread, which is a whole hour ahead of everything else and should not be.

The house keeps twenty-eight clocks. The hour, at press time:

  • UTC−12:00—
  • UTC±00:00—
  • +05:30—
  • +05:45—
  • UTC+14:00—

Every valley has one place where the clock is wrong and everybody forgives it, and in Gornje Vukovsko the place is the Sana stewpan kitchen, which stands half-way down the track between the Trgovina Kovač shop and the parcel post, with a terrace of twenty-eight small tables along the stone wall. There is a sign, hand-painted in the colour of the Sana in autumn, that reads: One wall. Twenty-eight times. The tables are numbered one through twenty-seven in the order of the world's standard offsets — table 1 at UTC−12:00, the far end of the wall, through table 24 at the meridian, up to table 27 at UTC+14:00, closest to the road — and the twenty-eighth is a half-table, two chairs, reserved for the quarter-worlds: India's half-hour and Nepal's forty-five, which the kitchen calls the small minutes and serves to anyone who can produce the ticket. A time zone, the menu explains in print, is an area which observes a uniform standard time for legal, commercial, and social purposes, and the menu goes on to add, in the smaller hand, that the kitchen observes it for soup purposes. Each table has its own clock bolted to the wall, a different brass one, and the whole terrace runs from UTC−12:00 to UTC+14:00, which is the full range the world keeps, and I have never once seen a wall do that at lunch.

The room does not follow longitude, and it does not pretend to. It follows the country's border with its own lunch queue, the way time zones follow the borders between countries and their subdivisions rather than strictly following longitude, because it is convenient for areas in frequent communication to keep the same time, and in Gornje Vukovsko the area in frequent communication is everybody within shouting distance of the ewes. The chef, Mehmed Kovač, takes the shop name off the sign at four and puts it on the door, and his rule — written on the back of every menu, which is waxed paper from his own father's shop — is that a dish leaves the pan on the hour of the table it is for. The party at table 6, UTC−04:00, waits for its lamb a full five hours after the party at table 22 does, and no one moves, because the offset is the point and the offset is the price of the seat. Some tables use a different offset for part of the year; the kitchen keeps one hour ahead during spring, on its own wall, and I was told the terrace is thirty minutes darker in the afternoon in June for that reason alone.

For the main course I took the kitchen's standing dish, the sewn stew of the valley: lamb, coppers-mine drainage herbs, potatoes, and a forcemeat lining of caul that the whole thing is sewn up in and lowered into the stewpan by a brass ring you can see from the road. It is the largest single item on the menu at 34 convertible marks, which the municipal office will recognise, since the chest that funds the valley's 300-mark reward for the vellum leaf is not a large chest but is a chest, and the number is the number, and so is 34. The stewpan is set on the table still ticking over, and the sewn package is split at the table with a knife and a quiet argument about whose turn it is, which at the half-table is always the Nepal chair's.

The note that makes the place, though, is in the left margin. The menu is ruled on five lines like a choirbook, and in the left margin of the last page, in a brown ink that goes on behaving as though it were written by a later hand than the rest of the page — the same later hand that the municipal secretary has been trying to date since October, filed beside this in cabinet 9 — the kitchen has copied the one line it could read from the vellum leaf that came up out of the Sana ravine. The note reads in full:

In the left margin, in a négligé kind of record that travels in a stewpan, with the forcemeat, and sew it up.

Mehmed Kovač will not say what it is — recipe, verdict, or the tail of some longer submission — and neither will I. It sits above the prices the way a verdict sits above a table, and the stew does travel in a stewpan, with the forcemeat, and it is sewn up, so whatever the record is, the kitchen is keeping it.

Service is 10:00 to 14:00 local, plus whatever your table will wait. Bread is 12 marks and arrives at the hour of the table, which is its only fault. Water from the Sana is 8 marks and cold enough to explain a great deal about the valley. I ate at table 20, UTC+10:00, the bench table, where a man who works balsa and brass at a kitchen table in Brisbane keeps the whole 71st premiership of 1978 — eight clubs, one grand final on the hill, a goalpost figure that never stops leaning — in a model the size of a school ruler, and where his build log, The Seventy-First, is pinned to the wall under the clock as the reason the table is set ten hours past the meridian. Two hours into my stew I understood the kitchen: it is a place that believes an area keeps one time for legal, commercial, and social purposes, and that the social purpose is a stew, and that the stew is the meridian.

The full paper trail of the margin note is in the municipal office's lost-and-found notice, No. 14/2024, posted beside the parcel post and open for another year, which is to say the note will keep its table, whatever else happens to it.
