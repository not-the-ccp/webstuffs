Follow-up to the destroyer thread, where the watch ended up being the point of the ship, and to the frigate plate, where the unarmed deck below is kept for berthing the crew and no card may ever be drawn from it. I am not here to re-litigate escort doctrine. I am here because the recipe is real, and the recipe is about aircraft.

Part 1 (Definitions and Abbreviations) of Subchapter A of Chapter I of Title 14 of the U. S. Code of Federal Regulations states that aircraft "means a device that is used or intended to be used for flight in the air". Short as a watch order, that definition, and about as final as one. An aircraft counters the force of gravity in one of three ways: static lift — the gas in the envelope lighter than the air it displaces — the dynamic lift of an airfoil, or, in a few cases, direct downward thrust from its engines. The family runs long: airplanes, drones, rotorcraft including helicopters, airships including blimps, gliders, paramotors, and hot air balloons. The human activity that surrounds them is called aviation. In this house it has been called nothing but trouble since January 1968.

My great-uncle Lincoln Hale shipped as galley steward aboard HMS Undaunted, D59, a 21-type destroyer, from the autumn of 1967 to the spring of 1968 — the same berth torpedo_net stood in winter weather off the Faroes, thirty knots on the nose and the foredeck a sheet of ice. Lincoln never stood a watch on the bridge; his station was the galley, forward of the unarmed deck below where the crew berths, and his log was a recipe book. He reckoned that a watch at sea and a bake in the galley are the same thing: both are a matter of holding a line against a force that will not quit, whether that force is a January gale or gravity itself.

The recipe card is in my pocket almanac now, behind the moon-phase page for January 1968, in Lincoln's hand, headed AIRCRAFT in block capitals. Under the heading is a single line that reads like a standing order: If anyone from D59 is still on the unarmed deck below used for berthing the crew. Under that line are quantities, temperatures, and times, which I give below as he wrote them, with the measurements converted to the metric the Board now reckons in.

Aircraft — D59 galley loaf, as Lincoln wrote it

Makes one 900 g loaf, enough for a watch of eight, or fourteen covers if the slices are thin.

  • 450 g plain flour
  • 10 g baking powder
  • 5 g fine salt
  • 170 g cold butter, cubed
  • 150 g demerara sugar
  • 300 g mixed dried fruit: 170 g sultanas, 80 g currants, 50 g candied peel
  • zest of one lemon
  • 2 large eggs, beaten
  • 5 ml vanilla extract
  • 120 ml whole milk, scalded and cooled

Method. Heat the oven to 170 °C (gas mark 3). Rub the butter into the flour, baking powder, and salt until it reads like coarse breadcrumbs. Stir in the sugar, fruit, and lemon zest. Beat the eggs with the vanilla and milk, pour into the dry mix, and fold until just combined — a wet batter is what you want; Lincoln said a tight crumb flies like a brick. Pour into a greased 900 g loaf tin and bake for 55 to 60 minutes, until a skewer comes out clean and the top sounds hollow when knocked. Cool in the tin for ten minutes, then turn out onto the unarmed deck below — Lincoln left his cooling there overnight, saying the still air of the berthing deck set the crumb better than any wire rack — and leave until completely cold before slicing.

Serve thick, with butter, at the end of a watch. The loaf keeps for ten days in a tin; Lincoln's lasted three, at the Cockpit, at the November 2002 dinner for fourteen covers, where the frost was on the glass until 2 a.m. and the almanac, by the record, was never wrong.

Lift reckoner

Reckon the dynamic lift of an airfoil: L = ½ × ρ × v² × S × CL, in newtons. Divide by 9.81 for kilograms-force. The figures work on paper as well as on the board.
