Card No. 7 — The Society Kitchen Book · Room 3, the old rehearsal floor, Flinders Street, Melbourne

The Election Night Roast

In great-aunt Nell’s hand, with the red side of the ledger left in · serves the whole gallery, the guest list, and D.W. twice

The long preamble, which is the whole of the recipe’s business

The roast belongs to the Australian Labor Party, or rather it belongs to the people who used to belong to it, and to the people who still do, and the card says so, in the margin, in Nell’s ink, which is the same red as the rosette and the same red as the flag of the party and the same red, the committee has ruled, as the sleeve of the club’s kettle. The Labor Party was founded in 1891, being descended from the labour parties founded in the various Australian colonies during the emerging labour movement, and it is, for that reason and no other, the oldest active party in the country. It sits on the centre-left of the political spectrum, it is the major social democratic party in Australia, and its main rival is the Liberal–National Coalition, which we have referred to, in this book and in no other, as the other side of the bill. The Party has been the ruling party at the federal level since the 2022 federal election, and it currently forms government in five of the eight states and territories. When the treasurer read that last sentence out loud at the meeting of 3 July, the room did what rooms do, and the committee resolved, one to zero on the ground of the other six being asleep, that the roast be the recipe of record for the winter issue.

The recipe was developed the way these things are developed: slowly, and across more elections than the card would list. Nell set it down in 1943, in a kitchen in Fitzroy that ran from the workshop, with the kettle on the right of the door, which is the same arrangement as Room 3, and the club keeps that arrangement because it is a good one. She had learned the bones of it from her father, a shearers’ delegate who said that a party is a machine and a roast is a machine and the man at the arrowhead of the trivet is the same man as the man at the arrowhead of the platform, and then he went to the union hall. The card has been carried through the 1945 year, the 1972 change, the 2007 change, the 2013 year, and the 2022 election, at which the whole society sat in Room 3 from dusk until the last seat was counted, the shortwave on 6410 kHz for the second feed, and the roast in the oven for the first. The guest list had a place for James Ashby; the place kept warm. When the Coalition’s colour left the map, D.W. lifted the lid, the steam came up the length of the card, and the line that follows went back in Nell’s hand.

There is one more thing to be read before the method, because the club’s rule is that a line is kept as it comes, in the same hand it was written, and the rule was learned from the museum’s placard for Thomas Cook, Case No. 47, which keeps its one uncorrected line of the temperance minute book in the clerk’s hand, as received, Groyne Street style. The back of Card No. 7 carries one such line. It has been offered up for emendment four times. It is printed here exactly as it stands on the card, in the same temperate hand as the fare:

The Romans followed the Knave was standing close by a brown and gristle; chop the bacon; add the seasoning and herbs, and fried bread crumbs.

The committee declines to emend.

Three readings stand, as is the custom of the house. The first, by D.W., is that “the Knave” is the name of a butcher’s knife, and “the Romans” is the name of the street where Nell’s father bought the bacon, and the line is a line of instruction after all, merely badly worn. The second, by the treasurer, is that it is a broadcast fragment that wandered in off 6410 kHz and wrote itself into the card, and that the Arrowhead Society’s Newsletter No. 9 already holds its twin from the shortwave log, P305, and that the two lines are a pair, kept word for word, one in the air and one on the paper. The third reading is that the line is the recipe, the whole recipe, compressed by time into a single sentence, and that the method below is only a translation. The committee declined to rule. The line stands as it stands, and this is the practice we borrowed from the Loughborough minute book, and we are grateful to the museum for the precedent, and the kettle is on the right of the door.

For the red side

Serves the whole gallery, the guest list, and D.W. twice · one roast · the oven does the long middle of it

Things

  • one brown roast, the kind the butcher knows without being asked, standing close by the Knave
  • one slab of bacon, in rashers, for the base and for the top, in that order
  • the seasoning: salt, pepper, and the teaspoon of paprika Nell kept in the blue tin by the kettle
  • the herbs: thyme from the union hall window box, rosemary cut with the cabinet-make’s square, one bay leaf held like a ticket
  • bread crumbs, fried in the bacon fat, to the point where the room smells like the count is ending
  • one onion, halved, face down, as a witness

Method

  1. Light the oven early. An election is won in the morning and a roast is won by being in the oven before the morning is over. Preheat to the point where the windows of Room 3 would fog, had Room 3 windows.
  2. Chop the bacon. Lay it across the base of the roasting dish in a single layer, the way the party was laid across the colonies in 1891, colony by colony, before the founding put them in one party.
  3. Set the roast on the bacon, standing close by the Knave, that is to say within reach of the knife. Rub it with the seasoning and the herbs. Tuck the bay leaf under one shoulder. The onion goes in face down to bear witness to the whole proceeding.
  4. Roast for three hours. Do not open the door in the middle of the long middle, which is the rule of the roast and the rule of the leadership and the same rule. When the last hour is in, add the fried bread crumbs around the edge, and they will do what crumbs do, and the committee will not discuss it.
  5. At the end, stand the dish on the trivet at the arrowhead of the table, pull the bacon to the edge of the platter, and serve the red side of the family before the blue. The guest list is fed last, which is why Ashby’s place kept warm.

Keep it as it comes. If a line on this card does not read like a recipe, it was written by someone who knew what it meant, and the society’s rule, learned at Groyne Street and kept ever since, is that the line stands in the same hand it was written. Webstuffs will have a copy of the card, printed, on the shelf, in no one’s hand except its own.
