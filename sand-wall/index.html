The Moroccan Western Sahara Wall

Submitted by Y. Berrada · Form 3C · Room 214 · Tuesday of the eleventh week, 9:40 a.m.

B+keep the wall going

2,700km

The wall is approximately 2,700 kilometres long — 1,700 miles — and it runs south to north, through Western Sahara and into the southwestern portion of Morocco. I checked the number three times and it changed every time, because the wall is still being moved.

1. What it is

The Moroccan Western Sahara wall — the Berm, also called the Moroccan sand wall, in Arabic al-jidīr ar-ramliyya, literally “the sand wall” — is a berm, which the dictionary in Room 214 defines as a bank of earth or sand built up to act as a barrier. Ours, being built by an army with heavy machinery, is a bank of earth and sand roughly twenty-four metres high in places and thirty to fifty metres wide, and it does the job the definition promises. On its western side lie the Moroccan-occupied areas, which Morocco calls the Southern Provinces; on its eastern side lie the Polisario-controlled areas, the Free Zone, nominally the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic. That is the whole sentence the wall was built to speak, and everything in this report is its footnotes.

Why a wall? The report in the department file says it plainly: it was built in order to restrict the independence-seeking Sahrawis to the eastern part of the desert, away from the region’s natural resources, which sit on the Moroccan-occupied side. Most of the world’s phosphate ore is dug out of the hills around Bou Craa, the Atlantic coast between Laàyoune and Dakhla holds one of the richest fishing grounds on the seaboard, and the oil surveys all begin at the fence line. The wall is not a border in the polite sense of a line on a map. It is a border that has been given a height.

2. The places I had to look up

  • Laàyoune (El-Aaiún) — the big city on the west, on the Atlantic, where the trucks come down the coastal road.
  • Dakhla — further south, at the mouth of the valley, famous for its lagoon and for being on the right side of the wall.
  • Smara and Lagouira — the older town to the north, and the fishing village that keeps its nets out of the argument.
  • Bou Craa — the phosphate mine in the hills east of the coast, the reason this report exists.
  • Tifariti — the town on the east side that the Free Zone calls its capital and which some maps have printed as the Gibraltar of the Sahara, because of what it is said to be worth if it ever moves.
  • Tindouf — not on the wall at all but close to the Free Zone, in Algeria, where the camp towns of Aít Ben Haddou, Boumadi, Dhak El Aīach, El Oued and Rabouni hold more than 160,000 people under canvas.

3. How it was built, in the order the file gives

Construction began in 1980, under a military operation the file calls Sword 51, with the extension the following year under Hammer 52. What started as a single line became, over the years, a system of three roughly parallel walls, the third of which was opened in 2004 and runs a reported 2,727 kilometres. The material is the ground itself: sand, gravel and iron ore, dug out of the desert and pressed back into it with earthmovers and rollers, because concrete is expensive and sand is not. Between the walls run tank tracks wide enough for armoured patrols, and the western edge of each berm is watched from trenches and towers with floodlights that the night patrols on the east side describe as a second sunrise.

The detail I found hardest to get out of my head is that the wall is not finished and cannot be finished. The wind runs off the Atlantic all day and shoves the sand back east-west across the desert; the sand creeps over the western face and buries the trenches, so the army digs them back out, and digs them back out again. A structure of this length, made of loose material, in the middle of a moving surface, is a process rather than a thing. We measured this in class (Section 5) and the class came to the same conclusion the desert does: the wall is a verb.

4. What is on each side

West of the wall

The Southern Provinces: the cities, the ports, the phosphates of Bou Craa, the fishing, the roads, and the administrative life that moves the trucks. The wall keeps all of it on this side by design, which is the sentence in the second paragraph of Section 1, said again from the other bank.

East of the wall

The Free Zone and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic: Tifariti at the mouth of the ravine, the camp towns at Tindouf, the committees and the radio. The wall keeps the people who built nothing and claim everything on this side, and both descriptions in this file are written in ink that has not dried.

My note on the map, which I have left in: the distance between Tifariti and the nearest point of the third wall is the length of one school corridor, and the distance between Bou Craa and Laàyoune is the length of the coastal road. The wall is very long and it is also, locally, a short walk. Both figures are in the file.

5. The Long Wall: the game we ran in its place

Because the wall cannot be visited, Mrs. Calloway ran us on a board instead: a sand tray ninety centimetres wide on the front bench, eighteen weeks long, three teams. Each week a team played one of four moves — sand, gravel, ore, or patrol — and added a segment to the model wall; sand raised the height, gravel widened the base, ore was spent on the mine at the north end of the tray, and a patrol had to be able to walk the whole track without being buried, which the wind did to us weekly from a jam jar. Every move was written into the class register by the team on duty, and the register was kept open on the bench for the whole term, which Mrs. Calloway explained at the first lesson was the whole point: the wall is recorded or it is not there.

At the end of the eighteen weeks our wall on the tray measured 127 centimetres, which at the scale of the tray is a wall of no meaningful length at all, and the register held 1,106 entries, which at the scale of the register is a wall of no meaningful end. The three teams finished level. No one on the bench objected to the result, and I think that is why we have not argued about the game since.

On the last day she closed the tray with a piece of card and wrote, on the back of the rules sheet, the one line from her that the whole form has copied down:

At the end of any game that keeps the register.

— back of the rules sheet, in Mrs. Calloway’s pencil, the eleventh week of the term

It took us a week to agree on what it meant, and the class’s answer is this: a game whose moves are written in a register that stays open never finishes, because the register is the referee and the referee is still on duty. The real wall works the same way. It has been moved for decades, it is moved again every winter, and the ledgers of the men who press it down have never once been closed. The sentence is not a conclusion. It is a forecast.

6. Where I checked

Mostly the department file, which is thinner than the argument. The sound of the patrol track, which I had not believed until I heard it, is described in a field diary kept at the Lodge at the Gate, where the technician counts repeats the same way the class counted them, and rules that nothing on the middle mile should ever be hurried. The Reader at the college, before he died, ruled on the margin of a copy of this file, in his hand: a boundary kept like a register; cite it, do not correct it — a note he would have been right to publish, and he kept it, like everything else, in the obituary and record of Elias T. Thorndon, No. 212 of the Registrar’s Ledger. The rest of the college’s shelf, where the file itself is stored, is listed at the front page.
