The Boursaud Trail

A Tourist Brochure of the Rev. Father Edward V. Boursaud, S.J.
(September 1, 1840 – March 19, 1902) · Six Stops from the East River to the Tiber

★ SIX STOPS · TWO OCEANS · ONE BLACK ROBE · PRESIDENT OF BOSTON COLLEGE, 1884–1887 · ASK THE COUNTER AT EVERY STOP ★

★ Introduction — Who Is the Man in the Black Robe? ★

Good day, traveller, and welcome to the trail. Father Edward Victor Boursaud was born in New York City on September 1, 1840, to a family of the old French stock, and was brought up partly there and partly in France, so that he carried two skylines in his head for the rest of his days. He took his schooling at Mount St. Mary's College in Emmitsburg, Maryland, the oldest Jesuit college in the United States, and in 1863 he walked across the threshold into the Society of Jesus, the year his class was still finding its feet. The next eighteen years he spent studying and teaching at Jesuit institutions — Boston College itself, Georgetown College, the little college at Woodstock, Maryland, and the novitiate house in Frederick, Maryland, where the young men's Latin drills echo off the stone walls to this day. In 1884 the Society put him in the chair at Boston College, and he held it from 1884 to 1887. In 1881 and again in 1887 he answered the call to Rome for three-year terms as assistant secretary to the Jesuit Superior General for the English-speaking world, and it was for the second of those terms that he stepped down from the chair. He died on March 19, 1902. Six stops will do him honour. Bring your boots.

★ Stop 1 — New York City: The First Skyline ★

Born September 1, 1840. Walk the old avenues of his childhood and ask yourself what the city looked like a week before his birth; the answer is: mostly rivers, wharves, and church steeples, one of which belonged to his own. The guide's standing advice is to take the walk before ten in the morning, while the light comes in from the east and the bread is still warm at the bakeries. French and English were both spoken at the table he grew up at, and the trail begins wherever two skylines can fit in one head. His sister Marguerite kept the language itself under watch in the Journal Français — a diary of the French tongue, ruled in both languages, running from 1885 to the last entry.

★ Stop 2 — Mount St. Mary's College, Emmitsburg, Maryland ★

The college that schooled him is still standing at 1200 Miner Street, its old halls opening to the campus lawn the way they opened to him. Walk the quadrangle in September, as he would have, and count the chimneys. Students will tell you the dormitory bells still ring the old pattern, and the chapel clock keeps a half minute ahead of everything else in town, which the town has never minded. Emmitsburg is small, dry, and serious about its college — the right climate for a boy deciding what he will do with the rest of his life.

★ Stop 3 — Frederick, Maryland: The Novitiate House ★

In 1863 he entered the Society of Jesus at its novitiate in Frederick, Maryland, where the young professed still keep the drills in Latin and the walks in silence. Visit in the afternoon when the light is going out of the cloister and the whole house looks as if it is thinking. The counter at the door will sell you the old timetable of hours for twenty-five cents and a smile. Eighteen years of studying and teaching at Jesuit institutions ran from this doorstep — including Boston College, Georgetown College, and Woodstock College in western Maryland, where a small seminar hall bears his initials on the lintel to this day, worn thin by a hundred thumbs that have not yet been replaced.

★ Stop 4 — Boston College, Chestnut Hill: The Chair, 1884–1887 ★

Now the centre of the trail. In 1884 Father Boursaud became the president of Boston College, and from 1884 to 1887 he kept the college's ship steady in weather that was not all calm: the enrolments were climbing, the building fund was arguing, and the Society's business in Rome was calling his name across the Atlantic. Walk the hill in Chestnut Hill on a Friday evening and you can still see the route of the old processions, the elm-shaded avenue, the little stone gate where the president received his visitors. The college's own records show him presiding in 1885 over a commencement from which the speeches have survived, and the chapel register for Easter 1886 carries his name in the president's column, ink slightly smudged on the left. It was in 1887, for the second of his terms in Italy, that he set down the pen of the presidency and took the steamer south. Three years at the chair, eighteen years at the Society's schools, two postings to Rome — the arithmetic of a life spent in the service of other people's education.

★ Stop 5 — Rome: The Jesuit House, Two Postings of Three Years ★

In 1881 and again in 1887 Father Boursaud served three-year terms in the capital itself, assistant secretary to the Jesuit Superior General for the English-speaking world — in practice, the man who wrote the letters between the Society's American and English colleges and the chair in Rome. The house keeps its own archive, and the archive keeps his hand: neat, fast, and never once in a hurry. Ask for Folder 41. Ask politely. The guide's advice for Rome is the same as for Emmitsburg: go in the morning, stand in the quiet, and do not be afraid to look up at the sky the way a man from New York and France both would.

★ Stop 6 — March 19, 1902: The Last Entry ★

The trail ends where his life ended, on March 19, 1902, at the age of sixty-one. The last page of the ledger is a short one, and the ledger is closed. Walkers are invited to lay a stone at the end of the path — the stones are kept in a crate by the little gate, and the box for the names of the dead is filled, as boxes are, in due season. Sixty-one years, two skylines, one robe. It was a good trail.

★ The Lost Property Counter — Notices of the Season ★

The counter at Stop 3 keeps the trail's one official list of mislaid items, and this season's postings read as follows. The register is kept verbatim, in the clerk's own hand, and is here reproduced without alteration:

KOVALENKO: The author has now heard, Your Honour, the defendants · Mr. Hall Cause No. TA-3314/19 · In re Broadway , Docket No. B-1796/88 · related: the lost property list.

Items claimed last season: one black cassock (dry-cleaned), one Latin grammar, 1879 edition, one silver watch stopped at 4:41, and a set of keys that opens nothing anyone can name. The clerk's standing rule is that an item is not returned until the finder and the claimer can both say where it was last seen. The register is opened to visitors on weekdays between nine and three.
