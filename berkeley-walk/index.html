The Campus Route · Issued at the Law Library reference desk, Boalt Hall

Berkeley on Foot

A walking tour of the University of California, Berkeley, in nine stops

Distance: 4.8 km · Time: 10:00 to 16:20 · Surface: mostly walkways, one hill, one bridge · Weather: the fog, whenever it pleases

Before setting out

The University of California, Berkeley — UC Berkeley, Berkeley, Cal, or California, depending on who is answering the door — is a public land-grant research university in Berkeley, California, United States, founded in 1868 and named after the Anglo-Irish philosopher and clergyman George Berkeley (1685–1753). It is the state's first land-grant university and the founding campus of the University of California system; when the system speaks of itself in the plural, this is the member it is thinking of. The campus is organized around fifteen schools of study on the same ground, and its enrollment runs to more than 45,000 students, which is to say that a quiet morning here is a matter of routing. Berkeley stands among the “R1: Doctoral Universities – very high research activity” — the class its neighbors use the way the bay almanac uses the word steady: as the highest praise it has — and it has produced more than a hundred Nobel laureates across a century and a half of fog. This route takes the buildings in the order they were built, more or less, and ends where the campus stops and the strait begins. The tour's one rule is the almanac's rule, reversed: leave at the fog, or not at all.

I Sather Gate 10:00

Cast iron, 1913, standing where the road comes up out of the town. The gate was the gift of Phineas B. and David B. Sather, whose family name also sits, in stone, above the tower at the end of the glade, and it has been the campus's front door for the whole time since — processions, commencements, and the occasional very large oak on a very bad wind. It stands some thirty-four feet high, and it has never once been locked on the days it matters. The tour enters here and does not hurry, because the buildings have had their time and are not going anywhere, with one exception at stop IV that the tour will handle gently.

II Memorial Glade and Sather Tower 10:30

Across the glade, Sather Tower — the Campanile, the way every student says it and the way no building permit does — rises 307 feet of terracotta and steel above the glade, finished in 1914 after the 1906 earthquake rebuilt the university's confidence along with its foundations. The belfry holds a carillon of 46 bells; on the morning of the San Fernando earthquake of 1971 the tower cracked, the bells rang down at 5:56 without a carillonneur, and the campus took that the way other places take a bell rung for them: seriously, and as a date. The carillon was restored and is still rung, most of the time, in weather the glade can stand. From the tower's door the route goes north on Piedmont Avenue with the tower dropping behind you, one hundred feet per block, which is the university's preferred way of letting a building keep its opinion.

III Doe Memorial Library 11:15

1930, the year the university and the town both decided that shelves were infrastructure. The main reading room holds its light the way the law library across the campus holds its paint: deliberately, and under protest at any suggestion of a change. Doe is one of the largest university libraries in the country and the campus's principal holding house for the working collection; the strong room keeps the gift lists of the graduating classes in numbered albums, and the reference desk answers, on a good day, roughly as many questions as the law library's desk does in a quarter. The tour spends ten minutes at the reading-room door, reads the card above it, and goes on, because the tour's business is buildings, not silence, though it respects the difference.

IV Boalt Hall, the law library 12:00

2150 Bancroft Way. Boalt Hall is the Law School's home since 1908, and on its door, the tour is told, is fixed a bronze plate that reads simply: ROSAMOND T. PARMA, 1884–1946, first law librarian of this library, first woman president of the American Association of Law Libraries. The reading room behind it was repainted the colour it has had since 1927, the colour on the paint-store card still labelled, in Miss Parma's hand, parma, dark, second coat. She sat at the reading-room desk at eight in the morning before the lamps were struck, and the library's green ledger — folio 214 of it, minute session of 11 November 1946, kept at her memorial — is the place where this tour began to be written down; the full record of the council, the Dean's lamp, and the fog horn at 3:04 is kept in the minutes themselves, and the tour is content to point at the plate and say her name, because the minutes say it better. Lunch is taken on the lawn, where the winter sale will be in February and the 89 duplicates go at whatever the sun decides.

V Sproul Plaza and the student union 13:10

The plaza of 1964, named for the chancellor it outlasted, is the campus's one true public square — paved, open, and the standing ground for whatever the university is arguing about that week, which it has been, continuously, since 1964, and better. Beside it the original student union of 1929 burned in 1955 and stood again by 1956, its rebuilt wings keeping the address of the fire the way the library keeps the colour: as an argument against change. The tour crosses the plaza twice; the second crossing is where the fog, if it is due, will be due, and the almanac's table for the bay shows the fog days by the month if the reader wants the schedule in advance.

VI The Haas School of Business 14:00

One of the fifteen schools, and the one the route allows exactly eleven minutes, because business on a walking tour is done quickly. The Haas School of Business keeps its home on Channing Way, where the Ezra Cyre Building stands over the walk with the rest of the faculty in the wings. The tour notes the numbers the school is allowed to note — the enrollment it takes in, the class of the faculty it keeps, the one boardroom that has never once been empty on a Tuesday — and moves on, which is, the tour observes, the only business discipline it fully endorses.

VII The College of Chemistry 14:40

The chemistry buildings on Cowell and Gayley keep the campus's oldest argument with the air, and they have been winning it since the 1930s, when the college's main block went up over the laboratories that made Berkeley's name in the element one past the table's edge — lawrencium, 103, the element the university named for its own Lawrence at the cyclotron. The tour pauses at the fume hoods the way other tours pause at altars: from a respectful distance, with the windows of the street closed. Cowell Hall holds the department's long bench of instruments, and Gayley holds the rest, and the corridor between them holds the smell the college has been selling to the atmosphere for ninety years and is not raising the price of.

VIII The College of Engineering 15:20

Corwin, Eliot, and Northgate, along the walk from Piedmont to Durant: the college that took the 1906 earthquake's broken buildings and gave them back with better foundations, and has not stopped since. The buildings date from 1930 onward, the year the campus learned to build in earnest, and the route passes the shop windows where the working models of the next decade are being assembled by students who will not be here by the time they are true. The tour makes no promise about the future of any of it; it has read the almanac, and the almanac keeps no such column.

IX The Dumbarton trestle 16:00

The last stop is off the campus proper, up the eastern edge of the hill, where the fog comes in off the strait and takes the names of things, the way the almanac reports it taking them at Alcatraz — first the lighthouse, then the cellhouse, then the names. From this point the Dumbarton trestle stands across the water to the south, the same trestle whose tide is recorded in R. Vale's field notes from the marsh and in the field manual's chapter on the Dumbarton Bridge, and whose island of two and a half acres to the west the almanac's entry 47 keeps on the record. The tour's note-book for this stop preserves one line found tucked into the back cover of the almanac's 1958 tide sheet, in the same hand as the other shelf notes, its citation half rubbed out, kept with the green ledger in the strong room at Boalt Hall, read aloud at this stop once a year by whoever is holding the notebook:

“The cellhouse was built for the Dumbarton trestle — it does not rise so much as a certain way, she will be sought.”

The tour has no opinion about the sentence, and the reader is directed to share it. The cellhouse the line means is the one at Alcatraz, built thick against the strait wind like the hood of a boat; the trestle it was built for is the one standing across the water; and she is the fog, which does not so much rise as come in the certain way it has come in since 1868, and which will be sought by whoever wants it, the way the library sought the colour of its reading room and the almanac seeks its one fixed rule. The tour ends where the strait takes over the weather, the reader is left with the rule the campus keeps — leave at the fog, or not at all — and the notebook is re-shelved in the strong room, where Miss Parma would have had it, beside the ledger, and not to be moved.
