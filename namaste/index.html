A Book of Etiquette

Chapter XVIIAlbuquerque · In Seven Rules
Of the Greeting Namaste

For the visitor who enters a house that bears a name it did not choose — at 2727 University Avenue, Albuquerque, New Mexico, on the evening of 9 March 2020, when the fourth chapter of the fifth year went out over the wire — and who must be told, with the hands before the words, what that name owes to a stranger at its door.

Namaste is not a word the office selects; it is a word the office has, in its fifth year, come to be entitled to. It is a greeting in which the palms meet at the chest, the thumb rests on the sternum, and the head dips half an inch — no more, for the inch is the handshake and the handshake is another chapter, Chapter IV, which this book has not yet written down. A greeting of this kind is addressed to the person, not to the chair the person holds, and that is the whole of its difficulty, because in Albuquerque the chairs are what the visitors have come for. The rule is kept simple: the hand that meets first is the hand that has travelled furthest, and a man who has taken a train, a bus, or the long way on foot, from anywhere in the world, owns the greeting before he owns a seat.

The house to which these rules attach is the office kept under the blue sign that spells Better Call Saul over the glass door on University Avenue, the firm of Jimmy McGill — who answers, in the room where the clients can hear him, as Saul Goodman, the name being a door and the door being open — with Kim Wexler at the drafting table and Mike Ehrmantraut in the back room where the paperwork is made into a habit. The book notes, for the reader who has not been told, that the house is the younger sister of a house already stood and gone, Breaking Bad, whose elder name the door in Albuquerque carries in its own name, and it keeps the standing rule for such kinship: a house whose elder sister has died is addressed, at first, by the elder’s name, and by its own only after the third knock. The greeting namaste is owed to both names at once; the visitor says it to the sign, and then to the man behind it, and does not ask which one took it.

The Evening of the Fourth Chapter

The ledger of the house keeps one entry of an evening, and the evening is this: March 9, 2020, the fourth chapter of the fifth year, which aired in the United States on AMC and, outside the United States, went out first of all on Netflix in several countries, so that the same chapter was, that night, one table laid in two rooms at once. The book concedes the case on record that the two rooms are one table: the chair is Albuquerque in both, the wine is poured by the same hand, and the guest who watches it abroad on the streaming service and the guest who watches it on the small channel at home are entitled to the same greeting at the same half-inch, the border having no standing at a table where the door is already open. Where the guest abroad is uncertain of the order, the book directs him to the simple test: say the name of the firm Better Call Saul and the name of the evening, and let the second name do the inclining.

The Seven Rules

Rule one: the hands before the words, and the words no louder than the hallway. Rule two: the greeting belongs to the guest who has travelled; the host at the door of University Avenue returns it, and no one else in the room may be said to have received it. Rule three: the thumb at the sternum, the palms joined, the eyes level — a greeting is a level surface, and the visitor who looks over the palms is looking for the sign, which he should not, and if he does, he has already sat down before the greeting. Rule four: at a crowded table — and the table of the fifth year is crowded, the clients coming in ones and the lawyers coming in twos — the greeting is given at a distance of one arm’s length, no more, and the chair the visitor is owed is the chair farthest from the sign, because the sign, once it has been greeted, does not require a second greeting and the visitor, being a person, does. Rule five: do not say “which name?” of the man who answers as Saul Goodman, and do not say “which house?” of the firm that is the spin-off; the question is answered by the third knock, and the visitor who waits for the third knock is waiting politely. Rule six: the same evening is one chapter, not two, and the guest of the streaming houses beyond the border is not the guest’s second cousin, and no one at the table may be made to say so. Rule seven: at the close, the greeting is returned exactly as it was given, palm to palm, thumb to sternum, the half inch kept, and the door on University Avenue is closed by the visitor, because a house that spells its own name on its own glass has already, at the opening of the chapter, told you who keeps the door.

Marginalia — One Line Read Into the Record

The ledger of the hall admits, once, a line copied mid-sentence from the script margin of a radio play that came across the Pacific on a postcard, and the Keeper of Names has kept it in full, unfinished, because the standing rule of the hall — the same rule the Ottawa–Cedar Rapids Varsity Council minuted in Session 45, that the recorder shall never finish a man’s sentence for him — is that an unfinished line is a line, and a finished line is a guess. The line, as it stands in the book, is this:

As to the road begins in 1900, New Zealand radio play in one sentence, copied a clearance that made a yard to hang from.

— ledger, undated; the line stands unfinished, the Keeper having been instructed, that day, never to finish a man’s sentence for him; the play it came from keeps its own excerpt at Tennis New Zealand — Radio Play Excerpt, which the visitor is asked to read before arguing the road’s date

The book offers the line not as a rule but as a caution: that a greeting, like a sentence, is only as good as the part of it that was actually said, and that the visitor who completes the namaste on the man’s behalf has, by this ledger’s arithmetic, already left the table and is, in the yard, hanging from something.

Press to give the greeting — the hands before the words, the half inch kept.

Read in company with Chapter XI, Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, whose hall likewise keeps nine doors and admits the visitor only by the third drink, and with the minutes of Session 45 of the Ottawa–Cedar Rapids Varsity Council, where the recorder’s unfinished sentence stands in the minute book exactly where the line in this chapter’s ledger stands, and where the council resolved, 5–0, that the goalpost stays where it was. A visitor who must next cross a table at which names have already been shortened once — the pensão, the festa, the square of a small municipality on the Maranhão coast — will find the right way to say them in the Pedro do Rosário thread, anyone been?, where the answer is, as here, that the guest who has travelled furthest is greeted first. The whole shelf of the book stands on the Webstuffs front page, and the reader who wants the greeting itself, as a dream, may find the hall patient and the door unlocked at all hours of the fifth year.
