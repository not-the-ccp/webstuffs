The Geologic Time Scale

1785–2026 · of the Kupres valley, formerly of Sèvres · survived by four eons, six eras, twenty-four periods, and one hundred and twenty-six epochs

The geologic time scale, that great ledger of the deep Earth, died on a Thursday in Gornje Vukovsko, in the pantry of the Sana stewpan kitchen, where he had kept house for forty-one years. The hour of his death is the one question the valley still cannot close. By the civil reckoning it was the hour of the wall; by the exact reckoning of the atomic clocks in Cabinet 9 — the same cabinet that has kept the placard for International Atomic Time beside his own card — it was 37 seconds earlier. The municipal office has declined to say which hour shall stand in the register, which is the correct treatment of it, and the only person in the valley who had ever meant to say was gone.

He was born in 1785 in the sense that James Hutton first gave him a world long enough to need him — deep time, no beginning visible — and he was given his name and his manners in 1859, when Charles Lapworth of Manchester coined “Paleozoic” and the old divisions stopped being argument and became grammar. In the middle years he learned his real trade: reading the rock record with the principles of chronostratigraphy, placing one sequence of strata against another until every outcrop on Earth had a place in line, and then, in the later decades, letting the geochronologists nail his boundaries down where they stood with radiometric dates — the 66-million-year line where the iridium shows, the first shelly fossils of the Cambrian, the ash beds, the magnetic stripes, each boundary weighed and dated to within the width of a filing needle. It was, the register notes, a life of facing things the right way up.

He is survived by his divisions, who were with him at the end: the eons first, then the eras, the twenty-four periods, and the hundred and twenty-six epochs, most of them young by his reckoning and several of them barely named. He was mourned, as he had been in his working life, by the Earth sciences in full — geologists, paleontologists, geophysicists, geochemists, and paleoclimatologists, who had used him all along to place the timing and relationships of events in geologic history — and by the smaller company that fed him: the Sana kitchen, the chef Mehmed Kovać, and the wall of twenty-eight clocks that he had never cared for. He was a man of strata. The stews left the pan 37 seconds early by his reckoning, and he was the one who knew, and he never once mentioned it, which the register counts among his best manners.

The last line of his life was not written in the register at all. It was written in red ochre ink, on the last leaf of the build log of The Seventy-First — the 1978 Brisbane Rugby League grand final, Eastern Suburbs against Fortitude Valley, in balsa and brass — which the builder of the model had pinned under the clock at table 20, the one table in the world that runs Brisbane time at lunch, at UTC+10:00. That log, with its thirty-two match figures and its eight little name plates, is on file as the build log of The Seventy-First, and it is the log the scale had grown up against, the one that kept its hours ten hours past the meridian on his account. The entry reads, and the museum preserves it word for word as it stands on the page:

“Beat up the coast, the Valley under the clock at table 20, UTC+10:00 — and a match figure set facing the wrong way is a first.”

— last leaf of the build log, entered at the bedside of the ledger · the weather had beaten up the coast all night, the Fortitude Valley figures stood under the wall clock, and for the first time in a season of thirty-two careful bodies, one was set facing the wrong way

It was, for a builder who had written in the third entry that a ball figure facing the wrong way is a lie a model can’t recover from, a first of the worst kind: the one error the model had not been allowed. And it was, for the scale, the last thing he ever oversaw — the last sequence in the wrong order he would ever have a hand in setting right. The curator read the line into the death register before closing it, and the municipal office, which has dated most things in the valley without trouble, let this one stand undated, exactly as it let stand the brown-ink margin on the back of the stew menu. A first, both times.

The service was at the kitchen, on the hour of the table, and the sewn-up lamb stew left the pan 37 seconds early, as it had all along, and now no one in the valley keeps the difference anywhere except in the price. The blank plate held by the municipal office in Cabinet 9 has been engraved at last: it bears the strata column, the K–Pg line marked at 66 Ma, and the small note that the ledger is closed, the boundaries dated, and the stew still early. The table review of the kitchen will not be amended, on the grounds that a review filed in the afternoon of one life is not rewritten for the morning of the next. The scale rests. The strata, as he insisted all his life, do not.
