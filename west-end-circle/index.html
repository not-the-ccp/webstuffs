  Circular No. 23  ·  the spring term, 2002  ·  price, one shilling or a story

From the Chairs

Members, — What we study this term is not a style but a habit. It is the habit that took hold in literature, in painting, in music, and on the stage in the first years of the present century: a self-conscious refusal to carry on as before. The movement has no one founder and no fixed manifesto, and this is, I am told, its first embarrassment and its last. What it has, and what it has in abundance, is an insistence that the new way of seeing — experimental, abstract, turned inward, suspicious of the ready-made — is not a decoration upon the old order but a replacement of it.

The date the books give is the late nineteenth century, and the reasons they give are plain ones. Western life had gone secular; science had taken the seat of honour at the dinner table; and a great deal of what had been called morality, optimism, and convention had come to feel like a coat cut for a man who had stopped being tall. The modernists felt what they called a growing alienation from all three, and they did not feel it quietly. They wanted to change how human beings in a society interact and live together, and they set about it with the tools at hand: the broken sentence, the fragment, the stream, the square canvas, the twelve-tone row, the building of glass.

It is the building of glass that has made the study local. We keep our room at the west end of the Forum, the glass box by the river that opened in 2001 over the ground where the old Central Library burnt in 1994 — the same winter this club was founded, meeting then in a temporary premises behind the chandler's. The new building is three buildings in one, and it keeps its own clocks; the steel frame shows through the glazing like the ribs of a large, well-behaved fish, and the brick runs around it like a belt so that the glass is never quite free of its masonry. We have argued at length that this is modernism as a piece of furniture: an experiment that still knows it is a library. The diary our own E. kept at the Forum in the first snow — 14 December 2001, seven inches on the roof, entered in the blue book on the kitchen shelf — is filed in our archive as Document W.1, and the entry is printed in full at The Forum, Norwich — Diary Entry, 14 December 2001.

The Wall Card

Every member has seen it, and every member has been tempted to amend it, and no member has. It hangs in the reading room, pinned with a brass paperweight shaped like an apple, above the second shelf from the window. It is a single line in one hand, and it reads, word for word, exactly this:

“Entremets of apples mixed with the stock, onion, and serve with the stories I left on the west end has the warmest lamp in Norfolk.”

The circle does not pretend to have parsed it. It was heard, more or less, in the winter of 1998, in the temporary premises, during a discussion of whether a sentence may be allowed to be two sentences at once; it was written down by D. Whitfield, who was on the door and not listening properly, which we consider the correct posture; and it has been on the wall ever since. It is a mangled sentence — a menu that has walked into a confession and sat down beside an address — and we keep it mangled on purpose. The movement we read began by listening, very carefully, to what the old order would not say, and by writing it down before it changed its mind. Whatever the card is, it is one of those: a thing that arrived already broken, and has since behaved with great dignity. The one clause everyone agrees on is the last. The library at the west end, as visitor #286 of the Forum's own guestbook put it, has the warmest lamp in Norfolk, and the circle reads there to two in the morning in the dark months. We have checked the lamp. It is still the warmest.

Ledger of Notices

  • Meetings. Thursdays at 7 o'clock, the reading room at the west end, term ending 2 May. This term's paper is the modernist sentence; next term's will be the modernist door, and members are asked to bring keys.
  • The snow. Seven inches were measured on the Forum's roof in December, from the east stair, with the carpenter's stick, and the winter ledger reads it back in the same hand. The blue book remains on the kitchen shelf, where it keeps its habits accordingly.
  • Subscriptions. One shilling per term, or one story, told or written. The treasury (R. Okafor) confirms that stories are paid in full and that the appetite for them has not changed since 1994.
  • Lost. One brass paperweight, shaped like an apple, last seen holding down the minutes of the 12th of February. If it has migrated to another shelf, it is asked to return; the card is holding by a single pin.

Correspondence from Yerevan

Our sister minutes-keepers, the Naga Listeners' Club, write from the Chancery of the Hrazdan to report that a case has now closed in which their ledger, as usual, ran at sixteen frames per second. In Case No. 1726 of 2024, Aghajan v. Petrosyan, a waiter at The Pink Table sued a diner of Table 9 under the law of reciprocity, in its positive form and in its negative, and both parties were found in default, each in his own way. The court fined the defendant one khash. We note, for the file, the defendant's own account of how he paid his table for forty-one years — with his chair, with his silence, and with the stories he left on the menu board. It is, we think, the only sentence in the record that rhymes with our wall card, and it is why the card is no longer the only one of its kind in the circle. The Naga Listeners are asked to keep their ledger open to us; we shall keep ours to them, at the same speed.
