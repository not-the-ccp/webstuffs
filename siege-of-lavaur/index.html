A Book of Etiquette of the Languedoc
Being a Guide to the Manners of the South, for Travellers of the Northern Hosts

Chapter III

Of the Month’s Long Table,
and the Siege of Lavaur, 1211

✦ ✦ ✦

Set down by the Master of Ceremonies of the Crusader Table, from the camp on the hill above the Tarn, in the spring of 1211, and corrected twice in the field.

1211

§ 1. The Setting of the Table

In the south of France, where the town of Lavaur stands upon its hill above the river Tarn, a siege is a kind of long dinner to which one side is invited and the other side is, as it were, the roast. The present chapter concerns the manners observed at such a dinner in the year 1211, when Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester and leader of the Albigensian Crusade, came against the town to do what the crusader effort has done, and intended to do, elsewhere in the Languedoc: to take the Cathars out of the south of France, which they were reputed to be, in considerable numbers and in poor taste. The garrison of Lavaur, under the viscount Amalric and the castelans of the hill, resolved to eat the dinner out. The siege began in the late March or the early April of 1211, and the table was not cleared until 3 May of the same year, when the garrison, having finished its provisions and its arguments, surrendered.

The Master of Ceremonies advises the traveller who finds himself at a siege to remember that in the Languedoc the host is the one with the machines. The town, though it holds the high ground, is expected to be civil about the mangonels. The Earl of Leicester kept his camp on the southern hill and did not, by any account that survives, raise his voice before his second course was laid.

Marginalia

The month of the siege ran, by the camp calendar, from the last week of March or the first week of April to the third of May — some twenty-eight to thirty days, which is to say about a month, which is to say the length of a very well-kept Lent. No banquet in the Languedoc, the Master notes, is kept longer than a siege, and none is ever so badly fed.

For the reader who wishes to compare manners: the feast of the house of Artois, kept in the cook of Count Robert II and set down from the Arles expedition to the Golden Spurs, is a table of the same century and a better-supplied one, and is docketed at length in the Pottage of Artois, Recipe Card No. 43. There the quantities survive; here, regretfully, only the etiquette.

§ 2. The Sittings of the Month

The etiquette of a siege in the south, as practiced at Lavaur, admits three sittings. At the first sitting, the besiegers pitch their camp, survey the walls, and make what the French call la politesse of engines: the mangonel is presented, the ladders are offered for inspection, and no one is yet hurt. It is at this sitting that the besieged are obliged to answer, courteously, whether they will send down bread or send down a delegation. The garrison of Lavaur sent down neither, which the camp ledger records as a breach of manners so flagrant that the whole month had to be spent on it.

At the second sitting, which is to say weeks three and four of the siege, both sides eat what the hill gives them. The manners observed at this sitting are few, and the chapter lists them all: one shoots over the wall, and one does not shoot the wall; one answers an arrow with an arrow and not with an insult; and neither side, whatever the hunger, may eat a herald. The Earl de Montfort, who had fought at the relief of Béziers and at other long tables further north in the crusade, was known to keep this last rule with particular strictness, and the garrison, knowing it, took advantage of the rule with particular freedom.

At the third sitting, on the third of May, the garrison surrendered. The etiquette of surrender in the Languedoc is the one sitting at which the manners are entirely the surrenderer’s: it is he who names the hour, the stair by which his knights descend, and the ground on which they lay down their spurs. All three were granted. The surrender was kept in good form, which is the only kind of good form that remains to a man who has fed on his own horses.

§ 3. The Counting of the Tables

The chapter must now set down the figures which the camp ledger sets down, and it sets them down in the manner of a bill: eighty knights of the garrison, taken with their horses and their spurs, were put to the sword, and with them, in the same hill and the same morning, some four hundred of the Cathars who had taken the Consolamentum and sheltered in the town with the garrison. The Master of Ceremonies, who is obliged to record the arithmetic of a surrender before he may record its manners, records it here in full, in ink, and without apology, because a bill of fare is a bill of fare. The town itself was burned the following day, which the ledger enters against the garrison’s account and marks, with a small and very dry hand, paid in full.

Readers are referred, for the arithmetic of the wider crusade and its many longer tables, to the front matter of this book at the office of the Master of Ceremonies, and for a chapter of the same house that keeps its ledgers by the salt and the sea rather than by the Tarn, to Chapter VII of the Dry Coast, on the whale’s feast at Swakopmund, where one hollowed head does the work of eighty knights and no one is marked paid in full.

§ 4. The Standing Orders

The chapter keeps three standing orders, which the traveller at a siege table in the Languedoc is expected to observe, and which the camp of 1211 observed in the order given, the second being the one the town could not, and the third being the one the town would not.

  1. Order the First. At a siege in the south, never let the bread run out before the argument does; a garrison that is hungry is a garrison that has answered, and its answer is the wall.
  2. Order the Second. If the host keeps a month-long table, he is bound, at the end of it, to settle the bill before the weather; the Earl de Montfort settled his on the morning of the fourth of May, which the ledger calls, in its own modest way, the season’s earliest reckoning.
  3. Order the Third. The herb course. Here the chapter must repeat the standing order of the whole book, in the words of the book, as it stands in the front register and admits no paraphrase: “One shall not say what the parsley remains, under Section 12.” The Master adds, in smaller ink, that at Lavaur the parsley did not remain, and that therefore no one, under Section 12, said anything at all, and that this is the quietest line in the book.

So the chapter ends, as the ledger ends, with the table cleared and the hill given back to the weather. The reader who has kept these manners at a siege in the Languedoc may set his hand against the page; the reader who has not is referred, gently and without further comment, to the front register, where Section 12 stands, as it stood in 1211, and as it stands in any year in which parsley is on the table at all.
