The Nakameguro Annex · Eastern Counter of the Album Keepers

Tokyo

Frequently Asked Questions, set down in writing at the counter, forty-seven steps down from the Meguro bridge

Nakameguro — — — The attic, Portland — — — the counter is sixteen hours ahead; do not apologize for it

問 一 What is Tokyo, exactly?

Tokyo, officially the Tokyo Metropolis, is the capital and most populous city of Japan. The population of the city proper was over 14 million as of 2023, which is more than the entire boathouse congregation has ever been, and we say that with pride. It is Japan's economic center, the seat of the Japanese government, and the seat of the Emperor of Japan. When a member asks us to describe the city in one breath, the counter answers in one breath: a metropolis of twenty-three wards that behaves like a single large record, all sides playing at once.

問 二 How big is it, really?

Bigger than the city proper admits. The Greater Tokyo Area, which includes Tokyo and parts of six neighboring prefectures, is the third-most populous metropolitan area in the world, with 33 million residents as of 2025. For scale the club keeps one figure only: the attic's reel counter would need to turn for nearly four years straight before it read as many revolutions as the metro area has residents, and it has not kept up once. You cannot drive across it. You can, however, commute across it, and many of the 33 million do, daily, in both directions, without flipping.

問 三 Where does it sit?

Lying at the head of Tokyo Bay, Tokyo is part of the Kantō region, on the central coast of Honshu, Japan's largest island. The Bay is the city's inside sleeve: everything the city wants to keep is docked there, and everything it wants to send is loaded there. The annex's own building stands on the opposite gesture of the coast, on a river — the Meguro — that runs east a short way and gives up into the Bay. Water is the city's way of keeping a side B.

問 四 Who runs it?

The Tokyo Metropolitan Government administers Tokyo's central 23 special wards, which formerly made up Tokyo City; various commuter towns and outlying municipalities ring them, and beyond those the hills run on to Izu, where the city simply gives up and becomes weather. A ward is, in the counter's plain words, a track: 23 of them on the central side, each with its own pressing, its own pressing plant's worth of streets, and a share of the backbeat. The government sits in Shinjuku, which is to say, it sits on the busiest crossing in the country and gets on with it.

問 五 Why does the Album Keepers keep a floor in Tokyo at all?

Because the reissue press lives here. The Nakameguro Annex occupies a former listening room whose landlord runs a noodle shop on the ground floor and a small lacquer-and-vinyl works two floors up; the 2023 gold-lacquer reissue of We Are Fearless was pressed there, in a run of nine hundred, the sleeves stamped by hand before the glue dried. The Gallery 4 placard at the museum — Contemporary Worship Music — tells the genre's longer story and gives Fearless BND its un-embarrassed entry; the Annex is where the genre's vinyl actually smells like Tokyo, which is to say, like noodles and warm varnish. Members of the club, founded on 24 February 2017 in the boathouse, are admitted to the Annex on the strength of the same witness: one complete, uninterrupted listening, whichever counter sits in the chair across from you.

問 六 Where does the first copy of a reissue go?

A question asked at every pressing, and always the same answer. When the gold lacquer came off the press, the first run was allocated by hand, one name to a sleeve, the sleeves fanned on the counter in a half-circle like a hand of cards. But the 21st century's album sales went first to the Secretary. Copies one through forty-seven were addressed to 2234 NW Flanders Street, name H. M. Voss, title Secretary, room the whole building, with the instruction the club's charter requires: listen in order, and say so. The remaining eight hundred and fifty-three went to the Nakameguro counter, where they sell at the rate the noodle shop sells broth — steadily, and never on a Tuesday.

問 七 Does the Annex bid in the Lot 47 auction?

It does, and it has lost, and we keep the receipts. The Flanders Te Deum, the sixteenth-century polyphony leaf found in the boathouse beams, remains at Flanders & Flanders — Lot 47 in the auction catalogue — and the club's bid, made from the attic in a chair that creaks in E♭, was outbid three times. The Annex is not permitted to bid on the same leaf from the other side of the planet; the Secretary drew that line in lacquer. What the Annex may do is play the leaf's four voices on a Tuesday evening, unbid and unbothered, and that is what it does, with the tenor holding its one pitch against the rest, as the tenor always has.

問 八 What time do the Annex listenings run?

The club meets the first Thursday at 19:30 in the attic; in Nakameguro that hour arrives at 11:30 the following morning, which the annex has learned to call Thursday-bright. The two counters keep the same session by the same clock rule: the attic begins, the Annex follows, and the recording must be finished before either says good evening or good morning. The clocks above the counter run both cities at once, and the reel counter — annex unit, not to be confused with attic unit No. 2 — reads its revolutions in both. The newsletter travels the other way: The Long Side, No. 47, reaches the counter with the Friday post, which is the same post that carries the noodle shop's delivery notes, and no one here tells them apart.

問 九 What, if anything, does the city owe the hush?

More than one would expect. Tokyo is a city of 33 million and it still keeps the hush between the final chord and the next amen: the platform doors close on a full train, and for the length of one bar nobody speaks. The Annex plays the last track of We Are Fearless on the 147-1 train on clear nights, at a volume the conductor allows and the passengers accept, and the hush arrives on schedule, which is more than the boathouse roof ever managed. Members returning to Portland carry the practice home; it is the annex's one export, and it does not go into the sleeves. The counter keeps one copy of the Lamplighters' ledger, The Lamplighter's Ledger, No. 82 — the vigil a small club of lamp-keepers kept for the fifty-eight dead in a Baghdad church that autumn — and the register's order is read from it once, in full, on a Thursday, in the hush.

問 十 How does a member ask the next question?

In writing, to the Secretary, who answers in the newsletter at the rate the questions arrive. The Annex counter keeps a box — forty-seven question slips at a time, the box no deeper than a record sleeve — and anything written on the reverse of a gold-lacquer sleeve counts as postage. If your question is about the city itself, the answers above are the annex's standing ones, and they are true to the numbers: 14 million in the city proper, 23 special wards, 33 million across the metropolitan area, one Bay, one Honshu. If your question is about the record, the museum's front door is where it is kept: return to the museum and ask there, where the audio guide plays the whole side, in order, and never stops at the flip.

Set down at the Nakameguro counter, in the room above the noodle shop, by the hand on the Eastern watch. The Annex is the eastern counter of the Album Keepers; the Atlantic is the flip side. Press the seal once per listening — no more; the lacquer counts.
