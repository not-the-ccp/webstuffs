Field Notes on the St Cuthbert Gospel

being a natural history, such as it is, of a pocket gospel book of the early eighth century — ninety-four vellum folios, 138 by 92 millimetres, in the earliest known Western bookbinding to survive — made from Chester to Durham in the Aprils of one damp year, with one flood and one reconstruction.

2 April · Chester · the leaves 32°F, rain in the morning, dry by noon; wind S, force 2

The book is kept at Chester Cathedral, and I am told the cathedral keeps it the way a larder keeps its one unbroken loaf. What is to be seen first, and I ask to be seen alone with it, is the text: ninety-four folios of vellum, the Gospel of St John set down in Latin in an uncial hand that the printers have called, with some envy, a model of elegant simplicity. The pages are 138 by 92 millimetres — under my calipers, 138.2 and 91.8, which I set down and then crossed out, because a book is not a beetle and does not wish to be calipered — and the whole is one of the smallest surviving Anglo-Saxon manuscripts, a pocket thing, made to be carried, the way one carries a nest to show a friend. The vellum is in outstanding condition for a book of this age: the skin shows its two faces, hair and flesh, the way a hedge shows its two faces to the two weathers, and the script lies on the surface with almost no bite. The text is essentially undecorated; there are word-rubrics at the opening of the chapters, and that is all. I note that the undecorated page, being the plainest surface in the building, is what the visitors lean nearest to.

3 April · London, the reading room · the leather 39°F, fog; the room held heat like a hive

The binding is not in Chester. It went to the British Museum in 1910, and the two halves of the book have lived apart for a century, which is the first odd fact to hold about it, and I held it a whole morning. The cover is goatskin, and it is the earliest known Western bookbinding to survive. Under the lens: the cross on the front cover is not painted and not inlaid but cut — a strip of the skin itself, turned up on a fold and pinned through a row of little holes, each hole still open after twelve hundred years, each one a stitch-hole the size of a gnat’s eye. The whole cover is worked in cross-stitch, a net of stitches over a finer net of stitching, four to six thousand by my slow count across both boards and the spine, sewn in waxed linen thread so fine that it lies in the leather like a root lies in clay. A binding is a skin made to hold other skins; I am at a loss for a closer parallel in nature, and I have thought of the cocoon all morning — a silk thing, a few millimetres thick, that has outlasted the city it was spun in. The back cover carries, in later letters, the words that named it for a saint: Cuthberti huius evangelium — this is the gospel of Cuthbert’s. I set the sentence down as it stands, because a naturalist’s first duty is the exact phrase.

5 April · Durham · the pyx, 1104 41°F, clear; the cathedral yard held a smell of stone and sheep

It takes its name from Saint Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, who died in 687 on the island of Inner Farne, off the Northumberland coast, and lay in his coffin through the centuries while the sea worked at his door. The story the yard tells, and the story is the story: in 1104, when Bishop William Odham opened the shrine for the translation of the body, the coffin gave up, with the relics and the silver pyxes, a little pyx of its own, and in the pyx, wrapped in silk, lay this book — in the saint’s own keeping, or in the keeping of his house, for a span of years I can only bracket between the ninth century and 1104. Five hundred and seventeen years, if the early date holds, between the book and its discovery. I write the number out and read it twice, because the number is the whole of the natural history here: a pocket gospel, carried to the sea-island, kept in a dead man’s pyx, and found by a bishop with a chisel. There is no better preserved book of its age in the West, and the preservation is not an accident; the book was stored the way a hermit stores everything — shut up, dark, dry, and loved at a distance.

9 April · the terrace · the flood 37°F, rain all day; the Ouse above the terrace by eleven feet at midnight

It took the season book — the one from January, with the weather, the wainscot sketches, the price of the eggs at Bishoppalmer, and the almanac pasted down on the flyleaf — and the second pair of spectacles, and the net, and nothing else of consequence, the net being the least of the three. I keep what I can of it here in the margins of this notebook, which was nailed to the desk. The loss, the almanac detail alone was worth keeping. It is the one line I am reconstructing now, from memory and from the cathedral almanac, and I set it down as reconstructed: on the night of the translation, the moon rose at 4:41, low over the farne, and the shrine was opened before it cleared the water. I do not know if the 4:41 is true. I know that I wrote it, and that I crossed nothing out, and that the Ouse has since retired to its banks and left my desk with one leg standing.

11 April · back in the reading room · the copy of 1892 34°F, sleet; the windowpane went over like ice on the pond

For the record, and for any reader who will not have a chisel: in 1892, the printer and scholar John Gregory of Chester made a facsimile of the whole book, cover, stitches and all, in forty copies, and the first of them now lies on the shelf by the door with a red ribbon in it, and I am told to treat the ribbon as a marker and not pull it. The original text stays at Chester; the original leather stays in London; and the two of them, which were one book and are now a pair, are the reason this notebook has a second page for April. I close the entry the way the book closes — small, dark, and dry — and I leave the reader with the fact I was set to note and could not bring myself to stop noting: the binding and the ninety-four folios are both in outstanding condition. A pocket gospel made to be carried, carried, and kept, and still, in the reading room, under the lens, the holes in the leather open as they were opened — or stitched — in the eighth century. It is the one specimen of the season that has not been moved by water, by flood, by museum, or by time.

E. F. M. — in the reading room, sleet on the pane
