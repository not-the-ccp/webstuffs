The Bavarian Stage • A Parlor Game of Memory, in One Leaf

Der Bärenkopf

Official rules — 1913 printing, from the back of the menu of the Gasthof zum Bären near Rosenheim, re-struck on white paper by the Munich printer of the stage-door keeper’s address book. A copy survives in the script drawer.

Four to six players. One deck of 32. One brooch. An afternoon, or, in the last winter, a whole season.

HAUS Gasthof
zum Bären

§1What the game is

Der Bärenkopf — “The Bear’s Head” — is a parlor card game invented at the kitchen table of the Gasthof zum Bären in 1908 and played there, and wherever the landlady’s script drawer travelled, ever since. It is a trick game with a memory rule at its center, and it is the only game in the drawer whose rules were printed rather than written, because the landlady would not let a single rule survive in more than one hand. It takes four to six players, a German 32-card pack, one separate card the size of a menu card on which a bear’s head is printed in one ink, and the landlady’s silver brooch in the form of an edelweiss. The brooch is the banker’s token and changes hands at every new deal. Because the brooch was worn on screen in every one of her episodes at the bear’s table, it is not hard to see why the token and the character stayed so firmly together in the house’s memory.

The separate card, the Bärenkopf, is dealt face-down to one player, who must play it as though it were the ace of hearts. Nobody is told who holds it. The game is won not by catching the bear’s head, but by being the player who names the player who holds it, and is believed, before the deck runs out.

§2Setup and the first deal

Players sit in the order of the table of 1908, which the landlady fixed and never varied: she took seat one, the postmistress’s brother seat two, the stage-door keeper’s widow seat three, and the rest of the room as it found itself. The banker is the player who most recently named the Bärenkopf correctly; at the very first deal, the landlady. The banker pins the brooch to the table’s cloth, deals clockwise, five cards to a hand, and lays the Bärenkopf card face-down on the table, where it sits for the whole round and may not be touched, turned, or whispered about.

§3A round

Play runs in tricks. The banker leads, and the player to the left follows suit if she can; the highest following card wins the trick, or the ace of the led suit takes it if the suit is broken. The winner of a trick leads the next, and may — once and only once per round — turn the face-down card and read the trick aloud from its back. Tricks containing the Bärenkopf are worth nine; a trick taken while the brooch is pinned on the winner’s own cuff, the banker’s courtesy, is worth five. When the deck is exhausted, the player still holding the Bärenkopf pays three into the pot, and the naming begins again: any player who can say whose hand it was in, and who is believed by the table, becomes banker and takes the brooch.

§4Scoring, and the ending

The first player to reach 99 points wins the table. There is a second ending, and the leaf takes the trouble to print it: the game also ends when any player, asked on the last trick to name the three cards she played in the previous round, cannot. The players of the house called this vergesslich, which is the only word in the rules not translated, and the house considered it the more honorable of the two endings, because at least the table had been paying attention.

§5Two variants

The Munich version

Played with six and with the memory rule doubled: each player must remember not three but six of her own cards, and the one who fails does not lose the table, but owes the pot a shot of what the pot contains. The variant is named for the university clinic across the river, where a certain Dr. Kraepelin kept, in the years when this manual was being played, a notebook of the same shape as the banker’s log and with the same terrible neatness. The house never said the two notebooks were related. The house said everything.

The Kraepelin version

Played exactly like the ordinary game, except that the banker keeps a written log: every trick, every naming, every refusal to be believed, in a small ledger carried in the coat pocket. The log of the winter of 1915–16 was never finished. The leaf instructs the reader that this is as it should be, and that a log may lawfully stop, but may not be started again in the same year.

§6House rules, added in the landlady’s hand

  1. The Bärenkopf card is never shown to a player’s face, only to the cloth.
  2. A naming made over a cold coffee does not count.
  3. The seat of the inventor is never given up, for the whole of her winter. (The log shows seat one empty, and the brooch still pinned, for eleven weeks. The table played on without a banker, which the rules do not provide for, and which the log does not comment on.)
  4. Whoever finds a beetle in the drawer does not clean the drawer. This is a rule, not a complaint.

Also included: a silver brooch in every one of its after calm.
