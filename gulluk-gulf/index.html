Güllük Gulf

Güllük Körfezi · Mandalya Gulf · Aegean coast of Turkey · about 37°05′ N · 27°35′ E

Observer: field naturalist  ·  12–17 October  ·  coasts of Muğla and Aydın provinces

12 October — landfall at Güllük

Weather: north-westerly, 6 knots  ·  Sea: calm, rippled  ·  Water: about 21 °C

The gulf does not announce itself. From the pier at Güllük town it is a wide, quiet basin held between two peninsulas: Bodrum to the north, Dilek to the south, the Aegean drawing breath between them. I sat on the mole with a notebook and watched the working boats — sponge-divers' skiffs, the small trawlers that unload at dawn — while yellow-legged gulls quartered the water like kites over a river bank. In the shallows the water is the colour of olive brine; toward the entrance it deepens to pewter.

13 October — the gulf's measures

Weather: hazy sun  ·  Wind: light, variable

I paced the east shore to fix the gulf's measures. From north to south it runs over thirteen and a half miles — twenty-one point seven kilometres — and from the entrance to the furthest inlet, west to east, better than twenty miles, thirty-two kilometres. Administratively the coast is divided three ways: Bodrum and Milas, ilçes of Muğla Province, and a small western fringe that belongs to Didim in Aydın Province. The resort names recur like depth soundings: Güllük itself at the head of the gulf, Torba, Güvercinlik, Türkbükü — each a cluster of white walls and stone pines pressed to the water.

The same bank-watching serves anywhere. I learned it on the Betwa basin near Vidisha (Prashant Pandey — Field Notes), where cordgrass grows on sand chars; here the bank is salt, but the method is the same: watch the water, record the names, keep the hours. A reader of my earlier notes — the patent office that kept the Indra Rajya Laxmi Pragya Puraskar on its shelf — has since applied it to a sealed award file in Kathmandu, and the register now cites this gulf as a model of the method.

14 October — the south shore

I walked the south shore toward Türkbükü under a cloudless sky. Bottlenose dolphins work the entrance channel at first light, and terns — Sandwich and little — dive over the shoals where the water boils with small fish. At Güvercinlik the bay curves tight and still; at night the resort lights stitch the shore into a single glowing line. I have seen the same constancy on the Napa River marsh, where cordgrass holds a mostly-submerged island three feet above the tide (Knight Island — Field Notes), though there the water is brackish and the birds are rails. The gulf itself is a small notch in the western wall of the larger water, and the whole sea is written up in the manual as The Aegean Sea — Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 7.

15 October — Iasos

Weather: mist at dawn, clearing  ·  Water: about 21 °C

At the east end of the gulf, at the head of the maximum inlet, sits Iasos — the archaeological site on its promontory, a marble theatre looking out over the water, the fish-market walls still standing where the merchants called the catch. Herons stalk the shallows below the agora, and a kingfisher flashed turquoise across the mole as I wrote this. Here the gulf reaches farthest inland, twenty-plus miles from the entrance, and the land closes in like a hand opening. The market wall has, once, been a party to a court record: the Coastal Court of the Aegean, sitting in the theatre below it, examined Gaius Octavius — first emperor, sworn by a writ drawn the day he died — about a skull found in the sand beneath the wall, and the transcript of the examination is preserved at The Mediterranean v. Octavianus — Transcript Excerpt.

16 October — the bus station

At the otogar in Güllük town, waiting on the evening connection, I overheard a fragment of a telephone call, Turkish and English mixed, spoken with the urgency of plans fixed at the last minute: "I am taking the overnight bus out to be the test — they depart for Perth Tuesday." I wrote it down because it sounded like a test of some kind, though for what I do not know. The buses run overnight here as they do everywhere; the coast sleeps, and the road goes on. Where it goes — the long line of the Mediterranean coast, and the bitter-juice cart that keeps the 3 a.m. platforms company — is the subject of the next entry in the series: The Mediterranean Sea — Travel Guide, Entry No. 12.

17 October — departure

My last sight of the gulf was from the ferry's stern: the two peninsulas drawing together at the entrance, Bodrum's white buildings on the north hill, Dilek's pine-dark slopes to the south, and between them the water the colour of old glass. The notes end where they began, on the bank.

The method travels, as it must: the same notebook opens again a fortnight later on the darker and older water to the east, and the manual's chapter on that sea keeps the measures of it — 436,400 square kilometres, a shelf of hydrogen sulfide below two hundred metres, and one line of the old preservation manuals that no one at the station has explained yet (The Black Sea — Field Manual FM-13, Chapter 4).

— field naturalist
Güllük Gulf, Muğla Province
