Dream Dictionary · Entry 732 · Dreamt 14 September 1846, and again, as the dictionary allows, whenever the room is dark enough

The Kot

कोट · कोट पर्ब

a place-name, an armory, a massacre · all at once · a meeting that was not a meeting

The Kot is, in this entry, the armory of the palace at the north foot of Hanuman Dhoka in Kathmandu, the long low room where the palace kept its guns, its powder, its swords, and its opinions of one another — and the night of 14 September 1846, on which the dream has always insisted. On that night Kaji Jung Bahadur Kunwar and his brothers — Bahadur, Bir, Ram, and the small brother Ambar, who carried the largest knife — killed some thirty to forty of the palace court in the armory itself: civil officials, military officers, and palace guards, among them the Prime Minister of Nepal and Chautariya Fateh Jung Shah, a relative of the King, with other senior ministers and army generals. The meeting had been called by Queen Rajya Laxmi Devi, the Queen Mother, and it had been called because that very same night her confidante, Kaji Gagan Singh Bhandari, was murdered while performing worship in his prayer room. The dream keeps the sequence exact, because the dream was never the part the night got wrong. The Kot meeting was called. The Kot meeting turned ugly.

In the dream the armory stands at the end of a hall of hung weapons, and the hall is lit by one lamp that is not allowed to go out, which is the dream's one law, and it is kept. At the head of the hall a clerk of the armory stands at the black gate with a list of marks. The marks are not letters in any alphabet the dreamer will later trust; they are notches, ticks, and two small flowers scratched in by a patient hand, one of which is clearly a marigold. The dream hands the dreamer the clerk's own order, written in the armory's own hand, and the order runs word for word, and the entry keeps it so, because the night has already taken everything else it wanted:

The Clerk was instructed to read the marks, paused, and then — only when the flowers appear, with great curiosity.

And the flowers do appear. On a sill in the armory, in a chipped clay jar that the dream does not explain and does not need to, marigold blossoms open, one by one, in the middle of the room, and the reading of the marks proceeds only on the side of the gate where the blossoms have come. The rest of the gate the dream leaves unread. This, the entry notes, is the dream's arithmetic: thirty to forty names, some of them the names of the court's very senior, and the gate gives back only the side it has flowered. The lamp does not go out. In the dream it never does, not then, not afterward, not in any of the years the dream re-runs the night at a slower rate, and not when the room is re-lit, a hundred and forty-four years on, for a different declaration. The dreamer is the clerk for one moment, and the clerk's hand is steady, and the hand is not the hand of anyone the dream names.

Interpretation

To dream of the Kot is to dream of a meeting called for one purpose that arrives as something else. The Queen Mother called it for Gagan Singh Bhandari, murdered that same night at his prayers, and the meeting went to the armory to say a thing about a murder and came out of it with thirty to forty fewer people and one more Prime Minister in the making. Jung Bahadur, Kaji of the western province, came in by a side door with his brothers and their men and went out, in the dream's telling and the year's, as the man who would hold Nepal's prime ministership until his death. The dictionary interprets the flowers thus: a gate that is read only where the blossoms come is a record that admits itself incomplete, and a record that admits itself incomplete is the only kind that can be lived with. The clerk's pause is the dictionary's one rule for such rooms. You do not read a gate while it is being written on. You wait for the flowers, which take their time, and then you read with great curiosity, and you stop where the blossoms stop, and you keep the lamp. The pause, not the reading, is what the night is about. The night, in the dream, is about the pause.

Associated imagery

  • A long low armory under Hanuman Dhoka, the walls hung with guns and swords and the opinions of the court, one lamp burning
  • A black gate in a black door, notched with marks in a patient hand, two of the marks clearly a marigold
  • Thirty to forty names in a small clerk's hand, and the gate giving back only the side it has flowered
  • Marigold blossoms opening in a chipped clay jar in the middle of the room, one by one, unexplained
  • Four brothers, the smallest with the largest knife, entering by a side door
  • A queen in the hall above, having called the meeting for a confidante killed at his prayers that same night
  • The Prime Minister and Chautariya Fateh Jung Shah, a relative of the King, on the floor of the armory where the guns were kept
  • A lamp that is not allowed to go out, and does not, for a hundred and forty-four years
  • A second column in wider type, set beside the first, the reasons given in return, read by everybody and argued by no one

On record

“The lamp was already burning when I came in, which I understand to mean it had been burning before the meeting. The clerk gave me the order and the list. I read until the flowers came. They came at the left of the gate, at the marigold marks, and I read the left side with great curiosity, because that is what the order said, and because the left side was where the gate had been patient. I did not read the right side. The right side is still being written on. I understand this.”

— Dreamer of record, the watch night of 14 September 1846 · Entry 732

Comparison and conclusion

The dream places the armory in the palace this dictionary has already entered at length. Hanuman Dhoka is the same durbar from which, on 9 March 1990, the King withdrew the Panchayat charter and admitted the parties to a new constitution, and the bindery at New Road keeps that fortnight in the 2046 Folio — A Working Record of the 1990 People’s Movement, bound with the March switchboard callbook pasted in whole. The callbook keeps two columns, the demand first and then the reason given in return, and it is the second column, the quieter one, that the bindery sets in the wider type. The entry recognizes the column at once. It is the same column the armory keeps: the reading that comes only when the flowers appear, the one nobody argues about and everybody reads. One hundred and forty-four years separate the armory night and the declaration, and the lamp, in the dream, was not moved between them.

Entry 732 stands also beside the minutes of a much smaller and much safer room. In the Minutes No. 112 of 2014 of the Council of Aldergate & the Ravine, a clerk was instructed to read the marks of a sleeve — a capital N, two city names, one quarter note standing in the streets of a small town — and paused, and then recorded the desk's reading in full, and the reading was accepted as it stood, being ungrammatical. The council filed it under music, unscheduled, and the entry notes that the clerk of Aldergate and the clerk of the armory kept the same pause, at the same price, one of them in a neon-orange ink and the other in marigold. If the Kot comes into your sleep, do not hurry the gate. Take the lamp. Wait for the flowers at the left of the marks. Read with great curiosity, and stop where the blossoms stop, and carry the second column home with you, because the second column is where the reason is.
