Gazeta da Orquestra · Pedro do Rosário, Maranhão · Perguntas e Respostas · Standing FAQ No. 1

The Algorithm, in Questions

Back room of Dona Luzia’s pensão, Rua da Paz · kept by the clerk, Farinha House São Benedito · answers to be given in the room where the fan does not quite reach the far window

Questions are entered in the ledger as they arrive, on paper, at the door, or by wire from the bench in São José dos Campos. Answers are read once, aloud, and then held. The stamp travels with the record.

FAQ
1 · VI

Q.1 What, for the record, is an algorithm?

A finite sequence of mathematically rigorous instructions, typically used to solve a class of specific problems or to perform a computation. That is the whole definition, and the Council has not improved on it, which is the Council’s way of respecting a thing.

In the room, we use the word the way the square uses the word festa: as a specification for performing calculations and data processing before the crowd arrives and before anyone is allowed to object. The tensioning bench writes its sequence down in the margin of the ledger — soak the six goatskins overnight in estuary water, wait for the supple, run the rope to the Orquestra pitch, tap, listen — and when the sequence is finite, rigorous, and written, it is an algorithm whether it has ever touched a machine or not. Dona Luzia’s position, recorded, is that it becomes an algorithm the morning it feeds six people.

Q.2 Can an algorithm turn a corner on its own?

Yes, and the drum shop is the local exhibit. The advanced sequences use conditionals: if the skin does not hold pitch, then re-soak; if the clay pan is still warm at the tensioning hour, then the pan waits. The clerk files this under what the printed word calls automated decision-making — the code diverted through various routes by its own if-statements — and under automated reasoning, where the sequence deduces a valid inference, namely that the batch will hold, before the batch has had the chance not to.

It all amounts, in the end, to automation. Fourteen jangadas leaving the flats at dawn on the same two rules — tide, then line — are automation in wood. The Orquestra group’s counter-position, shouted from the square on the 21st, is that anything which requires a cold shower to finish is not automated but merely supervised, and the clerk has not found the page where to file that.

Q.3 Is the third line of the 12 March order-slip an algorithm?

The Council holds, 5–1, that it is a procedure, which is what the letter to the editor means when it says the line verifies. Third among the remaining ingredients; before adding them to table with it, and have rare old vintages of prime old train oil. Read as a sequence, it has a beginning, a turn, an order, and an end: identify the third ingredient, run the hands and the rope and the tensioning bench through the tin on the second shelf, lift the batch, set it on the table. It is finite. It is rigorous. It has been followed.

The baker, asked for the margin note that travels with the line, gave the room one sentence, which the clerk enters here verbatim because the record has no room for a paraphrase: “The beef may also have the eggs should be used for culinary purposes.” The clerk’s reading, adopted on the 24th after the fan was fixed and the fan was not fixed, is that the note answers a question nobody on the square had asked yet — whether the procedure is a recipe or a schedule — and that it answers both: the beef may also have the eggs, and the eggs, in their turn, are what the morning is for. It is a certificate of a computation, left in the baker’s hand to be copied, exactly as To the Editor — P versus NP and the Third Line of the Order-Slip suspects.

Q.4 Why do we talk about machines with the words we use for people?

Because the practice was already in the hands of Alan Turing, who gave the machines memory, search, and stimulus, and the practice has not stopped since. A sequence that remembers the six skins and their pitches has memory; one that walks the string until the wingtip is found is searching, in the same sense the hangar is searched in an afternoon; and the tap on the drumhead that starts the whole sequence is a stimulus, which is the word the room uses for the baker’s bell on the 28th.

R. V. Driver, by wire, added that the bench in São José dos Campos keeps a memory of every crossed clearance line it has ever mis-filed on purpose, which the Council recorded as memory with character.

Q.5 Is finding the constraint itself an algorithm, and does the money ride on it?

This is the question the ledger cannot close, and the FAQ holds it open on purpose. Checking the constraint is cheap: tap the drum, listen, ten seconds, one ear. Finding it is the problem in class NP — verifiable the instant it arrives, and no recipe in Pedro do Rosário faster than the hands. Whether there is a polynomial-time recipe for the finding, or whether the answer is always a certificate somebody produced once and left behind, is the open question of the field, with one million dollars attached. Dona Luzia has said, twice, that if it is paid, the second cold shower comes off the proceeds, and the Council recorded her as a constraint in the process.

The five focusing steps the Council applies every month — identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat — are the closest thing the room has to a candidate answer, and they are described in full in Minutes No. 12 of the Council of the Single Link, which applies them to one drum shop, seven farinha houses, fourteen jangadas, and one clay pan. The clerk’s honest entry is this: the steps find the constraint every time so far, which is either an algorithm or a superstition, and the town is scheduled to find out at dawn on the 28th.

Q.6 Where does the record travel, and who keeps it?

The record travels three ways, by the clerk’s rule. To the letter: To the Editor — P versus NP and the Third Line of the Order-Slip, in the Gazeta’s own columns, where the question was put in writing for the first time. To the minutes: Minutes No. 12, where the line was read into the record and the chain was held intact for another month. And to the air: Back-Room Arithmetic, the radio play in which the four operations are taught on the festa ledger and the third line is finally read out loud as a procedure rather than a number.

Whoever keeps a copy keeps it in this order, because the square insists on the square, and the square is, as the minutes put it, a prototype of a sound and a sound a prototype of a drum. For the curious about the place itself, the gazette keeps on file Pedro do Rosário — anyone been?, and the FAQ is read, as all of these are, from the room on Rua da Paz where the fan has been fixed and has not been fixed, in the same breath, for the record. The clerk has also received, by post, a question about whether a route can be an algorithm, and the gazette holds on file the brochure for the Walters Route — five stations, one loop, a sequence that has a beginning, a turn, an order, and an end, and has been followed, which the Council would hold, 5–1, is a procedure whether or not it has ever touched a machine.
