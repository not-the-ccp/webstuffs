The Lagan Valley Weather Almanac

The Celts, a weather front

Entry No. 31 · The Celtic Season · Compiled in the record room, BT1 4AB

Station 9 — Lagan Valley, Laganmore to the sound of Belfast · gauge No. 3, staff replaced 1988 · standing reading taken each Saturday at 10:00

Card 31 Definition of the subject

The subject of this entry, Celts — also the Celtic peoples — is a collection of Indo-European peoples of Europe and Anatolia, identified by their use of Celtic languages and other cultural similarities. The almanac does not settle the question of who may be called a Celt; the interrelationships of ethnicity, language and culture in the Celtic world are unclear and debated, and the compiler has entered them all under one card anyway. The major groups carried on the card: the Gauls; the Celtiberians and Gallaeci of Iberia; the Britons, Picts and Gaels of Britain and Ireland; the Boii; and the Galatians, who took a dialect with them as far as Anatolia. In current scholarship, “Celt” primarily refers to speakers of Celtic languages — six of them still living — not to a single people, and the almanac reads them as one front, the way six streams sometimes drain one valley.

Card 31 The front, by date

The Celtic front moves slowest where it is oldest. Galatian is logged in Anatolia in the first century AD; the Boii are entered from Bohemia to the Po valley and once reached the coast near the head of the Adriatic, and are held in the record room, as a disputed lot of one people with the Newry consignment notice, at Lot 41 of the Ledger’s Small Ads Sale, Catalogue No. 12; the Celtiberians held the Meseta through winters no rain gauge would have credited. Closer to the room where this card is kept, the front runs along the open border: Breton at Quimper’s covered market, Irish in a taxi off the meter between Derry~Londonderry and the Causeway Coast, Welsh at Caernarfon on the harbour wall at twenty past six in the morning, Scottish Gaelic on the Inverness seafront bench with the wind left on the tape, Cornish from the written sources in Truro’s chapel yard, and Manx over the radio mast at Balla, restored fifty years after it was last needed. The whole of it was cut in one sitting at the Crown Liquor Saloon, Belfast, between 8:20 and 8:21 pm — the exact minute the Matchnight Committee in Bedminster closes its meetings — on Lagan Records LL-04, two LP, 33⅓ rpm, pressing of 1894, rerecorded. The city that kept the seat of that front, Ancyra and now Ankara, receives its own chapter in the household book at Chapter IX of A Book of Etiquette for the Keeping of Capitals, where the Galatian year is read as a rule of the exchange and the centre seat keeps the minutes.

Card 31 The field

The almanac keeps one rule about evidence: a thing is not evidence until it is spoken somewhere it is still spoken. The compiler’s note to the LL-04 sheet names the field directly — the Welsh and Breton languages still being spoken in the field — and Pezron’s link between the classical writers and the languages still being spoken there is entered on the same line. A witness has since been found; her evidence is preserved in the field. She was taken at Quimper, eleven minutes of conversation about whether the market still closes on the hour, given for a Samson & Goliath linen towel and logged under track A2 of the same sitting; the field, not the record book, is where her evidence lives. The red shirt, folded in the spring of 2014 and draped over the tape reel as a windscreen, is spoken for in the small ads at The Lagan Ledger’s Northern Ireland column, and is not entered on this card as a crop, a textile, or a witness.

Card 31 Company on the shelf

This card keeps company in the record room with the liner notes for Lagan Records LL-04, The Northwestern Fringe, six tracks for the six living languages recorded between 8:20 and 8:21 pm, and with the Ledger’s small ads across Northern Ireland, where the same record was announced the week before it was pressed and where the red shirt is still owed a reward of one pint. The card before this one — Entry No. 27, arable land — measures the same ground from Laganmore to the sound of Belfast, and is kept at card No. 27 of the almanac; corrections to both cards are taken in pencil only, and postmarks are accepted from either side of the open border. The front was measured once more, from the same ground, in Report No. 27-B of the village school, on the biosphere, where the six living voices stand in the house with the barley, and where a kept line stands too, meaning or no meaning. The dream side of the same field is kept in Natural Resources — Dream Dictionary, Entry 27-B, where the front has crossed a storehouse of the natural heritage, the barley stands in rows labelled components, and the barometer that held at 1,018 on the open day still refuses to be moved.
