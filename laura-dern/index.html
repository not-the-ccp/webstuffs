Hall C Concession Desk & Lost Property Office

Sunset Boulevard Theatre · Backstage Corridor, Between the Dressing Rooms and the Exit Sign

CLAIM
No. 1967-D

FOUND, BACKSTAGE, 11:40 P.M. One American Actress, Her Star, and a Coat of Five Globes

Deposited at the concession desk by the usher on the north rail · held under a red dress · all items intact, all items legible

Description of the Property, as Found

One actress, American, born 1967 — the desk has verified the year on the driver's licence, and it matches the year of the star on the sidewalk, so the two are filed together. Laura Elizabeth Dern, daughter of actors Bruce Dern and Diane Ladd, the daughter in this claim having begun her own career in the 1980s and risen through it the slow, on-camera way: Peter Bogdanovich's Mask (1985), Joyce Chopra's Smooth Talk (1985), then David Lynch's Blue Velvet (1986) and Wild at Heart (1990), in which the red dress found beneath her coat was last worn. She received a nomination for the Academy Award for Best Actress for her role as the orphan Rose in the drama film Rambling Rose (1991); the orphan's script came with the coat, marked ROSE on every page, one dog-eared corner at the number eight. Recipient of numerous accolades: one Academy Award, one Actor Award, one BAFTA Award, one Primetime Emmy Award, and five Golden Globes — the coat has five globe pockets, all occupied, and the desk will not describe what is in them, being not the desk's business.

The property was found at 11:40 p.m. on the north rail of the backstage corridor, sitting upright and fully dressed, holding the red dress over one arm and a folded claim tag in the other hand. When the usher called out, the property looked up, said Thank you, and stayed seated until the desk finished its form. It is, the desk notes, difficult to misidentify, and has not asked to be.

Condition Report

  • Star, sidewalk, 1967: polished, five-pointed, one chip on the eastern point repaired in a later decade; the desk has waxed it twice without being asked.
  • Coat of five globes: all pockets present, all pockets occupied, lining undamaged; the fifth globe sits slightly loose and the desk has stopped checking.
  • Red dress: one, as found, folds cleanly, the fringe is intact, the denim buttons are all there, and it was, by the tag's own record, the dress a film of 1990.
  • Script, Rambling Rose: all pages, one dog-ear, the orphan's name on every sheet; to be returned with the property or filed separately at the property's choice.
  • The property herself: found upright, in possession of her licence, her thanks, and, on inquiry, her sense of humour, which the desk files under good.

The Claim Tag, Transcribed Verbatim

The folded claim tag in the property's hand was written in a steady, theatrical hand, and the desk transcribes it word for word, in the same discipline this corridor keeps for everything it finds. The first line is a set of directions: the north rail, the south rail, or the rail that is the screen. The second line is the desk's strangest entry, and the desk sets it down exactly as found:

And the coach points north, south, or to a film that was not, in its Persian-language version, and that is issued in the hall needed a patch.

Read against the corridor, the line holds. The coach is the film school's old shuttle, the one that still runs the lot on Saturdays and keeps its painted arrow on the roof; it has pointed north, to the soundstage, and south, to the lot gate, for forty years, and has more than once pointed straight at a screen showing a film that was not, in its Persian-language version, a film at all — a Persian dub of a picture that no Persian picture was ever cut from, the negative never made, the titles in no language the projector knew. And the hall is Hall C: the 1990 re-release in this hall needed a patch, a single burnt reel patched over by the projectionist in the winter of the dress's fame, and the patch is still in the print, a small bright square every time the scene turns. So the tag is directions and condition in one sentence: which rail to follow, which film the arrow may not mean, and which hall will show its repair. The desk finds it the most sensible nonsense it has transcribed this decade, and keeps it.

How to Claim

Claims are received at the concession desk between showings, or by letter to the address on the star. The claimant must (1) name the property's parents and one of the five globes, (2) state which rail is the screen, and (3) be able to say the tag's second line from memory, since the property has said it herself more than once, on camera, without flinching. The desk will not press the claimant on the contents of the globe pockets. The property may be collected with the star, the dress, the script, and the patched print, all travelling together, as a lost-and-found file keeps its items: intact, legible, and waiting for the right name to be called at 11:40, on the night.

Cross-filed in this office. The desk keeps the sister holdings of the same corridor on the adjacent shelf: Lot 41 of Sale No. 27, the sealed vial whose consular stamp is read the way this tag is read — uncorrected, as a tolerance — and Entry 314 of the Dream Dictionary, on the armoured animal that walks out of its plates, which the property herself once described, off the rail, as the most honest thing a body can do. Both files cross-reference No. 1967-D, and both keep their ink slow. The desk has also been asked to hold, by arrangement, one page of a court transcript — Cause No. 1987-L, the Record of Lionel Andrés Messi, Court of the Trophy, Rosario — in which a keeper of the cabinet testifies that an office is entered in exactly one copy of the ledger, and the desk finds the discipline familiar: hold the item, keep the line word for word, and wait for the right name to be called.
