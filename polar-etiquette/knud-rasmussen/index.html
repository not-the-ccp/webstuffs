Supplement to Chapter IX.
Questions and Answers on Knud Rasmussen

Knud Johan Victor Rasmussen, 7 June 1879 – 21 December 1933
Greenlandic-Danish polar explorer and anthropologist

Chapter IX of this volume was kept, in the end, as a book of manners, and so is this supplement. It is set down in the form of questions put to the reader by the office of the drift, which continues its correspondence at the kitchen door of the hut, and answers drawn from the record of one man who read that book of manners all the way through and then did what it said. The man is Knud Rasmussen, born at Odense on 7 June 1879 — the very year the diary of the drift was printed — the son of a Greenland whaler’s mate who had known the ice and would not be contradicted by it, and who died at Godthåb, now called Nuuk, on the 21st of December, 1933, with the lamp, by the record’s account, already trimmed for ten o’clock. He is spoken of, in the record, sometimes by his Danish name, sometimes by the Greenlandic one, Mikellik, and both names are kept in the directory, filed once each, correctly, at K and at M. The questions follow.

I. Who is this Knud Rasmussen, and why does a book of manners take up his name?

He was a Greenlandic-Danish polar explorer and anthropologist who, between 1912 and 1933, made five expeditions to Greenland and the Canadian Arctic, learned Greenlandic and Inuktitut as working languages, kept his accounts in pencil at the lamp, and gave the study of the Eskimo — for which the polite title has since been found to be Inuit Studies, or, in the American universities, Greenlandic and Arctic Studies — its first European father, which is why he has been called the “father of Eskimology.” The manners take up his name for a simpler reason: he kept them. The office of the drift has checked the record five times, once per expedition, and finds not a single flag waved “in sport.” A name like that has to be filed somewhere, and this is somewhere.

II. What has he to do with the Austro-Hungarian expedition of 1872–74, which he did not live to see?

He was born in 1879, the year the diary of the drift was printed in Vienna, and the first expedition book in his cabin was a copy of it, water-stained and read through. In 1912, founding his first station at Thule, he is recorded as carrying the diary in his sledge baggage among the coffee, the lamp oil, and one unopened tin of coffee, which was never hoarded. In 1921, fitting out the sledge party at Cape York, he copied out the six rules of the hut by hand, in a clerk’s hand, and pasted them above the kitchen door, which was the door. The Land of Franz Josef, which Captain Weyprecht named in the spring of the drift, was not far from the sound his 1926 ship lay in that August, and the officers are on record as saying that the ice there moved at the same modest pace it had in 1873, which is to say, faster than a man’s reputation can recover from it.

III. And what exactly is Eskimology, since the name was found to be a mistake?

Eskimology was the name the discipline carried in the universities from about 1920 until the 1980s, and it is the name still carried by one stubborn journal in the American midwest, which the office of the drift notes has done nothing to deserve it. The study itself is the study of the Inuit and Greenlandic peoples — their languages, their law of the sledge, their etiquette of the kill, which is the etiquette by which the meat of the first day is served hot and the soup of it the same day. Rasmussen’s contribution was the method of living with the people long enough to be told the rule twice: once at the table, and once, years later, when the student had left the room, to a man he did not know he was listening. The second telling is the real one, and the reader of etiquette will imitate it.

IV. What is the matter about the Northwest Passage, and is it true?

It is true, and the office has seen the ledger. In the winter of 1921 and the spring of 1922, Rasmussen and the Danish navigator Aa. Holm, with a sledge train of Inuit and greenlandic dogs, ran the full length of the Northwest Passage by dog sledge, from Greenland along the Arctic shore of Canada to the Bering Sea, the first European to do it, in a season in which the ice was, in the record’s phrase, “unremittingly kind.” The party went out by the flag under the rule of Chapter IX, and the flag went up for every one of the four hundred and thirty-six days on the ice, which is a long flag-day, and the men of the Thule station have been known to wave at passing ships out of the sheer habit of it, which is not the rule but is not far from it. Holm, who kept the sledge ledger, is entered in the directory under H, and the office notes that his hand was steady.

V. What did the household ledger actually say about the green sledge?

The ledger of the household at Thule, 1921, keeps an entry of the sort the office is obliged to quote, being an entry of the sort this book exists to quote. The instrument runs: “The second sledge, green, of the Thule train, was delivered to my said wife, Aka Rasmussen, to keep, to mend, and to drive as she sees fit, free of the claims of the expedition, this being the whole of the transfer and all of it.” To which my said wife had a good one. The phrase is archaic contract English, picked up in a whaler’s cabin and used once in a life, and it means, in the plain speech of the record, that she approved of it — that the green sledge was a good one, and a good transfer, and that the household did not trouble to find a modern way to say so. The green sledge is not in the record as surviving; the approval is, and that is the better of the two, being the thing that does not rot.

VI. How is he to be addressed — Knud, Mikellik, Captain, or something else?

At the Thule station, in company, he was Mikellik, and a letter to him ran “Dear Mikellik,” not “Dear Captain,” which is the style of address the record allows to settle in accordance with the season. To the Danish and Greenlandic governments he was, in the orders and in the printed reports, Captain Rasmussen, and to the universities he was the father, which is a style of address that is given and cannot be requested. The rule, which the office shares with the neighbouring chapter on the etiquette of the name Erni, is that a name already shortened is not shortened twice: Mikellik is the working form, Knud is the name kept for the letter to the bank, and no one at Thule is on record as having tried “Knudde.” In Inuktitut, which the record is obliged to pronounce flatly, as the Erni ledger of Szczecin pronounces its own name, the name sits plain and unsyllabified, and answers.

VII. What were the five expeditions, and where do they stand in the directory?

First Thule, 1912–12: the founding of the station at Thule, on the northwestern cap of Greenland, with a train of Inuit and some three hundred and sixty dogs, the largest sledge train the ice has seen from that quarter; the station is still there, now called Qaanaat, and the kitchen door keeps the pasted rules. Second Thule, 1921–24: the crossing of the Northwest Passage, the household ledger, and the green sledge. Little America, 1925–26: the Canadian Arctic, the sound of August, and the phrase “unremittingly kind” used once more, which the office notes is a phrase the record is not ashamed to repeat. East Greenland, 1930–33: the last and the longest, the coast of the fjords run by boat and by sledge, the diary kept in pencil, the lamps trimmed at two and at ten. And the fifth is the return, which is always the fifth expedition in the record, and which is what the drift did, and what the manners of the house continue.

VIII. What is the final entry, and where is the book of manners now?

He died at Godthåb on the 21st of December, 1933, in his fifty-fourth year, and the last entry in the ledger of the station is entered, in the record’s account, the same evening, by the hand that kept it: “The lamp was trimmed at ten. The green sledge is in the shed. The drift has ended. The manners of the house continue.” The book of manners itself — this one, the one with the River Holme coming down from the porch light — is the one that kept the whole of it, and the reader is invited to return to Chapter IX, where it stands, and to take this supplement down beside it, the way a ledger is taken down beside the lamp, and to leave the two, at last, exactly where the house left them. For the rest of the volume, filed among the keepable things, see the cover of Webstuffs.
