HMS FORMIDABLE (67)

The Log, Kept and Amended · Illustrious-class aircraft carrier · R.N., 1940–1946

NEW! The clerk's apology is now posted in full, with the crossed-out line preserved.

Devonport, the old clerk's room, 21 November 1998
To: The Quartermaster of the Log Book, HMS Formidable (67), H.M. Ship, in reserve
From: C. E. Wexley, ship's clerk, 1941–1943 (ret.)

My dear Quartermaster,

I write at last to apologize for the two nights my hand put on the ship's log that should not have been entered the way I entered them. The book is yours now, and the apology should have gone in the book's own hand a long time ago. It goes in mine, and it goes now.

The first was the morning of 29 March 1941, off Cape Matapan. Our fighters had come back from the sweep over the Italian battle fleet, the Mediterranean Fleet was still writing its part of the war, and I let a line go into the log the log was not built to hold: Formidable form, good. I know what I meant — a run of form, the ship's own — but the clerk's room has been quarrelling over that line for fifty-seven years. Let me break it down for you once, the way your uncle would have broken it down at the desk. The first clause is your uncle's rule, stated on a good sign, meaning a run of form, the minutes of the log. Your uncle held the book from commissioning day, 13 December 1940, straight through the Home Fleet months and the transfer down to the Mediterranean as her crippled sister's replacement, and his rule was short: one line a night, nothing re-inked, and no compliments to the ship, because the ship keeps her own score. I broke the last of those three the morning at Matapan, and the line is still in your book, which is as it should be.

The second night was 11 May 1941, off Souda Bay. The dive bombers came at eleven and did not stop; six hits, the funnel down, we limped to the mole with the deck still smoking. I wrote the damage in three lines and then — and here is the whole of my apology — I crossed out the second. The crossed-out line says two more on the starboard edge, and the old ink is still there in your copy. By your uncle's rule it should have stood, and it does stand, entered and not erased. I never asked for it to be restored. I never asked for anything about that night to be re-inked. The log stands, and I am sorry I was the hand that reached for the correction.

After Souda we went under — the docks at Rosyth, then out to the Indian Ocean in January 1942 with the Eastern Fleet, and Madagascar in May, where the cover for the landing at Diego Suarez was flown off the same deck the dive bombers had marked. All of it is in your book, one line a night, entered and not enforced. Where my hand failed, the book did not, and that is the difference between a clerk and a ship.

I enclose my card. When the log is ever bound again, keep the crossed-out line in, ink and all. A run of form is the ship's, not the clerk's, and I am sorry for the one morning I borrowed it.

Yours in the log,
C. E. Wexley, ship's clerk (ret.)

P.S. For the guestbook, the Illustrious-class figures the clerk's room keeps: 23,000 tons standard, 31,000 full load, 46 aircraft, and the name Formidable, which your uncle said we never did get around to spelling right in the first edition of the muster book.

Also on the boards

The clerk's room keeps company with two other boards that hold a similar line: the destroyer thread on The Household Board, where the fast escort ships keep their own almanac and the frigate turns out to be in the record all along, and The Man Who Kept the Keys, where a Master of the Household keeps the duty almanac to the minute and every state dinner at the Cockpit is one line a night. Both boards agree with this log room on the standing rule: everything entered, nothing re-inked. The same rule keeps a line whole on a family recipe card in Aunt Walburga’s May Eve Cake, Card No. 9, where a provenance line out of the garage ledger stands entered and unexplained, the way the crossed-out line in this book stands entered and un-erased. And for those who keep the whole collection, the front page of the Webstuffs is where this log room is shelved.
