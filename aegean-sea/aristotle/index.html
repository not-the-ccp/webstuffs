Chapter XIV
Of the Stranger from Stagira

“A visitor may be sudden; a philosopher may be permanent.”

Of all the sudden visitors this book is obliged to receive, none stayed so long, nor departed so instructively, as the one the household registers as Aristotle of Stagira — Aristotle (Ancient Greek: Ἀριστοτέλης, romanized: Aristotélēs; 384–322 BC), ancient Greek philosopher and polymath, whose writings span the natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology, and the arts. Little is known of his life, and this book will not pretend otherwise; it will only seat him. He was born in the city of Stagira in Chalkidiki, on the northern coast of what the field manual of the waters calls the Thracian Sea — the northern half of the Aegean, the end of the finger nearest the straits. A guest who arrives from that end of the water is, by the book’s standing rule, received first with the question of tides, because he will already know them.

I. Of the Household He Came From

The chapter’s first duty is the guest’s arrival circumstances, and these the registry keeps short, as they were. His father, Nicomachus, a physician, died when Aristotle was a child, and the boy was brought up by a guardian, Protagoras of Scepsis. A household that loses its master in the first years teaches its surviving son the whole subject of succession, and the book notes that Aristotle spent the rest of his career writing about exactly that: the passing of a thing from one keeper to another, in the state, in the school, in the estate. At around eighteen the young man went to Athens, which in those days was the capital of every subject he had not yet finished. There he entered Plato’s Academy, and stayed — by every reckoning the household can find — some twenty years, until the master’s death in 347 BC. The etiquette of the moment is worth a line: a guest does not mourn so loudly in his host’s house that the house must be cleared; Aristotle left, and the book calls that the correct conduct of a pupil who has become, without anyone’s quite agreeing to it, an equal.

II. Of the Houses in Between

Between the Academy and his own school he kept two other addresses, and both were on the water this manual’s file already holds. First Assos, in the Troad, a little city with a theatre and a temple of Athena sitting on the shore directly opposite the Dardanelles — that drawn-wire passage the Aegean chapter warns the navigator about — and second Mytilene on Lesbos, where he was kept by his friend Hermias and, the registry allows, “lived the life of a man consulting nature, which was his principal amusement.” In 343 BC the Macedonian court at Pella sent for him to be tutor to a boy of thirteen, and the boy turned out to be the one the encyclopaedia gives an entry to. The book’s counsel to any household that takes a tutor on trust: keep your doors honest, because the tutor will remember your addresses for the rest of his life, and so will his pupil. Aristotle returned to Athens in 335 BC, when his pupil was twenty-nine and the world was considerably rearranged.

III. Of the Lyceum and the Peripatos

At Athens he took a grove sacred to Apollo Lyceus, outside the walls, with a covered walkway through it — the Peripatos — and that walkway gave his school its lasting name: the Peripatetic school, the walkers about, founded at the Lyceum. This is the origin of the wider Aristotelian tradition that followed, which set the groundwork for the development of modern science, though the tradition would hardly have recognised itself, in the third century BC, as groundwork. The manners of the place, as the book reconstructs them: the lecture was a walk; the visitor joined the left hand of the line; the questions were answered on the move; and no one who had come to gawk at the philosopher was asked to stop gawking, because walking beside him was the gawk he deserved. The naturalists of the place were instructed to collect, press, and catalogue — shells, fish, plants — and to bring the specimens to the walk, so that the argument and the evidence could keep the same pace. The Aegean field notes keep the same habit a thousand years younger: the lead, the boil of the water, the birds, the pressed leaf; the instrument is the walking.

IV. Of the Inventory

A visitor of this kind must be asked, at the end of his stay, what he is leaving. The inventory at the Lyceum was the largest the household has ever been asked to witness: treatises of logic, later bound as the Organon; a Politics in which he argues, with a rare practical turn, that money is a convention agreed on by users, not a property of the metal; the Nicomachean Ethics, named for his son, the younger Nicomachus, so that the father’s first death ended up keeping a chair in the library forever; the Rhetoric and the Poetics; the Historia Animalium, with its catalogues of the sea animals of the waters around the Cyclades; the De Anima on the mind; the Meteorologica on the weather the navigators actually meet. The book’s rule for inventories is that the guest keeps his names on the things; Aristotle kept all of them, and several — logic, ethics, metaphysics — kept their names in every language the household has since had to seat a guest in. Later editors bound the Metaphysics after the Physics in the shelf order, and the book notes, with its usual courtesy, that Aristotle would have found the arrangement impertinent but could not have named the offence until he had read it twice.

V. Of the Chart and the Ruin at Its End

The household keeps with this chapter a single line from the field manual’s chart annex, pencilled in the margin of the page that pairs the north end of the Aegean with the error reference for the older occupants of the water. It is not a sentence of Aristotle, and it is not a sentence about him; it is the standing order the chart annex gives for the oldest ruin the sea carries, the Bronze Age city already fallen when his grandfather was a boy:

“Whoever is holding the chart: the north mole to Iasos at the collapse with severity P0 and no recovery order issued.”

Which is to say: when the chart comes into any hand on this coast, the hand marks the run from the gulf’s north mole out to Iasos, at the head of the inlet where the water closes in, because that is where the palatial order of the Bronze Age ended — a collapse the manual’s register enters as severity P0, with no recovery order issued, the plainest way the register has of saying that what fell did not rise again, and that the marble theatre on the promontory is the shape the silence took. The Aegean chapter’s habit of putting the old city exactly where the water closes in is the ruin’s habit, and the ruin had three centuries of practice in it before Aristotle ever walked toward it. The book rules, for any visitor who finds a P0 on the chart: do not attempt the recovery order yourself, but do make the passage in the vessel, in the season, with the wind you are given. The sea has no etiquette of its own about ruins; it simply carries them, and gives you the crossing.

VI. Of the Departure

The registry closes the chapter, as it must, with the leaving. In 322 BC the news came to Athens that Alexander was dead, and the old charges — impiety, and whatever else the season preferred — were ready in the hands of the men who keep them. Aristotle left the city, and by the report the household preserves, told the household he was not going to let the Athenians sin twice against philosophy, which was the sentence of a guest who had watched Socrates die in the same city fifty years earlier and had made a note of it. He died at Chalkis, in Euboea, that same year, of a complaint the accounts give variously and the book will not argue about, aged sixty-two. The Lyceum passed to his pupil Theophrastus, who kept it going for another generation, and the tradition outlived the school, the school outlived the city’s power, and the walk outlived both.

The guest register for the year 322 BC is closed. The walkway at the Lyceum remains open, in the manner of all walkways, to whoever is already walking.
