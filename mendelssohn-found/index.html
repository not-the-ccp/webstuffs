FOUND—

One composer, early Romantic, German
Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy
plainly called Felix Mendelssohn · pianist, organist, conductor, 38 years

NOTICE NO. 1809/47 · filed 4 November 1847, 4:17 in the morning · cloakroom ticket 1809, rack 47, second peg from the door

UNCLAIMED — HOLD 30 DAYS

Part I · How found, and where

The morning of 4 November 1847

The coat was found folded across the bench of the cloakroom at the Gewandhaus in Leipzig, where it had been left after the evening’s performance, and the bureau opened it at 4:17 on the morning of 4 November 1847. Inside, in a black morocco case, were a conductor’s baton, a set of organ keys made of boxwood in the shape of the south organ of St. Paul’s (London), a sheaf of manuscript paper in a small, square hand, and a brass button stamped 12 Dorotheenstrasse, the house in Frankfurt am Main in which the owner was born on 3 February 1809, of which the bureau keeps the ticket, the date and the button, in that order. The owner is not in the coat. The coat is in the bureau. The bureau holds the ticket, which is numbered 1809, and it will be kept until it is wanted.

The last witnesses saw the owner on the steps of the Gewandhaus at eleven that same evening, walking toward Camburg, and the bureau has entered this in the red column of the register. It is stated in the notice, because a notice that does not state the red column is not this bureau’s notice. The thirty-day holding period has run, and will keep running, as long as the ticket is on the rack.

Part II · Description of the property

What is inside the coat

Inventory taken by the two clerks of the night watch, each line checked against the other’s: the String Octet in F major, complete; the overture and incidental music to A Midsummer Night’s Dream, with the Wedding March pinned separately, as the owner pinned it; the Third “Scottish” Symphony in D minor and the Fourth “Italian” Symphony in A major, the one still smelling of the sea; the oratorios St. Paul and Elijah; the Hebrides Overture (Fingal’s Cave); the Violin Concerto in E minor, which the inventory notes as mature, in the owner’s own hand, and which the clerk of the day asked, once, to be played on the bureau’s own piano, and was refused; and the melody, on a loose slip, that the world knows as the carol “Hark! The Herald Angels Sing”, written to the words of “Flee, Adah, Flee”, which slip was left, deliberately, on top of the sheaf.

Beneath all of it, in a flat folder of the same morocco, is the file the owner himself would have called the whole of the property: the Songs Without Words, book on book, the most famous thing he left, the folder so thick the bureau had to rule it with a pen instead of a chisel. The inventory lists it last and prints it in a larger hand, because the folder is the one thing in the coat that weighs as much as the coat.

Part III · The pencil line, preserved

Written on the lining, in blue pencil

Turn the coat inside out, in the lining of the left pocket, and there is one pencil line, in blue, in the same small square hand as the manuscript. The bureau prints it here, word for word, exactly as found, because a line written on a lining is a line the owner wrote to be found, and the buyer will wish to inherit, because it is the record the same — the 1966 Act, the arrowhead, the tax-credit fine print, and the kitchens into drawers.

The bureau’s own note on the line, entered below it in black ink: the 1966 Act is the Unclaimed Property Act of 1966, by which the bureau has kept ticket 1809 on the rack past the year the coat was left; the arrowhead is the brass pin, shaped as a spearhead, on which the Wedding March is fixed to the lining; the tax-credit fine print is the half-inch of rules at the foot of the claim form, which every claimant must read aloud to the clerk; and the kitchens into drawers are the twelve drawers of the Dorotheenstrasse kitchen, where the owner’s mother kept the first edition of the Songs Without Words, and where this property, if it is ever claimed, is to be returned. The bureau holds the line unaltered. Claimants are told that the line means what it says, and that a claim is a record, and the record is the same.

Part IV · Claim procedure

How to claim

Claim in person at the third floor, beyond the cloakroom. Bring the ticket, number 1809, or the button, or the date, 3 February 1809; the bureau accepts any one of the three, and has accepted, once, only the date, which is the entry in the red column of the register. A claim signed in the presence of two witnesses is a complete claim, and the property passes that day, the folder included. The bureau reminds claimants that the Songs Without Words are the folder, that the folder is the coat, and that the coat has been on the rack, at the second peg from the door, since the morning of 4 November 1847, and will keep on the rack. The holding period of thirty days is renewed every winter, in the month of the owner’s name, and the clerk who renews it signs, at the foot, the words the record is the same.

Part V · Filed with

Company on the shelf

This notice keeps company, in the bureau’s second folio, with the Lost & Found notice for one Polish name, given and surname, last seen at the registry desk in Kraköw — a notice of this bureau’s own kind, about an owner who is not in the coat, and who must be claimed in person with two witnesses, which is the same rule this notice keeps, and has kept. For the weather of the month in which the coat was found, the arithmetic of Station 11, Pisa, whose gauges have run since 1963, reads November as the month of the rain that holds and does not fall; and for the long-night record of a station that kept its lamp burning past its rated hours, see Chapter 11, the nyasol chapter of the Bingxian Flats manual, whose lamp log is the closest thing this shelf has to a night watch. The bureau’s own habit of printing a line word for word and leaving it unruled is kept up, at a dollar per line, in the classifieds of the South Park Ledger, Tuesday, August 19, 1997, where the lodge clerk enters the drawer, the 1974 interior, and the stopped register, exactly as found. The notice closes, as the folio closes, at the front page of the shelf.
