Drainage Basin

3 Composition / information on ingredients

The basin is not a single material. It is a hierarchy: smaller basins nested inside larger ones, merging at river confluences in a branching pattern that reads, on the 1981 quadrangle, like a river's own family tree. The perimeter — the drainage divide — is a succession of elevated features, ridges and hills, and it is the container for everything inside. Water that falls west of the divide belongs to the Pacific directly; water that falls east belongs to this sheet. There is no appeal process. Elevation 3 ft (0.91 m), the USGS figure of 1981 recorded at the southeast shoulder, is the maximum height the substance reaches above its own water.

4 First aid measures

If in contact with mudflat
Do not run. The channel to the mainland at spring tide is a boot-sucking mudflat strewn with ribbed mussels and the weathered shells of Olympia oysters; remove boot in three slow stages, mud first, then sole, then pride.
If swallowed (brackish water)
Expect 16 °C and salinity of roughly 12 parts per thousand. Do not taste a longjaw mudsucker found gasping in a disturbed tide pool; it is brown, patient, and waiting for the water to return, and it is not yours to assist in any hurry.
If hearing a kicker-call at 05:30
Stop. It is a California black rail (Laterallus jamaicensis coturniculus) in the pickleweed. No action required; that is the point of the rail.

5 Firefighting measures

Not flammable. The substance cannot be ignited and has never requested to be. In the event of fire adjacent to the divide, extinguish with water from inside the basin; the basin already has an excess and considers the contribution a donation. Firefighting crews should note that the substance's first-aid response to fire is to flood, and that it will do this calmly, on schedule, twice a day.

6 Accidental release measures

A release of this substance is called a flood and is treated as a routine event rather than an accident. Containment is by levee; the levees are the only approved spill kit. During a full release the marsh edge, cordgrass, and pickleweed surrender in that order, and the highest hummocks go under in the order they were proud. Personnel should withdraw to the dry shoulder, note the tide, and remember the station's standing instruction for any spill of this kind: the river brings what the Bay forgets, and the station's own field notes (Knight Island — Field Notes) keep the receipts, entry by entry, from the 12th to the 15th of October. For crossings during a declared release, see the chapter on the shortest of the bay's crossings, three lanes each way with the bicycle lane to the south (Dumbarton Bridge — Field Manual Chapter).

7 Handling and storage

Handle only at slack water. Landing should be made at the southeast shoulder, where the elevation claim is strongest, and departure taken before the ebb to the 0.4 ft low at 14:20 — the departure at 16:10 on the 15th was the model. Store the substance where it already is: inside its own divide. Do not attempt to store the divide separately from the basin; the ridges and hills are load-bearing. Storage temperature: 12–16 °C, fog burning off by 09:00 in clear cold dawns. The substance accepts no refrigeration and no transfer of ownership; it has been in storage here since before the quadrangle.

9 Physical and chemical properties

Appearance
From the launch at Mare Island Strait: a dark smudge upstream in the channel, a thread of cordgrass and pickleweed holding against brown water. At mean high water only the highest hummocks show; at spring tide the channel shrinks to mudflat. The map calls it an island; the tide calls it a suggestion.
Density of convergence
Total. Every tributary, confluence, and smaller basin inside the divide resolves to the single point at the mouth. There is no second outlet, no overflow valve, no “or nearby.”
Viscosity (margins)
High in the old creek beds, low where the gumplant holds the last dry ground.
Wreckage carried
Mylar balloons, one fishing float stamped with Chinese characters, the skeleton of a cigarette lighter. Filed, as the notes put it, in the station's junk drawer beside a folder for Ghent-Eeklo, a boundary of four lines briefly drawn.

10 Stability and reactivity

Stable for the duration of the tide cycle. Long-term stability is the subject of section 13. The substance is reactive with time: each spring tide takes a little more of the perimeter, and the drainage divide answers by standing where it stands, a succession of ridges doing the only containment the basin has. Reacts with wind (west, freshening) to produce stillness at the surface of San Pablo Bay, where great egrets stand above the shallows and hold the scene together. Incompatible with century-scale planning. Compatible, indefinitely, with a salt marsh harvest mouse that runs across a boot heel and vanishes into a burrow without comment.

11 Toxicological information

Toxicity is expressed locally. The longjaw mudsucker is the designated indicator species: found gasping in a disturbed tide pool, brown and patient, it signals that a pool has been disturbed, not that it is dying — it is waiting for the water to return, which is on schedule. Cordgrass, pickleweed, and alkali heath are treated as inert and are in fact the substance's working tissue. The northern harrier is the substance's immune response, quartering the marsh all morning, low over the cordgrass, and it should be reported but never treated. No cumulative effects are known in any organism that has declined the basin's invitation, because none exists.

12 Ecological information

The basin is ecologically non-mobilizable: it is the ecology, at 4,387 square miles, and nothing around it that is alive is outside its accounting. Species logged to this sheet's file: California black rail, Ridgway's rail, salt marsh harvest mouse, northern harrier, great egret, longjaw mudsucker, ribbed mussel, Olympia oyster, and one salt marsh harvest mouse running across a boot heel, listed twice because it was seen twice. All are covered by the convergence clause of section 3. If an ecologist objects, the tide table is available at the station.

13 Disposal considerations

The substance disposes of itself, slowly, and on schedule. Disposal method: submergence, twice daily, complete at spring tide. Disposal site: the same site; the basin does not move its own waste. Prognosis on the Knight Island component: three feet, give or take, the USGS figure of 1981 holding true, and the island will not last the century at this rate of rise. For now it holds — cordgrass, rail, mouse, and the slow quiet of a drowning place. Do not accelerate. Do not certify the disposal complete until the divide itself has moved; the divide has no history of moving and no intention of starting.

14 Regulatory information

Nomenclature is regulated by region, and misnaming this substance is the most common violation on record. In North America the product is commonly called a watershed. In other English-speaking places “watershed” is used only in its original sense — the drainage divide line — and to call the whole basin that is to confuse the container's edge for the contents. Approved alternate names: catchment area, catchment basin, drainage basin. All four names refer to the same 4,387 square miles; the sheet follows them. No permits are issued, none are required; the basin predates the permitting system and will outlast it, or the permitting system will have to do its own submerging.
