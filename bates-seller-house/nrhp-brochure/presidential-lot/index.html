LOT 14

The President of the United States

Catalogue A · Lot 14 · One (1) lot · Offered without reserve

Description

One (1) office of the President of the United States of America: head of state and head of government of the United States, director of the executive branch of the federal government, and commander-in-chief of the United States Armed Forces. The office was created in 1787 and first exercised by George Washington, who took the oath of office in 1789 and served two terms before the seat moved to Philadelphia and then, in 1800, to the Executive Mansion at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue, Washington, D.C. — the White House, whose cornerstone was laid in 1792 and whose Oval Office has held the Resolute desk since 1880.

The lot comprises the office with its papers and powers: the oath of office (administered once per term, on a Bible or by affirmation), the veto (retained; overridable by two-thirds of both houses), the pardon power (intact, except in cases of impeachment), the State of the Union (delivered annually, in writing or in person), and the commander-in-chief commission over the Army, the Navy, and the militia of the several states. Also included: one inkwell, one pen used to sign legislation, and the blotter stain at the top right of the desk, consistent with coffee, iron gall ink, and at least one declared war.

The power of the presidency has grown since Washington took office in 1789. Presidential power has ebbed and flowed across two centuries, while the office has played an increasing role in American political life since the beginning of the 20th century, carried into the 21st century with expansions during the presidencies of Franklin D. Roosevelt and George W. Bush. The oath has been repeated, on occasion, by the same hand, and the office remains one of the best-known seats of power in the world.

Provenance

Consigned by the docents of the Bates–Seller House, 2234 NW Flanders Street, Portland, Oregon — the Queen Anne built in 1892 for lumber merchant William H. Bates and kept after 1908 by Dr. James Seller, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1989, with its conical tower, fish-scale shingles, Willamette Iron Works fence, and copper whale weathervane, listed in its own right in 1994. The lot was recovered from a portfolio marked PRESIDENT behind the fourth drawer of the consulting-room cabinet — Tinctures, Bandages, Instruments, Ledgers — and was previously in the custody of the Flanders Street Revenue Reading Society, whose Assessor's Arrowhead, No. 47, minded the small print of the 25 percent rehabilitation credit and held that rates are a matter of politics, not statute.

A marginal slip, in the hand of the clerk at Anoka, reads: Listing is a decision the town made and the rule has been the wonder of every kind. The desk has kept it.

Condition

The office shows wear consistent with age and use: the oath has been taken more than forty times, the veto pen has been laid down and picked up again, and the eagle on the paperwork does not enter the home of the buyer. Listing is a register, not a cage: the sale conveys no restriction on curtains, parties, or the color of a portico, though the successful bidder should expect a friendly phone call before demolishing anything. The docent's shh applies throughout the viewing and the sale.
