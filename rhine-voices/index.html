NIGHT TRANSMISSION · STATION R-1147 · 119.7 k·kHz, long wave · clear, no crackle

Voices from the Rhine

A radio play excerpt · four voices, one river, eleven centuries · the Ashkenazi Jews from the Rhineland to the Vistula, read as they were kept

ON AIR
80 90 100 119·7 130 140 150

[SOUND: a river at night, water over stone; a small ferry lamp swings; a reed-pen scratching in a cold room. The night wind carries, from somewhere below the town, the faint sound of a shofar, one long note, and then the sound of a bell that is not a church bell, a sabbath bell, and the two of them going on together for the length of this broadcast.]

ANNOUNCER (a woman’s voice, level, the voice the station has had since the beginning)

You are listening to Station R-1147, night transmission. This programme is about a people and a river. The people are the Ashkenazi Jews — Ashkenazim, the Ashkenazic Jews, a distinct ethnic subdivision of the Jewish diaspora, the name drawn from Ashkenaz, the old Germanic word for a land of the north. The river is the Rhine, and it will stay with you for the whole hour, because it is the river they came down and the river they left up.

They emerged, as the record of it was kept, from the Jewish communities that consolidated during the tenth century in the Rhineland — western Germany — and in northern France, having migrated there from centres such as the Italian Peninsula and the Southern Levant. Three towns in this programme, three that keep the oldest memory: Mainz, Worms, Cologne. In Worms there is a synagogue rebuilt in 1267 and 1286, and in front of it two lions, carved, seated, watching the road. The lions are the only witnesses we have given moustaches.

[SOUND: the river closes in; then a door opens on a house with a wooden porch and a door that does not quite lock; a woman is setting a table for seven; the sound of a knife on a board stops.]

RUTH (of Worms; her voice is close to the microphone; she has been waiting for it to be close for a long time)

The summer my daughter turned eleven, 1096, the travellers came down from the hills in the west. They said they were going to Jerusalem, and they said they had no road, and they said we would show them, because the Jews had the keys. The town rector stood at the church porch and said God’s will is done, in a small voice, and went home. The crier of the town cried nothing. My husband — his name was Eliezer, a wool-merchant, he kept his accounts in a book with a wooden cover — my husband put the lamp in the window first, and the lamp in the window is the worst thing, the lamp is how they found us.

We went into the upper room. In Mainz that summer, two hundred and fifty of the community went together into the cathedral and did not come out; they killed one another before they would let the strangers in, so that the strangers found no hands they had to break. I keep this in the programme because the keeper keeps it in the folio, and a folio that leaves out a number is a folio with a hole in it.

[SOUND: water again; a chain; the ferryman’s boat lifting over a stone; then, far off, a choir of the city cathedral singing a plainchant in four parts, unhurried, as though the war were a season like any other.]

FERRYMAN (of the ferry at Bingen; old; he has made the crossing for forty years)

A ship of the storm, is what they called a summer when the Rhine rose. And there are those who said that a year is a prefecture of water — one prefecture in the spring flood, one in the summer low water, one in the autumn mist, one in the ice, and that a ship that could wander before the storm for four of them had earned the name of the keeper’s. That is the line, if you want the line.

But what I watched on that river, 1096 to 1147 to 1196 to 1298: carts going east. Every massacre, the carts went east a little further. The Holy Roman Empire grew more shut — no new synagogue after the burning of 1147 in Worms, that is the rule I can prove, a rule the town itself wrote — and east of the mountains the doors were being opened. In 1334 the king of Poland, Casimir III the Great, sent word into the towns that a Jew who settled in his realm would not be put to death for the blood of Christ, and he set out a year of protection, and after the year, protection again. The Black Death came through in 1348 and 1349 and the Rhine towns called it the Jews’ fault for the wells; the king’s roads east were not closed by it. The carts went east.

[SOUND: a new room; a fire that is not a burning; a scribe’stool; a quill; the wind of the Vistula instead of the wind of the Rhine, a wider wind, flatter.]

SCRIBE (of Kraków; young; his voice is the first one in this programme that sounds rested)

Kraków, 1406. The new quarter is called Kazimierz, after the king, and the king is still alive, Casimir the Great, the third of the name, and the streets are being laid out for the people the Rhine sent us. I am copying the charter into the town book: no Jewish house is to stand nearer than two hundred paces to a church, which is the price, and — the other clause — that a Jew who comes may keep his law, his courts, his measures and his markets. Two hundred paces is the whole of the price. I underline it twice, because I want the next scribe to know it cost two hundred paces and nothing else, and I underline it because my father crossed the mountains with it folded in his coat.

[SOUND: the shofar returns, one long note; the sabbath bell with it; between the two, the scratch of a reed-pen in a cold room that is now a warm room; the river is still there under all of it, the Rhine and the Vistula running into each other in the night air.]

KEEPER (of the folio; no name in the programme; the voice reads the folio exactly as the folio is written, and where the folio is odd, the voice does not smooth it)

I keep the folio of the night transmission, and I set down what the voices have given me. The river: the Rhine, west bank, four towns, Mainz, Worms, Cologne, Bingen. The year the lions of Worms were left facing the road: 1267, and again 1286. The eastward road: opened by the protection of Casimir III the Great, widened by the Black Death years, 1348 and 1349, and paid for at two hundred paces in Kraków, 1406.

And from the ferryman’s page, which I set down whole, because a keeper who trims a title trims the ship: The keeper will note the title of "a dining room that a ship of the storm can wander before it for at least four prefectures since." I do not know what it means. The ferryman knew it. It is set down, whole, in the folio of R-1147, and the folio will keep it the way the lions keep the road.

[SOUND: the broadcast ends where the broadcast began; the shofar, the bell, the water; then the station’s level, and the announcer’s voice, lower.]

ANNOUNCER

This is Station R-1147. The Ashkenazim went from the Rhineland to the Vistula and from the Vistula onward, and the folio of this programme ends where the record does not: on the road. There are other night transcriptions in this miscellany — the fire-watchers’ broadcast through the night of 14–15 November 1940 is kept at Coventry, 14 November 1940: A Radio Play Excerpt, and the sluice logbook of a drained lake in the Krummhörn, four metres below the waterline of the world, is kept at The Pump & Polder, Newsletter No. 47. Both of them hold their nights the way this folio holds its river. Return to the Webstuffs front page when the level comes back. The river will still be there. It is the longest voice in this programme, and it has been speaking since before the first of the voices on it.
