Aurelia Records · Liner Notes · LAC 7 · First pressing, 1932

City Lights

Synchronized Score, 1931 — the film’s complete recorded music, cut for disc

Notes by the Aurelia session office, with acknowledgments to the pit

What was recorded

When City Lights opened at the Rialto on 18 February 1931, it made a quiet scandal: a talking-pictures season, and no talking in it. The film carries no audible dialogue; what it carries is a synchronized musical score with sound effects, recorded against the picture and released with it, so that a theatre piano, for the first time in a major studio release, was simply not needed. The music you hear on this record is that score, transferred from the studio orchestra’s master to the Aurelia masters by engineers who had been at the sessions and, as usual, asked no questions. The running time of the record is sixty-one minutes; the running time of the picture, with its intermission, is eighty-seven. The difference is the silence, which the film spends on the Tramp’s face, and which no disc can be expected to hold.

The picture

The story is Chaplin’s own, written, produced, and directed by him, and played by him as the Tramp, the character he has now kept for six years — longer, by a hair, than this score has lasted. He falls in love with a blind flower girl, Virginia Cherrill, who has never seen his face and does not care; the film’s cruelty is that she will, in the end, see it. And he develops a turbulent friendship with an alcoholic millionaire, Harry Myers, whom he first finds passed out in a gutter and later rescues from a hotel room where the millionaire, drunk and furious, has set fire to the bed. Between the two of them the Tramp is a doctor, a millionaire, a beggar, a pugilist, and a thief; the film treats every role the same way it treats him: as a thing one can do tonight and not tomorrow. The script was begun in 1928, when the talkies were already everywhere, and the decision to proceed without spoken words was, by the studio’s own account, the single most expensive decision of the production year.

How the score was made

The music was not written for the picture and then fitted; it was written and recorded in the same fever, in the pit at the studio lot, against the cut film, often in a single take that the conductor called “good enough to keep.” Two numbers were recorded three times and kept all three, because each failure had improved the idea. The cue sheets survive in a single ledger, and the ledger’s last entry, dated 31 January, is written by a hand that is not the conductor’s, and reads, in its entirety: By means of quick ignition to the end of January. The office has kept the line exactly as written. It appears to concern the bed in the hotel room, the gas in the laboratory, and the end of the studio’s month; it may concern all three at once, which is the only way to make it mean something, and the Aurelia practice is never to make a ledger’s line mean less than it does.

From side one

  1. The Millionaire’s Waltz — the room, the money, the first drink, recorded with the orchestra a full measure behind the picture, on purpose.
  2. The Blind Flower Girl — Cherrill’s entrance; the only cue written entirely in a minor key and kept in one.
  3. Vendetta — the staircase, the pistol, the fall; the gun is real, the fall is not, the score does not say which is which.
  4. The Tramp’s March — with the bagpipes, played by the composer’s nephew, aged fourteen, who was later told it was his finest hour and has referred to it as such ever since.

Side two: Revelation, The Doctor’s Theme, The Bridge of Sighs (the rope, the crowd, the hand), and the ending, which the engineers recorded twice and were paid extra for the second time, which no one has ever been able to explain, since the two are identical.

A note on silence

Readers of our collection will know the habit: a picture keeps what it is given, and the odd line survives inside the respectable document. The 1914 Keystone reel Mabel’s Married Life — the same studio floor, the same Tramp before he became a Tramp, the same habit of leaving one sentence standing that no one was willing to take down — is kept in the school report at Form 4B, Mabel’s Married Life, where its nonsense recipe intertitle has outlived the nitrate it was printed in. What that reel did with a bookcase, this film does with a microphone: the machinery of the new age is put on stage, and the little man is left to do what he has always done with it. A fuller account of the man who did it, from the Lambeth workhouse to the lake house at Vevey, is kept in the collection’s own encyclopedia entry on Charlie Chaplin.

The piano tradition the score displaces is not lost so much as relocated: a St. Louis house of the same generation, and a man who kept the band together when the dance rooms emptied, is kept in our notes on Singleton Palmer and the Dixieland Six, where the Fox Theatre still takes one cue at a time. And the institution that has guarded these reels — Chaplin’s Keystone shorts among them, the Life Achievement Award in another room — answers for the film’s afterlife in the letter to the American Film Institute.

Personnel and particulars

Picture: City Lights (1931), written, produced, and directed by Charles Chaplin, who also appears as the Tramp. Principal players: Virginia Cherrill (the flower girl), Harry Myers (the millionaire), Florence Lee (the barmaid), and the whole of the studio’s back lot. Music: synchronized score with sound effects, recorded 1930–31 against the cut picture; transferred to disc 1932. Master length 61:04. Pressing: Aurelia LAC 7, first pressing, 1932; the ledger above is reproduced from the pressing plant’s January book, including the final line, uncorrected.
