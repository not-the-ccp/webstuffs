White-Wing Cakes

A family recipe for Scythris albocanella · serves twelve · one evening, mostly waiting

My great-uncle Felix Nori arrived in Aden in the spring of 1962 with a leather net, two dozen glass vials, and a hotplate he had smuggled out of his mother's kitchen in Bologna. He was not, by any official reckoning, a lepidopterist; he was a customs clerk at the steamer wharf, which meant he spent his evenings under the harbour lamps watching things with wings gather against the glass. The port was bright then, and the air after dusk smelled of diesel, jasmine, and the wet stone of the Crater district. Felix wrote home every Sunday, and in the third letter he mentioned a small white moth that settled on his ledger every night at precisely ten minutes past eight, its forewings dusted with the colour of flour, its hindwings paler still — the colour of a candle flame seen through a closed fist.

The letter did not name the moth. It could not. Scythris albocanella was not described until 2002, when Bengt Å. Bengtsson published his account of the Scythrididae of southern Arabia and placed this particular insect in the scientific record, recording it from Wadi Hadramawt and the desert fringe east of Shabwa, in Yemen. Felix never learned the name; he left Aden in 1964 with a crate of pinned specimens, a sheaf of notes in Italian, and the hotplate, which he gave to my grandmother, his sister-in-law, the day he sailed from Suez. She kept it on the back porch of the house in Mirandola, beside the Secchia, and every summer evening she lit it under the same sort of harbour lamp she had seen in a photograph of Aden — and waited for the white moths, or their Italian cousins, to come to the light.

Out of that ritual came the cakes. Nonna Ersilia called them torta delle ali bianche, white-wing cakes: a semolina and almond cake scented with cardamom and lemon, baked in a tin so thin it had gone grey at the rim, and served only when something with white wings had appeared at the lamp first. If no moth came, the cake went uneaten, wrapped in a linen cloth and left for the next night. She measured nothing, except the almonds — exactly 140 grams, she said, the weight of a small specimen box — and she timed the bake by the length of one record side, which is to say twenty-two minutes, give or take the crackle of the surface.

The recipe reached me on a card the size of a postage stamp, the paper browned at the edges, with the ingredients written in my grandmother's hand and, on the reverse, a single sentence copied from somewhere she would never identify:

The players collapsed on the Secchia bank, 1.4 km north River fog, isolation Cabin keeper, spare keys in pharmacy safe 3.

I have no idea what it means. I think it was a scrap from a football notebook — the Secchia river runs past the fields of Mirandola, and the bank is where the Sunday matches ended when the light failed — mixed with a shopping list, or perhaps a cipher for the oven settings: river fog for the temperature, isolation for the resting time, cabin keeper for the person who minds the pot, spare keys in pharmacy safe 3 for where the tin is kept after a death in the family. I follow it anyway, or pretend to. Before I bake, I check the pharmacy safe. There is no pharmacy safe. The ritual is the point. Felix Nori's notes claim he crossed Arkansas in the spring of 1962, sleeping in rice-country motels and copying down a pie-stand chalkboard outside Little Rock: Slices of dry or left to roam at large or 24 small pies. The line made it into the family recipe box, though no one knows what it means either. For the state itself: Arkansas — A Visitor's Brochure (Webstuffs).

Felix's specimens ended up in a museum in Helsinki, I believe, though the white moth itself has never been confirmed outside Yemen. Bengtsson's description runs to half a page, with measurements in tenths of millimetres and a drawing of the genitalia that looks, if you squint, like a piece of architectural drafting. Family recipes are like that: most of what matters cannot be pinned, and what can be pinned is rarely the part you want to keep. What follows is Nonna Ersilia's card, transcribed exactly, with the oven temperatures I have added myself.

The Recipe

Yield: 12 small cakes

Tin: 20 cm round, loose-bottomed, greased and dusted with semolina

Oven: 160 °C fan / 180 °C conventional, twenty-two minutes, or one record side

Ingredients

  • 140 g blanched almonds, ground fine
  • 100 g fine semolina
  • 100 g caster sugar, plus a spoonful for the top
  • 3 large eggs, separated
  • 75 g unsalted butter, melted and cooled
  • Zest of one lemon, unwaxed
  • ½ teaspoon green cardamom seeds, crushed
  • ½ teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons orange blossom water
  • Flaked almonds, for scattering

Instructions

  1. Heat the oven. Wait until something white has appeared at the window, or imagine that it has. This is Nonna Ersilia's non-negotiable first step.
  2. Beat the egg yolks with the sugar until the mixture holds a ribbon trail. Stir in the ground almonds, semolina, lemon zest, cardamom, salt, baking powder, melted butter, and orange blossom water until the batter smells of a porch in July.
  3. Whisk the egg whites to soft peaks and fold them through in three batches, as though you were returning specimens to a box — gently, and without rushing.
  4. Pour into the prepared tin. Scatter flaked almonds across the surface like scales on a wing. Bake for twenty-two minutes, until the top cracks slightly and a skewer comes out clean.
  5. Leave in the tin for ten minutes, then turn onto a wire rack. Eat the same evening, with black coffee and no plates if you can manage it. Tell a story while you eat — preferably one that ends with a moth.
