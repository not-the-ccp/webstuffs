The Machinery Restaurant Trust · Council · Minute No. 47 of 2024

The Matter of the North Hall
and Mr. Cantor

Held in the long room above the pass, 11 Aleph Terrace · present: nine of eleven members · the two absent members having been counted by correspondence

MACHINERY RESTAURANT TRUST · 14 November 2024 Held At 19:00, in the manner of heroic legend, alliterative from first line to the north.

The recital above is entered into the record word for word, as ordered by the Chair, and is not to be re-ordered.

Item 1 The name of the man

The Chair, E. Venn (of Venn’s Corner, who had brought the chairs from the larder in two sets), opened the minutes at 19:00 sharp and reminded the council that the North Hall, refit this year at a cost of €41,200, still hangs out its old name, the Waiting Room, which nobody had chosen and everybody tolerated. The Clerk, M. Zermelo, then read from the pass ledger the name proposed for the hall: Georg Ferdinand Ludwig Philipp Cantor, [ˈɡeːɔʁk ˈfɛʁdinant ˈluːtvɪç ˈfɪlɪp ˈkantoːɐ̯], pronounced in the trade as KAN-tor, because a man who founded set theory should not be mumbled. Born 3 March 1845 in St. Petersburg (Old Style 19 February, the ledger keeps both dates, old above new), he studied at Zurich and Göttingen, took the chair at Halle in 1881, and founded there the seminar which became the German mathematical society and the journal in which he published. He played a pivotal role in the creation of set theory, which has become a fundamental theory in mathematics; the council noted that this is the kind of sentence a minute exists to survive.

Item 2 The one-to-one correspondence of the chairs

Cantor established the importance of one-to-one correspondence between the members of two sets, and the council applied the rule to its own furniture. The Clerk called off the numbers on the chairs, one to one, and found the hall held forty-four numbered chairs against forty-three names on the guest book. Mr. H. Dedekind moved that the surplus chair be given the number ℵ ω and kept for nobody, and that no two chairs ever again share a number. The Chair objected that the hall would then have more chairs than sitters, and Mr. Dedekind replied, correctly, that that is precisely how the matter was meant to run. The motion was carried eight to one, the one vote being the Chair’s, who voted against out of professional jealousy of the diagram bearing his own name.

Item 3 The diagonal, read into the record

The council then considered the proof which the minutes regard as the reason the hall exists. Cantor defined the infinite and the well-ordered set, and he proved that the real numbers are more numerous than the natural numbers — that no list, however long, however patient, however much of it pinned to the wall, can contain them all. His method of proof, the diagonal argument, implies the existence of an infinity of infinities, and the Clerk read it aloud from the blackboard copy, in the old alliterative manner, because the recital at the top of this minute required the house to keep the north line heroic. When she finished, the room was quiet in the way a pass is quiet at the top of the service, and B. Russell, who had been quietly counting the corners of the blackboard, said ℵ 0 and sat down. The cardinal and ordinal numbers and their arithmetic, which Cantor defined, were adopted as the standard of the kitchen: the ovens are cardinals, the course of the evening is an ordinal, and a dessert served after every other dessert is understood by the staff.

Item 4 The green pin and the vellum leaf

For the naming, two objects were fetched and laid on the pass. The first was the rosette pin, green and unworn, which a naturalist of the Flemish arrondissements has described at length in the CD&V field notes, where it is pinned to the inside of a lid and reported not to fade; the council agreed it would not fade, on the strength of the report, and fixed it to the new sign. An account of what the hall did with the pin that night, remembered second and broken in the middle of a sentence, is filed in the Pocket Dictionary of Dreams, Entry A-119, between aleph and altar. The second was the vellum choirbook leaf, the “Flanders Te Deum” of lot 47 at the Flanders & Flanders sale, in whose tenor a sustained pitch is held while the other voices move above it; the kitchen sang one verse of the Gloria over it, in the old manner, one voice against another, before the leaf was rewrapped in its paper and the salt returned to the rack.

Item 5 The end of the man, and of the minute

It was recorded, without motion, that Cantor died on 6 January 1918 in a sanatorium in Halle, at the age of seventy-two, after years in which his work was opposed by men of high name and his own health failed him; the council noted the opposition in the past tense, and the health in the past tense, and did not dwell. The North Hall is hereby the Cantor Hall, the sign is hung, and the surplus chair keeps its number. The Clerk will send a copy of this minute to the two absent members, in strict one-to-one correspondence, one copy to one member. There being no further business, the minutes were signed at 21:14, which the Clerk recorded as a well-ordered time, and the hall stood up and went down the north stairs.
