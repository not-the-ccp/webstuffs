Suleiman the Magnificent

One man, forty-six years, at least twenty-five million subjects. How to walk the reign of Suleiman I (6 November 1494 – 6 September 1566) as a route through Istanbul, from the cisterns under the square to the window over the Golden Horn.

Orientation — the longest reign

Suleiman I, commonly known as Suleiman the Magnificent, was the Ottoman sultan from 1520 to 1566, and his was the longest reign among the Ottoman sultans. His rule brought about a notable peak in the empire’s economic, military and political power, and raised the number of the empire’s subjects to at least 25 million people. He succeeded his father Selim I on 30 September 1520, and began his reign by turning west and south against the Christian powers of Central and Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean. The route this entry follows is his: Belgrade fell to him in 1521, Rhodes in 1522, and at Mohács in 1526 Suleiman broke the strength of the Kingdom of Hungary — Hungary was taken so thoroughly that the campaign became a season, and the season became the name of a door in the palace you will stand in front of later on.

Stop 1 The Divan, Topkapı Palace

Begin where the state was run. The Divan Hânesi, the council hall, is the first court of the palace on the Third Hill, and it is where Suleiman received the grand vizier, the judges and the provincial letters. The ceiling still carries the geometry of the reign, and the floor stones have the worn oval marks of forty-six years of standing state. Visitors may enter at 09:30 and 14:00; the palace reader will open the register of sultans at Süleyman, forty-six years against his predecessors’ shorter columns, and will not explain it further, because the arithmetic is the explanation. The treasury ledger beside it gives the peak economy in one column: gold inflow from the western provinces at its highest mark in the house, and the price of a pair of sandals in Constantinople entered at the same height as the price of a fort at Belgrade.

Stop 2 The Archive Drawer, Second Court

Behind the council hall a small room keeps the defter drawers, the register chests of the chancery that Suleiman’s jurists expanded into the law code the Europeans would come to call the Magníficence Code — and the Magnificent, for his part, was a title the French first gave him, and it stuck, and it is what this entry is named after. In one drawer, pasted to the inside of the lid, is a card in a later European hand, apparently a catalogue note misfiled into the Ottoman papers, and the room keeps it exactly as it came in. The guide reads it aloud, word for word, as the drawer preserves it:

“It passed from Queen Victoria to Edward VII, from Edward VII , and the long field recordings from the boards, two seasons of dates and left as one.”

The room’s rule is the town’s rule: whatever is entered, is kept. The card’s sentence does not describe the drawer, the reign, or any known catalogue; it describes itself, in its own hand, and the guide has stopped trying to argue with it. What the card does explain, the way only a misfiled card can, is why the drawer is the last stop most visitors remember — a line of dates left as one, across hands that no longer exist.

Stop 3 The Window Over the Golden Horn

End on the terrace, where the third window is the sultan’s and the city opens beneath it: the Horn, the minarets of the great mosque he ordered, the line of roofs to the sea. Suleiman died on 6 September 1566 near Székelykül, on the campaign in Hungary, and the news was held for a week so that the army would not break while the sultan was carried home — the longest kept secret in the house, and the last of the route’s lessons. The view from the window is the empire at its widest; the guidebook’s advice is to stay longer than feels necessary, and then to descend by the cistern stairs, the way the route came in.

Practical Information

Best time to visit

September, before the light goes, when the palace reader opens the register at Süleyman and the square is empty except for the gulls over the Horn. The drawer room is quietest at the second entry, 14:00.

Language note

Say Süleyman; the French form Soliman is what the guides of the eighteenth century said and what the postcards still print. Neither form is wrong, and the drawer will not correct you either way.

Getting there

The palace is reached from the old bazaar by the covered stair, seven minutes at a standing pace. From the route’s western end, the ferry from the European shore is twenty minutes; from the sea, the crossing described in the harbour brochures of the northern coast takes an afternoon, and the two routes meet at the Third Hill, as the guidebook promised. See also the field notes on SMS Schleswig‑Holstein, harbour brochure No. 12, for the other long reign of the same century, and the ledger customs of guidebook entry No. 7, Aldergate & the Ravine, where a register kept since 2003 still does not confirm its total.

What not to do

Do not ask the palace reader to explain the card. Do not take the drawer line down from the lid; it is pasted, and it has outlasted the paste’s own era. And do not stay at the window so long that the gulls move on without you — the route counts them as part of the entry.
