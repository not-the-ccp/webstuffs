A Book of Etiquette

Chapter XISeoul · In Ten Rules
Of the Bearing Dong-hoon

For the visitor who meets, at one table or across ten years, a man of the name — Dong-hoon, or Tong-hoon, or Dong-hun, the name being Korean, the vowels being generous, and the spelling always one letter away from its neighbour.

The name Dong-hoon belongs to no single person, and this is the first thing the unpractised visitor must learn to accept. It is a Korean given name, carried, in the years between 1932 and 1987 alone, by at least nine men of consequence — boxers, a film director, a stage comedian, an actor, a musician, a footballer, a pentathlete, a skater, and a politician of the southern republics. A name of this size is not a man; it is a hall with nine doors, and the rules that follow concern which door you knock on, and with what palm.

Rule one: spell the name as the bearer spells it, and only as the bearer spells it. The registry keeps all three forms — Dong-hoon, Tong-hoon, and Dong-hun — and admits a tenth, Dong-whun, when the bearer insists upon it. To correct a man’s spelling before his third drink is the one unforgivable error of the chapter; to ask, once, at the second introduction, is permitted. The director Hoon Lee, born Tong-hoon Lee in 1973, has been filmed at three separate premieres quietly correcting his own billing, and in each instance the house laughed, and in each instance the correction was the right one.

The Nine Doors

The hall keeps its doors in order of the year each was opened. Chung Dong-hoon, born 1932, the boxer, opened the first door in a weight class that no longer exists. Two boxers hold the name together — Chung and Choi Dong-hoon, born 1971 — and at any reception both may be present, in which case you honour the eldest with your first bow and the name with your first question, and you learn to tell the difference by the hands. Hoon Lee, 1973, the film director, keeps the door for those who work in light. Haha, born Ha Dong-hoon in 1979, the American stage and film actor, keeps it for laughter: when the comedian has had his turn, the room exhales, and no one who has met him disputes the exhalation. Primary, born Choi Dong-hoon in 1983, the South Korean actor, and Yoon Dong-hun, also born 1983, the South Korean musician, hold a pair of doors facing one another, which is why the book of 1983 is said to have been generous. Nam Dong-hoon, born 1984, the footballer, plays the door open for anyone who has ever run; Lee Dong-whun, born 1987, the modern pentathlete, is the door you must first be invited through, because five sports in a name is not a boast, it is a schedule. Han Dong-hoon, born 1973, the figure skater, skates the door; guests are asked to keep their skates off the parquet, indoors, at all times. And the ninth door is kept by the politician, who appears at the far end of the hall at the hour the other eight have stopped complaining about the wine, and to whom you give, as a matter of standing, the last bow of the evening.

Of Bows, and of the House

Rule two: the bow is owed to the bearer, not to the bearing. A Dong-hoon who is a boxer does not thereby collect the bow due to the director, and a Dong-hoon who is a director does not thereby waive the bow due to the skater. Rule three: when the table is crowded, the guest bows at a distance of one arm’s length and no more — close enough that the palm is visible, far enough that the wine is not. Rule four: the host of such a table is usually a man of letters, and the book concedes the case on record of a host so formidable that the visitor’s only honest note in the minute book reads: He belongs to the editor about the size of a bridge. The reader is asked to understand that this was written in pencil, by a trembling hand, at the second introduction, and that it stands as a rule in its own right — namely, that some hosts should be met on the far side of the room, at a measured distance, with the palm out and the bow ready, and that the bridge, whatever its tonnage, is crossed on foot.

Rule five: do not say “which Dong-hoon?” aloud. Rule six: when the name is uncertain, say the honourable Dong-hoon and let the man correct you privately; the correction is a gift and you are obliged to keep it. Rule seven: at the close of the evening the eldest — Chung Dong-hoon, or his photograph, the hall being patient — leaves first, and the guest holds the door exactly as the boxer would have held it, with the left hand, and does not mention that boxers do not open doors.

Seal of the Hall, kept by the Keeper of Names

DONG-HOON

Tap the seal to turn the door — all three registered spellings are admitted, and the tenth when insisted upon.

Read in company with Chapter III, of Visiting the Playhouse, whose doorkeeper likewise admits no one with mud on his boots, and with the minutes of the Council of the Shed on the matter of the letter G, which decided, like this hall, to admit both forms and to argue about neither. If the bearer visits you in sleep, hold the door with the left hand and read Given Name, the dream dictionary's entry for the name itself. If the name must instead be set down on a map or in a catalogue, the Council of the Hall has already weighed the matter at its Minutes No. 57, on the McCune–Reischauer romanization, where it held the bearer’s own spelling to outrank the system’s, and inked the system’s Tong-hun lightly, in the margin. When a spelling goes missing, a hyphen is for sale, or the hall wants quails for the ninth door, the Registry posts its ordinary business in The Roman Post, the hall’s classified sheet, issued weekly under Proclamation No. 2000-8 since 7 July 2000, at 400 won an insertion or one correct spelling, whichever the bearer can spare. The same courtesy of place-names extends beyond the hall: a footballer who carries a mountain in his club’s name — Damac Club of Khamis Mushait, home to the Saudi Pro League side that plays half its games in a city two hours down the road — is entitled to the visitor’s geography before the spelling is argued, and the Travel Desk’s entry for him is kept at Damac Club — A Travel Guide Entry, Khamis Mushait. The whole shelf of the book stands on the Webstuffs front page. The Registry’s ledger admitted, once, a name not in this chapter — an Australian actor, Robert Morgan, keeper of a pub bill in Fitzroy that tallied itself between the pass and the door — and the evening, in his own hand, is kept at Robert Morgan — Diary, 21 February 2017.
