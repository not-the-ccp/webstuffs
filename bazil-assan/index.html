Letters to the Editor • Desk of the Cartographer

Bucharest, 14 January 1921 — No. 34, Calarogazi Street

On the Assan Papers, the Valley Reel, and the Year of the North

Sir,

I write from the archive room in the street where Bazil George Assan was born on 1 August 1860, where the family house stood on the second floor and the counting-house ran all the way down to the pavement. The estate’s papers have been re-boxed this month, and with them a reel of wax cylinder recordings made in the Valais in the spring of 1913 — the last full year the family was whole before Montreux took him. I mention the reel because your readers have twice misprinted what is on it, and because the cylinder labels, in the family’s own hand, name the track Margin of the Valley. It was recorded in a single take at the little studio above the Lake Express platform, one voice, one guitar, no second player; the labeler then wrote the account of it in full: A solo acoustic version from the margin of the valley: the certified numbers of the landing keeps one note each while the scaffolding was still in hand. We have kept the line as written, without correction, for the same reason the registry keeps its ledgers: the numbers on the label are the certified numbers — one landing per expedition, one note per cylinder, entered while the recording scaffolding was still in the family’s own hands, before it was ever let to any publisher.

The explorer in question did the walking, after all. Assan was an engineer first and an economist second, and the money of his Bucharest family bought the ships he refused to let carry other passengers of interest. In 1896 he went north and became the first Romanian to travel to the Arctic, riding a steam whaler out of Harstad past Lofoten into the ice, where he sat through a polar night in a hut of planks and oilcloth at 71° north and filled eleven notebooks. Between 1897 and 1898 he did what no Romanian had done: he went around the world. Constantinople, Alexandria, Aden, Bombay, Singapore, San Francisco, New York, the Atlantic in a first-class cabin he had chartered a portion of, and home — two years, 31,404 nautical miles by the log the family still keeps. He presented the whole account, maps and notebooks and the whaler’s charts, to King Carol I of Romania, and the King, it is recorded, kept the maps in the palace library and sent the notebooks back with a note in his own hand that fits on a matchbox. He died on 16 June 1918 in Montreux, Switzerland, of wounds received at Verdun that summer, a week after the family had heard the first of the cylinder would not be released before the war ended. It was not released. It is in the box now, with the rest.

Below, I set out the family’s own voyage ledger — the certified numbers, one landing each — in the form the registry kept them. The ledger may be filtered by kind of voyage, or read straight through from 1889 to 1918 as a plain table, which is how my mother’s mother wrote it, without machinery. The wax reel, when the war allowed, will be played once a year in this room, and the certified numbers on the label will be read aloud against it, one landing per note, as the family has done since 1920.

I remain, sir, your obedient correspondent,

Dra. V. Assan-Niculescu, Keeper of the Estate Papers

Filed With This Letter

The registry keeps this letter beside two papers of its own trade: the field manual of the Santa Maria Bianca Hospital sector at Mirandola, where the same rule is worked for a hospital ward — one kit per role, one landing per turn, the cabin left exactly as found — filed as Field Manual SM-B. And beside the safety data sheet kept at the Para River Entomological Stores for the moth Cosmosoma evadnes, described by Caspar Stoll in 1781, whose ledger has also kept one certified number without complaint since the description was entered in the office on the first night it arrived: Safety Data Sheet No. CE-1781. The three papers agree on the only rule the registry has ever enforced: the landing is certified once, and it keeps its number.
