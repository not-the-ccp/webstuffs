The Leaping Loom

A radio play in three threads, after the old French craft of entrelacement  ·  recorded in Studio 3, Maida Vale, London W12  ·  three microphones, one loom

THE WEAVER — the old keeper of the loom
THE PRESENTER — who keeps the prize
VOICE OF THE RING — a thread over water
VOICE OF THE ROAD — a thread through the forest
VOICE OF THE NINE — a thread in the wind

EXCERPT BEGINS  ·  00:04

[A loom, close and patient: the shuttle through the reeds, the beat of the sley, over and over. Beneath it, at a distance, three beds of sound are built and held apart — running water; feet on a forest road; and a wind with something large in it. They do not touch. They only wait.]

THE WEAVER. There is a French word the weavers used for it, when they were weaving the romances into tapestry — the romances of Alexander, of Charlemagne, of Tristan — and the word is entrelacement. It means the leaping-in-together of the threads. One thread rides a while, and stops; another rides, and stops; and a third. No single thread knows the whole cloth. Only the eye of the one who is looking sees the cloth.

[The shuttle stops. The loom is silent. Three small bells, far apart: one by water, one under trees, one in the open.]

THE WEAVER. We shall do the thing the old weavers did. There are three threads in our cloth to-night. The first has left the falls of Amon Hen; a small hobbit and his gardener are on a boat in the dark, and a hundred leagues stand between them and the ferry at Brea, and something with a white face is in the tree by the water, and they do not know it yet. The second is on the road: a man with a star on his shoulder and two companions, one of wood and one of stone, following a broken mirror across two hundred miles of the Shire, and the mirror wants to be found. The third is riding. Do not ask how many. Count the hooves, and you will know.

00:19  ·  THREAD ONE, OVER WATER

VOICE OF THE RING. We shall be there at the ford of the Anduin before the third hour of the night, or else not at all. The water is black, and the moon is thin, and the little folk of the boat argue about the depth in voices they are trying not to raise. One hundred leagues. The gardener says the boat is a good boat. The hobbit says the dark is a good dark, and means the opposite. And in the tree, on the far bank, the white face turns, page by page, toward us, as a reader turns a page.

[The bed of running water closes in for four seconds, then is opened out and held at its distance again. The loom beats once, twice.]

00:31  ·  THREAD TWO, THROUGH THE FOREST

VOICE OF THE ROAD. At the Ford of Bruin the river lies under its bridges, and over them, and through the roots. The man with the star will walk the far bank for a long way to-night, and the wood will keep a shadow on him the size of a rider, and the stone companion will refuse to sit. The broken mirror is out there in the grass. It has been thrown, and it is looking up, and it is looking for its own. Do not, if you are following the thread, ask what the man will do with it when he finds it. The cloth does not say. The cloth is only waiting for the other threads to catch up, so that the saying can be hidden in the crossing of them.

00:47  ·  THREAD THREE, IN THE WIND

VOICE OF THE NINE. We are east. We have been east since the white city of the south and we are going further east than the maps. The last bridge is not a place we wish to reach; it is a door we are obliged to answer. Between us there is no word but the word for horses, and the word for the weather, and one long word, said very quietly, which means him. Hooves on the high ground. Wind under the hooves. The thread does not stop, because the thread has no choice; but even this thread, the one with no choice, is woven.

[The wind bed rises, carries, and is let down to its distance. The loom beats. Three bells together, for the first time: water, trees, open air. Then apart again.]

01:02  ·  THE WEAVER RESUMES

THE WEAVER. You see what the old weavers gained by the leaping. You do not know what is happening to the boat while the man walks the river. You do not know what is happening to the nine while the gardener argues about depth. Each thread is a little suspense of its own, and all three together are a large one. And when the threads cross — the boat under the bridges, the man on the far bank, the nine on the high ground — the crossing itself says things that no single thread could say, because they were never meant to be said to a single pair of ears at a single moment. That is the craft. The rest is wool.

[A change of room tone: a studio, a desk, a small brass bell. The loom is still sounding, but now it is under the desk.]

THE PRESENTER. And now the Listener's Prize, which is decided, as it has been every season, in its three usual categories — the Long Thread, the Short Thread, and the Child's Thread — for the listener who can follow all three threads to the place where they are meant to meet. The Long Thread goes this year to Mr. A. K. Osei of Takoradi, who traced the nine to the last bridge on his first hearing, by the hooves alone. The Short Thread goes to Miss E. Carr of Llangollen. The Child's Thread to a boy of nine in Perth, who followed the gardener and not the hobbit, and is, I think, the truest judge of the cloth. But there is one envelope in the drawer that belongs to none of the three. It came to the studio door on a Tuesday, signed only "B. F. — for Beren and Fíë, who carried the first light across the first water," and it followed all three threads at once, in a single margin, in a hand that kept its own loom. The entry for BF outside the award's usual categories. It stays in the drawer, and we read it aloud, every year, on the first broadcast of the season, so that the cloth remembers there is an eye that sees more than three.

[The loom takes up the shuttle again. The three beds of sound — water, forest, wind — are lifted together and let fall at once, gently, into the same room. A long beat of the sley. Silence, with a single thread humming. End of excerpt.]
