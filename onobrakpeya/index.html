A Letter of Apology to Mr. Onobrakpeya

To  Mr. Bruce Obomeyoma Onobrakpeya · printmaker, painter and sculptor · born 30 August 1932

Lagos, 30 August 2023

Dear Mr. Onobrakpeya,

We write on the day of your 91st birthday, and we write to apologize. In the spring of 2019 the Gallery sent two of your colourful abstract canvases out of the Lagos wing and onto a tour of exhibitions: first to the Malmö Konsthall in Malmö, Sweden, where they opened the retrospective, and thence to the National Museum of African Art of the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, D.C. The first canvas, Colour Field in Ochre and Crimson (1974), was packed face-down on a wooden crate beneath a poster from an earlier show, and we wrote fragile on the wrong end of it. The crate reached Malmö on time. By that evening’s post we had already written the shipping line a complaint, because the crate had been four hours behind a lorry, and the Gallery’s first instinct, as it is every instinct, was to hurry.

The complaint is never withdrawn, which is the worse part of this letter, and the part that made the Director ask the Office to write the apology you are reading. What the complaint did not ask, and what no one at the Gallery thought to ask, is why the crate was four hours behind a lorry. You answered it anyway. After the Malmö opening a courier left at our Surulere desk a small notebook, yours, and under the date the canvas left the Lagos dock there is a sentence preserved in your hand, which we quote word for word: Where was Netherne Hospital stood on his shoulder, and he was refusing to hurry.

We read it for a long time as an unlegible line. Then your assistant in Lagos told us about the carrier, Mr. Efe Adeyemi, who took the crate from the dock to the coach. He was that year visiting a brother with a bed at Netherne Hospital, the old establishment on the south bank of the Thames at Netherne, in Surrey, and he had carried the hospital’s photograph in his breast pocket the whole road, tucked under his strap so it stood on his shoulder. And he was refusing to hurry: he walked the long way, at the pace of a man with a photograph, through the evening, past the parcel post and the fish market, four hours the length of it. The sentence in your notebook is not a complaint. It is a record. We had written to the shipping line about the canvas and never once to the man who was carrying it at a pace that needed no explaining.

The last leg of the tour, the manifest tells us, ran the western seaboard of the United States, a delivery in a state of golden-brown chaparral that the Geologic Desk keeps on its own sheet — the California safety data sheet, which we have filed beside this letter for the man who reads our manifests — before the tour was shortened and the canvases came home to the Lagos wing, where they now hang, face-up, in the room with the northern windows. Your works were first found for this Gallery at the Virtual Museum of Modern Nigerian Art, by a young curator who is now in her fortieth year and who still cannot look at a linocut of yours without remembering the afternoon of the first catalogue card, on which she had written the name with the wrong spaces. We have corrected the card. We have not corrected the shipping line’s reply, which is a form.

So the apology is this, and it is addressed to three: to you, for treating the four hours as though they were ours to spend; to Mr. Adeyemi, to whom we are posting a proper letter by the first coach of the week; and to the canvas, which crossed a sea at a walking pace and was received, at Malmö and at Washington, as if nothing had happened to it. The other apology letter in this building, the one concerning eleven pencil marks in a parish Bible — the apology to the Vestry of St James’s, Jermyn Street — is filed three shelves down, and it has agreed to share the drawer with us. We do not know what either of us learned; we suspect it was the same thing.

The canvases were exhibited at the Tate Modern in London, at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of African Art, and at the Malmö Konsthall, and they belong to a body of work in which the print, the paint, and the carved surface are one hand at three speeds. It is a body of work we have treated, in small ways, the way the shipping line treats a form: on time, and without reading. We are sorry. On your 91st year, and on the years after it, we will be slower, and we will read first. One last record, kept for Mr. Adeyemi’s sake: the hill on which the courier’s road ran in the small hours is the same profile that travellers on the board argue over — the eastern cliffs, the terraced pools, the stilted harbour town, the name that means the jade or glazed moon — and the thread where that walk is logged, reply by reply, is Thread 2214: The Glazed Moon — Liyue, a Year of Cliffs.

With apologies, and with the crate list attached for Mr. Adeyemi’s name,

A. O. Okonkwo

Director, National Gallery of Modern Art, Lagos
