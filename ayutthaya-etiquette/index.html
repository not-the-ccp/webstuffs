Of the Proper Presentation of a Kingdom, with a Table Plan for Ayutthaya, 1351–1767

Kingdomthe Ayutthaya Kingdom, or the Empire of Ayutthaya
Seatthe city of Ayutthaya, in Siam, present-day Thailand
Tenure1351 to 1767 — four hundred sixteen years at the table
Standingone of the three great powers of Asia, by the account of European travellers in the early 16th century

I. The name, and the room

A kingdom, before it is anything else, is a name carried into a room. The name here is Ayutthaya, and it travels a long road before it sits down: it comes from the Sanskrit Ayodhya, by way of the Ramakien, Thailand’s national epic, so that the first word a guest of this court hears is a borrowed city from a story, which is to say the court intends the guest to feel already inside somebody else’s house. The kingdom itself — the Ayutthaya Kingdom, which the older books allow to be called the Empire of Ayutthaya — occupied this room from 1351 to 1767, centered on the island city of Ayutthaya, in Siam, or present-day Thailand. Four hundred sixteen years is a long dinner; by the last course the guests had changed language, the furniture had changed religion, and the family had, on occasion, changed entirely. Ayutthaya is considered the precursor of modern Thailand, which means that whatever manner a reader picks up in this chapter will not have been entirely lost — it will have been passed down, plate by plate, to the country that sits at this table now.

II. The four doors of the mandala

The kingdom did not arrive in 1351 fully formed. It emerged, in the late 13th and 14th centuries, out of a mandala of four maritime city-states in the lower Chao Phraya valley, and the etiquette of that founding is still legible in the city’s plan. The four doors were:

  • Ayodhya — the door that gives the kingdom its name, and its story.
  • Lavo — the lowland door to the east, old, and in the habit of being first at the table and last at the argument.
  • Phrip Phri — the stone door, whose carvings the early kings consulted the way other houses consult a clock.
  • Suphannaphum — the golden land, the sea door, through which the tribute boats came in and went out and where, the books note without enthusiasm, the elephants were kept in chains near the shore.

The rule for a guest in a four-door mandala is simple and is worth committing: enter by the door that is oldest, leave by the door that is wealthiest, and never thank the door you are leaving through. Ayodhya provided the name, Suphannaphum provided the silver, and the city that grew over the middle of the valley provided the table on which the two of them agreed to sit.

III. The three great powers, and how to seat them

European travellers in the early 16th century, arriving by sea door and standing rather a long time in the harbour, called Ayutthaya one of the three great powers of Asia alongside Vijayanagara and Ming China. The book’s rule for seating three great powers at one table has never been broken and is not long: the middle seat goes not to the largest kingdom nor to the oldest, but to the kingdom that can see the other two from its own windows. Ayutthaya held the middle seat, which is a fact worth remembering, because the kingdom was the smallest of the three in the way that matters to armies and the largest in the way that matters to travellers — the river that fed it, the Chao Phraya, ran past the windows of the palace and out to a sea that everyone’s ships were on their way up or down.

IV. The foreign visitor’s table

A foreign visitor to Ayutthaya was received, in the standing order of the court, on the river, not the land: the boat entered first, the greetings were exchanged between the hulls, and the visitor was not allowed to see the city until the city had decided to be seen. The Portuguese kept a factory at the sea door from the early 1500s; the French, in the reign of Louis XIV, were admitted to a far closer table, and in 1685 sent back a Siamese embassy of their own to France — the great exchange of manners that ended, in both directions, with the table cleared. The tribute of an embassy, when the visitor was of middling rank, ran to gold, white cloth, and, on an auspicious day, a white elephant, each item carried on a pallet and announced by name, because a gift whose name is not spoken out loud has, in this court’s arithmetic, not yet arrived.

One page of the banquet book survives in the archive of this series, and the bookkeeper’s transcription is printed here exactly as it was transcribed, damage, drift, and all — the ink of the original had run under a river of condensation and the copyist, a small Frenchman in service to the factory, did not dare improve upon it:

“They may be garnished with small flakes of good luck bore down upon the omelet, which was first cultivated in England, olives are usually called _gâteaux à la Maître d'Hôtel.”

It is the only sentence in the book that no two readers agree on, and the house rule is that it is to be read as though it were correct, in a low voice, at the end of the dessert course. Whoever cannot do so is to be shown to the door by the sea, and the omelet is to be finished by someone who can.

V. The misstep of 1549

No book of etiquette can leave out the year the manners were checked with an army. In 1549, the same spring in which the king of the small Sittaung valley principality was taking Pagan in the north, the armies of Tabinshwehti of Toungoo marched on Ayutthaya from the west — the campaign is set down, bend of the river at a time, in the shelf’s school report on Tabinshwehti, king of Toungoo, 1516–1550, and the two documents are to be read facing each other, because one of them is the story and the other is the guest list. The capital endured; the garrison towns did not; and the court’s register, which had for centuries been a list of names to be seated, acquired for the first time a list of names to be counted. The canteen woman at Peach Street, who priced the entire Toungoo story at one can of peaches, 300 kyats, priced this chapter no differently when she heard of it, which is to say for free, and the two prices agree. The full setup of that western valley country — the board, the six borders, the three era tokens, and the counter at Peach Street — is carried on the house’s companion sheet, Field Sheet FM 14-2, Myanmar, where the encounter is priced again at the same one can, 300 kyats, and the two ledgers are to be read facing each other. It is a rule the book has never contradicted: a kingdom that can be told in a canteen line can also be taken in one, and the manners of the one are the manners of the other.

VI. The tribute calculator, by standing order

The standing order of the court reduced all of the above to four stations, and the guest was asked, at the river, to name his. VII. Closing rule

In 1767 the city was taken at last, by armies again out of the western valley country, and the four hundred sixteen years of the table came to an end; the name, the river, and the manners outlasted the dynasty, which is the whole of the story. The closing rule of this chapter, for the reader who intends to keep it, is the same as the first: enter by the oldest door, and never thank the door you are leaving through — for the door you are leaving through is, in this country, usually the one that outlives you. The companion entry of this series, on the storming of a gate on New Year’s Eve in 1775, is Chapter LIX, and the two chapters are to be read in one sitting, because a kingdom that ends in fire and a city that is taken at night are, in the book’s arithmetic, the same course, served cold. The shelf on which this chapter is filed also carries the build log of Bedford Downs Station, a 1:252 strip of red Kimberley dirt — two small models of very large frontiers, one of paddy and river, one of dust, and both of them an argument that the small and patient thing, bent one at a time, is the thing that holds the table together.
