The Trundle

St Roche’s Hill · 4 miles (6 km) north of Chichester · West Sussex, England

AdmitOneWalk freely

What it is

From the lane off A272 the hill shows its shoulder: a broad, low knoll of chalk and loam, crowned with a ring of grassed rampart that has stood since the Iron Age. This is The Trundle, one of the great hillforts of the South Downs, built over a site already marked by a causewayed enclosure — a form of early Neolithic earthwork found across northwestern Europe. Causewayed enclosures were built in England from shortly before 3700 BC until at least 3500 BC; they are characterised by the full or partial enclosure of an area with ditches that are interrupted by gaps or “causeways” at intervals, so that an ancient visitor was made to cross in and out of the dune of earth on a processional footing. The Trundle keeps all of it: the older ditch in its lower fold, the Iron Age rampart and its double ditch above, and four thousand years of weather between.

The Chapel of St Roche Panel I

On the summit, facing the south-west, stands what the parish calls simply St Roche — a chapel dedicated to the plague-saint of Montpellier, built towards the end of the 14th century, when travellers on the Chichester road were offered here a place to say their beads and rest their beasts. By 1570 the records agree that it was in ruins: roof gone, chancel walls at half height, the font standing open in the rain like a well. The masons of the 1600s left it exactly thus, and the churchyard ivy has been the only rector since. Climb the final bank and the nave is yours for a hundred paces — look along the south wall for the original lancet, its tracery cut in 1390s stone, and for the shallow hollow where the altar once warmed the floor.

Windmill & Beacon Panel II

Subsequently, the hill took to working. A windmill was raised on the crest to grind grain for the farms of the north Downs, its sail plan catching the same wind that fills the sails of the trawlers two miles south over Chichester Harbour. A beacon stood beside it: the Trundle was lit in the chain of hill-fires that ran from the Downs to the Weald in times of invasion and of celebration — 1588 among them, when the smoke was said to be visible at Winchester. What remains is a low circle of flints where the mill floor was, and a stump of the beacon platform from which the view still goes a long way: the Lullworth plain to the east, the Sussex coast when the air is still, and Chichester cathedral square to the south, a dark point with a light on it.

The Meeting Place Panel III

In the post-medieval period the site was occasionally used as a meeting place: parishes from the four corners of the hundred gathered here for hill-fairs, musters, and the settling of grazing rights, under the open sky and, as the old ledger has it, on the strength of a shared fire. The vestry keeps the ledger’s last surviving entry, and the page is worth the walk by itself. It reads:

“V. Of adjournment The fire was looked to, the windows down.”

— The Trundle Meeting Ledger, final entry, hand of the 17th century, preserved in the vestry at Chichester

Ash in the urn, draughts cut off, company dispersed down the four lanes — the whole of a hill meeting, compressed by a tired clerk into one sentence, and it has outlasted the fire, the windmill, and the chapel roof.

Making the Walk Panel IV

Approach by the A272 north out of Chichester; the lane to the car park at the foot of the hill is signposted at the second bend, 1½ mile from the town’s ring road. The ascent takes 25 minutes at an unhurried pace: first the sheep walk with its waymarkers every 40 yards, then the bridle path over the rampart, then the open turf to the summit circle. Allow 90 minutes in all if you mean to read every panel and sit a while in the chapel nave. Bring water in summer; the hill gives shade to no one. The down walk follows the ditch line, where the causeway gaps are visible as pale breaks in the grass at first light. On the way home, Chichester’s own pages hold the rest of the day: the dining column of the city keeps a small room on a harbour lane reviewed course by course — see The Silkmen, Auckland — Restaurant Review · The Second Half — and the parish paper carries the notices of the hill’s own keeping, from chapel plate to meeting places — Church of England — Classified Advertisements.
