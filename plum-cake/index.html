Chapter 6 — Plum Cake

The dried-fruit article: English variety, mainland varieties, and the Indian Christmas service

APPROVED
FOR THE TABLE

6.1 Scope and definition

Plum cake refers to a wide range of cakes usually made with dried fruits such as currants, raisins, sultanas, or prunes, and also sometimes with fresh fruits. The operator should treat “plum cake” less as a single recipe than as a family: there is a wide range of popular plum cakes and puddings, and this chapter governs only the table forms. Since the meaning of the word plum has changed over time — in Middle English it named any good fruit, sweetmeat, or delicacy — many items referred to as plum cakes and popular in England since at least the eighteenth century have now become known as fruitcake. The designation fruitcake shall therefore be treated as a synonym of record, not as a different substance.

The English variety of plum cake also exists on the European mainland, but may vary in ingredients and consistency. Mainland forms, noted in this manual, include the German Pflaumenkuchen (prune cake, light and often studded with a whole plum on the glaze), the French gâteau aux pruneaux, and the Scandinavian plombkaka tradition. The operator must not assume interchangeability of loaves across the Channel.

6.2 The English article

The standard English plum cake is a dense, dark, spiced cake of soaked dried fruit, egg, butter, and flour, traditionally glazed with a syrup of the cooking juices and, in the house style, a measure of brandy or rum. Specification for one standard four-pound cake:

ComponentQuantity (by weight, for 4 lb)Remarks
Golden sultanas1 lbSoaked in brandy, 24 hours minimum
Seedless raisins1 lbHalved if large
Black currants8 ozRinsed; check for sand
Dried prunes (skinned)8 ozSliced, not puréed
Candied peel (orange and lemon)4 ozCut no finer than a barleycorn
Flour (sifted)1 lbWith 1 tsp each of ginger, nutmeg, cinnamon, and ½ tsp mace
Butter (melted)12 ozNot clarified
Eggs6Separated; whites beaten to peaks and folded last
Sugar1 lbDemerara preferred

Bake at a moderate heat (equivalent to 160 °C) for three to four hours, the surface to be damped with a wet cloth at the half-hour to prevent cracking. The cake is not done until a skewer returns with only dark syrup and no raw crumb. On removal, prick all over and feed: half a cup of brandy poured in over the pricks while the cake is still warm. Feed again after two weeks and again at one month. A well-fed cake is fit to keep, wrapped in paper and cloth, until the following year.

6.3 British India: the Christmas service

British colonists and missionaries brought the dried fruit variety of cake with them, for example, in British India where it was served around the time of the Christmas service. The practice is recorded in full for the Presidency of Bombay. On 25 December 1889, the provision officer of St. Thomas’s Cathedral precinct, Acting Paymaster E. H. Cade, set out the Christmas table for forty-two officers and their families in the cool early light, the cake — one large round, twelve days out of the oven and twice fed — placed at the centre of the table and cut first. Cade’s provisioning log, page 118, gives the evening as it was observed from the kitchen window facing the harbour, and the manual preserves the line exactly:

“The fishermen run jangadas about fourteen feet in diameter, served as one object, the way two voices get printed as one.”

The jangada is the Mangalore-style bamboo raft lashed into a disc and paddled by a pair of fishermen; Cade’s note compares it to the cake itself, one disc cut from the whole, and to the two voices — English hymn and harbour — carried together across the water. The same table is noted in the Calcutta and Madras registers for the years 1886 to 1893, in each case the cake served at or near the time of the service and in each case, the registers say, “no complaints entered.” For the broader practice of getting people where a celebration was due, see the Thomas Cook gallery placard, Case No. 47, on the first organised excursion of 1841 and the temperance minute book that kept its one line.

6.4 Handling, storage, and hazards

  • Moisture. Dried fruit taken from the store shall be weighed before soaking, not after. Water is the enemy of the crumb; the sultanas are soaked in brandy, or in brandy thinned with a very small quantity of warm water, and no more.
  • Alcohol content. A twice-fed four-pound cake carries a residual alcohol content fit to keep it; do not attempt to remove it. If the operator is asked to make a cake for a temperance table, substitute the syrup feed and shorten the keeping life to one Christmas.
  • Heat. Do not store in a sun-room or within six feet of the range. In the Presidency heat, the English article keeps best in the coolest part of the pantry, wrapped twice, and is served the same day it is brought to the table.
  • Ageing. One year is the minimum for the dark varieties; three years is the standard. A cake of seven years’ standing, found in a Bombay store room in 1897, was served at a regimental dinner and reported “hard at the edge, good at the heart.”
  • Serving. Cut narrow, serve with thick cream or custard, and offer the pruned quarter to the senior person at the table, as is the custom in the English house.
