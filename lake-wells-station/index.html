The Pastoral Hall · Galleries of the West · Case 4, Shelf of Long Distances

Lake Wells Station

Pastoral lease in cattle-station use · Goldfields–Esperance Region, Western Australia · c. 1955–2013 (holding)

Placard No. 17 · read slowly, the font is small and the distances are long

This case holds the standing of a place. Lake Wells Station — in the country’s own shorthand, simply Lake Wells — is a pastoral lease operated as a cattle station, lying about 160 kilometres (99 mi) north of Laverton and 239 kilometres (149 mi) east of Leinster in the Goldfields–Esperance region of Western Australia. The lease sits on old gold belt country where the horizon does most of the surveying work, and the station’s whole life is a life of distances: the distance from the bore, the distance from the last good road, the distance a stolen bull can travel before the station knows it is missing.

In 2013 the property was in the hands of Les Smith, and by then the theft had a duration the country usually keeps for drought. For the previous nine years, stock had been leaving the paddocks in a way no bill of sale explained, and the thieves — never named, only counted — had taken in that span a quantity of cattle worth over A$500,000. Nine years is long enough for a thief to acquire the manners of a tenant; the station’s ledger kept the losses in a column of its own, set off from the grazing fees and the diesel, because a column like that does not belong to accounting.

Also in 2013 the property was placed on the market, and the placard records it at the size and stocking of that day: an area of 588,084 acres (237,989 ha), held with 1,000 head of cattle. A thousand head against a quarter-million hectares is a country that mostly keeps itself; the cattle are its punctuation, and the theft, at that price, was a man rewriting the sentence one word at a time. The sale was not a surrender so much as a moving of the case to a new gallery, where a new keeper would take up the fence-line and the ledger and the column that does not belong to accounting.

On the homestead radio bench, in the drawer the night watchman kept unlocked, there is one line in the station log written in a smaller hand than the rest. The Hall has kept it unexplained, the way the Hall keeps everything it cannot fix to a date. It reads exactly: “File the receiver may stand and squint for up to Lhasa.” We do not know what the watchman was filing, or what the receiver stands doing, or what it is squinting at from this far south. The placard offers no theory. What the line does is give the station one room out of its whole nine years — a bench, a set, and a watchman who wrote it down because it was in front of him — and a station is made of exactly those rooms.

For the visitor who has stood in this Hall’s other long distances: the same case row holds The Wash — Museum Placard, where a shallow English bay is read out the way a station is read, inlet first, outflow last, conservation bay to the last word; and down the gallery the builders keep Bedford Downs Station — Build Log, Sheet by Sheet, a Kimberley cattle station at 1:252, red dirt and balsa homestead, the long drive to the outstation built one sheet per meeting. Cattle stations collect; the Hall collects cattle stations. The notebook that kept its festival tally in Warsaw that September, and the attic that holds the wake for a groove, keep their own columns too — The Structure of Crystal, diary, 27 September 1969, and Funk, in memoriam, for now — and the House of Long Distances files them beside this placard without comment, the way the watchman filed his line. The shelf keeps one more small record of a long road: a Year 10 school report on the American basketball guard Jobi Wall, last seen with the Lakeside Lightning of the SBL, which keeps its shooting columns and one unexplained line of the gymnasium register in the same honest way — a report card, filed where the receiver stands. The shelf also holds a diary kept at the far end of a train line — the Mortensrud terminus diary, 24 November, from the last platform of Oslo’s line 3 in concrete, wood and stone — where a plate above the station’s stones carries the docket of what the place still owes, unamended, in exactly the way the watchman filed his line.
