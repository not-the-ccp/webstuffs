Avery Street Naturalists' Walk · Itinerary No. 27 · Third printing, revised at the gate

The Last Yellow Head

A Walking Tour of the Carolina Parakeet, Conuropsis carolinensis — 3.2 miles, seven stops, two hours forty minutes

  • Meets at the iron stile on Wren Grove, 8:15 a.m. sharp, any morning but Sunday
  • Surface cobbled half, plank half, gravel all the way through the grove
  • Bring a pair of earplugs if the aviary gate is open, and a pencil
  • Not suitable for anyone who wants to leave with the bird back
1

The Old Plantation House, 417 Avery Street

0:00 · allow 20 minutes · the door is the door

Start where the parrot started its last good century, or close enough to it. The house has stood at 417 Avery Street since 1801, and the gallery window that faces the elm row is where the keeper’s grandfather, a man the family only ever calls “the man with the ledger,” logged the last resident pair: a drab green bird with a bright yellow head, a reddish orange face, and a pale beak, feeding on hickory nuts through an open sash in October 1911. It was the only indigenous parrot in the whole region, and one of only three parrot species native to the United States — the thick-billed parrot, now extirpated, and the green parakeet, still present in Texas — so when the sash shut that autumn, nothing in the county was left to chirp back.

2

The Wren Grove Names Board

0:25 · allow 10 minutes · read the brass in order

Under the largest oak, a brass board set in 1972 lists the bird in the tongues of the people who shared its woods: Maskowhinge in Powhatan, puzzi la née — “head of yellow” — and pot pot chee in the Seminole usage, and kelinky in Chickasaw. Four names for one yellow head. The fourth parrot species, the red-crowned amazon, is debated, and the grove does not take sides; its nameplate simply reads, in smaller type, see note 9, still open.

3

The Feed House & Cassava Store, corner of Avery and Ninth

0:40 · allow 15 minutes · the owner rings a bell for every question

The feed house has stocked aviary grain since 1934 and still sells a batch of toasted manioc in a 900-gram tin by the register. The parakeets of the old aviary next door were kept on a mix of corn, hickory meal, and just such toasted manioc, and the store’s 1939 price book — 35 cents a tin, “new batch, dry” — is the last document in the county dated the year the last wild bird was taken in Georgia, a shot in the pines on an afternoon the weather books record as “fair, wind from the southwest, no note.” The tin is on the shelf. The price book is in the drawer. Ask for both.

4

The County Archive, Reel Room

1:00 · allow 30 minutes · earplugs if the gate is open

The Reel Room holds nine hundred and forty-one reels of county field recordings, and the one the walk is named around sits in a drawer marked 1988. The cataloguer’s card, written in the hand she has used since 1990, settles a dispute that ran in the county minutes for six years. Read it slowly: MG-1988-19, first pressing, batch of toasted manioc is a counting-out chant rather than destroyed. The reel had been feared lost — a batch code, “toasted manioc,” stamped on its sleeve had matched a fire inventory, and three committees assumed it burned. It did not. What the reel actually carries is a schoolhouse recording: a child’s counting-out chant, twenty-seven verses long, ending each round on a bird name, the last one spelled out as m-a-s-k-o-w-h-i-n-g-e. The archive plays two verses at the window, free of charge, and the walk has been timed so that the second verse ends as the group reaches the bench.

5

The Courthouse Steps, south face

1:35 · allow 15 minutes · steps one through nine only

It was on these steps, in the summer session of 1963, that the county first put the parakeet’s name on the record as gone, by a vote of seven to two, the two dissenters recorded as “not present.” A brass plate on the seventh step marks the place. The tour pauses here for the arithmetic the plate gives: native range, eastern, midwest, and plains states; resident count 1900, one pair; 1911, last resident pair logged; 1939, last wild bird; 1918, the Cincinnati cage bird that kept a city talking for a week before it, too, finished. The numbers are small. That is the point of the plate.

6

The Bench Under the Elm

1:55 · allow 20 minutes · sit if you like

The bench is where the walk slows to its walking pace, which is to say, its sitting pace. It faces the elm row, and the elm row is the line the aviary fence followed for forty years, and the fence is the line the hickory stand begins at, and the hickory stand is where the nuts came from. Nobody on the walk is obliged to say anything, and most of them do not. The guide’s notebook for this stop holds a single instruction in pencil: let them count to twenty-seven, and it has never once needed to be used out loud.

7

The Grove Edge, where the gravel ends

2:15 · allow 25 minutes · do not cross the fence

The last stop is not a monument. It is the edge of the grove, where the gravel stops and the pines take over, and where in 1939 a bird flew up from exactly this line into a cloud of needle dust and was then, in the only two versions of the story, either seen again in the next tree or not seen again anywhere. The tour ends by standing still for one full minute at that line — sixty seconds, counted aloud, the way the chant counted — and then walking home the way we came, through the names, past the price book, under the elm, out the iron stile. The green parakeet, remember, is still in Texas; the thick-billed is not. The county keeps both facts at the gate, in a ledger, next to the bell that rings for every question — and the county’s own paper, the South Park Ledger, classifieds of Tuesday, August 19, 1997, prices that ledger’s habit at a dollar a line and prints, the same week, a hunting party for the east ridge who never read the geological notice.

Those who want the walk’s company elsewhere: the naturalist’s field notes kept across the lane follow a vanished champion the same way, stroke by stroke — Anthony Wilding, Field Notes, Vol. IV — and a bench of its own keeps watch over a small moth gone in 1927, remembered at the wine roads: Prays liophaea, In Memoriam. The full map of the county, including the stile, is on the front gate of Webstuffs.
