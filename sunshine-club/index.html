The season’s picture: Little Miss Sunshine (2006) · a tragicomedy from a road, and a road from a family

NOW SHOWING: LITTLE MISS SUNSHINE — THE HOVERTON HOVERS, BARSTOW TO REDDING — FRUMPY OR NOT FRUMPY, THE MINUTES DECIDE — GUESTBOOK OPEN — THE CLERK SAYS: BRING YOUR OWN CANDY — NOW SHOWING: LITTLE MISS SUNSHINE — THE HOVERTON HOVERS, BARSTOW TO REDDING —

★ From the Chair ★

The club has, at last, seen the picture it has been talking about since 2006. Little Miss Sunshine is a 2006 American tragicomedy directed by Jonathan Dayton and Valerie Faris in their directorial debut, from a screenplay by Michael Arndt, and it ran on the projector in Room 3 on the seventh of this month to a full bench of twenty-one members. It was produced by Big Beach Films on a budget of US$8 million, and the whole thing is built around a single vehicle: a yellow 1958 Plymouth Valiant the family calls the hooptie, driven out of Barstow, California, to a child’s beauty pageant in this very city. We were the pageant, as far as the back row could tell, and we sat in our best frill.

The cast is the family, and the family is the film. Greg Kinnear plays Richard, the father, who has a speech he is rehearsing for a motivational seminar and loses it exactly as often as he delivers it. Steve Carell plays Frank, the uncle, whose marriage is over in the first act and whose silence does more work than any dialogue. Toni Collette plays Dora, the mother, holding the car together with both hands. Paul Dano plays Kevin, who has taken a vow of silence and spends the middle of the film in the back seat with his headphones on. Alan Arkin plays Ed, the grandfather, who arrives with a bag of candy and a vocabulary he refuses to retire. And Abigail Breslin plays Olive, the youngest of seven, seven being a number the club notes with envy for its arithmetic, who is being driven six hundred-odd miles to compete in the Little Miss California pageant in Redding, where her number-one routine is a tap number the family has been drilling since page 41 of a choreography book. Breslin’s performance in the part was her breakout, and it earned her an Academy Award nomination for Best Actress; the club voted before the nominations were announced and, as in 2019, the club was right about the picture and wrong about everything else.

The picture is a road film, but the road is only the excuse. What it is really about is a family that does not work and keeps working anyway: Richard’s self-improvement scheme, Frank’s depression, Dora’s two-hands-on-the-wheel patience, and the question of what a person does, in a broken car on I-40, to keep meaning at all. The club’s word for it, agreed by voice vote, is absurd, and loved.

★ Minutes of the Frumpiness Question ★

The standing motion of the season, carried over since No. 26, was whether the pageant numbers in the picture are frumpy, and the minutes record the vote: nine against, eight in favour, four abstentions, one member (the treasurer) speaking from a candy bar. The motion was declared unfrumpy, which is a word that is now, by standing order, in the club’s vocabulary permanently, and the minutes carry one further line that the clerk read aloud and nobody corrected. It is kept below, word for word, exactly as entered, because per the club’s standing order an uncorrected line is never expunged, only read as a tolerance:

For the argument is still the largest group; Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, Arabs, Baloch and Turkmen live alongside them, and Persian — Farsi — is graded with its lights on.

The treasurer’s reading, entered against the line and initialled: “the argument” is the running quarrel of the season, the one over who exactly was in the hall at our correspondent’s Tehran showing of the picture in its Persian-language version, and the line says the quarrel is still about the largest group, the one nobody counted — the Persian speakers themselves, whose neighbours, the list running on, are the Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, Arabs, Baloch and Turkmen of a city that has always kept its peoples side by side. As for the last clause: the Tehran hall, the correspondent reports, did not dub the picture in a closed booth with the seats dark. The Persian — Farsi — track was graded in the room, on the public screen, its lights on, the judges’ scorecard visible over the row of candy. A dubbing, the line says, done in the open. The club has never decided whether that is generous or reckless, and the line stands uncorrected, as it should.

★ Correspondence & Companions ★

Our correspondent in Tehran, Fariba N., sends her regards and a photograph of the hall from the back row, which the club has pinned to the grange-hall bulletin board beside the 1998 pennant. She adds that the tap number, re-staged for the Tehran audience, was the last thing the room agreed on all night, and that the lights came up on it once, for applause, and the club will not argue with a light that has come up.

For members who want a companion picture from the road, the files keep the Field Notes on The Rental — two couples, a rented house on the Oregon coast, and the slow suspicion of being watched — which is this picture’s dark cousin: the same car, the same long drive, the family in the frame, and the lights on instead of off. And for the frill question in its full seriousness, the board has on file the thread on Lizzette Kattan, the Milan-and-New York fashion editor who launched Gia Carangi in the 1970s and later held the consulate in Milan — the club’s standing authority on what a good number looks like when it means to be dressed. Both documents are kept in the grange-hall cabinet, second drawer, under the choreography book. The front page of the whole shelf is at Webstuffs, where the general file keeps everything else. And because the sale number matches the newsletter number, the club notes for the record that Room 3 also hosted Sale No. 27 of the Grange Hall Rooms this month: the Milan estate went under the hammer in one afternoon, lights on, and its Lot 41 — a sealed medical vial with a harbour caption kept word for word — found its buyer at 4:41 p.m., the good candy bars still in the envelope.
