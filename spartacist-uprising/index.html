January 5 – 12, 1919
The January Storm over Berlin (Spartakusaufstand)

A week of low pressure, hard frost and gunfire · recorded at the station on the Unter den Linden · revised after the fact, as always

☀ The Weather of the Matter ☀

The system that moved through the capital this week was not, in the first place, a meteorological one, but the almanac has found it necessary to file it here under the weather, where all large movements are eventually filed. From the 5th to the 12th of January 1919, Berlin was the seat of an armed uprising — the Spartacist uprising, also called the January uprising, and in some of the older ledgers, less kindly, Bloody Week — which broke open into the German revolution still running from the collapse of the war in November. The cold was a fair companion for it: hard frost the whole week, a pressure trough that sat over the city like a hat taken off and set on the table, and snow on the 7th that the fighting kept off the streets by the simple method of melting everything at knee height and then blowing it back.

The front came from two directions at once, as fronts do when they have a grudge. On the one side stood the provisional government in the building at the Unter den Linden, Friedrich Ebert of the Majority Social Democratic Party (MSPD) at its head, holding for a parliamentary road and a social democracy, with the pressure of the times rising around it. On the other side stood the men and women of the Communist Party of Germany (KPD) — Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg of the old Spartacus League among them — holding for a council republic on the Russian model, the kind of low pressure that does not ease. Between them ran the barricades, from the Friedrichstadt eastward past the Behnelstraße, and behind both ran the troops: the provisional government's order, delivered in the morning of the 6th, had the Freikorps regiments marching in from the north of the city, through cold clear air at −5° and holding steady, which is more than can be said of any weather.

The week peaked on the 7th, at the daily minimum of the pressure, and eased on the 9th, when the pressure began to rise and the street fighting stopped in most districts — the surrender, in the ledger of the weather, having been called in like a front that has given up. By the 12th the cold was high, the sky was settled at 1016, and the station could close the sheet for January's first week. The almanac records for the permanent record that two further readings were taken in the weeks after, on the 15th, at a temperature the ledger does not print, and it will not print them here either. The barometer, as usual, knew first.

On the question of what the readings mean — whether the fall of the 6th presaged the 7th, whether the rise of the 10th presaged the rest of the century — the almanac's standing order applies. Serious inquiries only; the barometer fell a tenth of an hour in the cooking of this publication declines to interpret scenery. What the almanac will say is only this: the frost of that week held so long that people still quote it, and the pressure, once settled, has not fully done so since.

☂ Cross-References from the Shelf ☂

For a week of settled, fair-weather violence by comparison — the Rose Bowl at Southampton on 14 August 2010, a sell-out of over seventeen thousand under a summer sky, Hampshire finishing a twenty-ball chase with one ball to spare — the almanac keeps its file on the 2010 Twenty20 Cup Final, Hampshire v Somerset, which is what a clear-pressure day is supposed to feel like, only with boundaries instead of barricades. And for the long cold reading the whole affair really amounts to, the sleeper's entry is filed beside it: Sueño Azul, the Dreamer's Concordance, where a whole city lies down in a single blue sheet and the hands come away tinged when the whistle goes — the almanac's own hands, on the morning of the 15th, tinged in exactly that way, which is the whole of the domestic meaning and it is not a good one, but it is the one that holds. And the almanac records, for the shelf, that the history of anarchism has since been entered as deceased in the register of the Department of Ambiguous Histories, with the funeral split between an early service and a late one and the file left open on the middle of the shelf, which is how the almanac would have filed it: a large ambiguous movement, weathered through to the minimum, and never quite closed.
