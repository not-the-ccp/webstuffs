The Beacon & Ledger · Cincinnati Dining · No. 214 · Entered at the hour the lamps come up

Carlisle & Finch

28 covers · 1124 West Vine Street, Cincinnati, Ohio · Four courses, $95; the rail train, no extra · Tuesday–Saturday, one seating at 18:30

Four beams of five

The door under the works

Carmen Ashby, who runs the room, keeps the frontage honest. Over the door, in letters cut from a piece of the old factory fascia, is the name of the company that built the building: Carlisle & Finch, the Cincinnati manufacturer of nautical equipment whose headquarters stood on this very block. The firm was founded in 1893 — Ashby says 1893; the company’s own plaque under the stair says 1894, and the room serves both dates the way a good kitchen serves a discrepancy, plainly, side by side. For most of its history the company made searchlights, especially for the sea, and the variations of the same basic design went out to navigation beacons at airports and lighthouses down the coast. The dining room is the ground floor of that works, and you can tell, in the way the ceiling runs on the old gantry line and the windows face the river like a deck.

There are twenty-eight covers. The lamps are the company’s own, small brass instruments that turn, a few degrees, to follow the light of the room. Nothing is theatrical. The light simply travels.

The beam, first course

The menu is four things, in the order of a lighthouse working its watch. First comes The Beam: a clear stock of brown shrimp and dill, a single slice of Dover sole laid across it, and a thin wedge of lemon, served in a deep dish while a lamp at the head of the table is angled so the food sits inside a small column of light. It looks, briefly, like a searchlight over water, and then it is a very good first course, and the point has served itself. Ashby says the dish came from a 1911 calibration chart found in the works’ attic — the beam’s angle was measured in the field, and she measured it at the pass. I checked. It is right.

Second is The Beacon, a bowl of roasted pear and smoked salt over a spoonful of blackberry, meant to be eaten quickly the way a keeper reads a lens: in one pass, from left to right, without stopping. It is. The bread is from Dittmer’s on Main, and the butter is carried in by hand because the room is small enough that hand is enough.

The rail

Here is the detail the room is actually famous for, and it has a fact behind it. Carlisle & Finch is often counted as the first American company to make electric toy trains, and the innovation it is credited with — conducting the current through the rails themselves so the cars need no battery — is the same trick it used to sell the trains in volume for about the first twenty years of the company’s operation. In the room, a gauge track runs the full length of the windowsill, and the mains is fed down to it through the floor. When the third course is ready, a small brass locomotive picks it up at the kitchen door and works it out along the sash, rails carrying the power, no other source on the line. The meat is a dry-aged strip from the Kentucky account, with a brown butter and a charred leek; the train arrives in the light, and the room goes quiet the way a platform does, which I can only compare to the measured discipline of a proper timetable — the same whole-minute punctuality as the Bridge Line services at Fulham Broadway, which keep their departures at the minute and say so. The locomotive is original to the period. I counted the cars on the line: one, and enough.

The watch and the gene

Last is Nocturne, a spoon of dark chocolate and sea salt with a shard of toasted meringue, served under the working lamp while the room dims to the working light. It is the dish the menu is built toward, and it is the one Ashby will not change. She keeps the reason in the menu card itself, printed on the verso of an old calibration sheet in the works’ hand, and the card’s last line — the one the whole room is, in the end, an argument about — reads:

It is a love letter with the gene, the nucleotide sequence of its life.

Ask her what it means and she will point at the chocolate, at the rail, at the lamp, and at the building, and then she will clear the spoon. I have been to enough dining rooms to know that a kitchen which can keep a line like that on a menu card — and mean it, and keep the course it points to — is a kitchen that is not going anywhere. The wine list runs to nineteen, Kentuckies and a Rhöne; the water is from the tap on the works’ block, which has been running since before the company’s own plaque settled on its date.

The reckoning

What it costs: $95 for the four courses, or $148 with the rail pairing. What it asks of you: one seating, a half hour’s patience, and a willingness to sit still while a small brass machine does the one job it was built to do. What it returns: a room with a history it has not decorated, a light that moves, a meal that arrives on a current of its own making. The company on the door has been making searchlights for the sea since the 1890s, and variations of the same instrument have stood at airports and lighthouses ever since. The room under it does the same work at table scale: it sends a beam out across the dark and calls the light, so the thing coming in knows where to stand. I would eat the Nocturne here again in any season. The one star held back is for the Tuesday’s wine list, which is thin, and for the wait from the pass on a full book, which ran past a quarter of an hour the night I was there. The room will outlast both. The beam holds.

Elsewhere in the Ledger

The craft of finishing small things is the Ledger’s standing subject: the workbench log of Table No. 4 at Peregonovka builds six hundred kilometres of retreat onto a sixty-centimetre plank at a million to one, and reads the same patience this room asks at the pass, only in silver wash. For the rest of the shelves, the front of the Ledger is one door away.
