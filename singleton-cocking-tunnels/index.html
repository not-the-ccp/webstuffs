Lot 9
Singleton & Cocking Tunnels, West Sussex, Complete as One Consignment

The indentures; the 1865 bore survey in walnut boxes; the parish maps of 1967; and the standing winter count — five pages, four winters, one species found nowhere else in Britain

Estimate £22,000–£30,000 · Hammer £34,500 SOLD

I. The Consignment

Offered as a single, unbroken lot: the two disused railway tunnels lying in the downland between Chichester and Midhurst in West Sussex, the 1.9 hectares (4.7 acres) of biological Site of Special Scientific Interest and Special Area of Conservation that the parish has held since the line closed in 1967, and everything the parish kept to prove what winters in the bores. The consignment is the site itself, bound and carried: the indentures of 1864 and the regrant of 1968; the 1865 survey of both bores, set in walnut boxes, with the chainman’s field copies still in their pencil; the 1967 parish maps showing the last scheduled workings of the line; and the standing winter count, five pages written over four winters in two inks, with the species column that makes the room. The tunnels themselves remain where they are; the lot is the holding, the paper, and the count, and it is offered on the condition that the bores keep their dark.

II. On the Tunnels and the Line

The bores were cut for the Chichester & Midhurst line in the mid-1860s, twin crossings driven through the Sussex chalk to keep the gradient of the level valley honest — one through the ground above Singleton, the other through the ground above Cocking — and they carried goods and passengers until the running orders of 1967 gave the valley back to the hedgerows. Since then the chalk has done its quiet work: a floor of scree, a ceiling that holds its temperature through the February cold, draughts that run the length of each bore like a pipe with the end open. It is precisely this — the stable cold, the dry stone, the long unbroken dark — that the bats have chosen, and it is why the tunnels rank as the fifth most important hibernation sites for bats in Britain and the most important in the south-east of the country.

III. The Count

The winter count in the lot is the parish’s own, begun the year after closure and never let lapse. It is worked at night, at one pace only, each bore entered by a single lamp and left before the animals wake. Four winters are present. The standing record shows Natterer’s bats by the dozens, Daubenton’s hugging the ceiling at the mouth, Brandt’s clustered in the draught seam by the second lamp-length, and the brown long-eared in a single ledge the surveyor has marked in red ink and left alone. Against all of them, in the final column of the fourth page, is the entry that carries the estimate: the greater mouse-eared bat, Myotis myotis, three individuals in the Cocking bore, the only known location for the species in Britain — the country’s sole record, written down in a small hand, with no underline.

IV. Condition and Provenance

Indentures: sound, the 1864 seal intact. Survey: walnut boxes rubbed, the boards true, the field copies legible in both pencil and faded iron gall. Parish maps: folded once, the 1967 workings in red. Count: five pages, two inks, the red-ink ledge mark as described, nothing added since. The site is private land, and the lot is sold exactly as it stands — with no public access and no right of entry, the condition being that the buyer keeps the dark, keeps the count, and lets the fifth-most-important hibernation ground in Britain remain a ground with no path to it. Provenance: the Midhurst parish chest to the Southern Branch by the regular crate, crated the same week as the race papers of Lot 143 in Catalogue B, whose rod, the clerk notes, travelled in the same crate and smelled of pine sawdust and chalk dust together.

VI. From the Auction Record

Before the hammer, the question the room always asks: when, exactly, does the standing count open, in which week of the winter, and who decides the day? The room looked to the parish agent, whose surname the record prints in capitals. He answered without opening the survey, and the line is entered here exactly as the clerk wrote it down:

MR. PRESCOTT: In the week where the floor by one of them puts it.

That is to say: the count opens in the week the frost settles on the floor of one or other of the bores, whichever bore it is, and no later. The room understood. The lot went under the hammer at £34,500 with a club of the gavel the clerk noted as “firm, once, over the walnut box.” The bores keep their dark, and the count keeps its pace.
