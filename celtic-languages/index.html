Lagan Records · LL-04 · 2 LP · 33⅓ rpm · pressing of 1894, rerecorded

The Northwestern Fringe

being the six living languages, CEL-tik, in one sitting — liner notes by Meg O’Donnell, tape operator, Belfast

Compiler’s note

The Celtic languages (CEL-tik) are a branch of the Indo-European family, all six of them descended from a single hypothetical ancestor, Proto-Celtic, which no one has ever caught out and spoken to, the way the Committee has never caught the crossbar at Elland Road. The name “Celtic,” applied to a group of languages, was first used in 1707 by Edward Lhuyd, in Archaeologia Britannica, following Paul-Yves Pezron, who had made the explicit link between the Celts described by the classical writers and the Welsh and Breton languages still being spoken in the field. Lhuyd’s four specimens — Welsh, Breton, Irish and Scottish Gaelic — are the four continuously living voices on Side A of this record.

During the first millennium BC, Celtic was spoken across much of Europe and into central Anatolia, where the Galatians took a dialect with them as far as modern Turkey; the map of it would have filled this page twice over. Today the family is restricted to the northwestern fringe of Europe and a few diaspora communities, which is why we recorded it there: on the fringe, at the edge of the tape, where the signal is. If the Keeper of Names is still owing the family an apology, one is pending at Apology to the Indo-European Languages, and this record should be entered into the evidence.

LAGAN RECORDS LL-04 SIDE A · 33⅓

Side A — the four continuously living

  1. A1 Cymraeg (Welsh) Caernarfon, on the harbour wall, 6:40 am

    Recorded at the foot of the castle, a man buying a paper in the old language and nobody correcting him, which is the whole of the take. Nine minutes of it, because that is how long he took, and we did not interrupt.

  2. A2 Brezhoneg (Breton) Quimper, the covered market, one cold-soaped towel exchanged

    The only track on the record made in exchange for something. A linen towel, Samson & Goliath print, for eleven minutes of conversation about whether the market still closes on the hour. Mrs O’Neill’s method of laundering the tape afterward is on file.

  3. A3 Gaeilge (Irish) Derry~Londonderry, a taxi, off the meter

    The driver did not know he was recording. Neither did we, at first. The meter stayed off the whole time, which is the only honest number on this record. If you are from the Causeway Coast and recognise the road, do not reply to the ad; it has already been answered.

  4. A4 Gàidhlig (Scottish Gaelic) Inverness, the seafront bench, wind on the mic, left uncut

    Forty-one seconds of wind, kept. The Committee would have voted the wind down eight to one, but this is not the Committee. Mr. Parry’s eighty-ninth minute appears here at the end of the take, in full, struck once against the bench rail — woodwork, and back into play. His report of the night, as played in the room above Gate 13, is minuted in the Matchnight Committee’s minutes, Item 3.

Side B — the two revived

  1. B1 Kernewek (Cornish) Truro, the chapel yard, spoken from the written sources

    A language that came back from its books. The man in the yard had been learning for six years and had, by his own count, made exactly two full sentences in public before we arrived; the second one is the best sentence on the record, and it is about the weather. Revival, the ad would say, is not for sale; it is wanted, wanted, wanted.

  2. B2 Gaelg (Manx) Balla, the radio mast, 1974, restored 2024

    Recorded over a radio programme in a language that had been dead, on the island, for the space of a single lifetime. The mast hums in the background on the whole track. We kept it because the island has no other low end.

Recorded, and where

Cut in one sitting at the Crown Liquor Saloon, Belfast, between 8:20 and 8:21 pm — the exact minute the Matchnight Committee in Bedminster closes its meetings, a fact we knew and a coincidence the Committee has declined to comment on. One dented Carabao can, dented ninth of one hundred and sixty, provided the percussion on B2 and has been retired from service, as it should have been. The red shirt, folded in the spring of 2014, unwashed by request, was draped over the tape reel as a windscreen and is not for sale, being spoken for in the small ads at The Lagan Ledger’s Northern Ireland column, where this record was announced the week before it was pressed, under Community. The Ledger keeps the announcement on the same page as its hazard file for Yes, the debut single of McAlmont & Butler that first made a Lagan sound bankable in the UK; the full sheet, No. 1995-05-15, is on file at the Ledger’s safety shelf. The weather of that same hour, 8:20 to 8:21 pm, is logged on its own card at Entry No. 31 of the Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, where the fringe keeps its own barometer and card 27 is read beside it.
