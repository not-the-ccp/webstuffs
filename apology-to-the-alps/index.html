An Apology to the Alps

Letter of the under-steward, set down at the table and read, word for word, before the kettle was turned to tea

Newmarket, in the second week after the farce · posted in triplicate

To the Alps, all of them, from Nice on the western Mediterranean to Trieste on the Adriatic and on again to Vienna at the beginning of the Pannonian Basin,

I write on behalf of the Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club, and of the under-steward, myself, with our full and unreserved apology for the farce entitled The Alps, or the Silverley Bloom in Three Acts, as performed at the Ashley parish hall on the night of 21 November last by the company that styles itself, without irony, the Gemenasian Company. The company claims descent from Johan von Blanc’s Gemenasiska Sällskapet, and the claim is recorded in the obituary of Johan von Blanc, 1738–1796, which we have now read in full and found, in one long sentence, more entertaining than all our three acts put together. We did not, I am prepared to admit, so much as descend from anything as we fell: in the third act the range was mistaken for the club’s hillfort near Peterborough, and in the fourth, unpublished act, the committee of the range was invited to pay rent, weekends only, no iron economy required, the fire to be kept and the kettle on, the door not to be repainted.

The club has no iron economy and has no claim upon 1,200 kilometres of Europe, and never did. I was in the wings with my bed of charcoal from the second bloom, and I saw the house go quiet — 906,814 of them, or near enough, the count the club keeps and does not re-count — when the Matterhorn was measured out in nails of bog iron, and Mont Blanc, four thousand eight hundred and ten metres of it, was offered for sale at the rate set last Michaelmas. Eight countries were named from the stage: Monaco, France, Switzerland, Italy, Liechtenstein, Germany, Austria, and Slovenia. Not one of them was consulted, none was entitled to be, and I am sorry to each in turn. The mountains were formed over tens of millions of years, by the African and Eurasian plates, by thrusting and folding of marine sedimentary rock; they did not need a farce, and they certainly did not need my charcoal.

Worse than the play was what the play did to the club’s own record. The classifieds of Newsletter No. 31 — the Iron Age, Classifieds — still print the line about the brooch found after the Gemenasian Company’s farce, and members have since asked whether the silver penannular brooch is a mountain brooch, or the brooch the company took in payment, or the brooch the Alps kept from us. The village behind the parish hall is catalogued at Lot 217, Ashley, Cambridgeshire, and the cataloguers, to their credit, refused to price the range itself. The club’s letter of apology, this one, is the correction the ledger was missing, and it stands over every notice that follows it.

The retraction is drawn up, signed by the clerk, the treasurer, and the under-steward, and posted at the Old Fire Station, at the Ashley parish hall, and, to our own surprise, at the foot of the Salève near Geneva. The notice appeared in the course of twelve hours — before the fog lifted, by the count of the man with the kettle — and a copy is going up the Valais by post, and down again by the same post, with the kettle’s steam for its seal. Whatever damage the farce did to the range in the minds of 906,814 persons, the club will make it good in tea, in seat-by-the-kettle rights, or in cash, whichever the committee of the range prefers. The brooch is enclosed. It is not the silver penannular; it is smaller, and more tarnished, but it is genuine, and it may stay where it lay.

Please know that the hillfort will keep its fire, and that we shall not repaint the door.

Yours with the deepest contrition,

E. T. Silverley
under-steward, Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club
the Old Fire Station, Newmarket

Enclosure: one brooch, tarnished; one bed of charcoal, offered and withdrawn; one recipe line, “stock No. 105”, still unexplained and now, we believe, the Alps’ own.

Postscript: the club’s Newsletter No. 31 remains at the Old Fire Station’s noticeboard, where the retraction is pinned beside the hillfort lettings, and the kettle is still on.
