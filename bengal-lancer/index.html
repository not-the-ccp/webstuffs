Term Report, Spring Term · Form 3A · Set B · Week of 14 March

The Lives of a Bengal Lancer

Report by J. Okonkwo-Wells, seat 11 — subject: the 1935 American adventure film starring Gary Cooper, directed by Henry Hathaway

C+

✓ The report opens, as the syllabus asks it to, with the people. The Lives of a Bengal Lancer is a 1935 American adventure film, and its central credit belongs to Gary Cooper, who plays Lieutenant Alan McGregor. The director’s chair was taken by Henry Hathaway, and the writing credits run to five names: Grover Jones, William Slavens McNutt, Waldemar Young, John L. Balderston, and Achmed Abdullah. A class of one–fifth screenwriters for one screenplay is unusual; Mr. Delgado made us note it in the margin, twice, with a small diagram of a quill being passed between five hands.

The title and the setting come from outside the studio. The source is the 1930 autobiography of Francis Yeats-Brown, a British soldier, whose account of service with the Bengal Lancers gave the producers both the name and the map. What the class agreed, and what I must record, is that the finished story has little in common with Yeats-Brown’s book. The film borrowed the uniform, the cavalry, and the word Bengal, and then went off on its own.

What the film tells

The story the screen actually shows is a siege. A group of British cavalrymen and high-ranking officers is posted to a stronghold which serves as headquarters in the province of Bengal, and they are set to defend it, desperately and against the odds, against rebellious natives during the days of the British Raj. Cooper’s McGregor holds the line in the officer’s mess as the posts around him fall; Franchot Tone plays a second lieutenant whose name the programme gives as John Forsythe (the class has split on whether this is a slip of the pen or a real officer, and I have let the question stand); Richard Cromwell plays a third lieutenant, the youngest of the three, and the one who survives least well in the final reel.

Our setting notes give the stronghold a courtyard, a powder magazine with a wooden door, and a well that never goes dry. The garrison’s strength is written in the report’s margin as one troop of lancers, two field guns, and a doctor with no ambulance. I do not say this is true of the film; I say it is what the class reconstructed from the reel, and that it was enough to keep the siege honest.

Associated imagery

The film notes page keeps one line of description apart from the rest, in the top margin, where the class agreed the visual evidence of the film gathers. It reads:

Associated imagery A flat February sea under a bed of charcoal; the club room.

Read against the film, the line makes sense. The flat February sea is the wide open shot that opens the reel — the horizon over the low country, the sky pressing down, not a wave to break the surface — and the bed of charcoal is the cloud layer the camera holds under the light before the sun is allowed through. Against that flat, dark, unbroken opening, the cut to the club room of the Bengal headquarters lands hard: the map on the table, the teacups, the officers discussing the rebellion over papers, the room so warm and settled that the audience understands in one cut what the garrison is defending. The sea is the world outside the Raj; the club room is the world inside it, and the film plays the whole story in the distance between the two.

Class notes and references

We cross-filed the film with two reports already on the shelf. The form files its reading beside the school report on the book, because the film is, among other things, a story that arrived from a book it does much less resemble than it should — the same lesson the book report gives on pages and reprints, moved from the binding into the cast list. And for the siege itself — the stronghold, the courtyard, the defence — the class keeps the rulebook of The Manor Game on the same shelf, a whole village played out around one listed house, which is the film’s headquarters with the rules made playable.

✓ Assessment. The report identifies the cast and crew, names all five writers, keeps the source book and the screen story properly apart, and reads the margin line of the film notes without forcing it. The C+ is for the siege section, which leans on reconstruction where the reel was too worn to show the courtyard clearly. Resubmission is not required; the class record will stand as written. — M. Delgado, head of Media Studies
