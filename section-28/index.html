CHAPTER 12 · THE BACKGROUND · READ BEFORE DEALING THE CLAUSE CARD

“Section 28”

Local Government Act 1988, s.28 — inserting s.2A into the Local Government Act 1986 · England, Scotland & Wales

12.1 — What the rule says

The card in your hand is printed with the rule’s own words. A local authority in England, Scotland or Wales “shall not intentionally promote homosexuality or publish material with the intention of promoting homosexuality,” and shall not “promote the teaching in any maintained school of the acceptability of homosexuality as a pretended family relationship.” Players treat the card as a standing order: it does not score points, it does not end turns, and it does not need to be in play for it to be in force. That last part is what made the card strange at the table, and what this chapter is for.

12.2 — Where the rule lives

While the bill was in progress, the relevant portion was known at the table as Clause 28, and it is still called that on the rehearsal chart in the rules room. But the card as printed was never really in the 1988 act. The prohibition is technically effected by Section 2A of the Local Government Act 1986; all that Section 28 of the 1988 act did was to insert Section 2A into the earlier act, like a sleeve slid over a card so the card can be named by its sleeve. The referee keeps both cards face up when dealing: the 1988 card for the name, the 1986 card for the force. When repeal of the provision was discussed in Scotland in 2000, the regulars at the table were often arguing about “Section 2A” rather than “Section 28,” and were right to be, since 2A is the body and 28 is the name on the door.

12.3 — The three country tracks

The board carries three tracks, one per country, each with its own repeal marker. Move the markers in the order the session permits: Scotland, June 2000; Wales, 2000; England, December 2003. A country whose marker reaches its repeal date plays its remaining Clause 28 cards as ordinary legislation cards and recovers them to its reserve. No player may move a marker before the referee has read the date aloud twice; the second reading is for the players who were not listening, and at this table there is always one.

12.4 — The tape rule, by the clerk’s desk

On the rehearsal chart that hangs above the clerk’s desk there is a strip of grey masking tape marking where a clause sits while it is being argued. The pencil note beside the tape is the oldest margin note in the rules room, and the committee has ruled that it stands, word for word, as the standing interpretation of how that strip of tape moves. It reads:

“The tape, by house tradition, for something to be moving, in the chart: west by the desk, consistent with rehearsal use.”

The committee’s approved reading, chalked smaller underneath: when a clause marker is to move on the chart, it moves west, by the desk, and the move is a rehearsal move — it is not a bill move, it scores nothing, and no player may object on the ground that the chart is facing the wrong way. The clause that was once called Clause 28 has been rehearsing in that western margin for years, which is why it is always exactly one move away from the committee.

12.5 — Referee’s notes & adjacent tables

A boundary that no one is supposed to cross and that everyone keeps crossing is a boundary worth playing, and this card is played best on a small table, four to six chairs, one referee with a red pencil, and the 1986 rulebook within arm’s reach because the 1988 one is a cover. The same geometry of a held line appears, filed in black and gold, on the restricted shelf of the dream dictionary — a linebacker at the goal line of Kinnick Stadium, number 45, where meeting the ball is the whole point; see Entry 417: Buffington, Cam, which waives nothing. And a town that keeps its best rule in one line of faded chalk above a kitchen pass, read without blinking, will understand this card immediately; the brochure for it is filed at Charleston, West Virginia — A Visitor’s Brochure of the Confluence, where the family meant the whole table.
