A word for the reader out of the hall

These notices are set down in the official alphabet of the southern republic, the Revised Romanization of Korean, which the National Academy of the Korean Language developed between 1995 and 2000 and released to the public on 7 July 2000 by Proclamation No. 2000-8 of the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. Its principal office is the proper noun — the place name, the personal name, and the business name — and that is why this sheet is full of names. Personal names are the weekly trade; place names run second; business names appear on the third Saturday. The system does not romanise verbs, and neither does this paper. Anyone who reads Chapter XI of the Book of Etiquette, of the Bearing Dong-hoon, already knows the stakes: a name of nine doors, three spellings on the register, and a tenth admitted when insisted upon. What follows is the Hall’s ordinary business, as it is posted every week.

For Sale

ONE (1) HYPHEN, STANDARD ISSUE

Between Dong and hoon, slightly worn from nine years of service in one hall. The Registry holds that the hyphen is part of the name and admits no bearer who discards it; the bearers, several of them, disagree, and their disagreements are the reason this column exists. Price 600 won or one corrected billing. The Director Hoon Lee will not be receiving offers in person; he corrects his own billing and has been filmed doing so at three separate premieres, the house laughing each time, the correction right each time. See the hall’s rules at Chapter XI, Rule one.

RETIRED SYSTEM, 1939–2000, GOOD CONDITION

McCune–Reischauer, complete, with all its tables and its ɬ and its habit of spelling Dong-hoon Tong-hun. Last seen 1982, on a passport, a luggage tag, and the back of a crate carried along an orchard road in California, where a coach attacked it with a small bunch of parsley and the local society has never stopped asking what the parsley was doing there. Still found on old signs in good repair. Not for sale; for retirement. Bring it in, and the Registry will ink it lightly, in the margin, as the Council of the Hall resolved.

THREE (3) SPELLINGS OF ONE NAME, AS A SET

Dong-hoon, Tong-hoon, Dong-hun — sold as one lot, one of which is the true one and which one is negotiable at the second introduction, once only. The register keeps all three and admits a fourth, Dong-whun, when the bearer insists, which one bearer insists upon, at every event, in the order of 1987, on the schedule of five sports in a name. A set of four available at a 20 percent premium and one bow at a distance of no more than one arm’s length.

SIGNBOARD, STREET, ONE (1)

Korean proper noun, rendered in this newspaper’s own letters, bolted to a corner in the brick quarter of a citrus city that absorbed eight smaller towns in a single 1888 paragraph. The bolts are still true. The spelling has been corrected once, publicly, by a passer-by who owed the bearer a bow at exactly one arm’s length. The Registry will match the sign against the proclamation on sight and, if the sign has drifted, will set it right in the same handwriting as every other sign, which is the point of the whole system: one hand, one alphabet, no favour.

Wanted

BOXER, BORN 1932, FOR THE LAST BOW

The Hall’s ninth-door evening closes the way every evening of this book closes: the eldest leaves first and the guest holds the door with the left hand and does not mention that boxers do not open doors. Chung Dong-hoon is wanted, or his photograph, the hall being patient. Weight class no longer exists; the door does. Applications to the Keeper of Names, in either spelling the man has ever used.

ANYONE WHO SAYS “WHICH DONG-HOON?” ALOUD

Rule five, Chapter XI. The Registry is offering a reward of one private correction and one gift, which the finder is obliged to keep. Two suspects are on file from this week’s reception, both polite, both holding their wine at exactly the forbidden distance, and one of them a footballer born 1984, who plays the door open for anyone who has ever run and therefore owes the other eight doors a great deal of explanation.

THE VOWEL THAT INSISTS

One u, heard at the fourth door of the hall, in the name of the pentathlete, making of Dong-hoon a Dong-whun and admitting, as the registry must, the tenth form. Last seen on a schedule of five sports, which is not a boast, it is a schedule. The vowel is wanted back at the register or at liberty, whichever it prefers; the proclamation does not cover vowels, and the Hall has no jurisdiction to pursue them across a bow.

KEEPER OF A POLITICAL DOOR

The ninth door remains occupied by the politician, who appears at the far end of the hall at the hour the other eight have stopped complaining about the wine and to whom the last bow of the evening is owed as a matter of standing. No application is invited; the Registry notes that the door keeps itself, has kept itself since the first year of the proclamation, and will outlast this newspaper by the usual margin.

Missing · Lost & Found

MISSING: ONE (1) LATERAL, RETURNED 1999

The sound ɭ, the raised lateral, was missing from the register for the whole drafting period of 1995 and was last heard at a table of the Academy, refusing to be spelled. It was restored, in its own double form, in the revised tables, and is now the whole difference between an rr and a plain r in this newspaper. Anyone who has held a lateral of this description since March 1995 is asked to return it to the Registry with the receipt; the receipt is the difference itself. The Registry thanks the two consonants it confuses with. The sound has since been found on a menu, served in the double, at the brick-street counter that has built its whole floor plan around the going-around, and the Registry’s dining column has eaten there: the evening, course by course, stands at The Lateral — Restaurant Review.

LOST: A SOUND IN THE FIRST SYLLABLE

One t, heard once only, in the spelling Tong-hoon, at a reception where two boxers of the same name and two different years — 1932 and 1971 — were present in the same room, distinguished, per the book, by the hands. The sound was not missed; it was kept, which is the Registry’s word for it. No reward. The spelling it belonged to is on the register alongside its two neighbours, and the correction, if the bearer offers one, is a gift and must be kept.

FOUND: A NAME, DREAMED THREE TIMES

A Korean name, found in a sleeper’s minute book at 3 a.m., spelled by the dreamer in a form the register does not hold. The companion rule — a name dreamed three times is spelled as the dreamer spells it, and only as the dreamer spells it — has been applied, and the name is returned to the dreamer with its spelling intact. For the entry on the name itself, the Hall refers readers to Given Name in the dream dictionary, which keeps the door open, the left hand ready.

STREET SIGNS, CENSUS OF THE QUARTER

The Registry has completed its quarterly census of the proper nouns bolted to the town: every place name in the brick quarter, the orchard road, and the low row between the wash and the main street. All signs now stand in the official alphabet, including the one that predates the proclamation by fifty-one years and was set right, at public cost, on a Saturday. The census is open for inspection; the wash at the corner is dry eleven months of the year and a torrent in the twelfth, and the Registry’s rule, copied from the society that walks the grid, is to stand on the near bank.

Provisions & Catering

THE PROVISIONER FOR THE NINTH DOOR

For the hall’s evening of nine men, and for the boxers’ table in particular, the provisioner on duty reports, from the kitchen ledger and word for word: “QUAILS, being trussed like Snipe, with the vinegar; then boil the marrows until tender enough to make of what you will.” The ledger’s full account of the bird — the seven who eat, the live quail at market, the family that keeps page 76 — stands on the Fowlers’ shelf, where the quail recipe is kept with its long preamble. The provisioner reminds the hall that the marrows are boiled, not bowed, and that the vinegar is owing to no one at a distance of one arm’s length. The provisioner’s own paperwork for the sound the hall’s names are drafted in — the nasal, sealed in word-form as mouth, nose, and bring, nearly universal in human languages and standard issue on the register — stands on the file as SDS-NC-01, revision 9, at Nasal Consonant — Safety Data Sheet, issued under the same Proclamation, where the silent g of bring is classified as a hazard, the wash is ruled incompatible with the material, and the ledger’s supper-table line is quoted word for word. Orders to the kitchen before the second drink; after that, per Rule four, some hosts are met on the far side of the room, and the wine is not in the palm.

WINE, FOR NINE DOORS, ONE ARGUMENT

The hall has bought wine for nine doors and the politician has complained of it for one evening running. The Registry notes that the complaint, once stopped, is the signal of the last bow, and sells no wine, offers no argument, and holds the door exactly as the boxer would have held it. A case of the kind the eldest leaves first, with the left hand, will be collected from the back of the hall at the hour the other eight stop complaining about it.

SKATES, PARQUET, INDOORS

The skater Han Dong-hoon has removed his skates from the parquet, indoors, at last, in compliance with the standing request of the house. The hall thanks him; the parquet, separately, thanks him in its own way, which is a quieter way and has held its shape since 1973. Any skates found in the hall after this notice will be carried to the door, left-hand first, with no mention of boxers and no correction of spelling.

LAUGHTER, HOUSEHOLD SUPPLY, BY THE COMEDIAN

One supply of the proper exhalation, from the entertainer born Ha Dong-hoon in 1979, American stage and film, kept for the evening when the comedian has had his turn and the room exhales. No one who has met him disputes the exhalation, and the provisioner sells no substitute. The pair of doors facing one another from the generous year of 1983 — the actor Primary and the musician Yoon Dong-hun — will receive their own deliveries, at the same price, in the same hand, with the hyphen standard issue.

To Let & Personals

TO LET: ONE DOOR IN A HALL OF NINE

Parquet, bow included, no skates indoors, one arm’s length of wine to the nearest guest. Occupant to be born 1983 or prepared to be called generous, and to spell the name exactly as the register has it, or to insist, once, at the second introduction, on the tenth form. The lease runs for the whole life of the proclamation; the Registry renews at no charge, in the same handwriting as every other sign. Enquiries to the Keeper of Names, who belongs, per the minute book and the pencil note, about the size of a bridge, and who is to be met on the far side of the room, with the palm out.

H.L., DIRECTOR, CORRECTS OWN BILLING

Third premiere; house laughed; correction right. The Registry records this for the fourth time this year and notes that the man, born Tong-hoon Lee in 1973, works in light and keeps the door for those who do. No further comment is owed; the correction, once given, is a gift. — A.

FOOTBALLER, DOOR PLAYING OPEN

Nam Dong-hoon, 1984, is wanted to keep the door open for anyone who has ever run, at the evening of 14 March. He owes the other eight doors the explanation, noted above under Wanted, and the provisioner asks that the quails be sent with the marrows boiled, not bowed, and that the vinegar be left where the rule five suspects can find it, at a distance. The hall will hold its wine accordingly. — The Registry.

PERSONAL: ON THE PROCLAMATION DAY

To the reader out of the hall: the system you are reading was five years in the Academy’s drafting and one day, 7 July 2000, in Proclamation No. 2000-8, and it has spent the years since doing exactly what this paper does — setting names, place by place, person by person, business by business, into one alphabet, in one hand, on purpose. That is the whole of its principal office, and it is more than enough for one newspaper. — The Keeper of Names
