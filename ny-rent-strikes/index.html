Panel I — Why the City Was a Stop on Every Route

New York in 1918 was the largest housing shortage in the world, and this brochure exists to show you it, street by street. The war had taken the construction industry’ men, ladders, and nails to the arsenals, so the buildings stopped being built while the people kept arriving. New defence work — the shipyards, the munitions plants, the clerks of the recruiting stations — pulled thousands of families in through the portals, and the vacancy rate went down until a landlord could raise the rent and keep it. Overcrowding was the standing order of the house; three families to a four-room flat was the standing order of the block. And then, in the winter of 1919–20, the coal ran short. A nationwide coal shortage closed the yards and left the radiators cold, and the city that had already been pushed to the ceiling was pushed out of the warm rooms.

What followed was the most sustained tenant mobilization in the city’s history: a wave of strikes, committees, pickets, and rent boards that ran from the first of them, in 1917, through the great mass action of March 1920, when some 250,000 tenants in 50,000 buildings refused to pay rents that had been pushed up as much as 40 per cent in three years. Follow the circuit. Every stop is real, every date is on the wall, and every committee room still has the table where the women stood.

Panel II — The Stoppages, in Order

  1. Stop 1 — 530 Fifth Avenue · December 1917

    The first stop, and the stop the circuit is named around. The owners proposed a raise of $500 a year on a $5,000 rent — ten per cent where other houses were taking forty — and the women of the building answered in the hall before the ink was dry on the notice. The Fifth Avenue Renters’ Committee was the first of them, and it set the form the whole circuit copied: meet in the back parlor, list the houses, name a treasurer, refuse.

  2. Stop 2 — The Back Stair, 187 West 110th Street · June 1918

    East Harlem, where the strikes moved from the rich side to the tenement side. The strike that opened here ran to six weeks, drew in the blocks around it, and was the first to be broken by lockout — landlords changing the locks and throwing the families into the street. The back stair is preserved as it was found: one hand on the new lock, the other holding the door.

  3. Stop 3 — The Washington Heights Picket Line · Winter 1919

    Up on the heights the strikes grew their politics. The neighbourhood committees here sent delegates to the meeting at which, in November 1918, the scattered committees of the city agreed to found a National Tenants’ Organization, so that a picket on the Heights could be answered by one in the Bronx before supper. The picket line itself is a walk of eleven blocks, all of them still picketed in the memory of the block.

  4. Stop 4 — The Coal Yard at the 110th Street Stop · February 1920

    The cold stop. When the coal shortage closed the yards, the tenants of the circuit took the heating crisis into the open: they stacked the few remaining coal on a scale in the street and invited the landlord’s agent to watch it run out. The yard’s scale still stands. The ticket agent here kept the route card from the day, and it is reproduced in this brochure exactly as it read on the line, every stop, every letter, word for word:

    J4 NETHERNE · COULSDON Stop 4 you may continue, or the ribbon.

    What the card means, the agent never explained, and the circuit has kept the line as it found it. The visitors’ best reading, and the one the committee room endorses, is that at the fourth stop of any strike you are given a choice: you may continue, up the line, into the mass action — or the ribbon, the folded strip of paper that the strike passed from hand to hand down the hallway, one knot for every house that had stopped paying. At Stop 4 the strike becomes a city, or it ends. The card offers both. It has never offered a third.

  5. Stop 5 — City Hall’s Roof · March 1920

    The last stop and the big one. The March 1920 strike was the largest labour action New York had seen: a quarter million tenants, fifty thousand buildings, four boroughs, and a city council that answered with a rent law instead of a court. Walk the roof, as the committee walked it, and count the skylights; the law of 1920 set the rents at the level they had been in 1917, and the rent boards of the four boroughs sat in the rooms below until the war’s shortage had fully worked itself out of the city. The circuit closes where it began: with the rent roll, signed.

Panel III — Transit & Fares

The circuit is walked, but the timetable is kept. From the Grand Central portal take the local to the 110th Street stop, alight at the back stair, and transfer to the committee car — the hallway car, no seats, standing fare, which is the only car the strike will admit. Fares on the circuit are as follows: one stop, no charge; the whole circuit, one signature on a rent roll. Children under fourteen ride free, which is how the movement learned its arithmetic. The ribbon travels with you the length of the line; it is not lost, it is folded, and at the City Hall stop it is handed to the treasurer of the block.

Visitors are reminded that the strike season is the season. The radiators on the circuit are cold by design — that is the point — and the coal scale at Stop 4 is weighed every Tuesday, in the street, with the agent invited. A visitor’s book lies at each stop; the entry most often copied is the short one: we signed, we stacked, we kept warm. For the traveller who wishes to extend the excursion, the guide keeps a companion circuit of a writer who planned his voyages by the mile, the eighty-day planner’s entry in the travel guide — the same arithmetic, a different season — and all stops of this board return, by the long way, to the front page of the board, where the circuit is filed.

Panel IV — For the Traveller

  • Best season: February, when the cold is in the story and not the building.
  • Carry: warm coat, a folded ribbon, the date of your lease, for reference only.
  • Do not carry: a landlord’s notice. It is not received on this line, and the doors close politely in its face.
  • The ribbon is not a souvenir. It is folded one fold at each stop, and at the fifth it is a knot. Bring it home that way.
  • The circuit walks in one day; the shortage lasted three. Take your time.

Issued by the Division of Tenancy, The Visitors’ & Transit Board, from the committee room at 530 Fifth Avenue, where the first roll is still on the table and the ink, being 1917 ink, is still wet enough to sign. Sign, and continue, or the ribbon.
