The Clio & Lakes Line

Official Timetable and Conductor’s Commentary, No. 1 — The Twenty-first of January

In force since 21 January 1986 · All times local, Lake Michigan Standard · Published annually on the twenty-first of January

The Clio & Lakes Line is the annual passenger service of the twenty-first of January, the day in the United States set apart to the hug. It was first launched, and first run, from the platform at Clio, Michigan, in 1986, at the initiative of Mr. Kevin Zaborney of Clio, who has since kept the line’s calendar in good order: every year, on the same day, the same form. The line is not, strictly speaking, for transport. Its object is to carry passengers toward one another — toward family and friends at first, and, where the passenger will have it, toward strangers — and to recommend the practice of hugging more often than the ordinary year allows. Services are consensual in the full railway sense: the door of every carriage opens only for those who have asked and been accepted. The line is observed in many other countries besides, by correspondence and by imitation, and the platform at Clio keeps a wall of foreign timetables, all of them dated January, none of them in force in this state.

2. The conductor’s commentary

On the gauge

Against the pillar beside the Clio clock there is fixed a gauge of polished maple, two steps long, its ends worn bright where generations of thumbs have rested. It was there for the first departure of 21 January 1986, and it has been there ever since, re-hung each spring by the platform office. It is not for measuring the embrace itself; the conductor’s office has never sanctioned a measure of that sort. It is for measuring the distance at which one stands before asking. What it measures is not a specimen whose label has partly faded. The gauge reads personal space, in feet, and it is the only instrument on the line that all passengers are expected to consult, huggers included, before the first word is spoken.

On the conduct of passengers

The rule of the line, set down by Mr. Zaborney and carried in every conductor’s copy of this form, is the rule of asking first. Where the response is in doubt, the passenger asks; where the answer is no, the passenger thanks the other for the refusal, which is its own kind of service, and moves on without demurrer. Respecting the personal space of others is always important on this line, and some passengers are not huggers at all: they ride from Clio to Marquette and back without a single arm around another, and are no worse for it. The conductor’s office reminds passengers that the embrace is a favour, not a due, and that the timetable is a suggestion of company, not a bill.

On the fundraising specials

Some services on the day are run to raise money for charities, and the Fundraising Special, No. 4, is the oldest of these. Its fares are collected as they always have been — each embrace offered at the window of carriage D carries a token, and the tokens are counted at Midland, where the platform office keeps a crock of them beside the water tap. In 1986 the first special raised enough for a bench. The bench still stands at the Midland platform, and it is the least-worn seat on the line, because no one on this line sits in it alone.

On the corresponding lines

The holiday is kept in many other countries as well, and the platform at Clio corresponds with all of them. Readers who would see the manner of another line’s commentary — the halts, the halting, and the rule of the road set down in a conductor’s voice — may turn to the Epirus & Thessaly Line — Timetable & Commentary, Form TK-30, where the Komnenos rule is explained service by service; and to the Courant Line, Service LC, whose 05:52 departure has been kept since the first publication of 1752, a longer fidelity than this line’s by four decades, though this line may lay claim to the warmer platform. For the substance of the object the line carries — the book of the day, bound and reissued in its many editions — the platform office refers passengers to the school report filed on the book, Class 7B. The platform of Kampala, which in the line’s early years corresponded with Clio by imitation, kept besides its timetable a small dream ledger for the night of 26 January, the fifth day of the line, and the Keeper’s entry for that night — the classroom, the chalk in the tray, and the train that waits at the platform — stands in the dream dictionary at David Kato, Entry No. 47 of the Oneirocriticon. The whole miscellany, of which this form is one leaf, gathers at the front page.

This form is published once a year and in force only one day. Passengers are reminded that no reservation is taken, that no ticket is required of the heart, and that the Clio & Lakes Line has not missed a January in forty years. Next publication: 21 January, this day again.
