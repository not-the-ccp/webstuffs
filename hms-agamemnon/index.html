The Naval Register · In Memoriam · laid in the book at Chatham

HMS Agamemnon

Laid down 1852 · Launched at Deptford · Completed 1853 · Paid off 1856 · Broken up 1862

Lead ship of her class · 91 guns · second rate · Royal Navy

Entered on the book in 1852. She outlived the war by two years and the cable by three. The register keeps her, all the same, in the company of the finished.

I Of the Laying Down and the Nine Sisters

The keel went into the ground at Deptford in the year 1852, which is to say that for as long as most of her crew can be imagined remembering anything, Agamemnon had a name before she had a bottom. She was built to a 91-gun second-rate plan of the 1850s — one of a class of sister ships the Admiralty ordered as if it were buying a regiment of the same regiment — and she was the lead of them, the one the plan was made of, the one whose measurements the others would be checked against for the next decade. She came out of the water at Deptford in 1853, complete, fitted, her figurehead cut from oak by a carver in Greenwich whose name the yard books give once and never again: M. T. Harland. The yards of the time did not believe in naming things twice.

Commissioned under Captain A. C. Lyons in the autumn of 1853, her first posting was the Channel Squadron, the near-inshore guard, the fleet of the weather report. There is a line in her first quarter, dated November, that the register reproduces in full because the ship is gone and the line is not: “Wind NNE 5. Sea smooth. Nothing done but watch the nothing.” That was the Channel. That was the job.

II The Mediterranean, and the Flag of the Second-in-Command

She took on the Mediterranean Fleet the same year, and with it the flag: Agamemnon became flagship to the fleet’s second-in-command, Rear Admiral G. J. Onslow, and the second-rate became, in practice, a palace with a powder magazine. A second-in-command’s flag is a particular kind of furniture. It gives you the dinners and the dockyards, and it takes from you the first word of every decision, which the Admiral at the top of the line had already taken, at sea or ashore, in a country that had been fighting in the Black Sea for a season. The log for the spring of 1854 records the fleet’s passage through the Dardanelles in six days, which was considered, at the time, a good figure, and which is still a good figure.

The register notes that Agamemnon’s gunnery drill in the Bosphorus was witnessed, from a hillside, by a French admiral and a crowd of villagers, and that the French admiral wrote home that the ship “fires as if she is arguing about it.” She had 91 guns. The argument was on her side.

III The Black Sea: Sevastopol, 1854; Kinburn, 1855

The Crimean War of 1854–1856 is her chapter, and Sevastopol is the page. In the bombardment of the fortress in 1854, Agamemnon stood offshore in a line with her sisters and worked her broadsides at the Malakoff and the Conception batteries through an autumn that the ships’ logs describe, at their dullest, as “windy, with smoke.” The ship took no wound that winter. Her prize, in the arithmetic of that season, was not the fortress — that came to the land army, at a cost the register will not repeat — but the standing: a 91-gun second rate, the lead of her class, firing from the line as the war’s largest naval operation went on around her.

The following year the argument moved up the coast. At Kinburn, in the autumn of 1855, the squadron under Admiral Edmund Lyons — who had by then taken the top of the line — forced a line of forts that had been called impregnable by the people who had built them. Agamemnon was in the line, and in the account of the day she is the ship the shore batteries aimed at most, because she was the biggest, and because she was the flag. The forts were taken in a day. The forts had been a year in the news before that. The register keeps this proportion as its one piece of arithmetic: one ship, one day, the year spent.

For the officers and men who stood the winter of 1854–1855 in a Black Sea that froze at the edges, the ship’s company numbered, by the muster of January 1855, 562 souls, of whom 49 were sick on the day the ice first showed — a figure the surgeon’s log treats as a small comfort and the cook, in a margin note of three words, treats as “extra rations?” The extra rations were not issued. The cook’s question stands.

IV The Refit: A Ship Rebuilt to Carry a Wire

She was paid off in 1856, which on the book is printed as a death and is here recorded as such, because what came next was a resurrection that the Admiralty had to invent a hull for. The refit of 1856–1857 re-armed her not in guns but in spools: Agamemnon was re-built to carry half of the first transatlantic telegraph cable — the western half, in a drum of her own that sat, like a second keel, through the lower deck, from the fore hold to the well aft. A warship converted to a laying ship is a ship with her mouth full of wire; the refit drawings show 3,400 nautical miles of insulated copper threaded through a vessel that had only a few weeks before been a 91-gun second rate.

The first attempt, in the summer of 1857, failed in the Atlantic, the cable parting from the ship in a sea that the log calls “heavy, and not, in any sense, wrong to be so.” The wire went down at a depth the sounding could not take. The second attempt, in 1858, went the other way it could: the cable was completed, the first message crossed, and for eleven days the two continents spoke to each other through a copper wire that had been lying, half of it, in the hold of a Deptford oak. The eleven days were then, and are still, the entire service. The message service lapsed in the autumn of 1858; Agamemnon’s spools were emptied, her lower deck stood empty where the drum had been, and the ship was struck from the cable service with, in the Admiralty’s own phrase, “no further requirement.” She was broken up in 1862, and the register does not record who was left standing at the quay, because at Deptford, in 1862, nobody stood for anything any more.

V The Final Account

Ten years, on the book, for a ship: 1852 to 1862. One Channel, one Mediterranean, one Black Sea, two bombardments, one cable, eleven days of traffic, one breakup. Her sisters came and went around her — a class of 91-gun second rates that outlived the era of the wooden line as a whole, most of them finished in the same decade, in the same yards, by the same men who had not yet been introduced to the ironclad, which would take their place the way a verdict takes a jury. The term report on the Peresvet class, three Russian pre-dreadnoughts of a later and shorter century, is the register’s nearest cousin in this business of keeping a whole class of large wooden ships in one sentence; the method is the same, and the outcome, in both, is a class that did not get to see itself end.

The quarter deck of her final years was refitted for spools, not officers. The register holds that this is the correct last word on a ship: the deck was changed for what came next, and what came next did not come aboard.

VI A Line to Keep

The register keeps one line whole on her, from a shore notice filed against her by name, copied here exactly as found, in the hand it arrived in: The chapter’s own coast has since filed the matter closed in March with a green-and-white chevron is a very long time. The line is not a verdict; the register does not take it for one. It is a notice from the coast — the coast that watched the ship come and go for two decades of weather — that the file on Agamemnon has been closed, that the chevron on the flag was green-and-white, that March was the month, and that all of it is, in the coast’s own words, a very long time.

The Webstuffs shelf keeps the notice pinned where it can be read, and the register keeps the ship with it. Readers of the wider shelf will find the home page of Webstuffs to begin from, or the chapter on the etiquette of a polar winter aboard the Tegetthoff, for what a ship owes the people who winter on her — a debt Agamemnon paid, in the Black Sea, in 1855, and never asked to be paid back.
