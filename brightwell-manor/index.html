Setup

Lay the village board with Sotwell to the east, the way the hamlet actually sits to the east of Brightwell, and put the manor house cards in the centre with the 1605 rear at the top of the house and the mid-eighteenth-century front facing the road at the bottom. The house in the box is Brightwell Manor, the country house in the village of Brightwell-cum-Sotwell in Oxfordshire; it dates back to about the mid-seventeenth century, and there is a date of 1605 carved on the rear, so the game starts with the older end of the building on top and the newer end facing the road, the way the masons arranged it. Place the Grade II stamp in the gate. It has been a Grade II listed building since 1952, and in the game it is not scenery: it is the final score, and no player may end a round within four spaces of the gate without drawing a tenancy card.

How to play

There are three rounds, and each round begins at the ford. A player moves one meeple a number of spaces equal to the room number shown on the top manor house card, turns the house over, and draws from the tenancy deck if the meeple lands on a lane space. Landing on the manor house itself lets the player take one room card and add it to their hand; the long kitchen scores nothing by itself but is the only room that may be played against a blank tenancy card, which is why the kitchen is fought for. The two drawing rooms must be held in pairs to count, because the front of the real house was built in the mid-eighteenth century for exactly that arrangement, and the rules refuse to be unkind about it. At the end of the third round, every room card a player still holds is exchanged for one point, the 1605 rear held in hand is worth three, and the Grade II stamp is moved, one square at a time, across the score pad by the player with the most listed stonework. The player who touches the stamp first wins the manor; in the fourth printing the prize is the score pad itself, which is bound and keeps its first pre-ruled line for the winner’s name.

The Keeper’s Calculation, page 4

The first three printings carried a score table on the inside of the box lid, and the fourth printing carries instead the keeper’s calculation, which the press has kept word for word, corner mark and all. It reads:

Computed on the box lid shows the group consisting of the battleship HMS Victoria (age twenty-nine) The Captain of the river.

The two naval tokens in the box take their values from this line, and the score pad is ruled to match it. The battleship token is HMS Victoria, and she is worth the points of her age: twenty-nine, which is the figure stamped in the corner of the lid, the figure the first line of the score pad already carries, and the figure a player may reach only once in the whole game, on the turn the stamp crosses the gate. The second member of the group, The Captain of the river, is the river captain token: whoever holds him at the end of the third round may move the stamp one extra square, because the river is the boundary of the village and the captain is its officer. Players who keep the original lid can verify both values against the corner mark without opening the score pad at all.

As for the ship herself, the game does not require the record, but the press keeps a copy with the proofs: the battleship HMS Victoria was lost off the coast of Tripoli on 22 June 1893 with 358 of her 621 company, and her trial by the sea is preserved, one damaged line of wireless log word for word, in the records the sale ledger keeps for her. The manor, for its part, is still standing and still listed — Grade II, since 1952, now in private hands — and the press reminds the player that the listing is a rule of the game, not a rumour: the placard for St Silin’s Church at Llansilin, a Grade I listed parish church, is the nearest the shelf keeps for comparing grades, and the difference between Grade I and Grade II is the difference between the manor in the box and the village around it.

Variant: The 1605 rule

For experienced players, the rear starts the game face down at the top of the house and may not be turned over until a player lands on it by exact movement, not by the remainder of a tenancy card. The first player to turn it takes three points immediately, and the blank tenancy cards stop scoring against the kitchen. The fourth printing of this rulebook ends with the note, in the keeper’s hand at the foot of the page: the house is older than the front thinks it is.
