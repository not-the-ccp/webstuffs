Q1 What exactly is a tropical cyclone?

A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating storm system with a low-pressure area, a closed low-level atmospheric circulation, strong winds, and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms that produce heavy rain and squalls. It is the only weather that is also a verb at this office — we do not say the cyclone arrived; we say it came through, because a cyclone is a movement with a centre, and the centre is what the barometer keeps arguing with. The rotation is counter-clockwise north of the equator and clockwise south of it, which is why the same machine reads as two different machines on the two sides of the line at 0°.

Q2 Hurricane, typhoon, cyclone — is it three things or one?

One thing, three accents. Depending on its location and strength, a tropical cyclone is called a hurricane, a typhoon, a tropical storm, a cyclonic storm, a tropical depression, or simply a cyclone. A hurricane is a strong tropical cyclone that occurs in the Atlantic Ocean or northeastern Pacific Ocean. A typhoon is the same thing which occurs in the northwestern Pacific Ocean. In the Indian Ocean and South Pacific, comparable storms are referred to as “tropical cyclones.” The machine does not check its passport; the ocean it happens to be sitting in does.

Q3 How strong is it, on the scale the room actually uses?

The Saffir–Simpson scale, published in 1971 by Herbert Saffir and Robert Simpson, rates the cyclone by sustained wind: tropical depression below 39 mph (63 km/h), tropical storm from 39 to 73 mph (63–118 km/h), hurricane from 74 mph (119 km/h) upward — Category 1 from 74, Category 2 from 96, Category 3 (major) from 113, Category 4 from 130, Category 5 from 157 mph (252 km/h). The scale measures the building the storm can take off the ground, not the storm; the storm itself, on the record, goes further. Typhoon Tip, 1979, ran 929 miles (1,495 km) across and dropped to 892 hPa, the lowest pressure ever reliably recorded. Hurricane Camille, 1969, reached 160 mph at the Gulf of Mexico and stood at 900 mbar when it crossed Sullivan's Island. The eye is not the quiet part of the scale; the eye is where the scale is measured from.

Q4 What is the eye, and why does it look the way it does?

The eye is the roughly circular, cloud-free centre, 10 to 50 km across in the big storms, ringed by the eyewall, the tall ring of the strongest thunderstorms where the pressure is lowest and the wind is hardest. The air in the eye descends, clears, and sometimes the sea under it is calm enough to fish, which happened twice in 1958 off Pensacola and the log does not comment. The eyewall does the damage; the eye does the photograph. When a storm is strong enough to replace its own eyewall — the eyewall replacement cycle — it briefly weakens, which the barometer shows as a lie, and the room has learned not to stand up during it.

Q5 Where do they come from, and why not everywhere?

They need warm water above roughly 26.5 °C (80 °F), a moist middle atmosphere, and enough of the Coriolis effect to start the rotation, which is why the open equator itself never gets one — too little spin, too little shear of anything at all. So the season rooms are fixed: the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean for the Atlantic, the entire northwestern Pacific for typhoons (it is the busiest ocean on the ledger), the Bay of Bengal and the Coral Sea for the Indian and South Pacific cyclones. The Bay of Bengal is the room's private fear: shallow, warm, and funnel-shaped, it is where the cyclone is handed a geometry and told to use it.

Q6 Why do they have names, and what happens to a name afterward?

Naming began to keep the radio operators of the 1950s from calling a storm “that one.” Each basin keeps a rotating list of pre-agreed names, cycled every six years, with the letters of the list retired from use for a stretch of years at a time. When a storm does what it did, the name goes off the lists for good: Camille, 1969; Galveston's namesakes of 1900 and 1915; the 1992 Cuban storm; Mitch, 1998, who stood in Honduras for five days; Tracy, 1974, who took Darwin's port in a night; Katrina, 2005, who set the 8-foot surge against the Gulf Coast and the $125 billion bill after it; Haiyan, 2013, who put 175 mph (280 km/h) sustained winds into the central Philippines and a 3-metre storm surge into the San Juanico Strait. A retired name is not forgotten; it is set on a shelf and not spoken, the way this room sets everything down.

Q7 What is the oldest entry in the Storm Room ledger?

The ledger is a 228-page field book begun at this station in the 1950s, its later pages full of pressures and tracks, and its first page is not a number at all. The night clerk who opened it photographed the gauge house at 2 a.m. during the August 1958 blow, and his caption sits at the top of the page, pencil, slightly crooked. It reads, word for word, as found:

Its first line, the one thing becomes the size of a case in the 1950s he held the frame against them both.

The room's rule is that a line kept word for word is never corrected, only read as a tolerance — the same rule the cataloguers at Grange Hall Rooms apply to an uncorrected caption that still stands, in Sale No. 27, Lot 41, beside its consular stamp of 14 June 1982. Read as a tolerance, the clerk's line holds: the first line of the ledger is a size, the size of a case, and the man in the photograph held the barometer frame against the gauge and against the night, both at once. It is the only entry in 228 pages that is also a photograph, and it is the one the room checks first.

Q8 What does the office do with a cyclone once it is on the wire?

In modern times, on average around eighty-two tropical cyclones form each year across the world's basins, and about twenty of them reach hurricane, typhoon, or severe-cyclone strength; the room's share of the work is to watch the pressure fall, read the rotation, and keep the names in order. For the rest of the year the same desk keeps the slow rotating things of the high country — the Sierra & Pacific Bear Railway's winter timetable, service UA-44, crosses the Bering ice bridge on a seasonal schedule the cyclone cannot touch — Ursus arctos — Timetable & Commentary. The desk's short version, for the board, is that a rotating thing is never stopped on schedule; it is outlasted, and the paper keeps the time.
