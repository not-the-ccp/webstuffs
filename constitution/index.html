The Framing Line

Constitution of the United States of America

Sheet No. 43 · Working Timetable with Commentary · slow service · one only · seven articles in the consist · the line is patient, as are the articles

The desk, which has ruled in Maine as Sheet No. 41 and kept the flitch at the back of the register, keeps this sheet as its No. 43, because the question was asked across the border — twice, in one letter, by the boatwright — and the border, once crossed, does not let go: what is the supreme law of the United States of America, and when did it leave the station? The answer, in the form the register allows it: a line. Not a wall, a line. A line with a departure, a consist of seven articles, nine stations at which it must be signed for, and a first day in effect on March 4, 1789, when it superseded the Articles of Confederation, the nation’s first constitution, the old line that had run the country on a lighter schedule. The framing — the drafting of the Constitution by many of the nation’s Founding Fathers — was completed at the Constitutional Convention, which assembled at Independence Hall in Philadelphia between May 25 and September 17, 1787, and the timetable below is the desk’s account of the run, station by station, the way a kept book keeps a run.

1What the line is

The Framing Line is the supreme law of the United States of America. That is its whole character as a line, and the desk will not run two of them: where any other statute, charter, or plat book stands on the same track, the Framing Line has priority, and the other thing is set back to a siding. It was originally framed in seven articles — the seven coaches of the consist, each coupled, each load-bearing — and those seven defined the foundational structure of the federal government: the legislative power in the first, the executive in the second, the states and the union among them in the rest, the money article, the amendments article, and the supremacy article, which is the headlamp. The line was worked for ratification, not for signature alone; a constitution is a timetable, and a timetable only runs when the stations adopt it. Nine states were the working quorum, and the nine arrived as the timetable below keeps them.

2The timetable, 1787–1790

Slow service, all-day. Miles are the desk’s own ruling, from Philadelphia, and are kept so the line reads true on the map it was drawn on. The ninth ratification — New Hampshire, June 21, 1788 — is the station at which the line, by its own terms, went into effect among the first states to convene; the desk, sitting in that very state, eleven miles of border from the window at Laconia, rules the fact in double line. The run was not finished, however, until every station had signed: Rhode Island, the last, adopted on May 29, 1790, and the line ran complete.

The order is the order of the signatures, which is not the order of the map, and a timetable is an argument as much as a schedule: the line went where the stations were ready, and the miles were whatever the miles were. The desk has marked the miles in one colour and the dates in another, as the border is ruled — double line for the state, single for the trend.

3The old line

Before the Framing Line there was the old one: the Articles of Confederation, the nation’s first constitution, which the new line superseded on March 4, 1789. The old line had run the country the way a light engine runs a branch — a league of the states with a single congress and no head of the executive, no separate court, and the power of the purse left at the stations, each state keeping its own fare. It was a working line; the desk will say so, because the border says so too, and a line that has been walked is not to be mocked for being slow. But the country outgrew it the way the township on the Calumet Shore outgrew its own borders: not by fault of the track, but by the weight of the traffic, and on 1 December 1889 one such border was annexed, as the Articles were superseded, whole and without advertisement. The old line’s book was closed on March 4, 1789, and the new line’s opened the same morning, the way the lakefront supper room opened its own ledger the evening a season changed — same floor, same windows, the account of itself carried over.

4The engines, from the margin

A timetable that does not name its engines is a schedule without a cause, and the desk has set these in the margin of the run. English common law first: the long line of the year’s case law on which every American line has its siding, and from which the Framing Line took its ballast without ever stopping to say so. Then the Enlightenment liberalism of the philosophers the delegates read aboard — John Locke, who argued that a government holds its passengers by consent and must be answerable when it does not, and Montesquieu, whose separation of the powers the seven articles carry like three cylinders: legislative, executive, judicial, each doing its own work of the motion. The framing was done by many of the nation’s Founding Fathers in that one hall, in that one summer of 1787, between May 25 and September 17, and the frame they left on September 17 is the frame this line runs on. The desk adds, in the hand the register allows it: a constitution is not a timetable that only shows the departures. It is the part of the machine that also shows what shall not be done, and the blank space of the page is load-bearing, as the border is, as the empty drawer at the Laconia desk is, the drawer that holds, as it has held, nothing, and is kept for the reason it does not hold.

5The refreshment room’s card, kept word for word

The refreshment room at the New York platform — the port station, where the line’s argument was longest and the trains sat longest — kept, as the tea meeting at Hyde Park kept its gallery, a card for every evening of the run of 1788–89: what was set down, what was cleared, and which cups the cook had washed twice, the cup being the smallest unit of the ledger, as the mile is the smallest unit of the timetable. The card for the evening of the signing, kept by the supper keeper in a hand the desk has been told not to copy and has copied anyway, holds one line in the season that no clerk has explained and no passenger should be asked to explain. Set down between the note on the oysters and the note on the quartet, it reads, word for word:

The company files these beside the receipts and will do for the various domestics.

Which is to say, in the keeper’s own practice, what he meant and what he kept: the cards — these, the small paper of the evening — were filed beside the receipts, in the same drawer and in the keeper’s own order, and they would do, that is, would serve, for the various domestics as well as for the passengers: the cook, the waiter, the lamp keeper, each of them accounted for on the same card as the gallery, the ledger making no difference between the one who sits at the supper and the one who sets it, because a kept book keeps the whole house and not only the part of the house that signs for itself. The room did not say so in those words; the room said it the other way, and the desk keeps the room’s words, for the same reason the State Border Answers Desk keeps the boatwright’s flitch line at the back of the register: the strangest line in any kept book belongs to whoever wrote it, and once the desk has explained it, the line does the rest of the remembering.

6Standing rules and the shelf

First: the line is the supreme law, and no other sheet on the shelf has priority on the same track. Second: the ninth station is ruled in double line, and the double line is not amended on the second Thursday. Third: the old line is not mocked; it ran the country until March 4, 1789, and the new line supersedes the way a border supersedes a swamp — dry where it was wet, and honest in the twelfth month. The desk keeps this sheet in the same pocket as Sheet No. 41, Maine, the state the kettle knows best, whose flitch is to be the whale’s, and the two sheets agree that a kept name is a kept law. The 6:15 that brings the table to the supper room on the Calumet Shore — the service rated in the lakefront ledger of Product Sheet No. CSE-0001, Lot 1, the township itself — is this line’s cousin by the timetable, and the desk files it beside this one for the reason the company files its receipts: without advertisement, and for good reason. The whole shelf, this sheet and its neighbours, stands on the front page of the desk, where the keeper has ruled the lot numbers in the same hand as the miles. The line is patient, and the line will not wait for the argument to finish.
