3.1 Designation and extent

Anatolia is a peninsula in West Asia, and it makes up the majority of the land area of Turkey. It is the westernmost protrusion of Asia: the farthest the land of the continent reaches toward Europe before the water takes it. The waters take it on four sides. The Mediterranean runs along the south; the Aegean holds the west; the Propontis — the Sea of Marmara — sits to the northwest, and the Black Sea closes the north. The Propontis is the hinge, and it carries two chokepoints: the Hellespont on its eastern mouth toward the Mediterranean, and the Bosporus on its western toward the Black Sea. A ship that has passed both has been across the whole of Asia without once leaving Asia; that is the trick of the place, and this manual's other chapters stand on it.

By measure the survey sheet calls the peninsula roughly 480,000 square kilometres, held between two ranges: the Pontic range along the Black Sea coast, which is the range the chandler's country belongs to, and the Taurus range along the Mediterranean, which is the range the southern trade winds climb. Between the ranges the land rises into the central plateau — the dry, table-land that water runs off, the country of wells and caravans and the long roads that cross it at a walk. The eastern and southeastern limits are not drawn on this sheet; the survey's rule is that they have been expanded either to the entirety of Asiatic Turkey or to an imprecise line from the Black Sea to the Gulf of Alexandretta, and the sheet keeps both readings rather than choosing, the way the bench keeps both the objection and the ruling in the same folio. The whole of the record that gave the Pontic series its first docket was kept inside this extent, on the Pontic range, at the place where the range meets the sea and the quay meets the road.

The two gates are not the same instrument. The Bosporus is a channel that runs in both directions at once — surface and depth moving apart, and every helmsman on the strait learns the difference the hard way in the first winter. The Hellespont is narrower and more one-minded: its current is a single argument, and the old name for it, the Hellespont, is the name the western side of the water still uses, while the gate itself answers to the town on its shore. Between the two gates the Sea of Marmara keeps its own field notes, and this chapter refers the reader there rather than repeating the measures: the field notes on the Marmara — its two-way currents, its eighteen minutes of light, its snowmelt — are the sister sheet of this one, and the two are filed together in the manual drawer. The bench that sat above the chandler's quay at Smyrna held its docket in the shadow of exactly these waters; the sister docket, Mediterranean v. Octavianus, Case No. 14, stands in the same drawer, read aloud in the face of an objection, the method the Pontic series follows.

3.3 The land between the waters

The Pontic range runs the whole northern shore and gives the sea its grey, its cold, and its weather. Down its faces the rivers come short and fast; the Halys — the Kızılırmak, which throws its silt so hard the sea off the north coast runs the colour of tea — is the longest of them and the one the survey sheet marks first. Inland the land opens: the plateau, the wells, the long dry roads. On the southern shoulder the Taurus throws up the high passes, and the rivers that come over them — the Meander, the Cayster — run west to the Aegean in the braided beds that gave one of them its old name.

The towns the manual keeps by name are the towns the ledger keeps by name. On the northern shore: Trebizond, the old city on the harbour; Samsun, where the plateau road meets the sea; and Of, between them, the chandler's village on the quay, where the state road from Trebizond comes down to the water and the bell tower stands on the hill above the schoolroom. On the western and southern coasts: Smyrna, where this series sat on the bench; Gallipoli, where the Hellespont narrows and the peninsula's neck is at its thinnest; the inland towns of the plateau, where the escort roads cross dry. The names stand in their places on this sheet the way they stood in the ledger: one each, in order, no duplicate, no gap — the same rule the relay sheet of the wire keeps, stated for the shore.

3.4 Standing orders of the chandler's desk

  1. Rope the hawser before you name the vessel. The name of a boat stands or falls with the rope on the hawser. The Agios Sotiris, forty-two feet, ran the cabotage between Of and Trebizond; she is named in this manual because her hawser was roped, and she is named once, in the record, where she stands.
  2. Keep the ledger and do not strike the name. The ledger of the harbour office at Of is forty-one pages, opened 1909, and holds 358 names. A name kept in a book outlives a name kept in a head. The bench of the Pontic series ruled the ledger read, not struck; the manual adopts the ruling as standing order for every desk under it.
  3. Record the bell. The bell in the tower at Of rang fourteen times on 19 May 1915 and has never rung fourteen times before or since. Whatever fourteen means, it means because it rang. The desk records the count; the desk does not spend it.
  4. The address is the record and the interstate sees. The state road that runs Trebizond to the south is the interstate of this survey: it runs between the provinces and it saw. Observations taken from the road, with the hour and the station noted, are admissible; that is the line the bench adopted verbatim, and it is law in this chapter as it is in the docket.
  5. Leave the lamp unextinguished. Adjournment is not extinguishment. The sitting of Case No. 21 closed at 12:02 p.m. with the lamp still lit above the clerk; a survey sheet filed in the same drawer carries the same order, and the desk that closes the drawer closes it with the lamp on.

3.5 Margin and closing

The margin of this chapter is entered word for word, on the bench's own method: the line is kept, not explained, the way the clerk keeps the line that makes the docket. From the file of the review of the Hall Street dining room, the one the wire clerks of the relay room wound the tape back to, the margin reads:

Strike the ledger keeps its receipts — kept a recipe card half-overwritten by static from the war years pressing.

The practice of the desk is to read the line once, enter it once, and move on, and so this chapter moves on. The peninsula is surveyed, the gates are measured, the standing orders are on the sheet, and the name is kept. The lamp on the desk above the chandler's quay is not extinguished, and neither is this page.

Chapter 3 is filed with the Pontic series at the trial bench, Case No. 21, with the sister notes on the Marmara at the coastal drawer, and returned to the register at the front of the manual.
