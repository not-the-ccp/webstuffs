CHAPTER XIV

Of the Pen That Outlived Its Owner:
the Twenty Recovered Newspaper Stories, 1966–1984

“A pen is a lease, not a deed. What it wrote is kept.”

I. On the Discovery

It is a fact of the newspaper office, and the first fact of this chapter, that a story once clipped is a story lost; that the pasteboard holds, and the memory does not. Yet the office keeps its own almanac. In the year 2023 a posthumous volume came from the press bearing the title A Stroke of the Pen: The Lost Stories, assembled from the archive drawers of two country papers, the Western Daily Press of Swindon and the Bucks Free Press of Aylesbury, and it holds twenty stories recovered from their old typescript: a collection of short fiction written by Terry Pratchett — the author, before the wizards came to his own handwriting — for the local column, under his own name and under two others, which is to say under two other manners. The etiquette of the matter is that the pen did not die when the hand did. It simply kept its proof. The recovered volume is, of all the editions of a writer’s life, the least grand and the most exact: it is the writer at the office bench, on the afternoon paper, signing his work the way a guest signs the hotel book — with one name when he is himself, and with another when the story is a costume.

II. On the Pseudonyms: Patrick Kearns and Uncle Jim

A writer may borrow a name for a story the way a caller borrows an umbrella against rain: it is understood, and it is not to be returned soaked. Two such names occur in the recovered collection. Patrick Kearns was the name put to the stories the author wished kept a polite distance from the one on the letterhead, and Uncle Jim was the name put to those written for the young column, where a writer who signs “Uncle Jim” has undertaken, by the signature alone, a whole manner of kneeling down. The etiquette book is strict on the point: a pseudonym is a second manner, and must be kept consistently; the reader of the Western Daily Press was entitled to meet Patrick Kearns as a permanent resident of the paper, and to find Uncle Jim at the same desk every Saturday. Both names, in the recovered volume, are printed with the courtesy the office always owed them — the byline, and nothing more.

III. On the Dates: A Ledger of Five-and-Twenty Years

The twenty stories do not sit together, as collected stories often must, in one drawer of one year. Seventeen of the twenty were initially published between 1970 and 1975, the middle stretch of the office bench when the column was young and the stories were young with it. Of the three remaining stories, one is from 1966, one from 1967, and the last story in the collection is from 1984 — a date that outlives the cluster by nearly a decade, which the ledger must record without surprise, for a pen that has signed Uncle Jim once is not obliged to hang up. The reader is reminded that the recovered volume is a collection and not a chronicle: the manners of a collection are that the stories may be printed in the order that serves the book, while the ledger keeps the order the years had.

IV. On the Foreword and the Introduction

In addition to the stories, the book observes the further manners that belong to a posthumous volume. Its foreword is by Neil Gaiman, who is to the author what the office is to the pen — a neighbour in the trade who was given the key and used it gently. Its introduction is by Colin Smythe, the author’s agent, who kept the files, the dates, and the correspondence, and who is the proper person to say, at the head of the collection, what the files say: that the stories were written, delivered, and paid for, in their day, like any other work of the bench. The order of the three — foreword, introduction, account, and then the stories — is the etiquette’s own, and the reader is asked to keep it, for the foreword speaks of the writer, the introduction of the work, and the stories, at last, of themselves.

V. On the Research of Pat and Jan Harkin

The recovered volume carries, as its final account, the telling by Pat and Jan Harkin, two fans, of the research they did looking for the stories themselves: the run to the archive rooms at Swindon and at Aylesbury, the drawers of the Western Daily Press and the pasted proofs of the Bucks Free Press, the years’ ledgers read in order, the bylines checked, crossed, and checked again until the twenty stood out of the pasteboard like birds from a hedgerow. The etiquette book has a standing notice on such research: that a recovery is a debt, and that the names of the recoverers stand at the head of it. Pat and Jan Harkin’s account is therefore not an appendix but a manner — the record of two people who did for the stories what the stories, in their day, did for the readers of the afternoon column. Their names are printed in the volume, and this chapter prints them here, for the same reason.

VI. On the Keeping of a Line, Word for Word

In the course of the research, one line was found pencilled in the margin of an office note at the Western Daily Press, on the sheet that had held the early typescript of the 1967 story, and it is here entered in the chapter’s ledger, in the chapter’s hand, and without correction. The line reads, word for word, because a kingdom that can read in ten years, and would keep what they think.

The etiquette book must make of the line what it can, and it makes this of it: the pencilled hand was keeping a rule about the reader. The kingdom is the readership — Swindon and Aylesbury, the afternoon column, the ten years in which the stories ran and the ten years after, in which the drawers were opened and the pasteboard was found to hold. “A kingdom that can read” is the office’s name for any readership able to take a story at its word; and a kingdom, the line continues, “would keep what they think” — that is, the kingdom keeps the line exactly as it was written, for the reader in ten years is owed the same line the reader in one was given, and the keeping is the reading. This is the whole manner of the recovered volume in a single pencilled sentence: the story is written once, the line is kept, and the kingdom that can read in ten years finds the line exactly where the hand put it. Chapter XIV therefore enters the line in its ledger word for word, with no smoothing of the grammar and no apology for the pencil, for the etiquette’s first rule over a recovered line is that it is not the chapter’s to mend.

VII. Standing Manners

The chapter closes with the standing manners to be observed by any reader who has the recovered volume in hand: to read the foreword of Neil Gaiman before the introduction of Colin Smythe, and the introduction before the account of Pat and Jan Harkin, and the account before the stories; to allow Patrick Kearns and Uncle Jim their bylines without comment; to keep the ledger of 1966, 1967, 1970–1975, and 1984 in that order; and, above all, to keep the pencilled line of Section VI as it stands, word for word, without correction, for the kingdom that can read in ten years. The pen that outlived its owner keeps its proofs; the reader’s manner is to receive them.

The sister chapters of the Guide keep their own lights: the table that bears the surname Abell is treated at Chapter IV, the long winter aboard the Tegetthoff and Isabella at Chapter IX, Of Winter on the Ice, and the whole shelf, in the order it stands, at the front of the house, Webstuffs. Return to any of them by the same lamp.
