Lot 128 — The Crittenden–Piner Folio, 2 March 2012

One folio of field records, one aneroid barometer, one set of photographs, and one pack of rating cards, kept through the night of the EF4 tornado that crossed Crittenden, Piner, Fiskburg and Morning View in northern Kentucky.

Estimate: $1,800 – $2,400 Realised: $2,100 — SOLD, Room 4, 14 March, hammer at ten forty-one

Description of the Lot

At about 6:36 in the evening of Friday, 2 March 2012, the first vortex of a cyclic supercell family touched down over Fiskburg in northern Kentucky, rated later at EF2 and lasting barely a minute. Seven minutes later the family’s main tornado struck, and what follows in this lot is what a household ledger looked at it. The tornado was violent, short-lived, and deadlier than anything the southern Cincinnati metropolitan area had seen in living memory: rated EF4 on the Enhanced Fujita scale with estimated maximum winds of 165 miles per hour, it ran a path of roughly 11 miles and a maximum width of 2½ miles, sweeping through Crittenden, Piner, Fiskburg and the hillside of Morning View, carrying homes off their foundations and laying out the main streets like cordwood.

The lot is offered as it was boxed in 2014 by the Crittenden firehouse, from whose shelf the folio had been kept since the night itself. The keeper was a volunteer on the Piner side of the line, and the ledger’s hand changes at 6:52 p.m., where the calm entries of weather and water pressure give way to a single repeated line in pencil: gone, gone, gone. Four of the 41 direct fatalities of the large two-day tornado outbreak across the central and eastern United States — the March 2–3 outbreak centred on the Ohio Valley, of which this tornado was one of only two rated EF4 — were taken from these two communities, and the folio names each of them in the margin of the barometer page, in the order the firehouse called them.

The photographs number eleven: four of the funnel against the orange dusk over Piner, three of the Fiskburg approach road, one of the Morning View cut-off, and three of the morning after, when the surveyors came with their poles. The pack of rating cards holds the EF2 and EF4 assessments with the damage photographs pinned to the wrong cards, a habit of the survey crew that the box’s own label forgives in a line of red ink. The aneroid, still running, reads within two points of the night’s lowest figure, and it is offered to its new owner with the understanding that it has never been wound back.

Contents of the Folio, in order

  1. Front flyleaf, pencil inscription of the keeper — see condition report
  2. The March ledger, weather and water pressure, 1–5 March 2012; entries resume 6 March
  3. Barometer page, four names in the margin, red ink underlined twice
  4. Eleven photographs, mounted, unlabelled, dust-jacketed in wax paper
  5. One pack of Enhanced Fujita rating cards, EF2 and EF4, survey pin-holes to the backs
  6. Two pages of firehouse call log, 6:36–7:15 p.m., 2 March, tape-bound at the fold
  7. One letter from the keeper’s widow, undated, one page, on firehouse notepaper

Condition Report

The folio is a cloth-bound ledger, 9 by 6 inches, water-browned at the lower board but dry throughout; the pages open flat, the spine uncracked, which the widow’s letter explains as luck rather than care. On the front flyleaf, in pencil, in the same hand as the calm entries, is written:

“The flyleaf gives only to lose a coffee.”

That is to say, in the widow’s words from the letter: the ledger had lived its whole life on the stove shelf beside the coffee can — the family’s record of rain, pressure and water, the thing the firehouse asked for first in any weather — and when the night of 2 March swept the room, the storm took the coffee, spilled it across the page, and left the book. She kept the line on the flyleaf because a ledger that has been in the weather and come out still opening is a ledger that owes its keeper nothing but the small, repeated cost, and the small, repeated cost, she says, was the coffee.

Photographs 4 through 6 are creased from a single fold, since made good; photograph 9 (the Morning View cut-off) is the finest in the set. The barometer is offered unopened, with its original case and its 2009 calibration card. The lot may be inspected at the Rooms, Room 4, 9 Gresham Court, on the three days preceding the sale.

Provenance & Notice

Kept at the Crittenden firehouse, 2012–2014; transferred to the keeper’s widow, 2014; consigned to these rooms, 2026. We note for the record that a storm lot has a longer shelf than any other kind of lot in this house’s books: Lot 47, the Niamey Papers, entered the same sale room with a radio watch-set that had been off the air for a year and still worked. The household records that survive the night are what we sell; the rest of the night is what we do not. Enquiries on this lot may be left at the Rooms or at the board where the copyists keep their threads, The Copyist’s Desk, Thread 214, where a crate from a rigger’s house once produced a fourteen-chapter Romans and a single mouse-eaten leaf; the house that kept the weather ledger would have kept that leaf too. The shelf of the sale, with every other lot it holds, may be seen from the front page of the webstuffs catalogue.
