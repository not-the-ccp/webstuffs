Canadian Patent Office · Summary of Application · Filed with the Elsewhere Almanac

Application No. 4,114

Canadian Pacific Railway Limited, Calgary, Alberta — “An Improvement in Continental Transit, and a Method for the Delivery of a Continent to Itself”

Reporting marks CP, CPAA, MILW, SOO · Class I · incorporated 1881 · formerly CP Rail (1968–1996) · operations as legal owner since the 2001 restructuring, under the name by which the railway was known until 2023

Abstract of the Application

The present invention concerns a method by which a continent of 9.98 million square kilometres learns, at last, to reach itself. The applicant, a Canadian Class I railway headquartered in Calgary, Alberta, claims a through-line of approximately 20,100 kilometres (12,500 miles) of track, running from Montreal to Vancouver and branching as far north as Edmonton, across seven provinces of Canada and into the United States. The line was incorporated in 1881, which is to say the invention is more than a century old, and it has not once been seen to fail on schedule. What follows is a summary of the claims, prepared for the almanac's files and filed beside the pressed cedar frond.

The invention is distinguished from earlier attempts — which turned out to be merely lines — in that it treats distance as a condition to be resolved rather than a fact to be endured. A sleeper at Vancouver is, by morning, a sleeper at Montreal; the Rockies, which the province keeps between its coast and its plains, become a matter of gradient; and the interior, which had hitherto been a place one passed through to get somewhere, becomes somewhere. The specification calls this the long delivery.

The Claims

  1. Claim 1. A railway, characterized in that it runs from Montreal to Vancouver without leaving the question of its own existence unresolved, and in that it is a Class I by the morning of its first run.
  2. Claim 2. The method of Claim 1, comprising a spiral of tunnels at Rogers Pass in the province of British Columbia, by which the train is delivered through the rock rather than over the rock, the spiral being wound, as the examiner notes, “one full turn, no more.”
  3. Claim 3. A method of keeping a province, characterized in that the province — British Columbia, 944,735 square kilometres of it — is kept between a Pacific Ocean and a range of mountains, with the invention threaded between them, and with the interior left, as the specification puts it, “to keep its own time.”
  4. Claim 4. The method of Claim 1, further comprising a winter run up the Nass River toward the village of Gitlaxt’aamiks, where totem poles stand in the village square and the winter salmon run is counted in tides and feasts rather than hours, the timetable there being the river’s own.
  5. Claim 5. A kettle, a crock of vinegar, and one sentence found in the margin, substantially as described in the specification and shown in Figure 3, the sentence being transcribed word for word because the office does not copy from memory.

The Figures

Figure 2 of the complete application is a drawing of the seven provinces in which the track lies, reduced to one cedar frond per province, pressed and filed. Figure 3 is the margin page itself, described in the Practical section below, where the examiner's sentence appears.

Practical

As a specification must be workable, the office records one sentence from the examiner's margin of the field copy, page 4, beside a pressed cedar frond and the tide log of the Nass River halt. It is transcribed word for word, underscores and all, because the sentence is what it is and the office does not improve its exhibits:

Practical Go between November and February, when the sugar until it thickens, _but do not cease at its highest.

Read against the rest of the file the sentence resolves itself. The examiner had the almanac's British Columbia field guide open on the desk: its Practical page says to go between May and September for the coast, and its sister guidebook says the same of November and February for the desert stop at Ekeschi, so “Practical / Go between November and February” is the note of a hand deciding, mid-claim, which season to believe. The sugar is the syrup on the specimen label — the office keeps its pressed specimens in a thin coat of it — and the instruction to cease not at the highest is the examiner's quiet amendment of the claim itself: do not stop the line at its highest point. Deliver through the spiral, one full turn, no more.

Disposition

Application No. 4,114 is allowed as filed, subject to the note above. The applicant is required to maintain the line, the spiral, the seven provinces, and the river at Gitlaxt’aamiks in working order, and to keep one province that still keeps its own time. Renewals are filed every fifty years; the first renewal, 1931, is on file, and the second, 1981, is the one in which the railway agreed to be called CP Rail for a time, and the third, 2001, is the one in which it took its present shape as legal owner. The specification notes, finally, that the invention has never once arrived, which is the only way a line such as this can be kept in force: the long delivery is never completed, and the margin sentence confirms that the office is satisfied the reason is not negligence.
