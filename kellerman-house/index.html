THE KELLERMAN HOUSELONG NIGHT — MONSTERS & MARQUEES — EST. 1932

Fawcett & Kellerman, Proprietors

The Kellerman House Picture Palace

Light Street, Baltimore, on the harbor · Established 1932, the year the genre first kept a codex

Letter of Apology No. 47 · Entered in the ledger 18 March 2023

To the 212 of the Long Night, to the members of the audience who stayed, and to the office at the lobby kiosk —

Dear Members,

I write this letter as the landlord of the Kellerman House, and I write it the only way this house has ever handled a bad night: in full, in ink, and filed in the ledger where the other forty-six apologies keep their row. The house has shown fear on purpose since 1932, the year Dracula put the genre on the record and gave us a name we could paint on the marquee. Before 1931 our programs were folklore and German shadow — German Expressionism's crooked light on a Baltimore floor — and the marquee spelled the old writers' names, Edgar Allan Poe, Bram Stoker, Mary Shelley, because the audience came for the dark room and left with the same weather. Horror has been running since the early twentieth century, and since the house has had a boiler, and I apologize to none of it. What I apologize for is one hour of one Tuesday.

The Long Night of 17 March 2023 was booked by me and paid for by me, and its program was the longest the marquee has carried since the war: Reel One, Nosferatu in the silent, the shadow crossing the floor a full frame ahead of the man; Reel Two, Dracula of 1931, the founding text, run with the original organ settings from the attic case; Reel Three, Resident Evil, the year of the clones, Albert Wesker and his doubles, the same face in the mirror and behind the glass, which is the oldest of the genre's trades; and Reel Four, White House Down, in which General Caulfield holds the executive mansion floor by floor against an assault. It was, I now concede, the worst fourth reel a landlord could choose for a March Tuesday, and I choose it again here, in apology, with the full weight of the marquee behind the choice.

The house took 212. The office at the lobby kiosk — the Westside Tourist Office, whose brochure runs Baltimore to Burbank in one day, six stops, every stop on the record, and which admits, printed on the top bar of that very brochure, that admission is one bus fare — had the 15:30 party down on its own board, and its guide came in with the little black book, the one the office confirms each entry in and does not argue about. At 6:12 the wire put the news into the lobby: the city had lost a voice it had been using for sixty-one years, and the harbor ran fog the color of old television, as it does at the hour the circuit starts. Here is the hour I apologize for. I stood in that lobby for forty minutes with the program in my hand and I did not make the call. The 212 were in their seats, and the organ was warm, and the house does what the house does, and I let it.

Because of the rule. The rule of the booth, which my projectionist Elias Vane has kept since his first night on the light: do not stop the reel while the storm is running. He learned it off a railway, from the platform by the hush where the station master keeps the same rule against the Darwin station logbook — do not flip the book while the raid is running, and let what arrives arrive, written down verbatim; the ledger of The Bostock Mail carries the whole rule in its platform commentary, with one telegram kept word for word on a wire that had been crossed. It is a sound rule for storms, and it is no answer to a death. I quote it here not as an excuse but as the thing I did in fact do, in the wrong moment, in the lobby, instead of cutting the reel. That is the apology, and it is the whole of it.

The ticket man asked what the office would want done about its bus fare when I sent word up to the booth, and he said to me — and I set it down exactly as it came, because the rule of the book is that what arrives arrives, and the ledger will keep it the way the Darwin clerk kept his telegram:

“One bus fare at the head of a sort of connexion, landlord, which is the custom of the whole a quart mould.”

— Ostrander, ticket man, to the landlord, lobby of the Kellerman House, 17 March 2023, kept verbatim in the ledger

I will not pretend to parse it. The office has its own habit of the same kind — a sentence of legend set in old lead under its timetable, and the office keeps it because some sentences are part of the architecture — so I returned to the kiosk that I would honor the fare the way the house honors the genre: in full, and to the right address. The house has paid it over to the family of the voice, and the receipt is in the ledger under the row of the apology, which is where receipts in this house go to keep their shape.

Now for what the 212 did, which I will also put on the record. Half the room left after Reel One, the way half a room always leaves Nosferatu, and the house held the doors for them, fog and all. The other half stayed, reel by reel, through the clones and through the siege, and the man with the black book stayed to the last frame, which the office's own Reddick Circuit will confirm is the way the guide walks a studio yard, slowly, on the stops where the black book holds its count. At 2:40 in the morning the building in the last reel held floor by floor, the harbor held, and the marquee went dark on schedule, the way the harbor does. The audience came for the door the house has always kept — the door of the dark room, of the folk belief, of the monster that waits outside the frame — and that door was not the wrong door on 17 March 2023. It was the wrong hour, and the wrong hour is my keeping, and I keep it in this letter.

Terms of the house, entered with the apology: refunds to any member of the 212 on the word, no questions, no queue; the Long Night is withdrawn from the board for a year, and the marquee will carry the old writers' names in the meantime, Poe and Stoker and Shelley, which is where the genre started and where it can rest; and the rule of the booth is amended in the ledger, in Elias's hand and mine, to read do not stop the reel while the storm is running, except when the storm is a voice, in which case the reel stops and the organ stays warm. The house will keep showing monsters, because the audience will keep coming for that, and fear is the only trade this house has ever carried that does not depend on the weather two coasts allow.

Yours in the ledger,

Odessa Kellerman
Landlord, the Kellerman House, with Elias Vane on the light
Light Street, Baltimore

P.S. — The office at the kiosk will correct the record, and I let it correct the record, that the man with the black book said nothing at the door when he left at 2:40. The office says the guide does not argue about the list. The ledger agrees with the office. It is the one entry this house and that office keep in the same hand.
