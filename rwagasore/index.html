The set number is not a coincidence. The year is the set’s number. No. 62, which should have been the same set with the box left open, was withdrawn before it was boxed and the paper used for the No. 63 wrapper.

The subject

The set depicts Prince Louis Rwagasore (Kirundi: Ludoviko Rwagasore; 10 January 1932 – 13 October 1961), Burundian prince, founder of the cooperatives of the lake shore, and the second prime minister of Burundi, who held the office for fourteen days, from 28 September 1961 until his assassination on 13 October. He was born in Belgian-administered Ruanda-Urundi to the Ganwa family of Mwami Mwambutsa IV, the king whose court the set reproduces on the far side of the base. He was educated in Burundian Catholic schools before crossing to Belgium for university, and he returned to the colony in the mid-1950s with a plan the administration did not follow: a chain of cooperatives, one for the fishermen of Bujumbura, one for the brewers, one for the cattle, each with its own ledger, its own stamp, and its own line of customers that did not stop at a chief’s gate.

The cooperatives, 1956–1961

Three of the forty-nine figures stand at cooperative desks, and each holds a real ledger, forty-eight pages, printed at the works in the hands of the original bookbinders. The fishermen’s cooperative was the first to register and the last to forget: by the winter of 1957 it moved more dried nyemwaga off the Bujumbura beach than the European traders’ combined, and the political reading of that fact began before the first meeting ended. The Belgian administration, which had been watching the venture with the particular patience of a landlord, took it over — the premises, the ledgers, the stamp — and in doing so handed Rwagasore the most useful object a founder can be given: a grievance, in writing, dated. The cooperative figures in the set wear the look of men reading that document for the second time.

The fourteen days

The centre of the base is the prime minister’s week, or the part of it that survives. On 28 September 1961, as the colony moved toward independence, Rwagasore was named the second prime minister of Burundi; the first figure of the set, in the green-and-white coat, is seated at the desk on that morning, signing the paper that made him the man who had to sign every other one. The rail head at the base’s edge shows the 10:14 from the lake terminal, the train that ran the prince between Bujumbura and the court at Gitega, its two carriages printed with the cooperative’s stamp, which the works have kept because a prime minister who had built his support on bargains would have ridden nothing less. Between 28 September and 13 October the set records markets, councils, one fishing boat returned to the beach, and a desk lamp. On the night of 13 October the lamp is on and the room is empty of everyone except the lamp, and the box was printed to say so: CLOSED 13 OCT — 13 OCT.

The lake stone and the rail head

Every No. 61 carries one hand-carved lake stone, grey, about the size of a thumb, cut from the Tanganyika bank at the works’ quarry and filed until it holds the shine of the water. The stone sits in its drilled socket beside the rail head, and the collector’s manual gives it a single rule, kept word for word from the quarry foreman’s card: “By rail with the rock tells you where the charts run out.” The manual explains the rule in a paragraph and then gives up; the foreman explained it once, to a surveyor, by putting the stone on the platform and pointing at the last mile of track before the line met the lake, and the set’s charts are printed to that line — the surveyed country, the rail head, the carriages, and then water, marked only in the shade of the stone’s socket. What is not charted is the lake itself: the set will not sell a chart of the Tanganyika, and the works suggest the buyer learn it the way the fisher figures in the set learned it, standing, with the rock in the hand.

Contents, figure by figure

Figures 1–7: the Ganwa court at Gitega, with Mwambutsa IV’s regalia in the display window of the box. Figures 8–19: the three cooperative ledgers, the fishers, the brewers, the cattle-men, and the clerk who kept the second set of books. Figures 20–31: Bujumbura on the week, the market of the 29th, the terminal of the 30th, the council of 4 October, the boat coming in on the 9th. Figures 32–44: the Belgian administration through its window, watching, patient, taking over the stamp. Figures 45–47: the prime minister walking, the prime minister signing, the prime minister with the lamp. Figures 48–49: the platform and the surveyor’s table, stone in the socket, charts running out at the waterline. Figures 50–52 are unpainted, and the box does not say what they are for; the works will say they are the buyer’s.

Condition of sale and the shelf

The set is sold from the Kigali works as boxed, stone and all, and a buyer who returns the lake stone without the rail head is asked to explain the rule before the refund. For buyers who keep the miniature shelf in order: the works file No. 61 beside Campaign Set No. 63, the Roman–Parthian War of 58–63, another set whose central figure held his frontier for a single winter and outlived it in the box, and beside the Seven Brothers Line timetable, 1871, whose literature-day special of 10 October is the nearest thing in the catalogue to a train that runs on a date that mattered. The front shelf is the Webstuffs front page, where the lake shelves are kept in the order the works printed them.

The prince the set depicts returned from Belgium in the mid-1950s to build the cooperatives; the administration took the first one; the ledgers were dated; the rail head ran on; and on the morning of 14 October 1961, a day the base does not show, the box was printed, the stone was socketed, and the charts were allowed, for the last time in the set, to run out at the water. The set stands on the lake shelf in the shade the stone makes. The fourteen days are all it has, and they are all it needs.
