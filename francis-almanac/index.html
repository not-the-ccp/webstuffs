Entry No. CXII The Umbrian Almanac of Weathers · 12th Year

Francis of Assisi

Francesco d’Assisi · Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone · c. 19 August 1181 – 3 October 1226 · Italian mystic, poet and Catholic friar · founder of the Order of Friars Minor, commonly the Franciscans

  • Observatory the hill above Assisi, on the ridge of the Umbrian valley
  • Span kept 45 years, from August 1181 to October 1226
  • Instruments one stone, one ledger, one lamp, the bells of San Pietro
  • Reviewer the Court of the Almanac, sitting in the old granary

I. The Subject, as Weather

The almanac keeps weather, but the Court has ruled that a life can be weather, and has ruled it twice, and this entry is the second time. Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone was born in a warm August front, about 19 August 1181, in a wealthy house in Assisi, in the Upper Valley, where his father dealt in cloth and the price of drapes was, by the household’s own measure, a kind of pressure reading. The house kept a high pressure for twenty-four years: the boy was a southerly surge of the market, out among the goods, singing, the east wind of him at Perusia in 1198 and again in the years that followed, when the campaign came and went and the boy came back to a cell in the Roccetta, the little rock on the edge of town, where his own house had put him and he stood at the wall, dry and cold, and said nothing for the length of one whole season. It is the only time the Roccetta is ever recorded at still air.

The turn came in 1205, when the cold front went through the house and out of it: the cloth unsold, the goods returned, the price he had charged the market given back at the door of the cathedral, and a life of poverty and religious devotion beginning in the lowlands, where he took to repairing the little ruined chapels around the town — first the Porziuncula, that small chapel of the Angels, which he rebuilt wall by wall — and caring for the sick, who were, in the winters of those years, the worst of the weather, and the ones who never left. Soon the wind gathered others to it: a band of followers in the city who took the lifestyle up, nine at first, then twelve, then the count stopped being kept, which the almanac notes as an overcast of the good kind.

The high of the entry is the great settled pressure of 1209: a journey to Rome, an audience with Pope Innocent III, and the Rule approved in a single night’s dark, unspoken and understood, so that the Order of Friars Minor — the Friars Minor, the little friars, later and commonly the Franciscans — had a rule before it had a name. By 1213 the first rule was down in writing, eleven verses; by 1219 the subject crossed east to Damietta and stood, unarmed and unafraid, in the camp of the Sultan of Egypt, a calm of no recorded wind; by 1226 the order ran to a thousand friars across the valleys of the world, the widest pressure spread this almanac has ever put under a single name. The last reading is 3 October 1226, in the small hours, passed through quietly, the pressure finally set. The Court will allow the almanac one figure for the event: he was canonised on 16 July 1228, two years and nine months on, by Gregory IX — the fastest settling the Court has verified, and it has verified it twice, the first time with the bell and the second time with the ledger.

III. The Corrections Ledger

The almanac is corrected by the Court, which sits in the old granary and keeps one ledger of everything it has ever locked and everything it has never once locked. The young Giovanni was locked in the Roccetta for a year, and the rock has kept, since, the numbered places: the stones along its walk, one after another, the way a gauge keeps its marks, so that anyone who has been inside the rock can stand on the third stone and know exactly where the year ended and the season did not. The ledger bears one line about the locking, and the Court reads it verbatim, in full, word for word, whenever a member asks which side of the wall the corrections stand on:

“Corrections to this Court will not be locked against us, so that the rock tells you where you are in their numbered places.”

Which is to say: no correction is ever held inside a wall from the almanac, and no reading of the Roccetta is withheld, so that the stones keep telling the number, and the number, which in this case is 1203 and never anything else, keeps telling the year. The Court finds the line consistent with the weather and with the man, and has put it under both.

IV. Companion Readings

The brass staff bolted to the wall of De Groote Mühle at Freepsum reads its minus 3.44 the way the Roccetta reads its numbered stones — a mark on the ground, read by anyone who comes to look, corrected by nobody — and the Ditch-Watch Society has pinned one copy of this entry in the wheel-house beside the staff, where the two of them read the weather together, and no better. The valley below the hill keeps its own count of the four and the four and the six and the one, on an eight-stop daytime walk beside the Secchia — The Mustard Walk — and the almanac has ruled that a flower code that is counted, not guessed, is a weather record of the first kind.
