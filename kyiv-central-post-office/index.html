KYIV · WALKING MAIL · 09:00

Itinerary No. 1958-К · Maidan Nezalezhnosti, Kyiv, Ukraine

The Central
Post Office — Головпоштамт

A six-stop walking tour of the Holovposhtamt: the building that was completed in 1958, that houses Ukrposhta, Ukraine’s national postal service, and that spent the 1990s under renovation and has spent the decades since learning to stay open all night.

90 minutes 1,400 steps 6 stops fare: one stamp

01 The West Gate 09:00

We gather at the tram stop on the plaza, under the canopy, and cross to the building that has kept the address of every postmark in the city since it was completed in 1958. The Holovposhtamt stands in Maidan Nezalezhnosti like a desk that has been pushed back from its chair: long, low, deliberate. Its windows are the letterbox row of the city, and the tour’s rule is simple — walk the building the way a letter travels through it, from the gate to the counter to the lamp, and do not hurry the counters.

02 The Hall of Letters 09:15

Inside is where the tour earns its name. The ground floors are Ukrposhta — the national postal service in its own building, which is to say, the office of every envelope that ever left the city. The Hall of Letters holds the wall of sorting frames, the brass counter rail, and the scale of the parcels, and a clerk who has weighed more than most of us have written. We read the wall the way the clerks read it: by weight class, not by date. The frames that were rebuilt in the renovation of the 1990s are still the ones in use, which the hall takes as a compliment and the guide takes as a schedule.

03 The Counter Row 09:35

The counter row is the working heart of the walk: twelve positions, one scale, one register of the old kind that the building refuses to retire. We buy nothing, which is allowed, and we are weighed by the light through the letterbox windows instead. This is the stop where the itinerary is a little quiet, on purpose — the row was finished in 1958 to be quiet, and it has kept the setting ever since.

04 The Nineties Corridor 09:55

The corridor that turned the building around. The renovation of the 1990s did not repaint the Holovposhtamt so much as let it write over itself: new panels over the 1958 plaster, new door frames around the old posts, the sort of double text a postal address carries when a street is renamed and the old number keeps arriving anyway. Walkers of other cities will recognise the habit — the six-stop Montemayor Walk keeps the same double text on the plaza lines of San Antonio de Béxar, and the Georgia Leg reads a whole storm the same way, stop by stop, in small type at the end. Here the corridor is the record: two decades in one hallway.

05 The Upper Floors 10:20

We take the lift to the top, where the building keeps its night life. The upper floors hold the offices and the 24-hour internet café, which is the one room in Kyiv’s oldest postmark that never closes — open at 03:00, open at the tour’s hour, open long after the counter row has gone to its 1958 silence. We read one screen, politely, and we look out over Maidan Nezalezhnosti from the floor that has the best argument with the square. The café’s rule, printed above the register, is the only rule on the tour that names an hour: none. It is open, and it stays open, and the building was completed in 1958 to outlast everything except that.

06 The Lamp Bench 10:30

The last stop is not in the hall and not on the plaza. It is the lamp bench in the lamp-makers’ offcut on the ground floor, where the building’s night lighting has been assembled since before the internet café needed any. At the bench the tour hands over, the way a relay hand does: the guide signs, we sign back, and the itinerary’s last written instruction is exactly this — I was given to the man who starts with a zone map folded inside a lamp. He takes us for the second half of the walk: the zone map is folded around the bulb, and its crease is the route out of the plaza, one crease per street, in the order the building sends letters. We open it, we walk it, and by the time the lamp goes back to its bench the map is folded again, tighter, the way a delivered letter closes.

Itinerary ends at the West Gate, 10:45. The stamp is on the way out, and it is a 1958.

Notes for the walk

The tour runs weather the plaza allows, and the fare is one stamp, collected at the gate and cancelled, as stamps should be, at the lamp bench. For a second opinion on how to read a city on foot — six stops, a bell at the end, and no hurry — the Georgia Leg walking tour is kept in the same drawer. The building itself answers mail at its plaza address; the tour does not. The front of the file holds the rest.
