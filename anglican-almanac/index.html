The system, as observed

Anglicanism, also known as Episcopalianism in some countries, is a Western Christian tradition which developed from the practices, liturgy and identity of the Church of England following the English Reformation, in the context of the Protestant Reformation in Europe. The station has watched the system develop for over four centuries and half a century of its own record, and the present reading is this: it is one of the largest branches of Christianity, with around 110,000,000 adherents within the Anglican Communion, and approximately 2,400,000 outside of the Communion, worldwide as of 2025. Adherents of Anglicanism are called Anglicans; they are also called Episcopalians in some countries — the almanac notes the local usage and does not argue with it, which is a policy it keeps for every foreign sky. Most are members of the national or regional ecclesiastical provinces of the international Anglican Communion, one of the largest such bodies anywhere in the almanac's coverage, and the provinces report to the station in turn, the way provinces do, each with its own barometer and its own weather vane pointed at the same cross.

Fronts and squalls, historical record

The almanac's own ledger of fronts, kept since the sky cleared in 1536 and the barometer was hung on the observatory wall: the English Reformation front of 1534–1540, when the break moved through the country and the old pressure fell in a single season; the Prayer-Book squall of 1549, Thomas Cranmer's first book, which came down quickly and startled the whole province; the ordinal and vestment squall of 1552, the second book, which the station records as drier than the first; a short low in the middle of the 1550s, named in the ledger after the queen and cleared by 1558; the Elizabethan high of 1559, which set the weather as it has largely stood since; and the 1662 settled high, which the almanac still lists as the controlling system — persistent, mild, and inclined to outstay every forecast made against it. Between the squalls the tradition kept its via media, the middle road, which is the station's name for a sky that declines to be either all sun or all weather.

The corrections ledger

The keeper of this station has kept the ledger of forecast errors since the first issue, and he settles every account in the ledger's single line, which the almanac prints, word for word, as the honest almanac must: “The rule runs both ways: what he did to you.” It is an old custom of tit-for-tat, the sort of weather folklore that outlives every forecast. The Yerevan court heard the same line — Case No. 1726 of 2024, Excerpt from the Verbatim Record — read into the record by a plaintiff over one table, one glass, and a cold lavash, and the court held it is not the law of the golden rule but an older custom, both parties in default. The almanac prints the ruling in the margin, keeps the line on the page, and charges no one anything, which is as far as a weather book can go. The line has since been read once more, in the same word-for-word manner, at the funeral table of the chair this almanac has tracked for 1,429 years; the In Memoriam of the Office of the Archbishop of Canterbury keeps that reading, and the ledger closes in the usual way. The ledger's one remaining account of its own is the Abbot low of 1611–1633, twenty-two years of the chair, and the station has set its apology to that weather down in its own form at An Apology to George Abbot, where the name is entered now beside the pressure, as it should have been entered in the first place.

Cross-station notes

From the Attica Ledger — Athens — In Memoriam, Final Entry — the club above the old bakery went for khash in Yerevan on Friday mornings, garlic and cold lavash, where the city is still cool; this almanac keeps the weather of that table on its own page: 6:40, the fountains of Republic Square still silent, the marshrutka drivers the only souls on the street, a pot simmered all night, which is the longest single observation in the whole record, and a lavash wind chill that no coat in the almanac's inventory has been able to answer. The station's own map of the route — from the fountains to the brandy house, through the Vernissage yard, to the card on the menu board — is filed in Yerevan — Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City, and the minutes of the Naga Listeners' Club, kept at sixteen frames per second, close every ledger this almanac opens. The Edendale lot, where the bit parts were waited on for forty years before it became a garage, reports fair: no casting, no callback, only the weather, and the weather is enough for the almanac, which has always been enough for the sky.
