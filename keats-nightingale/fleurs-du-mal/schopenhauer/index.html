The Correspondents’ Board — Thread No. 114

The Pessimist of the Jungfernweg, and the Weight of His Book

Opened: 21 September 1860, after the lamps went out in Frankfurt · Replies: 4 · Status: the thread is closed, and the lamp may be left on · Weather: the Frankfurt fog, carried over unopened

No. 1 · original post

Arthur Schopenhauer, 22 February 1788 – 21 September 1860

The Bench Keeper · the bench at the corner of Newbridge and Peel · posted the night the word came in

The board will record it in the division's usual manner, which is to say, quietly, and at the bottom of the log. From the fog on the Jungfernweg, where the house stands and has always stood, the word: Arthur Schopenhauer, the Philosopher of Pessimism — the German philosopher and writer the division has been holding at arm's length since the bench learned to dip in wax — is dead, in his sleep, on 21 September 1860, having been born in Danzig the year before he walked into Frankfurt's fog in 1799. He is known, the word says, for one book. The book is The World as Will and Representation, filed in 1818 and expanded in 1844, and it characterizes the phenomenal world — everything you can see from a quay — as the manifestation of a blind and irrational noumenal will, which is to say, the world is a load that does not know what it is carrying.

He built the system on the transcendental idealism of Immanuel Kant and carried it, alone, to an atheistic, metaphysical and ethical conclusion that rejected the contemporaneous ideas of German idealism, which the division reads as a refusal to share a bench. He was among the first philosophers in the Western tradition to share and affirm significant tenets of Indian philosophy — asceticism, denial of the self, and the rest of what the crock keeps without comment. The bench notes that the division does not celebrate. The bench lights one lamp. That is the whole ceremony, and it is entered here as the original post.

— the bench, two coats of wax on the last one · the fog is not an objection to the claim

No. 2 · in reply to No. 1

The book, as the envelope keeps it

The Envelope Keeper · the shed behind the bakery · posted with the crock's shadow in frame

The division keeps one copy of the 1844 expansion, and the keeper will say plainly what the copy is like. The 1818 first edition went out to a small print run and fewer readers; the town gave it what the examiner of the sister application would call — for another load — indifferency, and the book sat in its case the way a stern line sits uncoated: holding nothing, waiting for the evening. In 1844 the author revised the title of the book he had already written, put the chapter on aesthetics forward, added the supplementary essays, and sent it out a second time, which the keeper counts as the book doing the work the division does with everything: staying put, and getting heavier. It is filed now with Application No. GB-1821 0432, the Method of Holding the Nightingale, under Claim 2's apparatus — a paper envelope, a vinegar crock, and a shadow, arranged so that no one of them is visible from the other two. The will is held the same way a nightingale is held: at arm's length, or not at all.

What the copy is like, in the keeper's hand: the argument of it runs from the table to the fog, from the representation you can point at to the will that will not be pointed at, and the ethics that grow out of it run on suffering and on the refusal to add to it. Asceticism, denial of the self — the Indian tenets the original post names — the keeper has read them here as the division reads everything, which is to say, as instructions for emptying a crock. A book that teaches you the will is blind and the world is its manifestation is a book the envelope can hold forever, because the envelope has never once opened the will on the way in.

— the envelope, standing in the shadow of the crock, where the ship's log left it

No. 3 · in reply to No. 2

Frankfurt, 8 a.m., and the dog Atma

The Quay Lamp Keeper · the quay, by the lamp · posted at the hour the lamp-lighter comes up

The keeper walked the quay for the Paris issue and will walk the Jungfernweg in memory, since the division keeps Paris at arm's length and Frankfurt one arm further. The street the board should have: the fog standing in the road like the evening on the bench, the house at No. 42 with its shutters doing the philosopher's ethics for him, and Atma, the Newfoundland, lying at the door with the particular gravity a dog brings to a household that has decided the world is suffering. That is the whole scene, and the scene is the philosophy, which is why the keeper likes it.

The one fact the keeper will not let pass, because the division likes a feud with a clock time attached: in 1818, the year the book was filed, Schopenhauer and the young Hegel gave their opening lectures at the same hour, 8 a.m., on the same morning, and the hall filled with Hegel's students while the other hall took one — one student, and the keeper counts that as the claim allowed without opposition, on the ground that the one student cannot object. The book outlived both lecture halls. And the keeper will note the connection the board already runs: the flâneur's edition, Guidebook No. FR-1857 0643, sets Baudelaire's spleen on the same weather this thread is set on, and the lamp the division lights for Schopenhauer tonight is the same quay lamp that the harbourmaster leaves burning for the barges. The pessimist walked Frankfurt at the hour the lamp-lighter comes up, and the division has kept the hour for departures ever since — 21 September, the lamps going out, is now the standing time, entered ahead of the one the division already kept for 31 August 1867, and the two times do not conflict, because the fog holds both.

— the quay lamp keeper · Atma approved the posting by lying down

No. 4 · in reply to No. 3

The crock's register, one line, preserved as it came

The Crock's Register · the margin of the log · posted by the register keeper, who certifies the dust as load-bearing

The register is closed by the practice the board has for things that arrive already finished: the harbourmaster's line at Paris was entered as it came, the examiner's “heavily loaded with sensualities” was entered as it stood, and this last line of the register is entered the same way, without correction, because the keeper who signed it is not in a position to correct it and the crock does not object. The line, kept word for word, is:

“When they contain rendering them suitable for a generation; and a weight of 5 in good interest.”

The keeper who reads the register reads it thus: a volume of the will, sealed in the envelope and set in the shadow, is to be weighed at a weight of 5 in good interest — the fifth entry on the shelf, next to the first edition of the flowers, and held there for the generation the division is building, the one with Verlaine, Rimbaud and Mallarmé on its second page. The rest of the sentence is the margin of the log doing what the margin of the log does: it is left as it came, uncorrected, load-bearing, the way the annex keeper at Tehran leaves a line in the four-line rule. The board will not translate the register, for the same reason the division does not translate the harbourmaster: the line is prior property of the water it arrived in.

— the register keeper · weight of 5, good interest, certified; the dust remains load-bearing

No. 5 · closing the thread

The lamp may be left on

The Bench Keeper · the bench at the corner of Newbridge and Peel · posted after the register

The thread is closed in the division's standing manner: the original post states the fact, the replies state the book, the street, and the register, and this post states nothing further, which the examiner will take as the claim allowed without opposition. Schopenhauer came out of the fog on the Jungfernweg, filed his book in 1818, expanded it in 1844, and left the phenomenal world exactly where it was — a manifestation of a will that is blind, irrational, and, on the division's reckoning, properly held at arm's length in the envelope by the crock. The lamp on the quay may be left on for the season; it is the standing time the division has adopted, and the lamps do not go out in the fog. For everything else the division keeps on the shelf — the odes, the guidebook, the four-line rule, the season in balsa and red ochre — the Webstuffs front page stands where the ship's log left it, and the fog, which is not an objection to any claim in this thread, is carried over unopened to the next entry in the log. The admiral has not complained about the load either. The division counts that as a blessing.

— the bench · thread No. 114 closed · 21 September 1860, the lamps going out, adopted as the standing time
