Inventory of the French Republic and Empire — Lucca to Vienna, 1794–1815

Cream stock · gatefold · the record inside weighs what the crates weighed

BV − 1815

On the record

What you are hearing on this disc is a count. For twenty-one years, from the first campaigns in Italy in 1794 until the Congress of Vienna ordered the works home in 1815, French troops and officials took paintings, bronzes, gems, libraries and coinage from the conquered territories of the Republic and the Empire — the Italian Peninsula, Spain, Portugal, the Low Countries, and the banks of Central Europe — and wrote them into inventories so calm that the inventories read like weather. An unknown but immense quantity of art was acquired, destroyed, or lost along the way, by treaty, by public auction, and by seizures no one in Paris was authorised to make. Coins and objects of precious metal went first and in greatest weight: the Lisbon silver, the Milanese plate, the Mainz treasury — the money of the places, before the art of the places. This disc is the sound of that paperwork: the wax seals, the counting, the crate nails, and the second, slower counting of 1815, when the commission went through the same lists in the opposite direction.

The title uses the French, spoliations napoléoniennes, because the French said it this way in the treaties, and the treaties are the only witnesses who signed. The music, such as there is, is the creak of a convoy. There are no soloists. The soloists were carried.

Side A

  1. A1The First Consul’s Caravan (Lucca, 1797)4:12

    The opening move after the peace: the painted cartons, the bronzes, the first of the crates numbered in the old Brera hand. Dominique Vivant Denon, inspector of the arts, has signed for everything; the mules, on the tape, are doing the rest of the signing.

  2. A2Turin and the Royal Gallery5:03

    Sixteen days from the surrender to the first inventory in the old gallery: the ceiling canvases taken in place, still wet from the hooks. Jean-Baptiste Reynaud is on tape all the way through, in a voice that does not rise.

  3. A3Brera, Milan (1809)6:44

    The great room that will later be called the Brera, emptied to feed the new museum in Paris. What was not taken was priced, in this cut, for the auction: public sale, lots one through nine hundred and thirty-one, hammer falling twice on a single panel.

  4. A4Madrid, December 18083:58

    The capital opens under Joseph; the tapestry store and the royal cabinet are counted in one night, and a painter in the street — Goya, if the voice is his — is heard counting with them, which he would spend his next decade refusing to count.

Side B

  1. B1The Low Countries (1811)4:27

    The Dutch and Belgian collections arrive at the Louvre by barge and by rail; the crates are still stamped from the Amsterdam chandler. The inventory says “in good order.” The barge captain says nothing. He is on the disc.

  2. B2The Rhine (Mayence, 1813)5:51

    The Mainz treasury and the Nassau cabinet, taken as the line breaks. This is the longest cut and the least legible: half the tape is water, and the count under the water stops, twice, in the middle of a number.

  3. B3Lisbon (1810)2:39

    General Junot’s men in the royal treasury: the silver and the coins, the crown jewels wrapped in bank ledgers. Short, bright, and done before the rest of the disc has finished loading.

  4. B4Congress of Vienna (June 1815)7:00

    The return. The French commission goes through the lists in reverse and ships what the Congress names; roughly five thousand works cross back over the borders, and the crates come home in the same wood they left in. The last groove is the seal, pressed twice, the second time cooler.

From the runout

Let the needle play into the dead wax after B4. Etched there, in a hand the pressing plant could not match to any clerk on either side of the Rhine, is one line, kept word for word and unruled, the way a gap in a register is kept rather than patched over:

“So: what is the Rhine, and it does not say, and the memory of the Council of the Ship, 1975.”

No one at the plant wrote it, and no one at the plant reads it. We have set it beside the minutes of the Council of the Lantern, No. 43, the nearest council of anything on this shelf to a council of a ship, and beside Lot 27 of the Continental Divide sale, where the house proved that a river can be consigned, appraised, and described — which is, apparently, not the same as saying what it is. The Rhine on this disc stays, as the line says, unsaid: water under a count, and a memory from 1975 that no inventory before or after it can account for. The whole shelf, including the sleeve this record arrived in, returns to the front page, where the rest of the house keeps its other inventories.

Personnel. Recorded in the vault beneath the Brera stair, on the wax of the original crates, 1923–1971, transfers only. Inventories read in the flat clerk voice of the Republic, then of the Empire, then of the commission of 1815 — one voice, three governments, no soloists. Crates, seals and hammer strikes live. Master cut for cream stock at 33⅓; the disc weighs its own provenance. The stamp on the cover, Restitution due, Vienna, 1815, is a copy of the label that came on the crates going the other way.

Reprints. Brera Vault Records keeps the master, the crates, and the lists in both directions. Return this copy, if you must, to the lost-and-found desk on the front page; some records are unclaimed because the thing they describe has already gone home on its own crates.
