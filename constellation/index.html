Letters column · Thursday, 14 October · sector DR-7 · the editor reads every letter once, and no letter twice

Fig. 1. The relayman’s count of the evening stars over the sand bar, joined in the order he saw them. The seventh star is too dim to join, and is not joined. It has its own figure elsewhere, and its own minute.

To the editor of The Atlantic Line,

I write, as the station’s naturalist, on the subject of a constellation. A constellation is an area on the celestial sphere in which a group of visible stars forms a perceived pattern or outline, typically representing an animal, a mythological subject, or an inanimate object. I set the definition down in full at the top of the letter, the way I set it down on the first page of the notebook, because the difference between a pattern in the sky and a border on the sky is the whole of this question, and the register at La Caleta is not the instrument to draw it. The first constellations were likely defined in prehistory, which is a way of saying before anyone was paid to be wrong about them. People used them to relate stories of their beliefs, experiences, creation, and mythology; different cultures and countries invented their own, and some of those inventions lasted into the early twentieth century before today’s constellations were internationally recognized. The recognition of constellations has changed significantly over time: many changed in size or shape, some became popular only to drop into obscurity, and some were never written down at all, which is the station’s case for keeping everything written down anyway.

The old books give the count, and the count moves. Hipparchus worked from a list of about 900 stars; Ptolemy, writing down at Alexandria in the second century of the common era, fixed the figure the stations of the sky still argue about — 48 constellations, 1,022 stars, a catalogue of the visible. The modern demarcation is of 1930, when the boundaries were finally drawn along the lines of celestial equatorial coordinates, and the count settled at 88. Eighty-eight areas of the celestial sphere, every one of them somebody’s border, agreed by committee in a room I am not in, and held over the same sky the Taíno uprights were reading at 18°24′ north, long before 1930 had a way of being 1930.

At La Caleta the argument has a local turn. In Macoas cove the three uprights on the sand bar stand in a row and the middle one holds the December solstice on the line of the notch — azimuth 297, as the compass has it twice now, the second time by the relay dish on its mount, which has been standing in the same reading for sixty years without knowing it. Those three uprights are a constellation in everything but the title: a group of points, a perceived pattern, a border no committee has drawn because no committee has been asked. The gulls’ cairn at the headland, which I count at 1,879 because that is the number it gives and because a gull is not to be counted in either column, is the same pattern held on the ground instead of the sky, and the relayman counts it once a week by letter, in the register, which is where the cairn is kept. What the sky has of a name, the shelf has of a row.

I must mention, for the record, the name that came down the line at 16:41. The console printed a line that was not an error code, was not a checksum, and was not in the relayman’s hand, and the line carried the name Abell. The reader will find the family seated at a table in the Book of Etiquette, where the surname stands in for the forecast; I will only add what the cue sheet will not, which is that the name belongs also to a catalogue of galaxy clusters, and that a cluster catalogue, at the far end of the line, is the honest working name for the whole of the sky — the subject the liner notes call, in its full name, the other room. The dish took the whole Atlantic for the length of the line and no longer, and the length was one minute. A constellation held for one minute is still a constellation, the pattern is not cancelled by the checkout, and I say this to the canteen, whose ledger line I will not repeat, on the ground that it is kept word for word and so is this letter.

There is one housekeeping matter I wish to lay before you, because it was printed where the whole line can see it. In the register, under 13 October, the night relayman has entered, in his own hand, an order that has not been carried out and which I ask you to ask him, once and not twice, whether to carry out. I set it down exactly as it stands, for the manner of this line is to keep the line:

“Clean three of the Naturalist's Notebook , shelved against shelf seven and a half, beside the register, which takes the objects, the models, the specific question or field.”

Read the order the way the shelf stands and it is a sentence. Three leaves of the notebook want cleaning — the salt of the 9th of October, the minute of the 10th, and the entry on the astronomer of the 12th — and the notebook sits against shelf seven and a half, beside the register, which has lately taken, in the relayman’s way of taking, the objects, the models, and the specific question or field: the sky of sector DR-7, objects and all, entering the register by the minute. That the register takes the objects is right; the register is the instrument. That it takes the field as well is the question. An astronomer keeps his field outside the scope of the Earth, and a register kept against a shelf ought, I suggest to you, to keep its field outside the scope of the notebook, and I will carry out the cleaning of the three leaves myself in the morning, which is to say I have already cleaned them, and the order can stand, or can be cancelled, in the manner of an error code that was never an error code.

For the editor’s file: the notes the order concerns are shelved beside this question — the Astronomer liner notes, La Caleta Tapes LCT-48671, the nine tracks of the down hour, which ends on the minute and the name, and the Stone Sky field notes of the station naturalist, which end on the count — and the register they sit beside is Error Register No. 48,671, in the household archive at the front of the line. A constellation, it seems, is a group of things that keeps its shape because somebody keeps them together. The sky kept its shape in the cove for as long as the uprights stood; the shelf will keep its shape for as long as the register is counted once a week and not more. I am asked, in the manner of everything on this line, not to open the small wooden drawer. I have not opened it. The stars, for what they are worth, are all above it.

Yours for the record,
The Station Naturalist
La Caleta Coastal Station, sector DR-7 · 14 October · no line corrected
