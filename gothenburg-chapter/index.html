I. Of the city and its approach

Gothenburg — said GOTH-uh-burg in English, Göteborg in Swedish — is the second city of Sweden, after the capital Stockholm, and the fifth city of the Nordic countries. It stands by the Kattegat on the west coast of Sweden, four hundred kilometres from Stockholm, and is the gubernatorial seat of Västra Götaland County, with some six hundred thousand souls in the city proper and about eleven hundred thousand in the parishes and waters about it.

The city was founded by King Gustavus Adolphus by royal charter in 1621 as a heavily fortified, primarily Dutch, trading colony. In addition to the generous privileges given to his Dutch allies during the ongoing Thirty Years’ War, e.g. tax relaxation, he also struck a new mint on the crown’s canal, reserved the herring tithe for the crown, and commanded that every house within the ramparts be built of brick, lest fire take the town as it had taken so many German cities. The first governor was Bengt Gabrielsson Oxenstierna; the streets were laid out in the Dutch manner, with canals instead of sewers, and the city grew around the anchorage where the East Indiamen rode at anchor.

A visitor approaches by water, or by the great road from the east, and passes the gate under the king’s own gaze: the bronze Gustavus Adolphus on Gustav Adolfs torg points his staff down the canal toward the playhouse. It is good manners to raise the hat when passing the statue, and to pause at the bridge until any sedan chair coming the other way has crossed. The city is fortified, and the gates close at nine; he who is abroad after the hour knocks at the guardhouse, not at the door of his host.

II. Of cards and the hall

No call is made without a card, and no card is left without thought. The calling card bears the name, the street, and nothing else — no verse, no motto, no account of the voyage. On arriving in Gothenburg, the visitor leaves one card at the hall table of each house he intends to honour, and waits for the household’s own card in return before calling again. A card is never left twice in the same week without an invitation between, and a lady’s card is never left at all unless she has been previously visited.

The hall of a Gothenburg house is commonly paved with Dutch tiles — Delft blue, with a tulip or a windmill in the corner — brought west as ballast and laid east as courtesy. Wipe the boots on the mat before stepping onto the tile; leave the greatcoat, the sword, and the umbrella with the attendant; and do not ask to see the upstairs rooms until invited. The card case stays in the hand until the host speaks first; a case opened too soon is a ledger opened before the audit.

III. Of the canals and the ropewalk

The canals are the streets of the Dutch town, and they are treated as streets: one walks beside them, not over their railings; one does not wash linen in the Crown’s canal, nor throw refuse into the moat. A footbridge crosses the Stora hamnkanalen beside the customs house; give way to the man with the lantern, and to any sedan chair. Dogs are not permitted on the bridge at all — not even the small ones, not even carried.

Beyond the ramparts lies the ropewalk, where cordage is spun for the navy and for the East India Company’s ships: a straight walk of a quarter of a mile, the ropewalkers stepping backward as the strand is laid. The tar smells of pine and the sea. Do not stand at the end of the walk when a cable is being coiled, and do not offer the ropewalkers assistance — they keep their own rhythm, and a rope laid in haste parts in a storm. The East India House stands by the great canal: ochre brick, a cupola, and the Company’s flag above the door. Enter only on business, and leave a card with the clerk before asking after the Canton tea.

IV. Of the table, the butter, and the yolk

Gothenburg dines late, by candlelight, and the Dutch custom holds that fish comes first on Fridays, herring with rye bread and a thin scraping of butter — one scraping, no more. The butter is taken with the knife, never with the fingers, and the butter boat is passed to the left. A guest does not reach across the table for the salt; he waits, and the salt finds him.

Among the papers of the Comediehuset’s box office there is a clipped slip, in no hand that the bookkeeper recognizes, headed with nothing and ending with nothing, which reads:

Fearing in his hand, a clipping or a little butter, the yolk of 1 oz.

No gloss survives, and we do not supply one. Read it as the maxim it plainly was meant to be: hold the clipping lightly, take the butter sparingly, and let the yolk run where it will — which is to say, do not grasp at what slips through the fingers, and do not drown good bread in fat. The etiquette of the table is mostly the etiquette of restraint.

When the pudding arrives, the guest does not comment on its texture. He comments on the weather, or on the play, or on nothing at all. The host drinks the first glass; the guest drinks the second, and stops when the host stops.

V. Of the playhouse

The Comediehuset stands beside the canal, the first permanent theatre in Gothenburg, rebuilt by Johan von Blanc for four hundred seats, with oil lamps and wax candles in a chandelier of his own devising. Von Blanc, born Michel Le Blanc in France in 1738, actor and acrobat, directed the house from its opening season in 1780 until 1786, playing Harlequin and the jealous husband, the acrobat and the lover, sometimes in the same evening. Sailors from the East Indiamen, merchants, officers of the garrison, and apprentices sat together in the dark and laughed at the same jokes.

Conduct in the pit is the true test of the visitor. Speak only between acts, never during a speech; eat nothing noisier than a biscuit; bring no dogs, no whips, and no noisy friends. A whip — even a Saltfleet whip, twelve feet of bridle leather made for the Lincolnshire shingle — is left with the attendant in the lobby, carefully numbered, and returned when the curtain falls. When von Blanc died at Karlskrona on the twenty-first of January 1796 — “a fever of the chest,” the burial record says — the playbills at the Comediehuset were taken down and hung with black crepe, and the house stood dark for a week. Remember this when you are tempted to whistle in the gallery.

VI. Of departure

To leave Gothenburg is to leave a city of canals, of brick, and of Dutch tiles, and the visitor does so with one last card: left at the hall table of his lodging, with the hour of departure written plainly in the corner, so that the household knows he is gone and where he might be written to. Pack the tea caddy with care; the Company’s gunpowder green travels badly in damp. And whatever you do, do not carry the clipping with you — let it stay in the ledger, where the yolk of one ounce measures itself against nothing at all.
