The Bluegrass Tally

Field recordings of the 1876 United States presidential election in the Commonwealth of Kentucky — the counting, the horse country, the road from Cincinnati, and the twelve men in a raincoat

COMMONWEALTH TONIC SIDE A 4801 · 78

Recorded: the east chamber of the State Capitol, Frankfort, the fairgrounds pavilion, Lexington, and one farmhouse off the Harlan turnpike — November 7 to the morning of November 9, 1876.
Engineer of the horn: C. E. Vail, with his brass horn, three telegraph tickers, and the state canvass ledger, open.
Personnel: the county canvassing boards (as heard); a fiddle that will not be named; the telegraph to Richmond, until it went silent at 11:40 on the 8th; the rain.

I · What the record is

This disc was cut on the night of November 7, 1876, and the following night, when the Commonwealth of Kentucky went to the polls for the United States presidential election and chose twelve representatives — twelve electors, in the formal phrase of the statute — to the Electoral College, who were to vote in January for president and vice president. The horn sat where the canvassing boards sat. It caught them the way only a horn can: the tally clerks, the county clerks riding in with their sealed returns, the horse traffic outside the Capitol, the telegraph tickers on the windowsill competing with the rain for attention. It is not an album about politics the way a newspaper is. It is an album about a state that had just finished being cut down the middle by a war and had learned, in the years since, to vote that cut: secessionist counties, Democratic and horse-country, to the south; Unionist counties, Republican and coal-and-rail, to the north. The Civil War shaped Kentucky for not merely the rest of the nineteenth century but also the twentieth, and this record is the sound of the shape being measured out, county by county, before the ink dried.

When the count was done, the state had gone to Samuel J. Tilden of New York, the Democratic standard-bearer, by 127,152 votes to 124,203 for Rutherford B. Hayes of Ohio, with 4,944 scattered across the Greenback ticket of William E. McKenney in a manner that sounds in the master like a fiddle with one loose string. Twelve electoral votes to Tilden. The Republican Party had never carried Kentucky in this era, would not carry it in this era, and the record plays, on that fact alone, as elegy and as weather.

II · The slate, as heard

On Twelve Men in a Raincoat the Democratic slate of electors is read by a voice the clerks identified afterward as that of Judge A. G. Hager of the Fayette canvass, and the master preserves it in the only form the record had room for — the whole of it, without the county-by-county arithmetic that the printed canvass later supplied. It is a folklore artifact that still works, which is the whole of it, and declines to itemise. The twelve names pass through like a string of lanterns — one for each of the state's twelve electoral votes, two per congressional district, the districts drawn on the population returns of 1870 — and the listener is left to hear, under the reading, the two counties of the south of the state where the returns had been held up a day for a lost signature, and the one northern county where a Republican clerk read his numbers into the horn out of pure contempt for the rain.

The names themselves, where the reading is clean, are given in the sleeve as a matter of courtesy to the men named: two from the first district, two from the second, and so on to the twelfth, each announced with his county and his office in it, a judge here, a surveyor there, a former colonel of the Union cavalry there who did not seem to mind which party carried him. The Republican slate was not cut into this master. It exists, per the canvass, in the printed canvass alone.

III · What the weather did

The telegraph to Richmond failed at 11:40 on the night of the 8th and did not resume; what the south of the state had been reporting that night is therefore heard only at secondhand, in the form of the clerk of Nelson saying, into the horn, that the figures were “as we received them,” which is either a quotation or a habit. The rain started at half past one and kept going until the canvass was certified, and the horn — being brass, being on the windowsill — picked up a good deal of it. This is not a defect. A state that votes a civil war is going to vote some weather. The same office, at another table and a hundred years later, kept an unparseable line of a route log word for word rather than guess at it: the field notes kept along Interstate 90, Entry No. 22 of the Continental Line, where a naturalist on the longest freeway in the country files one garbled line and lets it stand. This sleeve does the same to the 11:40 gap: it is left open in the middle of Side B, in B3, as a rest of roughly seven seconds that no one has explained and nobody is asked to.

IV · Colophon

Pressed from the wax master by Commonwealth Tonic Records, Lexington, under the number LP 4801. Matrix 4801-A is 24 minutes, 7 seconds; matrix 4801-B is 25 minutes, 53 seconds. The fiddle was never identified; the clerks declined, on the record. The state's twelve votes are Tilden's; the presidency, as B3 plays it, was not. Nothing on this record is corrected, re-cut, or annotated in pencil. It is a ledger that plays like a ballad and a ballad that refuses to give up its arithmetic. For a sister habit of keeping one studio line word for word in a broadcast log — a break-up that never makes it onto the record — see the KHTT session papers on Falling Down, Studio 2. The complete shelf, with the other pressings of the house, is at the Webstuffs front page.

— Pressed in a cold room off the Harlan turnpike, December 1876. Handle the horn-side up.
