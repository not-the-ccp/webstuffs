Prairie Grove, December 7, 1862 — a night fight on the high ground

Opening the thread where the board has wanted one since the Cane Hill thread went to sleep. Nine days after Blunt's division of the Army of the Frontier had beaten the Confederates at Cane Hill on November 28, 1862, and was still posting itself across the crossroads of northwestern Arkansas, the First Corps of the Trans-Mississippi Army under Major General Thomas C. Hindman was moving down the Walnut and Little rivers with the whole intention of catching Blunt isolated.

What saved the day was the road. Major General Francis J. Herron brought up two divisions of the Army of the Missouri — the 3rd and the 4th, with Sturgis's 1st Missouri Cavalry Division in support — and the two columns arrived in the prairie on the night of December 7 to find Hindman already posted on the high ground south of the settlement of Prairie Grove, in Washington County, about five miles north of present-day Eureka. So instead of a pincer they got a night battle: Herron's men attacking uphill in the dark and the rain, Hindman's line held in the cornfield and the hollow, and the fighting going on, in one officer's words, “until we were all tired of shooting in the dark.”

The result was tactically indecisive — neither army could be called beaten — but it settled the winter. Hindman pulled back toward Little Rock, and Union control of northwestern Arkansas was secure for the year: Blunt's division and Herron's corps straddled the crossroads that fed everything from Fort Smith to the White River. Casualty returns run roughly 400 Union against 700 Confederate, and the thread keeps the arithmetic on that below, because someone always asks.

Ground rules: bring the names. Blunt, Herron, Hindman, Sturgis, Crockett, Fagan, Crawford. Bring the places: Prairie Grove, the Walnut river, Big Creek, Fort Smith. Bring the date, December 7–8. If you bring “the fight up there somewhere,” the moderators have a form for you.

Re: the high ground

One correction before the arithmetic, please. Hindman was not defending Prairie Grove so much as he was refusing to be outflanked. Once Herron's two divisions showed themselves behind him on the prairie roads, the attack-and-defend had turned around: the army that had been the hunters for two weeks took the rifle pits and the corn stubble on the ridge, and the Union army — the bigger one — was the one climbing in the dark. That is the whole shape of the fight, and everyone on the field felt it turn when Herron's men could hear their own boots on the going-up and Hindman's artillery was already sighted on the coming-down. The high ground was worth more that night than the guns, and the guns were worth more than the numbers.

Re: Blunt's staff log, verbatim

For the record, a leaf from Blunt's staff log, the one kept by the quartermaster's clerk who had been at Cane Hill nine days earlier and would not sleep through another night on it. The clerk kept his entries in a numbered sequence through the whole winter, and the morning page after the fight reads, in his hand and without apology:

“Entry 191, set down here as they were right to.”

Yes, it is as strange as it looks. The clerk meant the numbers: the 191st entry of his winter book, and the line he had written — men counted off the ground, wagons accounted for, the retreat begun on the road to the Arkansas — which he and the officers around him had agreed was the right thing to write. The board keeps the line verbatim because the board keeps everything verbatim; the same rule the public domain thread argues for, that a document offered as found is offered as found, and parsing is the reader's problem. Entry No. 191 in the clerk's book is the thread's number here, which the archivist considers a coincidence worth exactly one follow.

Re: Sturgis and the left flank

Give Sturgis his line. While Herron's infantry was going up in the rain, Brigadier General George L. Crockett's Missouri cavalry under Colonel William W. Allen — and the 1st Missouri Cavalry Division that Sturgis was bringing up — turned Hindman's left on the far side of the prairie and brought two pieces of Union artillery down on the Confederate guns that had been doing the night's talking. The artillery duel on the ridge went until about four in the morning. Then Blunt, who had been fighting a rearguard action with a division while waiting for Herron, made the call everyone on this board pretends to have made: withdraw, in order, up the road, and let the winter do the rest. He was right, and the clerk's entry says as much.

Re: the arithmetic

The returns, as near as the winter books will come: Union losses in the neighbourhood of 400 — killed, wounded and missing together, with the 18th Illinois and Herron's 3rd Division taking the worst of the going-up. Confederate losses nearer 700, with Hindman's own divisions of the First Corps — the brigades of Fagan, Crawford and Walker — paying the price of the guns that fired all night. Add the weather, a hard rain that ran the rifle pits of the ridge into mud, and you can see why neither commander claimed victory. Blunt's official report calls the action “indefinite” with the calm of a man who has already decided his division will be the one that keeps the crossroads.

For comparison, if the board wants its old friend the Truceless War thread, Chapter VI: there the numbers were twenty thousand unpaid mutineers against a state, and the arithmetic ended in Utica. Here the numbers are a few thousand men in a prairie in December, and the arithmetic ends with a river, a fort, and a winter road. Different war, same ledger discipline.

Re: the night clock

Pinned this, because the night is the whole story. The clock below steps through the hours of December 7–8 as the board has reconstructed them from the reports and the clerk's log. It works best with JavaScript; without it, the events are listed in order above the board, which is the whole point of writing them down twice.

22:00 The prairie, west of Prairie Grove

Hindman's First Corps closes on the high ground south of Prairie Grove and takes the rifle pits in the corn stubble. Herron's two divisions arrive by the prairie roads and the Union column is now the one that must go up.

Hour 1 of 6

  • 22:00, December 7 — Hindman takes the high ground south of Prairie Grove; Herron's divisions arrive and the attack begins uphill in the rain.
  • 23:30 — The Union infantry is thrown back out of the hollow; Crockett's men and the coming of Sturgis's division turn the Confederate left.
  • 01:00, December 8 — Artillery duel on the ridge; two Union pieces brought down on Hindman's guns and firing through the night.
  • 02:30 — Hindman, outflanked on both flanks and his ammunition falling off, orders the line to ground itself; the Confederates begin to peel off to the south.
  • 04:00 — Blunt, holding the field but with a division and a night of fighting behind him, orders the orderly withdrawal up the prairie road toward the Arkansas.
  • 06:00 — Dawn over the wheat prairie: a field of dead, a river to the north, and the crossroads of northwestern Arkansas kept by the Union for the winter. The clerk writes Entry 191.

Re: what it bought

Last post before I log off, and it is the one that matters. Tactically indecisive, yes; the returns barely separate, yes; Blunt gave the ground he had taken, yes. But strategically it bought everything the Army of the Frontier was posted to buy: northwestern Arkansas stayed Union for the winter of 1862–63, the road to Fort Smith stayed open, and Hindman went home to Little Rock with a corps that would not be looking up at Cane Hill again that season. Cane Hill had been the fight; Prairie Grove was the winter. The wreath in this thread's title goes on both.
