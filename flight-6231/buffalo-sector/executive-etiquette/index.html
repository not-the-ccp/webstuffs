A Book of Etiquette for the Frontier Table · Chapter VII

Of the Executive

▪▪▪

Being the manners due to the office that executes, enforces, and signs the manifest — compiled at Sector Buffalo, Lake Erie Frontier, Erie County seat, 278,349 souls.

I · What the executive is

Begin the chapter the way every chapter in this book must begin, with the thing itself, before any manner at all. The executive is the part of the government that executes and enforces law and policy. The powers of the executive may vary depending on the specific government office — a county executive holds the county budget and a captain of a ship holds the ship — but the office itself is universal: all political systems have an executive, from democratic regimes to dictatorial ones. A frontier town therefore never lacks one; it only changes the title on the door. The etiquette in this chapter applies to the office, not to the name printed on the nameplate, and the reader who masters the office will not be embarrassed by a new nameplate.

II · Forms of address at the frontier table

At Sector Buffalo the executive appears in four coats, and the host is expected to know which coat is in the room before the soup. The mayor of the city is answered Mr. Mayor and offered the second chair, because the first chair belongs to the table itself. The county executive of Erie County, the county of which Buffalo is the seat, is answered Mr. Executive and stands at the threshold only when he chooses. The governor of the state of New York, when he passes through on the lake road, is answered Mr. Governor, is seated before the others are, and is not asked where the weather report lies. The federal executive, in whatever distant city, is answered by title alone and by dispatch, because the distance does the courtesy for him. The game manual for the sector, Game Manual FM-B, Plate 9 — Sector Buffalo, keeps the same four coats in its standing orders, and the two should be read together, for the manual counts the crews and this chapter counts the chairs.

III · The drill, and the manners of a broken dispatch

No chapter on the executive at this frontier is finished without the drill, because the executive’s first duty is to answer a question the law has already asked. The fragment stands in the sector log, keyed to the same flight number as the position run of 1 December 1974, and reads verbatim: How many crew members were aboard Flight 6231 U.S. Every new dispatch officer posted to Buffalo is set the question in person, at the table, with the soup still on. The answer is three — Captain Harold D. Prentiss of Minneapolis, First Officer Samuel K. Okafor of New Rochelle, and Flight Engineer Miriam T. Delgado of Cleveland — one captain, one first officer, one flight engineer. A count of anything else fails the inspection and the leg is not flown. The manners belong to the question, not the answer: a broken dispatch is read once, filed verbatim, and never corrected at the table. The night of it is kept in the drawer of Hudson Bend Secondary School as item 9R–H2, reported by Celia M. Brandt, No. 12, marked by Mr. E. Halvorsen, and the etiquette book has a standing note that a dispatch officer who “fixes up” the wording of a broken message in front of the room has failed the chapter before the first course. The term report from which the fragment is copied is filed at Northwest Orient Flight 6231, Form 9R, Stony Point.

IV · The margin fragment

In the margin of this chapter, pasted in by a former keeper of the table and never explained, there is a slip of coast-survey paper. It is kept here word for word, as the book keeps all fragments:

“The beach is mangrove-lined, at low tide the flats at low tide the flats run out for kilometres, and the fix on the far bank.”

There is no mangrove at Buffalo; the lake is open water and the near shore is fill. And yet the fragment stays in the margin, because the last phrase is the whole of the etiquette. Fix on the far bank is the standing rule of this sector, taken from the survey chapter of Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 — the geographic coordinate system, where the fix on the slope at Harriman is entered to the minute and the datum is re-checked before the board is trusted. An executive who keeps his fix on the far bank, whoever sits in the first chair, has not yet left the frame of the room. The repeated words in the middle of the slip, where the flats are named twice, are a fault of the channel and not of the coast, and they are left exactly as they came, the way the crew question is left exactly as it was keyed.

V · Standing orders of the table

  1. Address the office, not the nameplate; the coat changes, the manners do not.
  2. Answer the crew question before the second course: three, and the names with it.
  3. File a broken dispatch verbatim; correction at the table is a failure of the chapter.
  4. Keep the fix on the far bank — 42°53′N, 78°52′W, the border due north — and re-check the datum before you commit a bearing.
  5. Be back at the table before the lake turns.

The protocol ledger

Set the office in the room and your own place at the table, and the ledger gives the address, the steps, and the standing rule for the course.

The ledger is empty. Choose the office and your place, and enter.
