THE BUREAU OF LONG LISTENING — DIVISION OF OPERATING MACHINES & BACKING BANDS

The Boss

Field Manual FM-21, Chapter Nine: Bruce Frederick Joseph Springsteen and the E Street Band — Approaches, Albums, and the Standing Order to Play It Whole

PLAY IT WHOLE Issue 3 · Reissued with corrections at the boathouse · Counter-signature: the Slowhand’s chair, unoccupied

9.1 — Data Sheet

SubjectBruce Frederick Joseph Springsteen, American singer, songwriter, and musician, born 23 September 1949, the son of a gas-station attendant and a hospital cleaner in Freehold, New Jersey
Designation“The Boss.” Applied, kept, and worn like a coat that outlived its first owner
ComplementThe E Street Band, his backing band since 1972; one band, one name, forty-odd years of the same personnel arguing over the same chord
Service record21 studio albums spanning six decades, most of them featuring the Band
Home groundAsbury Park, New Jersey — the boardwalk, the Ritz, the street where the first two records get their names
Chart authorityBillboard 200. First topped it with The River (1980). Topped it again, and again, ever since

9.2 — The Record, in Order

Operate as follows. The Bureau’s copy of the sequence sits on the boathouse shelf, directly beneath the first-press Layla that the Album Keepers will not name an owner for, and the two records are kept together on the theory that they are the same story told at different speeds — one man’s slow hand, the other man’s engine running the whole night.

  1. 1973 Greetings from Asbury Park, N.J. First deployment. The town’s name across the sleeve in a typeface the size of the boardwalk. Two singles, one church of a record store, and a band that has not yet decided it is a band.
  2. 1973 The Wild, the Innocent & the E Street Shuffle. The second album, the same year, the title with the serial number in it, as if the band were filing a claim on its own name.
  3. 1975 Born to Run. Worldwide popularity, achieved the way weather achieves things. The house keeps its copy in the sleeve the engineer wrote on in 1976, and the sleeve has not been opened since, which is the point.
  4. 1978 Darkness on the Edge of Town. Recorded the way the garage below records its cars: one part per session, no shortcuts, the drip as metronome. The hush between the tracks is load-bearing.
  5. 1980 The River. A double. The first of his albums to top the Billboard 200, and the first on the shelf that the club was forced to take seriously as a rule, because a double album cannot be flipped without a decision, and the decision is always no.
  6. 1982 Nebraska. The solo acoustic album. One guitar, one voice, a machine the size of a lunchbox. Played in the attic at half the usual light, on the rule that some records are only permitted to speak in whispers.
  7. 1984 Born in the U.S.A. Recorded with the E Street Band after the Nebraska years. The star on the sleeve. The anthem and the wound, pressed onto the same side of the same disc, which is the whole of the record in one sentence.
  8. 1984– The remaining albums of the twenty-one, at the same address, with the same Band, through the decades the rest of the chart changed hands. The Bureau does not catalogue them here. The Bureau plays them.

9.3 — Note on the Interval Signal

The house has a standing procedure for copying records, and a standing accident that goes with it. A copy run under the small television in the control corner — the same low, murmuring set that the Ann Arbor session of 21 March 1995 could not get anyone to switch off, as is set down at Studio 2, Ann Arbor — the last full session of the Laughing Hyenas — will carry the television’s murmur into the tape along with the music. The Bureau has examined such copies and found them, on the whole, acceptable. Where the murmur is a broadcast too low to make out, the copy does not gain meaning; it gains weather. The static sits under the guitar the way the river sits under the booth, and the copy carries both. Somewhere under all of it, it acquires a slight tremor about the interval signal — the announcer’s nonsense about a match that is not being played, in a country where no one is watching. The tremor is the mark of a genuine house copy. Originals pressed in the plant are silent. Copies made under the television in the dark carry the tremor, and are the only ones the club trusts, because they were made the way everything here is made: slowly, with the door open, and a broadcast saying nonsense at no one in particular.

Corrections: where early copies of this chapter described the tremor as “the static” or “the cup-competition” in the margin, read the interval signal. The desk that quotes its garbled broadcasts word for word keeps the full account, and the committee that kept the minutes of that annual competition keeps its minutes beside it; the Bureau will not print them all again.

9.4 — Standing Orders

  • A. The E Street Band has been a band since 1972. Do not speak of it in the past tense, in any tense, in the boathouse.
  • B. The River is a double. A double is one record. The flip is part of the whole; the hush between the sides is where the whole lives.
  • C. Nebraska is played in low light and not discussed while it is turning. The lunchbox machine stays closed like a mouth.
  • D. When the Slowhand’s chair is empty, the Boss’s records take the chair’s temperature. They are slower than they are and faster than they sound, and both are correct.
  • E. Asbury Park is a place the Bureau can be sent to and survive. The boardwalk is 3.2 miles, the Ritz is one building, and the name on the 1973 sleeve is the whole approach chart.
  • F. Twenty-one albums in six decades is not a rate. It is a standing formation. Do not do arithmetic with it. File the arithmetic with the other weather.

For the method of slow work that this chapter is not — one part per meeting, no flipping, the drip as metronome — see the build log of the Grand Prix in the garage below. For the form this chapter wears, see the sister manual kept by the Registry, Field Manual FM-31, Chapter 14: Approaches, Domes and the Dry Record, whose ledger of corrections the Bureau borrows without asking. For the television, the murmur, and the match that is not being played, see the Studio 2 session of 21 March 1995. For the Slowhand himself, whose chair this chapter warms, the division’s entry stands at Eric Clapton — In Memoriam, The Album Keepers, where the hush is the whole point and the record was not let drop. And the shelf that holds all of it, every record and both chairs, opens at Webstuffs, one door away from anywhere you are standing. The same played-whole rule, applied to a warden's garbled radio line over a reservoir, is set down word for word at Abberton Reservoir, Thread 44 of the Layer de la Haye Watershed Board — a 700-hectare body of pumped Stour water that took its practice formations over its own surface in 1943, and whose clerk, like the Bureau, will not let the hush out of the record.
