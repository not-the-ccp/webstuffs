The Eugenic Ledger

Sunday, 19 July 1970 · the island house, Edgartown · one year after the night · the pond is still doing its reading

FOLIO RECEIVED: the Friday post of the 17th, one envelope, no return address · CONTENTS: three pages of a state ledger, one clipping of an 1883 definition, one line in small type · COUNTER: 30,117

The envelope

The folder came up in the Friday post the way the leaf came up for Chapter 14 — The Crime: no return address, no stamp of any office the keeper could place, and inside, three pages torn out of a ledger. The pencil on them is not the ferry master’s, though he has compared it against his own in the same pocket and will not say. The pages are a count. The top of the first page reads STERILIZATIONS PERFORMED UNDER THE ACT, STATE INSTITUTIONS, FY. TO DATE, and the figures run down the right margin in three columns — the names, the class, the signature of the attending physician — and the keeper has done the keeper’s one arithmetic on them, because he could not help it, the way a ferry master reads the gauge before he can say he is reading it. The column does not stop. He stopped counting at 30,117, which is one more than the number the mainland newspapers had printed by that spring, and he has left the extra one in the sum on purpose. The county in that row is the one whose clerk signs the transfer slips the island receives every August.

Also in the envelope: a clipping of a definition, in the hand of the man who first set the word down. The word is eugenics — the science, or the belief, of improving the genetic quality of a human population, bred by those who held it up to the light the way one holds a leaf to the window — and it was coined in 1883 by Francis Galton, which is to say the word is twenty-three years older than the discipline it is supposed to belong to. Genetics was only proposed as a name for the science in 1906. The movement that carried the word out into the world — the clubs, the fairs, the little medal for the deserving family, the state boards of examination that decided who was a family at all — began in the late nineteenth century, right after the theory of evolution and natural selection got its second wind, which is the one the manual files under weather. A thing that began as a word about weather, aimed at the whole population at once, and ended as a signature on a state ledger. The keeper knows the ending because the ending is on the desk.

What the ledger is for

The pages do not explain themselves, but the clipping does, and the clipping is short. The aim, it says, is to improve the stock of the population: to inhibit the fertility of those the ledger holds to be inferior — and the word in the margin, in the same pencil as the column, is unfit, used the way the manual uses wrong, as if it were a reading rather than an opinion — often through sterilization, done in the state institutions, under the state acts, with the state physician’s signature in the third column; and to promote the fertility of those the ledger holds to be superior, by means of a medal, a line in the fair program, and one small annual grant. The office that kept the master of the ledger sat on the harbor at Cold Spring Harbor, New York, from 1910, and its superintendent published a survey of the states in 1922 that did to the acts what the ferry master’s survey of the two waters does to the boat: it checked that both ledgers agreed before anything was allowed to go in. The first of the acts was passed in Indiana in 1907, and New York followed in 1912. The famous case went up from Virginia in 1927 — a girl of eighteen, a mother of one, and a state that asked the courts whether it might keep her from having a second — and the court, in the keeper’s hand and with no opinion of his, says yes. He writes the word yes once and does not cross it out.

The manual in Chapter 14, §14.3 draws the line the keeper is drawing now. The injury to the individual, it says, is paid out in the private ledgers — the family, the hospital, the ferry master’s one pocket — but the wrong itself passes into the keeping of the community. A public wrong is a wrong that has changed hands. The folder in the Friday post is the one case the manual has never had to read as a field example, because in it the hands that held the wrong were the state’s own: the act that changed the hands was the law, the instrument was the institution, and the signature in the third column belongs to the state as the ferry master’s pencil belongs to the gauge. The manual files it in both ledgers at once, as ordered, and notes, without opinion, twice, that here the second ledger is the same as the first.

The line it will not give up

Beneath the torn bottom edge of the third page, in the small type that does the work of a footnote, one line. The keeper has copied it into this diary exactly as it sat, without reading it twice, in the way a clerk copies a figure he does not check against the kettle:

No: but here we may safely be said, must be paid out in the state — a public wrong A public wrong A public wrong .

The manual gives the leaf one annotation and withholds all others, and the keeper gives the line the same measure. He has a reading of it, and he sets it down for the one who follows. The line is the ledger’s own note on its own account. The first word is No, which is the ledger’s answer to the private ledgers of §14.3: the injury to the individual is not what it is paying out, and it knows it. Then: must be paid out in the state — the disbursement this office makes is the state’s, from the state, into the keeping of the state, and the state is on both sides of the entry, which is the one arithmetic the manual had not needed until now. The keeper can safely be said to understand it, the line says, and the line is not wrong about that. And the last figure, the one he does not check: a public wrong, written three times, the way a folio is written three times in a ledger — in the statute’s column, in the institution’s column, in the signature’s column — not three wrongs, one wrong, entered in triplicate so that it would not be missed by the one who audits. The act of the actuary, in the manual’s terms, is the paying out; the entry in triplicate is the keeping. The keeper has struck the word paid out of his own summary with a single line, the way one strikes a figure that has been copied wrong, and under it, in the same hand, has written kept. It is kept. That is enough, for the manual, for anything.

The line of the years, 1883–1983

The keeper has set the ledger’s years in a single line below, the way the track list sets the night: one event at a time, in the order they sat. Move the needle to a year and the line will hold you there; without the moving part the line stands in full, which is how it is kept.

Held at 1927

  1. 1883 Francis Galton coins the word eugenics; the discipline it names does not have a name yet.
  2. 1906 Genetics is proposed as the name of the science; the word is now twenty-three years old and belongs to no one.
  3. 1907 Indiana passes the first state act of the kind; the column in the folder has its first row.
  4. 1910 The record office takes its desk on the harbor at Cold Spring Harbor; the master ledger begins.
  5. 1912 New York follows Indiana; the states begin to agree with one another, which is the survey’s whole point.
  6. 1922 The superintendent’s survey of the states checks both ledgers; the boat is allowed in.
  7. 1927 The Virginia case: the court says yes to the state. The keeper writes it once and does not cross it out.
  8. 1938 The word crosses the Atlantic and takes another meaning there; the manual files that under weather and says nothing about it.
  9. 1945 The other war ends; the states do not repeal. The column keeps its rows.
  10. 1974 The acts begin to be repealed, one state at a time, in the order the ledger does not remember them.
  11. 1983 The last of the state acts is struck; the folder in the Friday post is now the column itself.

Standing orders, added

  1. The folder is filed in both ledgers at once, and the keeper notes, twice, that the two ledgers are the same.
  2. Copy the line as it sat. Do not check the third public wrong against the first two. It is the same wrong, entered in triplicate.
  3. The 30,117th row stays in the sum. A column that is copied with one left out is a column the state has already paid.
  4. A word older than its science is a weather word. Read it as weather, and do not let the ledger change the reading.
  5. The entry is kept, not paid. Do not flip it.
