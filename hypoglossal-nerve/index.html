Hypoglossal Nerve

Cranial nerve XII, the twelfth current — read at Nethergate Head on the 63rd day of 1998, 2 March, and kept in the season book under its own name, hypoglossal, the nerve of the tongue.

First observation — the reading of the 2nd

Station: the pilot house at Nethergate Head, Humber Estuary · Date: 2 March 1998 · Hour: 04:10 GMT · Sky: star-strewn, no moon, good for a long record · Temperature: 24°F · Wind: north-west, 21 kt, gusts to 39 · Barometer: 30.02 inHg, firm and holding

The subject observed for this entry is the hypoglossal nerve — known in the trade as the twelfth cranial nerve, cranial nerve XII, or CN XII, and in most of the books simply as the twelfth. It is a nerve of a single, unshared character: sole motor. The almanac notes that it carries no weather in the other direction at all, nothing back to the station, no touch, no taste, no sight; it is a sending-out current, the only one of the twelve the almanac files under all output, no return. What it moves is the tongue. It innervates all the extrinsic muscles of the tongue and all the intrinsic, one by one, with a single recorded exception — the palatoglossus — which belongs to the vagus nerve, CN X, and is priced in another column of the ledger. The almanac keeps the exception in view, because a current that leaves exactly one bank unattended is a current worth an entry.

Passage I — the twelfth current

Current count: twelve · Position: the last · Function: motor only · Barometer: firm since the nucleus

The almanac keeps the twelve cranial currents in their numbered order, and the twelfth is the one that never once stands in for anyone else. It takes no share in the eye, the ear, the smell, the face, the swallowing branch that belongs to the vagus, or the jaw; it has exactly one department, the tongue, and it runs the whole of it. A system with a single department and no return line is, in the almanac's trade, called a fixture — the kind of current you stop forecasting and start filing. The twelfth has been a fixture since the medulla first set it down, and it is still holding the same figure. Its name is plain: hypo, under; glossa, tongue — the under-tongue nerve, the wire laid beneath the organ it moves, which is the almanac's word for every honest piece of weather that works below the deck and is only noticed when it stops.

Passage II — the route: out, through, down, and up again

Source: the hypoglossal nucleus, medulla oblongata, as small rootlets · Channel: the hypoglossal canal · Low water: the neck · High water: the tongue

The nerve is born in the hypoglossal nucleus of the medulla as a number of small rootlets, and the almanac counts them the way the almanac counts rain — many, thin, all running to the same sea. It passes out through the hypoglossal canal, a stone channel cut clean through the skull base, and comes down through the neck, where it runs in the deep weather a long way from any surface, alongside the great vessels, past the jaw, in a lane it has held for the whole of the record.

Then it does the thing the season book was kept for: it turns back and passes up again, over the very tongue muscles it supplies, and enters the tongue. A current that goes down and comes back up over its own ground is the rarest shape the almanac prices, and it prices it the way the keeper of Nethergate prices everything fast, in his hand, from the log of the pilot house:

“The landing is my rule with anything fast.”

A gust is not priced at its peak; it is priced where it comes down. The neck, for all its length of lane, is therefore not where this nerve is read. The nerve is read at the landing — the tongue, the last mile, the side it ends on — and so this entry is kept as a landing, and it always will be.

Passage III — the weather of speech and swallowing

Use: tongue movements for speech and swallowing · Recorded motions: sticking out the tongue; moving it from side to side · Failure mode: the tongue deviates to the weak side when the current drops

At the landing the current has its work. It controls the tongue movements required for speech and swallowing — sticking the tongue out, moving it from side to side, drawing it back against the roof of the mouth — the full motion of the organ that shapes every syllable a person will ever say and every mouthful a person will ever keep down. When the twelfth drops, the almanac records, the tongue deviates to the weak side on protrusion, and the speaker's vowels go flat in the same way the Humber goes flat when the north-west dies down. Every long note sung by a congregation is, the almanac has cross-filed, a landing of this current held at one pitch: see the parish's Lost & Found Notice No. 47, Blaenwern, where the tune's long note at the end of the third line is held up by the same muscles this entry prices, and where the note has held, at every Good Friday on record, both times and every time. The quiet man of 26 May 1945 spoke his forty seconds into the wireless — My dearest fellow countrymen — and the hall that keeps his minute unamended keeps, beside it, the almanac's account of the current that moved the tongue for those words, filed as the obituary of Clement Attlee, Minute Book of Room 3. Speech is the twelfth current at work in plain weather; the almanac prices it, and the season book holds it.

Closure and cross-reference

The almanac closes this entry with the sky over the Humber cleared of moon, the barometer firm at thirty-point-oh-two, the twelfth current holding, and the tongue visible, faintly, in the lamplight of the pilot house. For the season book's other long-running entries in the same hand of the record, the almanac cross-references its own pages: the closing reading of 29 December 2004, Entry 2004/363, which held a steady figure over Bethesda and kept the circuit closed; the ledger of Notice No. 47, which keeps the sung long note; and the Webstuffs front page, from which the whole season book is reachable. Weather is only ever as good as its record; this one was read, priced at the landing, and is written down.
