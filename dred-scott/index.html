A Book of Etiquette for Firsts · Being the Eleventh Volume · Printed for the Firm of Hartley & Quill, Boston and Philadelphia

Chapter XXIII

Of the Petition, and of the Sitting at the Courthouse; Being a Discourse upon the Case of Dred Scott of St. Louis, and upon the Manners Due to a Man Who Asks

“The petitioner shall rise, shall state his name, and shall not be in a hurry.” — From the frontispiece, p. 3

It is a fault of our age that it speaks of petitions as if they were requests, and of requests as if they were small. The reader who would manage a petition—whether for a room, a post, or for something no schedule of the day is willing to name—will do well to study the manners of the man who carried his through three courts, over eleven years, and out of the door of the world with his head upright. That man was Dred Scott, born about the year 1799, and he is set down here not as a warning but as a master’s lesson, in the plain way the book has always kept its lessons.

The circumstances are these, and the reader is asked to take them in order. Dred Scott stood in the service of Dr. John Emerson, an army surgeon of the United States, who in the years 1834 to 1838 took him to Fort Snelling in the Wisconsin Territory, to the free town of Freeport, Illinois, and afterward to Fort Jefferson in Louisiana. It was the two free stations that did the work the law books were later so busy undoing: in Illinois and in the Wisconsin Territory a slaveholder who tarried with his property gave up his claim, by the law of the soil, for so long as he tarried. When Dr. Emerson died at Joplin, Missouri, in the autumn of 1843, Dred passed by inheritance to the doctor’s widow, Sarah E. Sandford, and thence to her brothers, the Sandford brothers, who became, in the language of the record, the men of the case.

His wife was Harriet Robinson Scott, born about 1806, who married Dred at Fort Snelling in 1836; they had two daughters, Eliza and Lizzie. It was the four of them, and no more, who asked what was asked. In May 1846 the petition was filed in the Circuit Court of St. Louis, with Frederick Benton Morris and Benjamin Sundial at the table, and the sitting began—which is the second half of the chapter’s title, for the sitting is where the manners are, and where they fail.

The book’s rule for the sitting is this: one sits with the file open, one does not sit with the file closed and the hope folded up in it. The Scotts kept their file open for the length of a single life. The circuit court of 1847 gave them their day; the Supreme Court of Missouri of 1852 took it back; and in March 1857 the Supreme Court of the United States, sitting in Washington, decided the matter against them by the voice of seven against two. Chief Justice Roger B. Taney delivered the judgment, holding that Dred Scott had no standing at all, that no person of African descent—enslaved or free—was a citizen under the Constitution, and that Congress had no power to bar slavery from the territories. Justices John McLean and Benjamin Robbins Curtis dissented, the latter by a long and patient writing that the reader is referred to, at the register, for the manner of it. A book of etiquette does not take sides in a verdict; it observes only that a verdict of seven to two is not the same animal as a verdict of twelve to zero, and that the two who were against it held their pens until the last line was signed.

The household kept its own account, beside the courts’, and it is in the ledger the family brought up from St. Louis, bound in dark green cloth, where the entries stop in the spring of 1856 with Harriet’s name closed in ink. The keepers have printed one of the ledger’s lines here, in the exact hand of the day and without amendment, because a book of manners must know what a household sounds like in a year without an answer: “When to go to the holders of the men sang until past midnight.” The line is not a record so much as a state of the house; the keepers are content to file it as such, the way the office of the Beechwood Branch timetable files its own unamended line under the entry for the theater—counted, lamp by lamp, and not called a deed.

What the reader carries out of St. Louis is the rule and the record of its keeping. In April 1857, before the verdict was even dry, the Emerson family took Dred back into their hands and hired him out to himself, which in the parlance of the yard was a freedom of the second kind, the kind the court could not grant. He sat at the card table, he cut his own wages, he attended his wife’s people’s business. Harriet had gone before him in 1856. Dred Scott died on September 17, 1858, at St. Louis, at the age of some fifty-nine years, of an illness the register does not name. Eliza and Lizzie outlived him and kept the ledger, which is where it is now.

The sitting, in short, is won or lost in the file, not in the gallery. The petitioner rises, states his name, and does not hurry. That is the whole of the chapter, and it was kept to the letter by a man who asked for the ordinary thing on an extraordinary floor.
