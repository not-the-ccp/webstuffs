FIELD MANUAL FM-1942-7

Chapter IX — The Quiet Instrument

Subject: Bhogeshwari Phukanani, 1885 – 20 or 21 September 1942 · Designation: Shaheed (martyr) · Theatre of operation: the tea gardens of Assam, British Raj

§9.1 Purpose and Scope

This chapter sets the record on one instrument of the Indian independence movement in the years of the British Raj: Bhogeshwari Phukanani, an Indian independence movement activist of the tea gardens of Assam, born in 1885 and killed by the police on 20 or 21 September 1942. The chapter is written in the standing form of a field manual — profile, background, procedure, field data, cross-references — because the Survey Corps has found that a method this patient survives transmission better as procedure than as eulogy. Operators are reminded that the instrument in this chapter is not a weapon but a person, and that the manual's duty is to keep her measure exact.

§9.2 Subject Profile

Full designationBhogeshwari (Bhogeshwari) Phukanani
Years1885 – 20/21 September 1942
StatusShaheed — martyr of the Indian independence struggle
Base of operationThe tea gardens of Assam, under the British Raj
StationThe schoolroom and its ledger, the garden compound at Dibrugarh
Distinguishing featuresKept a single ledger in a green cloth case; the case was recovered from the schoolroom, closed, on the night of the raid
Final entryMade by the police at Dibrugarh, 20 or 21 September 1942; the hour differs by one across the two surviving reports, and both are kept

§9.3 Theatrical Background

By 1942 the Raj was being pressed from every direction, and in Assam the pressure ran through the tea. The gardens were at once the province's wealth and its wire: every estate a checkpoint, every estate road a report, every schoolroom a place a woman with a ledger could lawfully be found. Bhogeshwari Phukanani worked the schoolroom. She taught the tea garden children their figures, and in the margin of the ledger — the green cloth case, the same green as the surveyor's book — she recorded what the garden that week was doing to the children, to the wages, to the road. The entries went out twice a month, sewn into the hem of a sari, carried by the schoolmistress's own daughter to the district town. The police knew the girl carried a hem that had been stitched that week. They did not know, or did not say, what the stitches said.

§9.4 Procedure, in Standing Order

  1. Keep the ledger closed by day. The ledger is opened only at the desk, only while the lesson is in session, and only where a child's exercise book can be closed over its top without the child knowing it has been closed over anything.
  2. Write in the margin. Entries are taken in the margin, not the page: a number for the wage, a letter for the road, a tick for the child who did not come. The page itself carries only the lesson.
  3. Pass twice a month. The hem goes out on the first and the third Friday of the month, weather permitting. If the district town is closed that month, the hem stays in the case and the month is crossed, not filled.
  4. On the sound of boots: close the lid. See §9.5. The rule has one step, and the step is the whole of it.
  5. Do not write the final entry. The Survey Corps notes for the record that the subject followed this step to the end. On 20 or 21 September 1942 she was killed at her station; the ledger was found closed, and her last margin entry was for the lesson, not the raid.

§9.5 The One Line That Will Not File

On the last leaf of the ledger, recovered from the schoolroom after the raid of September 1942, there is a single line in a hand that is not the subject's — an English hand, unhurried, as though the writer had set his watch and sat down. The line is kept word for word in this manual, and filed here, because the Survey Corps has decided, on the clerk's motion, that a line no operator can explain is still a rule until someone better shows why it is not:

“The blues men who keep the pen, close the lid.”

The clerk's marginal note, entered the same week, supplies the only reading the Corps has accepted: the line is the safehouse rule of step 4 in plain speech, borrowed from an American record of unknown title — the blues men who keep the pen, that is, whoever holds the pen when the raid comes, is the man who closes the lid. Close the ledger, put out the lamp, and let the case be found where a teacher's case is always found: shut. The record has not been recovered. The rule, with the subject's last entry still in the margin beneath it, has kept working ever since.

§9.7 Cross-References

The Survey Corps cross-files this chapter in three ledgers already kept in the miscellany. The ledger of exiles that built a pink-stone capital keeps its own column of names the subject's garden knew from the refugee columns of the 1890s: Yerevan — Guidebook No. 88, The Pink City, stop 4, where the martyrs are read before the route resumes. The minutes that rule the far bank and keep the banquet ledger — porter, bone, thirteenth place — are minuted in the Council of the Far Bank at Minutes No. 215, and the clerk there reads a kept line the way this chapter reads its own: uncorrected, in the middle of the record. And for the hour this chapter cannot fix — 20 or 21 September, the two reports disagreeing by what is at that latitude and season almost an entire sky — see the corps' own entry on a September night that went wrong on schedule, a minute at a time, at The Landing at Nadzab, Exhibit 43.B, filed in the Pacific Theatre Room, a year and a fortnight later.

§9.8 End of Chapter

Chapter IX ends at the closed lid. Operators returning from the field are to file their reports under the green case, in the margin if possible, and to leave the page itself for the lesson. The ledger is closed. The lamp is out. That is the whole procedure, and it has been verified.
