Found: a reckoning in the old style

Pittock Block, 921 NW 19th Avenue, Portland, Oregon · Found 14 January 2026

Field Notes · Green Mountain series · Entry no.

13

The item

A small leather pocket almanac, eleven centimetres tall, bound in green morocco with a brass clasp and gilt edges, its corners softened by pocket wear. It was lying open on the floor of room 412 — Dr. James Seller's old medical library, beside the building's cage elevator — next to a copy of Ethan Allen's Reason: the Only Oracle of Man, printed at Bennington. The almanac was open at 1 January. On the day it was found, 14 January by the hallway clock, the almanac itself read 1 January, thirteen days behind.

The inside cover bears the stamp Field Notes · Green Mountain series · Entry no. — the same series that keeps notes on Ethan Allen, the Green Mountain Boys, and the marble country of the Green Mountains — followed by the number 13, and beneath it a single name in faded ink: Ira Hall, with Bennington, New Hampshire Grants written underneath.

How it keeps time

The almanac reckons by the Julian calendar: 365 days in every year, with one additional leap day every fourth year — without exception. February gains its twenty-ninth day in 1832 and 1836 alike, in 1900 as in 2000, no century skipping the beat. Because it never corrects, it drifts: between 1901 and 2099 the Gregorian date equals the Julian date plus thirteen days, which is why Julian 1 January falls on Gregorian 14 January — the date on the hallway clock when this one turned up.

Where it comes from

The Julian calendar was proposed in 46 BC by Julius Caesar, as a reform of the earlier Roman calendar, which was largely a lunisolar one, and it took effect on 1 January 45 BC, by his edict. The gap between old style and new was eleven days in Ethan Allen's lifetime — his birth is recorded as 21 January 1738, old style 10 January 1737 — and it has grown to thirteen since. The reckoning still serves as a religious calendar in parts of the Eastern Orthodox Church and in parts of Oriental Orthodoxy, and in the Berber calendar; this almanac, printed at the office of the Vermont Chronicle in Windsor, Vermont, in 1832, simply never stopped.

The desk has also received, on one occasion, a copy of a patent specification that files this very thirteen-day drift as prior art in reckoning: the upland case, Application No. CZ-1832-0417, a method for the holding of the Ralsko Uplands, in which the examiner struck the garden scene from the specification but kept the note, and the whole upland — Liberec, Ústí nad Labem, and the Central Bohemian all at once — is held by gauge rather than by line (A Method for the Holding of Uplands — Summary of Invention).

The desk’s next drawer keeps a different reckoning: Ghent-Eeklo, the Flemish Parliament constituency used between 1995 and 2003, its seats apportioned between Ghent and Eeklo like leap days between years, until the boundary was redrawn and the file closed (Ghent-Eeklo — Safety Data Sheet).

To claim

If the almanac is yours, describe the leather, the name inside, and the date the almanac shows on the day you lost it, and collect it at the building manager's office, weekdays 8:00–17:00. If it is unclaimed after thirty days, it goes to the Oregon Historical Society, with the book beside it — a disposition the minutes of the Roger Ebert Council have already ruled in, the chair putting the item to the house and sending it away with the date it read, thirteen days behind.
