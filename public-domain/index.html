Who owns a weather report?

posted 04:12, the night the cement temperature fell to forty-one · from the reading room above the valley

Ledger No. 9 is on the shelf where everyone can lift it now, so I am asking the board what the shelf actually is. The Wanters ledger is a record of faults in a second release print: edge burn, drift, a smudged title card, one blank spoke at the end where the cause of none is written as none. It is also, or so the laboratory meant it, an explanation of everything it contains, because a fault that cannot be explained is a fault that will not repeat. My question is about the part nobody wrote down. The entries are facts: a date, a frame number, a temperature, a signature that reads J.M.S. — approved, the frame may go. If no one holds the exclusive right to a thing — if the right has expired, been forfeit, been waived, or never applied at all — then anyone may use or reference it without asking, and I want the board to say so plainly, in the forum, where it can be cited. Because that is what the shelf is. That is the whole shelf. The public domain.

Two more things while the kettle is on. The ledger's governing rule is that an entry stands in the record as found, unamended — the pencil line that belongs to no one is ruled around, not erased. I am told the Eiter almanac signs its tables in the same spirit, and I would like a copy of that signature, word for word, pinned to this thread. And I would like to know if the storm the almanac counted in fives — lightning, then the cake, then the rain — can be quoted without an owner. I suspect it can.

Re: Who owns a weather report?

posted 05:47 · twelve kilometres per hour from the north-east, dry rock, excellent friction

Plainly, then. The public domain consists of all creative works to which no exclusive intellectual property rights apply. Those rights may have expired, be forfeit, have been waived, or be inapplicable. Because no one holds the exclusive rights, anyone can legally use or reference those works without permission — that is the sentence I would pin to the shelf, next to yours.

The board has asked this before, so let me give the long list we keep on the boulder above the village. The works of William Shakespeare, Ludwig van Beethoven, Miguel de Cervantes, Zoroaster, Lao Zi, Confucius, Aristotle, L. Frank Baum, Leonardo da Vinci, and Georges Méliès are in the public domain either by virtue of having been created before copyright existed, or by virtue of their copyright term having expired. That is the whole mechanism, and it is kinder than it looks: it is a clock. A play staged at the Globe has been outlived by its owners. A symphony outlived its publisher. A silent feature outlived the studio, and the studio outlived its own name — I noticed the Wanters print left the lab under Associated First National Pictures and screens ever since under First National, the second name inked over the first. That is a picture of ownership doing exactly what ownership does: changing hands, then changing names, then, at the end of the term, stepping aside.

One caveat the almanac keeps honest about, because it is honest about everything. Some works are not covered by a country's copyright laws at all, and what is free in one parish register may be filed under a sheet in another. So: free, as found, in the place you stand. The station signs that off every season (ask fiddlerow for the exact line), and I chalk the same on the crag before anyone asks to tie in. No permission required. That is the whole post, and it is the whole doctrine.

Re: Re: Who owns a weather report?

posted 07:03 · gate trued, lamp oiled, no loss of picture

I have run a Westside projector long enough to say which half of the Wanters ledger belongs to no one: all of it, except the cabinet. The facts in it — the edge burn on Reel 2 at 12 in, the drift of a quarter of a frame at frame 4178 of Reel 4, the ink drop on the title camera lens of 29 September, the forty-one degrees — were made by the weather and the machines, not by an author. Cold cement cures stiff. A stiff splice slides. A gate out of true kisses the lamp housing. No one can own the fact that cold is cold; the almanac would have you count the feet of film the way it counts the seconds of thunder, divide by the rate, and know your distance, and the distance is free to everyone at the station at once.

WNT-024 is my favourite entry and I have told the board why. A splice made on a cold night loses one forty-eighth of a second of Miss Shearer's first garden scene, and the ledger records it anyway, because a ledger that omits a lost frame is a ledger that has made a decision it was not appointed to make. That is the doctrine of the public domain in the cutting room's own handwriting: set the loss down, count it anyway, let anyone read it. The frame is gone from the scene and it is also, permanently, in the record, where the record is public and the frame does not need a permission slip to be forty-eighths of a second. See the full reeling, codes and resolutions and all, at the Wanters error message documentation, Lab Ledger No. 9 — and if you want the hand that made the hand recut of that splice, the younger sister of the negative cutter who came up through Mayer's studio to bench No. 4, her page-per-reel ledger is set open beside the lab's at Margaret Booth, The Cutting Room, Vol. VII. The four splices and the bell-boy, in that order, are the night formula, and it is ruled around, not erased, exactly like the pencil line the station rules around.

posted 09:30 · the plating is running a frame behind on a cold night; we write it down anyway

The kitchen at Teardrops, 14 Fiddle Row, answering the question nobody asked us: yes, the storm can be quoted, and so can the cake. Count the seconds between lightning and thunder and divide by five for the miles; while you count, mix the butter, the sugar, and the eggs of one brown hen, fold in the flour and a pinch of cinnamon from the tin that travelled west in 1894, and bake for 35 minutes — roughly 950 feet of nitrate stock — and serve it warm, before the rain arrives. The recipe is older than every sheet the registry has filed on it, which is why the registry's sheet on the name Eiter itself lists handling rules for a word that outlives its bearers. We keep a plate for the person who does not come; that is the dining column's one complaint about us, and the column gave us four out of five, and the cold takes its forty-eighth of a second from our plating every winter and we write that down anyway, in the same spirit as the ledger.

And here is the signature you asked for, reel9, word for word, as the almanac prints it under its tables — we paste it on the back of our own receipts now, because a line no one owns is a line anyone may carry:

Station Eiter · all observations local · normals reckoned, not guaranteed.

That is the whole doctrine in the station's own hand: everything was measured here, by us, on our stone walls — January mean −3.2, July mean 18.7, record high 37.1 on 12 August 1946, record low −21.4 on 9 January 1985 — and everything stands open, reckoned and offered, without a guarantee, because a guarantee would make it someone's. The full entry, station normals and all, is at Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry. Quote it. The almanac is not offended; the almanac is in the public domain, which is to say it is in the public, which is to say it is where we all are.

One correction to the doctrine, offered kindly: not everything free is free by the clock. chalkdust9a's clock — expiry, forfeiture, waiver, inapplicability — covers the works that were once owned and then released. But some works were never owned in the place you stand, because they were never covered by a country's copyright laws, and some were given away on purpose, waived by the hand that made them, the way a cutting-room hand gives the night formula to the whole room. The formula is ruled around in the ledger — set down, taken place, page closed — and everyone at the bench can read it, and that is a waiver in the cutting-room's handwriting, and it is the best kind: the owner's own hand, releasing the line. The pencil line between WNT-049 and WNT-053, the one no one in the laboratory could make read like a fault, a cause, or a resolution — Since the last to stamp the page, and I must set down in 2015, and it took place — is the same gesture. It belongs to whoever held the ledger open that night, and to everyone who has ruled around it since, and to no one exclusively, which is the exact definition in the thread, and I am only the first to notice the thread was quoting the ledger the whole time.

If a reader wants the city the house unit filmed in, where the first of the error codes was raised, the board keeps a companion case in the same genre at Los Angeles — The Pacific Coast Almanac, Guidebook No. 47: the Silver Lake rehearsal rooms and the undeciphered chef's note, a page that will not explain itself and therefore needs no one's permission to stay that way.

Thread closed — the ruling travels

posted 00:01 · the page is ruled and closed; the line stands as found

Fourteen replies, one clock, one caveat, one waiver, and one signature in the station's hand. The thread has said what the shelf is, in the forum, where it can be cited: the public domain is everything whose exclusive right has expired, been forfeit, been waived, or never applied — from Zoroaster to the last Méliès reel to the ledger of a 1923 print that lost one forty-eighth of a second to the cold and wrote it down anyway — and anyone may use or reference it without permission, reckoned and offered, not guaranteed. The thread is closed not because it is finished but because it is public, and a public thread needs no further permission to continue being read.

Set down for the next reader: the ledger, at Lab Ledger No. 9, The Wanters (1923); the station's tables and the signature quoted above, at Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry; the kitchen that cooks the storm and writes down its frame, at Teardrops, 14 Fiddle Row; the cutting-room hand, at Margaret Booth, Vol. VII; and the city, at Guidebook No. 47. One more ledger for the shelf before the page turns: the dispatch register of the Luxembourg prime minister’s office in the Pierre Werner years, 1959 to 1984, has run its clock, and its 1,426 numbered dispatches now stand open to quote — the office’s own Error Message Reference, Series W documents the five term codes, and one unruled line about a ninth cup at the counter at Le Marin that no clerk could explain, kept word for word, which is to say: owned by no one, readable by all. The thread stands in the record as found, unamended. The page is ruled and closed. The line is set down, it took place, and the shelf is open to all.
