LOT 412

The Seventh Place at the Table

One (1) walnut dining chair, the empty seventh place, with its ledger of the workshop and without the hour; with one letter of the present owner, dated 14 October 2002, from the province of Perugia.

SOLD · USD 7,400

Description of the Lot

The lot is a chair. It is the chair at the seventh place of the workshop table in the house off the lane from Skyland, western North Carolina — the house and bench of Goingback Chiltoskey (20 April 1907 – 12 November 2000), Eastern Band Cherokee woodcarver, whose bear of thirty inches of cedar and whose railroad of forty-two boxcars are entered in this firm’s condition books only from a photograph and a ledger, since the firm was told to do both. The table seats six; it has always been set for seven. The seventh place, kept from before the war and never let down, is for the wood itself, as though the cedar were a guest who had simply been walking late. This is the chair that holds that place, in walnut, with a saddle seat worn by no one and a back rail that catches the afternoon light at four o’clock, which the ledger notes in the keeper’s sister’s hand as “the hour it keeps for him.” It does not rock, it does not creak, and the auctioneer will state, for the room, that it has carried nothing the weight of a coat for thirty-one years. The lot was hammered at seven thousand four hundred dollars, and it went out of the room in a cloth, not on a shoulder, because the buyer had read the manual.

The ledger is bound in tan board, its pages plain and ruled, taken over in 1989 by the keeper’s sister. It is offered with the chair and against every claim of the buyer that the two were ever separable. Its last clean entry, in ink, is the one the reader of this house knows: “I knew the wrapping had been found, not hired, and would keep what it means that the name with one empty chair.” The firm reminds the buyer, in the printed notes of this catalogue and at the hammer, of the workshop’s own rule of the difference: the goods are hired, priced, and delivered; the found things are kept without a price, because they carry the room’s meaning. The chair was therefore sold under one printed condition, clause (e) of the sale: that the seventh place remain empty in its new room, and that the buyer not set it a second time, because a place set twice is a place explained, and the house does not stand a repeated answer.

The State, by way of Provenance

For the benefit of bidders arriving by air: the lot comes from North Carolina (KAYR-ə-ɚ-LIə-nɚ), a state in the Southeastern and South Atlantic regions of the United States, bordered by Virginia to the north, the Atlantic Ocean to the east, South Carolina to the south, Georgia to the southwest, and Tennessee to the west — the state that is the 28th-largest and ninth-most populous of the United States, and which, along with South Carolina, makes up the Carolinas region of the East Coast. At the 2020 census the state had a population of 10,439,388. Raleigh, where this firm keeps its books and its condition rooms, is the state’s capital; Charlotte is its most populous city and one of the fastest growing in the country, its metropolitan area carrying an estimated 2,883,370 people in 2024. The workshop from which the lot comes sits in the other end of the state, off a lane with no sign and a gate the colour of tanbark, where the buyer is asked to read the room the way the manual asks: the Chapter XII — On Visiting a Woodcarver’s Workshop · A Manual of Craft Etiquette, in whose pages the seventh place is the only table set for a guest no photograph can catch. A second Carolina is kept on this site under a smaller name — the last yellow head of the Carolina parakeet — and the firm notes, for the record, that the state lends its name more easily to the things that leave it.

Sig.ra Lucrezia Fabbri · a hill town, province of Perugia · 14 October 2002

Dear auctioneers — the chair arrived in a cloth and stood by the kitchen window through the crossing, and I will tell you plainly what it has done here, because your catalogue asked the buyer to keep the lot on record. Florence and Tuscany roll out cypress lanes and vineyards; Umbria keeps its hill towns quiet and stone-coloured. The house is stone-coloured, and the chair is the quietest thing in it. I have not sat in it, and I have set it out no second time. The coffee is ready when the coffee is ready, and the window has kept the four o’clock light exactly as your ledger said it would. My father kept an hour for his mother's chair in the same house, and when he died we did not sell the seat; we moved it to the wall. I am only now sure whether I have found the chair or hired it, and I think your ledger knows the answer: a thing that comes up under a bench belongs to no bill, and a thing that comes up under a sea belongs to one, and both must be kept. Yours, L. F.

Condition & Standing Orders

(a) Seat unmarked; the absence of wear is the condition, and the firm will not insure against it. (b) Ledger complete, 214 ruled pages, ink entries through 1999, the sister’s hand from 1989. (c) One shaving of cedar in the cloth, found in the left pocket of the moving blanket; offered with the lot, free, because it was found, not hired. (d) The hour is not part of the lot; the firm declines to value it. (e) The seventh place to remain empty. No exceptions. The firm’s one standing order to the buyer, and the buyer has kept it, and the letter of 14 October 2002 is filed here as Lot 412.1.

All lots sold as described, with the firm’s printed conditions; found things excepted, and never priced.
