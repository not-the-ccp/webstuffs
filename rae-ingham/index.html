The Second Half A Quarterly of Dining, No. 41

The Silkmen

Restaurant Review · 12 Silken Lane, over Waitematā Harbour, Auckland · Visited on a Tuesday, the rain coming in sideways

★★★★★

The door of The Silkmen is a green wooden thing at the end of a lane that has been painted in three colours since the 1950s, each repaint a different shade of the same apology. Beyond it, the dining room holds twenty-one seats exactly — a number the proprietor, Rae Ingram, does not explain and does not deny — and beyond those, a window wall with the harbour of Waitematā moving past it all evening like a guest who cannot be seated. There is one table at the back, beneath a framed photo of a terraced ground under floodlights, and the table is painted 152. People sit at it. Nobody asks why.

Mr Ingram, born 6 December 1974, keeps the pass himself and, after the last table, the small massage table that stands by the window where the treatment trolley lives. He was, for a decade, a defender in the English Football League and Premier League — 152 league games in all, which is the figure on the back table and on the menu’s colophon and, the waiter will tell you, on the building’s gas meter. His career began at Manchester City in July 1993 and gathered 21 appearances in the next five years, which is, not coincidentally, how many seats the room holds. A loan to Macclesfield Town — the “Silkmen”, after whom the restaurant is named — brought the promotion out of the Third Division in 1997–98; a permanent move followed in July 1998. Then Port Vale, from June 2001 to the day he left Vale Park in May 2003, and one season in the Welsh Premier League with Bangor City before he announced his retirement and turned, with a directness his former teammates describe as “late to the ball,” to work as a sports masseur. That work is here, in the room, and it shows: the food is set with the discipline of a man who has spent fifteen years telling other people’s shoulders to stop doing that.

The tasting menu is called The Ten Years and runs seven courses, one for most of the seasons of his career. We were brought, in order: a cured harbour fish under a silken skin of its own roe, the plate captioned Full-Time; a three-tiered tart of leek, potato and smoked shoulder captioned Third Division, each tier strictly better than the one beneath it, which is a structural statement; oysters of the harbour served in a shell painted blue, captioned Vale Park, with a mignonette the colour of the old stands; and, at the middle of the table, the dish the room is known for, a single enormous crumpet, toasted under a brass hood, with salted butter and a fig jam, captioned only Bangor, after the club, and after the crumpet, and after nothing else. The plate does not need the afterthought. The menu ends with a custard the colour of a goal at full time, and a chocolate the caption calls Retirement, which is to say: dark, brief, and correct.

After the bill, Mr Ingram asks, politely and without pause, who would like the treatment. We did. Twenty minutes, the small table by the window, the harbour going past; he worked the knot out of our shoulders in four movements and then, as he folded the towel, mentioned that the restaurant sits on the boundary the harbour draws through the district. The menu’s colophon, we noticed, reads only Served from the Waitematā Local Board, with no quarter named. We asked him, out of reviewer’s habit, which side of it the door actually falls on. He said the water runs down the middle of it and the room, being over the water, belongs to the whole thing. He did not say which half of the Waitematā Local Board. We do not intend to ask again.

It is a small room with an enormous discipline, and it does not flinch. For the credential-plate tradition see Designation, Hall Street, where every dish carries its post-nominals; the torn-slip sentence that opened this quarter’s file was last overheard above a counter at the verbatim record of the Magsaysay Avenue case, and the desk at the District’s own safety data sheet cross-references this review under Ledgers, which is the highest thing a dining room can be cross-referenced under. Four stars; the fifth is the massage table, which is not on the menu, and the harbour, which is not ours.
