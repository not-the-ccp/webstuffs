DIARY OF THE CABIN LIBRARY · KITCHEN DRAWER, THIRD FROM THE BOTTOM · ENTRY 41

The Forty-Four-Page Man

Wednesday, 12 December 2012 · 11:40 at night · snow on the road up, none in the forecast · black coffee: second can, one third left

We went on at nine and forty, and the lamp on the salt shaker stayed lit until ten past, and I am writing it down now, before the night smooths itself out and I start remembering the good parts only. The group voted four to one to keep The Expansive on the hour, which as the hour itself predicted is how you will know it is good, and I am sorry it had to be the Iowa man who got the play, because tonight, filing the green book back under the pie-stand pamphlet the way it has been held all winter, I spent half the evening with the other one. The one whose name is on the spine of the 1928 abridgement and on every shelf in the country and on my mouth, DOO-ee, DYOO-ee, for nine public mistakes and counting.

The bulletin card for the hour was typed by the person with the good typeface and the slow hands, and it went up on the pie stand at ten, and every line of it read the way she typed it, including this one, which the tape picked up whole, which the group has decided not to strike, the way the house does with its lines:

ON AIR OZARK RADIO HOUR · TRANSMISSION 67½ The Expansive a one-act for one voice, one desk, and a good brown gravy, or plain melted butter with, and oh!

— bulletin card, pie stand, typed 12 Dec 2012; line kept as found

Here is the whole of the bulletin story, and it is small: she had the hour’s line down as a one-act for one voice, one desk, and the pie, and the pie sentence had already been argued at the stand that morning, gravy or plain melted butter, and she was thinking the pie sentence and typing the hour line, and the two met in the middle of the desk and neither of them left. There is no pie in The Expansive. There is one can of black coffee and a ferry. But the gravy line is true to the stand, and the stand is true to the house, and a salvaged line kept as a placeholder is a surveyor’s broken baseline, so we are keeping it. If R. Calloway hears it on the docket in Saint John’s, the harbour master can chalk it under the ice chart beside tee-bee five-fifty-five and the two will keep company, which is their trade.

Now the other man, since I am in the drawer and the ink is good. Melvil Dewey — that is the name, though he was Melville Louis Kossuth, Kossuth out of the fever year, 1848, a child’s generation with a politics in its name — was born on the tenth of this month, 1851, so this week is his birthday week in every library on earth and nobody is holding a ceremony, which I have come to think is the correct arrangement. He published forty-four pages in 1876. I have said it twice now. I will say it again because it is the hinge of the night: forty-four pages. The play we just transmitted is about a man in Ames, Iowa, in 1877, with a grudge, who had forty-four pages too, and who would not make a fence, and whose top letters grew, against his intentions, into the top floors in Washington. Two men, two states, two years apart, the same number of pages, and only one of them gets a system named after him. That is not a fair story. I keep meaning to say more than that and the drawer will not let me, so it stays a flat sentence and goes in the green book’s margin where it belongs, under a card, in pencil.

What I will say. He made the round hundred. Ten divisions, each a promise that this book belongs here and nowhere else — the sentence the Iowa man spent a scene refusing to write is the sentence Dewey wrote in 1876 and kept for the rest of his life, 1851 to 1931, eighty years of the same promise, revised through twenty-three editions after he was gone. He was the chief librarian at Columbia College, and he founded the school of library service there that still teaches the trade, and he founded the American Library Association with the first of its meetings in 1876, the same year as the pamphlet, which tells you what the year meant to him. He founded the Lake Placid Club up in the Adirondacks and was its first president, and the club house still stands and still takes members, and I do not know why that last fact stopped me at the pie stand, because a building standing is the least of the things a man like that leaves. He leaves the aisles. He leaves the 900s with their countries still deciding. He leaves me holding a 1928 abridgement, 214 pages, a green spine, and a pencil that out-votes him every Friday.

And I will say the rest of it, flat, in the drawer, because the house keeps its lines and this is one of ours, and because a classification that arranges the whole of a person is a classification that has not finished its work. The man who made the round hundred also made, in the rooms where the system was made, a record the system never got a shelf for: the harassment of the women who worked under him, in the catalogue rooms and the correspondence, kept up over years and kept down in his; the racism, stated; the antisemitism, stated, in print, in the years when the system was being written into the profession he founded and that I work in. The 1931 edition would be his last under his hand and the first to need the rest of it. I am not saying the schedule is his sin. I am saying the spine is his name, and the name is on every shelf in the house, and the house does not take the name off the spine any more than it re-shelves a salvaged line or takes the ferry out of its address. A house may bend its drawers so long as it tells the truth about bending them. This is the true line about the bend: the system is better than its inventor, in the ordinary sense, the way a river is better than its geology, and the system has been better since 1931, when it stopped being one man’s at all and became many inventors’ into a reservoir, black coffee and all. The twenty-third revision went to press in 2011 without him in it, and the twenty-fourth will go to press without the grievance, and the ferry, at six crossings a day and twelve seats and a place for one car, has never once re-shelved itself, which is more than can be said of a lot of people in Washington or in Columbia or in the Adirondacks, including men with Kossuth in the middle of their names.

The snow is heavier. The second can of coffee is a third full and precept five is satisfied for the week. I have put the bulletin card, gravy and all, in the card pocket of the green book, where it will file next to the pencil card about the run of form, and the three of them will hold the 1928 printing flat against the pie-stand pamphlet the way they have all winter. The other forty-four-page man gets the play. This one gets the diary, the drawer, and the entry he did not ask for, which is the entry he did ask for, in 1876, in forty-four pages, when he made the number a promise and the shelf a jury of strangers. Good night to the round hundred. Keep your decimals arguable, and keep your spines honest. — M.O.
