Restaurant review · the dining column

I Wanna Go

14 Platform Road, two stops short of the ferry · 26 seats, one long zinc counter, and a bassline in the floor

★★★★☆ Four out of five · reviewed over a Tuesday service, the week of the fourteenth of June

You can hear I Wanna Go before you see it. The dining room is the old goods depot at the end of Platform Road, and the soundman, who runs the two speakers above the pass, has tuned the house track the way the room deserves it: a heavy bassline you meet in the floorboards first, and drum fills that arrive in the gaps politely, in the manner of the English band New Order, which is exactly the comparison the soundman offers, unprompted, to every new table. There are 26 seats, the long zinc counter runs the length of the old loading bay, and the lamps are low enough that the service happens more by touch than by sight. The proprietor, who took the room over from the rail company in 2009, will tell you the name of the place is not a decoration but an instruction: you don't sit here, you get up and go somewhere, and the kitchen is somewhere, and the ferry is somewhere, and all of it is on the same road.

The menu is written by two hands, the way the room's music is. Max M. keeps the counter and the sauce work, and “Shellback” — the second cook, who handles pastry and the percussion of the kitchen, meaning the pans — writes against him; a third pair of hands, Savan, does the additional writing, which in practice means the herbs, the garnish ledger, and the small second thoughts at the pass. They have published seven editions of the menu book since the room opened, one a year, numbered in roman numerals the way a studio discography is numbered, and the seventh, the one on the wall now, is titled Femme Fatale, which the regulars read as a description of the room rather than of the food. The service is seven courses, set in the old economical hand on the back of the depot's stationery, and it runs to about two hours and fifty-three minutes, which the proprietor has stopped correcting and started defending.

The third course is the single. Every autumn the regulars vote, a poll of the kind the house keeps for nothing else, on which dish of the season gets lifted out of the running order and promoted to the third place, and the chosen dish is released, with a date, on the fourteenth of June, because a single, the proprietor maintains, is not a single until it has a date on it. This year the single is the Hi-NRG langoustine: six langoustines over a heavy bass of brown butter, drum fills of crisped shallot, and a plate turned from a piece of the depot's brass, so the dish is literally served on the building. It is the best thing on the menu and the house is correct to insist on it, third, in the middle, where it can be heard.

Course II is where the room becomes a document. It arrives with a ledger card — not a printed course card, a card torn from the depot's own ledger, the one the old stores office kept in the bay — and the card reads, word for word, with the comma exactly where the old stores keeper put it: “II. Of the Bearing Dong-hoon , and when the specimen car, brake van end, doors padlocked.” The second section of that ledger was kept by a Mrs. Dong-hoon, who catalogued the brass bearings of the stock cars, and the dish beneath the card is the bearing course: a small plate of the langoustine's less famous cousin, the butter-braised shore crab, laid over a turn of Mrs. Dong-hoon's brass. The specimen car at the brake van end of the line is still standing on Platform Road with its doors padlocked, the rail company left it that way, and the house will open it, the proprietor says, only when the bearing course retires. She may have outlived it.

The pre-chorus of the service is whistled. When the single is plated, the saucier on the counter whistles the hook — short, bright, unmistakable — and the room has taken to answering it, one table at a time, before the second pour. The critics in the trade papers have compared the whistled hook to a dozen whistles of more famous origin and then dropped it, which is how the house likes it: compared, not identified. The bread is a dense white thing from the bakery next to the depot's old signal box, and the second pour of the evening, the one after the bearing course, is the one the room remembers, because it arrives in glasses the stores office used for oil samples.

I want to go back. That is the whole of the review, and the name of the room, and the instruction. Seven courses, one single, one padlocked door. The menu book for the seventh edition will be reprinted in September with a corrected garnish ledger, and the bearing course will keep its card, comma and all.
