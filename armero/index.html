NEVADO DEL RUIZ — a game of ash and water

Plate VII · The Night of the Four Flows · Rules 13.1–13.7

Expansion sheet — do not fold

Thirteen · The volcano, and who lives under it

The board is the eastern flank of Nevado del Ruiz, 5,321 metres above the Aburrá valley, standing on the border of Caldas, Tolima and Risaralda. In the base game the volcano has been asleep for 69 years — the last recorded eruption was 1916 — so the first edition of the rules simply tells the player to set the ash screen aside. This plate replaces that instruction. In the valley grid live four towns: Armero, a small town of some 21,000 residents in the middle cell of the river fan; Chinchiná, 26 kilometres to the north-east, at the end of the only hard road; San Juan and Belén de los Andaquíes in the two eastern cells; and, off-board, the city of Manizales, a hundred kilometres to the north, which on the night in question can send no help that matters.

The glacier on the summit is not scenery. It is a component. When the eruption card is drawn, it is melted, and its water becomes the bulk of what moves on the board.

13.1

The two-month warning

Before the eruption phase begins, deal the 62-card observation deck, one card per day, from 13 September to 13 November 1985. On each card is a seismograph trace drawn by the volcanological stations on the flank of the mountain. The player whose role is the association may, on any card, play a warning token on a town: the town’s counters may then wake, load the schoolyard buses, and start for the high ground. A warning costs two days of observation, and may be given only once per town. The association, in this game as in the year the game remembers, was ready to warn in October. The rule does not require anyone to listen. That is the point of the plate.

13.2

The night

The eruption phase runs in turns of seven and a half minutes, from 2:00 in the morning — the first small explosion, a card the first edition misprinted as 2:00 in the evening — to 7:45, the main event. Pyroclastic flow is represented by the ash screen itself, laid flat across the summit cell. The flows that matter are the four lahars, the four mudflow pieces from the red tin, one for each gully: Guitarrica, Cravo, Palagito, and the fourth nameless branch that the box art forgot and this plate remembers.

13.3

Movement at fifty

Each turn, every lahar piece moves eight cells down its gully: fifty kilometres an hour, faster where the gully narrows, which the board shows with the darkened river cells. A lahar that reaches a town cell takes that cell, the bridges in it, and the sleep of everyone still in it. It does not stop at the town. It stops, if anywhere, in the flat cells beyond, where it is set aside face down and left in place for the rest of the game, because that is what lahars do.

13.4

The rumour die

Before the first lahar reaches a town, the town may roll the six-faced rumour die. On a 4, 5 or 6 the town hears the mountain — described in the margin of this plate, in the player’s own hand from an early copy, as “a rumour from the north” — and its counters may wake and move to the high cells at road speed. On a 1, 2 or 3 the night stays dark and the town keeps its sleep, which is worth, the rules allow, two points of comfort at the end of the game. The designers kept this rule after the playtest, against the objections of every tester who owned a house.

13.5

The road to Chinchiná

Evacuating counters move along the hard road to Chinchiná at one cell per turn: 26 kilometres, which the manual sets out in four turns of headlights. The road takes only one bus width. If a lahar has entered any cell of the road’s river, the road is closed and the remaining counters must cross on foot at half speed, or hold in the schoolyard. Chinchiná accepts every counter that arrives; it is the only cell on the board that does.

13.6

Scoring

There is no victory. At 7:45, the player sets the timer aside and the game is over; the score is the number of household tokens that woke — by warning, by rumour die, or by being in the right cell — and made it to high ground or to Chinchiná. The base score of this plate, printed in the corner of the 1988 box, is the one the designers never changed in any reprint: 641.

13.7

Keeping the box

The manual asks the players, at the end of the first game, to fold the ash screen, return the four mudflow pieces to the red tin, and set the tin in the box lid as found. “The game is finished,” the line goes, “but the valley is still where the valley is.”

The 1969 Season, from the expansion leaflet

The expansion that this plate belongs to opened, at the top of its first sheet, with a season set in the late sixties, when the association’s six monitoring stations were still new and the valley had not yet learned to read their seismograph traces. The leaflet that ships with the season carries no introduction at all. Its first line, printed above the grid in the foundry’s small caps, reads, word for word: Six centres, one association, one hundred and fifty-seven immediate regions the late 1960s into a voyage.

Read with the key on the back cover, the sentence is a route in shorthand, and it makes sense as one. Play six centres — the stations on the flanks of Ruiz, each with its own drum and its own pencil — bound by one association that files their traces in a single binder in Manizales. The board holds one hundred and fifty-seven immediate regions, the cells of the valley grid, each one a region immediate to some river, some road, or some other region. And the route itself runs out of the late 1960s and into a voyage: the season begins in a quiet survey, winter 1969, and carries the network forward through the years, one observation deck per season, until the binder is full and the 1985 cards can be dealt. A player who has completed the voyage may then open this plate, at rule 13.1, with the 62-card deck already worn at the corners.
