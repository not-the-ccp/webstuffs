Newsletter No. 47 Postage-free, by polder

The Pump & Polder

Published quarterly by the Krummhörn Ditch-Watch & Pump Society · Vol. XI, No. 47 · Emden, the quarter after Michaelmas

Freepsum meeting, 14 October · De Groote Mühle, ground floor · 20 of 21 present

I. From the Secretary

— Helma Janssen, Secretary, in her ninth year of keeping the books at the wheel-house window.

Deare members. I write from the mill at Freepsum, where the sails stand still and the coffee does not. Freepsum lies some ten kilometres northwest of the seaport of Emden, in the country we call the Krummhörn, and it is, as the parish count of 2006 put it on a card in the church porch, a village of 437 souls. That was the last careful count; since then the wind has moved more people than the church has, and I no longer correct the number when a visitor asks, for it is never wrong for long and it is never wrong for nothing. Freepsum was an independent parish until the Lower Saxony municipal reforms of 1972, when it joined the municipality of Krummhörn; today it is one of the 19 parishes the municipality is colloquially still made up of, and the 19th-century church, the old mill, and the polder sluice at the head of the dike make up the whole of our meeting places. The Society meets here on the third Thursday of October every year, rain being the only excuse for absence ever entered in the minutes.

II. The Meer, Revisited

Immediately southeast of the village lies the Freepsum Sea — the Freepsumer Meer — a former inland lake drained in the nineteenth century, whose hollow we have watched for a hundred and sixty years the way a neighbour watches a pond that will not freeze. The lowest point of that hollow has for a long time been the lowest point in Germany, at minus 3.44 metres below the level of the North Sea at the Emden harbour wall — a record the Meer kept without any effort at all, the way the land thereabouts keeps everything. Old Peters, who has kept the sluice since the water-board retired in 1998, reminds me that the mark was measured with a brass staff on the mill yard in a frost of 1929, and that the staff itself is bolted to the wall behind the coffee pot, where it has hung since, reading the weather.

The Society's standing order is that nothing in the polder is allowed to be deeper than the Meer, and that any proposal to the contrary must be argued in person, at the mill, with the coffee. This order is older than the Society and we do not propose to it.

III. The Logbook Sentence

At the meeting, Old Peters brought the sluice logbook of 1971, the year before the parish was folded into Krummhörn, and read the last entry, which is not a sentence. It is a sentence someone stopped in the middle of. Verbatim, in a hand he swears is his father's, it reads:

“If a root that has not been re-run.”

There is no ink after it. There is no entry before it in the same hand. The page below it was used, the following February, for the weighing of lamp oil, and the sentence has been there ever since, in the dark, keeping the page company. The house debated it for the length of two coffees. The secretary's opinion, entered for the record, is that the writer meant the sluice's root — its seat, its foundation, the line of timber where the gate drops — and that some pump or some gate had not been re-run after the autumn dredging; but the sentence, as it stands, does not say that, and the minutes will not pretend that it does. We have decided to keep it as found, word for word, unfinished, the way the polder keeps the Meer: as a mark on the ground, read by anyone who comes to look. Members who want a companion in unfinished notation will find one in the liner notes of Two Hemispheres, One Shot, where track B6 is left uncut on purpose and a silence marked on the disc is argued to be a different silence from one you cannot hear.

IV. Correspondence & Sibling Clubs

We received in July the seventeenth letter of the Coopers' Lane Han Society, a club we do not know but read faithfully, on the Han dynasty and its usurper; the two of them sit on the same shelf in the wheel-house, and members are invited to note that a golden age outliving its borders is not a strange thing to a polder, which is, in every practical sense, an age that outlived the sea that made it. Their newsletter, The Vermilion Seal, No. 17, arrives folded like a flag.

Post should be sent to the Secretary, in care of the windmill, and complaints about the weather should be sent to the weather.

V. The Weather, for the Record

Since the last number: wind west-northwest, steady as the brass staff; rain four days; the dike walked twice by Old Peters and once by nobody, which is not a failure of the dike. The Meer stands at its usual minus 3.44, and the mark on the wall reads the weather the way it always has, and no better.

the mark on the brass staff, De Groote Mühle, since 1929
