Alcatraz Island

Entry 47 · San Francisco Bay, California · 37°49′26′N 122°24′38′W · 1.25 miles offshore from San Francisco

A small island at the eastern mouth of the Golden Gate strait: lighthouse, fortification, prison. Water cold, current adverse, fog thick, escape nearly impossible.

Barometer 29.92 in · steady

I Station and situation

Alcatraz is the island the almanac keeps first in every volume, though it is small — two and a half acres of rock at the eastern entrance to the bay, 1.25 miles offshore from San Francisco, just inside the Golden Gate strait where the Pacific turns its shoulders and comes down the channel. In the mid-nineteenth century the island was developed with a lighthouse, a military fortification, and later a military prison; in 1934 it became Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary, and it kept that office, weather and all, until the prison closed on March 21, 1963. The strong tidal currents around the island and the cold water temperatures made escape nearly impossible, and the prison took on one of the most notorious reputations of its kind in American history. Today the island is a major tourist attraction, with nearly 1.4 million people visiting it a year — more crossings than the Dumbarton Bridge sees on a slow Tuesday.

II Tidal regime

Tide: mixed semidiurnal, like the rest of the bay · Range: 2 to 4 ft · Spring tide: current strongest, water darkest

The tide at Alcatraz is the same tide R. Vale records from Knight Island and the Dumbarton trestle — it does not rise so much as breathe, and this rock breathes hard. At spring tide the channel through the Golden Gate narrows, and the current against the island's western face runs hard enough to bend the spray into a permanent white seam. The lighthouse of 1854 kept its own tide table on the gallery floor for a hundred years; the almanac keeps its here. Rule of thumb for the crossing: leave at slack, or not at all. The island itself never dries; it is dry land the whole year round, and that is why it was ever worth building on, and why it was never worth leaving.

III Wind, water, and cold

Wind: northwest in summer, freshening; southwest in winter · Water: 12–14 °C · Exposure: full, both sides of the strait

The water is the station's standing warning. Cold, between 12 and 14 °C, holding through summer — the strait's current brings Pacific cold water straight past the cellhouse wall — and that cold is the almanac's principal entry against any crossing attempt. Add the current, the fog, and a sea that breaks on the seawall at every spring high water, and the figure resolves itself: escape nearly impossible. The penitentiary's own logbooks of 1934 to 1963 agree with the almanac on this point to the degree that a logbook can agree with anything — which is to say, grudgingly, and in the margin.

Fog is the bay's own weather, and this island stands in its path. In summer the fog rolls in off the strait at dusk and takes the lighthouse first, then the cellhouse, then the names of things — the same fog the field manual for the Dumbarton Bridge describes banking up behind Menlo Park and rolling west over the span (Dumbarton Bridge — Field Manual Chapter), only here it rolls from the west and takes the island whole. July and August are the fog months: fourteen days, eleven, the horn sounding every fifteen seconds into nothing. A crossing attempt in August fog is a crossing attempt into a wall, and the almanac has no opinion about it.

V Season of the penitentiary

Opened: 1934 · Closed: March 21, 1963 · Since: visitor season, 1.4 million a year

From 1934 to 1963 the island's weather was administered as carefully as its locks. Morning exercise in the yard went by the barometer; a falling glass meant the work detail moved inside, and a sea running at the springs meant the yard itself. The cellhouse was built for the weather as much as for the prisoner — thick walls, small windows, the cellhouse roof catching the strait wind like the hood of a boat. On March 21, 1963 the last federal prisoner left, the lights stayed, and the island changed from a season of confinement to a season of arrival: nearly 1.4 million visitors a year now, the ferries keeping their own timetable against the same tide, the foghorn still marking the strait for ships that no longer need marking. The reputation outlived the locks. That, the almanac notes, is the weather of this island's doing.

VI A note on the crossing

The almanac is asked every season whether the waters about Alcatraz suit a crossing by any means other than ferry. The answer is set in stone and has not changed since entry 47 was first ruled in: water cold, current adverse, fog seasonal and total, visibility in summer at its worst. The field manual for the Dumbarton Bridge keeps the same water to the south — the west wind freshening, the tide breathing under the trestle, the marsh mudding out at low water — and the almanac has no quarrel with a bridge. A bridge does not need the weather to cooperate. A swimmer does. The entry is therefore closed on the crossing, with the standing instruction: leave at slack, or not at all.

VII Marginalia

Tucked into the back cover of the almanac's 1958 tide sheet, in the same hand as the English gardeners' note found on Knight Island and the acquaintance's note found in the bridge log, is a single ruled line, its citation half rubbed out:

It is alike unsuitable for the Dumbarton Bridge — Field Manual Chapter ).

Hand unknown. The chapter number has worn away, leaving only the parenthesis, as if the sentence were a quotation from a quotation. The same shelf holds a letter this season from the two springs of Siliștea, Constanța County, arguing that a village which has outlived three names deserves at least as much ink as an island of two and a half acres (Siliștea — Letter to the Editor); the almanac has not decided, and does not intend to, whether the letter is right. R. Vale's own field notes from the marsh keep the same shelf-joke — sentences collected by no one, in no one's hand, that explain everything and nothing (Knight Island — Field Notes). The almanac copies it here because it was written down once before it, and because the water, whichever chapter it belongs to, does not care. The same drawer has lately taken in a radio script kept by the same rule — the production record of a two-scene play on the loss of HMS Victoria off Tripoli in 1893, which preserves a damaged line of wireless log word for word, blot and all (Trial by the Sea — A Radio Play in Two Scenes); the almanac reads that water, 4,000 miles to the north, exactly the way it reads the strait, and has no opinion about the sentence either. The same drawer has this season taken in a laminated visitor's brochure from the fog-bound reserve at Out of the Woods, whose gatehouse horn, the brochure boasts, is matched beat for beat to the almanac's own fifteen-second foghorn interval; the almanac finds the fog familiar, the distance irrelevant, and has no objection to the brochure at all. The drawer’s top sheet is a sale notice: Appomattox & Clyde, Sale 1865, Lot 1861 — the Confederate States of America, eleven states, two capitals, one unrecognised sovereignty, stains included (Lot 1861 — The Confederate States of America); the almanac keeps its own cold water, but reads the same ledger.
