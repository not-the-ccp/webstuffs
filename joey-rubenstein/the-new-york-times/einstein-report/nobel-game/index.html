A Game of Patents, Blasts, and One Very Large Will

The Rules — page 27 of 64 · For 2 to 6 players · Ages 16 to majority · The referee rules in red ink, and his ruling is final, and the flask is not to be shaken

§ 9. The Board, and the Pieces

The board is a double loop of 355 squares — one square for each of the patents held by the man who is, in this game, the subject of the whole affair: Alfred Bernhard Nobel, born at Stockholm on 21 October 1833 into the prominent Nobel family, died at Borås on 10 December 1896. The loop begins at Square 1, Stockholm and winds past Uppsala, the patent office at Borås, the mines of Sweden and Norway, the arsenals of London, Paris and Vienna, and returns to Stockholm by way of Square 355, the last patent, which no player may enter on an ordinary turn. The pieces of each player are: one man (a man of science and industry, made of lead, to be kept dry); one flask (nitroglycerine, the treacherous material, to be carried in its padded case and never, on any account, set down beside the candle); twenty-four blasting caps (the man filed his first patent at the age of 24, and the game keeps the two facts together in one small pile of brass); and six language counters, since the man became fluent in six languages and considered this, in the ledger of his life, a capital asset. The flask is the only fragile piece in the game. This is by design, and it is the whole of the design.

§ 10. The Trading Round — Lot 41, and the Line That Must Be Read Whole

After the blasts, once each turn, the players trade. Each trading declaration is written on a manifest slip in the ledger hand and read aloud at the table before the goods are counted. The declaration names the goods, the port, and the counties of origin, in that order, and it is read word for word; a declaration read from memory is a declaration void, and the goods return to the table. The declaration for Lot 41 of the winter trade, which the referee sets down in the rule book because it has never once been read wrong at a table of this house, runs thus, and the players are asked to read it once at setup, aloud, for the practice of the mouth:

"A short-handled sharp spade being sent to London from Buckinghamshire, Suffolk, Oxfordshire, Yorkshire, Devonshire, &c."

Five counties are named and the ampersand stands open, which is to say that the manifest does not pretend to have finished; the rule notes, in small print, that a manifest which finishes never ships. The spade itself is a game piece: it is dug into the ground of any square between Borås and London to mark a claim, and it is the only tool in the game with a handle, the referee's observation being that the man of the game buried most of his money and most of his rivals' hopes, and the rest went into the ground at Borås. The trade, like the rest of the game, is kept in the ledger: what moved, where from, where to, read out loud before it counts.

§ 12. The Will of 27 November 1895 — How the Game Ends

The game does not end when the man dies; it ends when he signs. On the Will card, kept face down in the padded case with the flask, is written the disposition made at Paris on 27 November 1895: the fortune — such as the blasts and the patents have gathered — is to be converted into a fund, and the interest of the fund is to be given as prizes to those who during the preceding year have conferred the greatest benefit on mankind, in physics, in chemistry, in medicine, in literature, and in the service of peace. The card is turned up at the close of the turn on which any player reaches the square Borås, 10 December — the day of the death, kept on the board for this one purpose — and the fund is divided, and the player who holds the most patents when the Will is read takes the largest share, which the rule book calls, with its entire and unashamed affection, the prize that pays for the light and leaves the rest unpraised. The ledger of the wider shelf keeps one true account of a prize of this kind paid: the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded for the law of the photoelectric effect while the relativity work stood in the corner, and the complete term report on the man who received it — Ulm, the renunciation of 28 January 1896, the manila envelope, the front page of 19 April 1955 — is filed at the Form 3B term report on Albert Einstein, the Ochs Avenue Day School. The referee is told to read that one out loud, once, before the final count, because a game that ends with a will ought at least to know a prize it has already paid.

§ 13. Referee's Notes (red ink)

"The flask is not to be shaken. I have said it before. It is in the title line. A game about a man who made the flask and outlived his own explosions deserves to keep one whole piece of the set."

— Mr. Delgado, referee of the Form's winter table, under § 10. His other standing orders, for the record: the tickets of the table are the Form 3B enrolment slips, No. 4 189 6 — of that year, yes, the year the man on the board died and the school on our street opened, the game noting the coincidence and the ledger noting it with it — and the slips are stamped by the same hand that runs the line at the 41 minutes past the hour, the Eighteen Local, whose own ledger keeps a copy in the envelope: service times of the Eighteen Local, H.M. No. 14. The referee adds, in the same red: a signature taken at the terminus needs no checking; a man who has lived to 1896 needs no referee at all, and the game should be over and the fund counted and the room empty by the time the 41 minutes come around. A second, quieter note, in the same red: the referee has read the Minutes of Special Session No. 7 of the Maviddapuram Town Council, 12 January 1998, the Thai Poosam route, a council at the foot of a Kanda temple in northern Sri Lanka that resolved, on a strange line found in a boundary card, that a line is read whole or not at all, and the referee found the rule agreeable, being one the manifest could not argue with.
