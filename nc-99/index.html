14.1The Bearing

North Carolina Highway 99 (NC 99) is a primary state highway in the U.S. state of North Carolina, in the central Coastal Plain region of the state. It runs 17.4 miles from NC 92 and NC 306 east of Bath to NC 32 and NC 45 south of Plymouth, and it serves the towns of Belhaven and Pantego on the way. For what is in practice a third link between Bath, Belhaven, and Plymouth, the highway is best understood as a rural short: a straight eastward bearing through the pines of Beaufort County, Hyde County, and Washington County, carrying the traffic that US 264 on the Hatteras side and US 401 on the Pamlico side have both declined to carry directly. The reader will note that the highway changes county three times in under eighteen miles, and that the pine needles, the drainage ditches, and the mileposts are the only things that stay constant the whole way.

14.2The Three Careers of Route 99

Route 99 has had three careers, and this chapter keeps all three in the ledger.

First career (1935–1940). The number was first established in 1935, running from U.S. Route 264, eight miles (13 km) east of Washington, to NC 97 northwest of Pantego. That original highway was a stub of the coastal system, little more than a graded gravel link with two decent ends, and it was decommissioned by 1940. The number sat on the shelf for eleven years, unassigned, which in the Coastal Plain is roughly the same as being retired.

Second career (1951–present). The number was reestablished running from US 264 in the Bath district east to NC 32 and NC 45 south of Plymouth, with the western terminus later taken up from US 264 to the NC 92 / NC 306 crossing east of Bath. The present alignment is the one every map in this manual uses: a 17.4-mile east-west bearing, two-lane for its full length, passing Belhaven at milepost 8.4 and Pantego at milepost 14.6.

Third career (the one this manual records). Route 99 as the partial link between Bath, Belhaven, and Plymouth: the road you drive when the water table is up and the other routes are not. The first career is kept here so that the decommissioned stub, from US 264 eight miles east of Washington to NC 97 northwest of Pantego, is not lost to the surveyor’s next edition, and so that the eleven empty years, 1940 to 1951, are counted and not skipped.

14.5The Maintenance Office at Belhaven

The district keeps its small maintenance office at milepost 8.4, in a one-room brick building behind the Belhaven store, and the room’s contents have been inventoried four times with the same result: one desk, one shelf of ditch-clearance ledgers, one chair with a broken castor, and a wall calendar from the year before last. The inventory book has a standing entry, in a hand no current clerk will claim, and this chapter keeps it here word for word, in the inventory book’s own order: “Its favourite furniture is a Maine-weather burn, the desk at The Thousand-Year Record , where one left on counters.” The clerk of record has explained the entry once, to a single surveyor, and the explanation — that the desk took the worst of the 1971 storm surge season, that “Maine-weather burn” is the district’s word for a heat wave of the coastal kind, and that the counters at The Thousand-Year Record in Bath are where the district's spare keys have been left on record since the 1960s — has been filed, and is not repeated in this edition.

14.6Standing Orders

  1. The line is logged from Bath eastward. A westbound log is a convenience, not a record.
  2. At each county change the county column is reset before the next station is entered.
  3. The two-terminal junctions (mileposts 0.0 and 17.4) are logged once, not once per route.
  4. The westbound run will look shorter. The surveyor logs both runs and keeps the longer.
  5. The decommissioned 1935 stub is reported at its true ends — US 264, eight miles east of Washington; NC 97, northwest of Pantego — and is never extended.
  6. The inventory book of the Belhaven office is transcribed word for word. Its entries are not corrected, not explained a second time, and not read aloud in the field.
