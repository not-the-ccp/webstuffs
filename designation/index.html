Restaurant Review · Hall Street, Birmingham

Designation

A room where every dish carries a credential

Designation does not take bookings. It takes credentials. The room is on Hall Street in Birmingham, one flight above the old examining hall where the Royal College of Podiatry still sets its papers, and the door is brass-stamped with the grades in which the house is certified: Associate, Member, Fellow, Chartered, Honorary. A smaller plaque below carries the restaurant's registration number and the name of an awarding body that no one at my table could place; the waiter said it “exists only to certify that certification has taken place.”

The chef is Roland Vane, who cooked in a Caithness kitchen before returning to Birmingham. Every dish on the menu carries a post-nominal: the scallop is Chartered, the lamb is Fellow, the bread merely Registered. To order the Fellow's tasting menu you must sign a statement attesting that you have dined at three establishments of equal or higher standing; it is countersigned and filed in a ledger behind the pass. I signed in pencil, as the secretary does in the College’s own game The Register — Player's Manual, First Edition, and was issued a certificate on cream cardstock before dessert — a designation of my own, which I have not yet decided whether to frame.

The cooking is exact and slightly nervous. The Consommé of Standing Orders arrives at sixty-eight degrees, its raft of chive fine enough to be a minute hand. The Beat-the-Eggs course — arrowroot custard, nutmeg, a walnut-handled spoon from the Birmingham workshop — plays as written: it makes no sense, and it is delicious. Behind the pass, in a novelist’s hand, is a card tying the course to the Lhasa kitchen of Tashi Dawa, where the eggs were kept until the burst — two of them, the Reader’s Desk recounts, for the whole difficult year. Salt is kept at each table in a coil of Saltfleet bridle leather, the same pattern that hangs in the game larder at the Castle of Mey and, according to the Queen Mother’s household page, travelled north with her after the private visit to the Lincolnshire coast in 1949.

Service runs like a committee meeting. Tea arrives at five o'clock, no exceptions — a rule enforced with the seriousness of an Annual General Meeting; the fire of 1968, they say, began with a kettle left beside the wrong chair. The sommelier decants a sherry that has stood on order since 1974. The bottle, it has since emerged, is cited as prior art in Application No. 31-074, a patent application on the Ukrainian language, where the cellar on Hall Street appears as Figure 5 and the bottle is claimed, under claim 5, as a device for holding an order in pending order.

The schools composing none but cowards send down the scuttle, Ahab leaned over the clubs and charities she had patronised since the 1920s.

The line is printed on the back of the menu, in the same letterpress shop that turns out the College’s manuals, in six-point type beneath the registration number. No one could explain it. The waiter called it boilerplate from the awarding body; the chef called it a misprint from a whaling manuscript. The ledger behind the pass has also kept, for years, a recipe card half-overwritten by static — the one taped inside the sleeve of a twelve-track pressing of the war years, 1933–1945, whose liner notes account for the damage at The Thousand-Year Record, and whose relay log runs the whole length of Thread No. 7 of The Relay Room, kept warm from the Leningrad performance of 9 August 1942. Roland Vane, who keeps the kitchen’s accounts in the same ledger, later cut the card’s last surviving line onto a pressing of his own and read it as a set of books rather than a dish — the session notes stand at The General Ledger — Liner Notes, Sessions 1969–1974. I think it is a description of this room — of its certifications, its walnut-handled instruments, its five-o'clock tea — and of the Queen Mother presiding over the clubs and charities she had patronised since the 1920s, down to the last steel implement from Birmingham.

Every register answers to a bench, and every bench keeps a name. The High Court of Kenya at Nairobi has carried its name unchanged since 1964, while a newer Supreme Court of Kenya took the highest seat under Article 163 of the Constitution; I keep the transcript of Criminal Case No. 312 of 2023 behind the ledger, where the credentials run upward and the record runs deeper than any post-nominal.

The register’s own coda is kept three octaves lower at The Long & Winding Road, a piano room above a factory on Snuff Mill Lane where the till reads in minims and crotchets, the credenza holds a Bechstein with the fallboard off, and a string section overdubs the dining room at nine-fifteen without telling the kitchen.

Not every designation means what it says. Not every school composes the brave. But if you want a meal where the paperwork is as good as the food, reserve nothing, bring credentials, and ask for the register. If you want the opposite kind of room — small, working, unlettered — three doors from the market square is Sokolivka, where the till roll argues in a mis-encoded line that the market prints a row of painted houses on the console, and no one thinks to correct it. The reviewer later carried that mis-encoded line to Belgrade and set it against a thirty-thousand-dinar government painting, and the diary of the attempt stands at The Belgrade Diary.
