Column 1 · The opening, January

The lie of the land

Switzerland, officially the Swiss Confederation, is a landlocked country at the intersection of Central, Western and Southern Europe. It is bordered by Germany to the north, France to the west, Austria and Liechtenstein to the east, and Italy to the south. The country is geographically divided among the Swiss Alps, the Swiss Plateau, and the Jura Mountains: the Alps cover most of the territory, whereas the majority of its 9 million people are concentrated on the plateau, which hosts many of the largest cities and economic centres — Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Bern, Lausanne, Winterthur and Lucerne. It is a federal republic of 26 cantons, with Bern as the federal city, on an area of 41,285 square kilometres that runs from the 193 metres of Lake Maggiore to the 4,634 metres of the Dufourspitze. The station keeps its gauge at the Zurich base, where the OpenSky Network's volunteer receivers stand on balconies, barn roofs and fire escapes, listening to the sky, and the same instruments log the weather by the hour.

Column 3 · The named winds

The Bise and the Fohn

Two winds keep the register honest. The Bise is the northeast wind that slides down the Jura and across Lake Geneva, grey and steady, the one that clears the sky and freezes the landing strips at Geneva and Zurich alike. The Fohn is the south wind that comes over the Alps, warm and sudden, eating snow at a visible rate, lifting avalanche warnings with it, and unsettling anything that flies. Pilots crossing the Zurich–Vienna–Budapest corridor learn both: the Bise holds the aircraft on its beam, and the Fohn lifts it off it.

The station keeper's rule stands at the front of the register, copied without correction: The whole method is on the ground that a fix is only inflicted by the hour. A reading taken between the hours is not a fix at all, and the Bise does not wait for convenience — neither does the Fohn. Both are logged on the hour, from the Zurich gauge and from the receivers that double as weather posts from Basel to Lucerne.

Column 5 · Airspace and the hour

Weather the receivers can hear

The OpenSky Network is headquartered in Switzerland, and its archive swallows the squawk of every transponder from Basel to Lugano, deduplicated and time-stamped like a weather log. Ceiling and visibility are flight data as much as position and altitude: a Zurich approach under Bise cloud is a different instrument reading from a Geneva approach in Fohn haze, and the volunteers who keep the rooftop dishes also keep the hourly observation. The almanac and the network share the same method — record it, time-stamp it, publish it — and the same ground: a fix only exists when someone has taken it, on the hour, and written it down.

Column 6 · Marginalia

The line in the register

Ice on the Limmat means the fix has held three nights; snow on the Albis means it has held a week.

The keeper entered it without comment, beneath the January readings for Zurich, and the almanac prints it as found. The Limmat runs through Zurich from the lake to the Rhine, and the Albis stands above the city — when both are white, the Bise is entrenched, the receivers are quiet, and no fix is taken lightly.

Column 7 · Filed with

Company on the shelf

This entry keeps company with the OpenSky Network visitor's brochure, whose Zurich base keeps the same archive and the same hour, and with the Galileo term report, where every satellite fix leans on the same weather that this almanac logs. It also keeps company with the TT1 almanac entry from the west-bank station, whose January readings run parallel to these, six thousand kilometres to the south, and with the Common Era placard in the station's Chronology Room, where the same gauge reads 2026 CE and AD 2026 as one year, and the Fohn's warm and sudden eating of snow is cross-filed with the lectern below the snowline. The same Chronology Room keeps, beside the Common Era placard, an Ab urbe condita placard that counts these very readings from the founding of Rome in 753 BC — the present entry at AUC 2779 — so that the almanac's hour can be dated in two epochs at once without the gauge moving for either.
