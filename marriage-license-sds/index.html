SAFETY DATA SHEET · MSD-55 · REV. 6 · 11 JUNE 1955

Marriage License

Oil on canvas · 22 in × 30 in · Registry No. 06-11-55 · prepared for the cover of The Saturday Evening Post

Product nameMarriage License (young couple, one window, one clerk)
ManufacturerNorman Rockwell Studios, 367 Main Street, Stockbridge, Massachusetts
Date of issue11 June 1955 (publication date of the Saturday edition)
Emergency contactLicensing Counter, Room 4, County Annex; ask for the clerk on the left-hand stool. If he is asleep, do not wake him; sign in the book and go.

1. HAZARDS

The substance is stable under ordinary conditions and has a long history of harmless use, but it presents two characteristic hazards that recur throughout the manufacturer's output: young love and ordinary life. Both are acute. Young love is visible within seconds of handling and tends to pool in the area of the window. Ordinary life is chronic; it accumulates in the furniture, the ledgers, and the clerk's face, and it cannot be scrubbed out. Exposure to the full contrast between the couple and the counter staff may cause the handler to feel, without justification, that the room is darker than it is.

2. COMPOSITION AND INFORMATION ON INGREDIENTS

  • Oil paint, dry-brush and wet-in-wet, on a primed canvas panel, 22 × 30 inches, stretched and keyed.
  • One male applicant, tan suit, arm placed around the partner, holding the pen for the joint signature block.
  • One female applicant, yellow dress, standing on tiptoe in order to reach the signature line; heels approx. 2¼ inches.
  • One clerk, seated at the counter, expression recorded as bored, both hands flat, attention level estimated at 12%.
  • One window, source of the principal illumination; surrounding room and furnishings rendered dark by comparison.
  • Application forms, blank except for the names, in duplicate, on the counter.

The painting was made for the cover of the 11 June 1955 edition of The Saturday Evening Post. Rockwell had a long history of licensing material: the office, the counter, the clerk, and the paperwork have all appeared on earlier covers, and this formulation is the one in which the paperwork is the point rather than the setting.

3. FIRST AID MEASURES

In case of prolonged viewing: step back from the window side of the image, sit down, and note that the man's arm is around his partner and that the woman is balancing on her toes, both of which are voluntary acts performed by adults of sound mind. If the bored clerk is imitated involuntarily, close the eyes for twenty seconds. If the yellow dress seems brighter than it is, the handler's own dark is not the painting's fault. Do not attempt to sign the application yourself; the signature block is already filled.

4. ACCIDENTAL RELEASE

In the event of accidental release of the tiptoe — that is, if the woman lowers her heels before the ink dries — the signature remains valid, the line is still crossed, and no remedial action is required. The clerk does not observe the release; the clerk does not observe anything. Confined release to the counter is normal and expected.

5. EXPOSURE CONTROL AND PERSONAL PROTECTION

The permissible exposure is set against the illumination differential: the difference between the light the couple receive from the window and the light everyone else in the room receives. Set the three dials to match the conditions on site and read the hazard class and the exposure limit. Under the default conditions of the published cover — 2,400 lux at the window, clerk attention at 12%, two forms on the counter — the differential class is III (elevated) and the limit is one full sitting, with breaks every twenty minutes for the clerk alone.

Illumination differential: 612 → Class III (elevated). Limit: one full sitting, 20-minute breaks.

If the dials do not move, the default reading above stands: 612 differential, Class III, one full sitting.

6. HANDLING AND STORAGE

Handle with dry hands and a straight spine. Store in a county annex, a municipal building, or a government office of similar character, at 18 °C and 50% relative humidity, away from fluorescent light, which flattens the window. The tan suit and the yellow dress should not be exposed to each other's reflections in the same glass case; the clerk may be stored anywhere, including out of sight. For the medium itself — the powdered pigment and neutral binder that behaves this way in a quiet studio — see the studio's own sheet, Pastel — Safety Data Sheet PM-04, Studio Washingtonville.

7. TOXICOLOGICAL AND OTHER INFORMATION

Acute effect: tenderness, with a half-life of one edition of the Post. Chronic effect: the handler begins to notice real counters, real clerks, and real windows, and is then in the same trouble as the painting. The two recurring themes of the maker's work — young love and ordinary life — are not ingredients but metabolites; they are produced in every dose, including the smallest. A related document on the short-film side of licensed partnership, the 1914 Keystone short Mabel's Married Life — Film History Report, Form 4B, is cross-filed in the same drawer and may be consulted for comparative exposure.

8. STABILITY AND DISPOSAL

The substance is stable. It has survived one century of reproduction without degrading, which is to say without becoming less dark in the corners. Do not incinerate; the window will not relight. Dispose of surplus prints in the municipal waste stream with the rest of the Saturday morning.

9. REGULATORY INFORMATION AND PROVENANCE

Licensing records, in the order kept: painted for the cover of The Saturday Evening Post, edition of 11 June 1955; entered in the studio ledger at Stockbridge the same week; photographed for the collector's calendar in 1956; exhibited in rotation at the county museum ever since. At the 2024 dispersal of the Rockwell working collection, the entry for this lot read, in the auctioneer's own shorthand: CATALOGUE CXL · Lot 9 · Three-downs demonstration, and Mr. Kelly bid $30,000. The lot sold on the fall; the counter, the forms, and the bored clerk all crossed with it. The original remains on display, and this sheet remains in force.

10. OTHER INFORMATION

Prepared in the licensing office of the Webstuffs annex, from the published cover and the studio ledger. The maker, Norman Rockwell, illustrated for American periodicals for over sixty years, and this is one of the covers in which the most important object in the room is a blank form. For the front page of the file, Webstuffs.
