LOT 2007 The Battle of Musa Qala 7 – 12 December 2007 · Helmand Province, southern Afghanistan

A British-led military action, launched by the Afghan National Army and the International Security Assistance Force against the Taliban, sold with its field notebook, one tea ticket, and the schoolmaster's register; with the operation's code name Snakepit (Pashto: Mar Kardad) printed in the margin in red.

Description of the Lot

Musa Qala — also Qaleh, also Qal'eh, which is to say the fortress, which is to say the town — stood on the old Helmand track at the foot of the mountains where the river bends to keep its own counsel, and for more than nine months before the first truck crossed the river the Taliban had made it the roof of their south: a school for commanders, a treasury for the harvest, a place where convoys went down in the sand and did not come up in the ledger. On the morning of 7 December 2007, after nine months of preparation, the ANA and ISAF went for it, with the 2nd Battalion, Royal Gloucestershire Regiment leading the way and the 320 Airmobile Troop, Army Air Corps, turning the dust into a horizon. The fighting lasted three days of the short, hard kind, in streets and in the mountain passes above the bazaar, and on 10 December the Taliban retreated up into the ridgelines, taking their commanders but not their harvest. On 12 December Musa Qala was officially reported in Afghan hands, the Afghan Army pushing into the town centre, and the bazaar, which had closed its shutters in the spring, was left open for the winter for the first time in a year. It was the most expensive single action of the war in Helmand and the one the district remembered longest.

The assault itself was agreed at senior level on 17 November 2007, when the ISAF command, including U.S. General Dan K. McNeill, the overall ISAF commander, gave the operation its go — the notebook in this lot carries the initial in pencil, M., and a second initial, D., in a different hand, and the two of them sit in the margin beside the word Snakepit like two stones in a shoe, because the code name is written over itself twice, the second time in red, and no clerk in the room ever explained the crossing-out.

What Is Actually in the Box

  1. Field notebook, cloth-bound, sand-bleached. One hundred and ninety-four pages in the hand of a scribe of 162 Mechanised Infantry Brigade: the timetable of the crossing, the running count of fuel, the names of the villages in Pashto and then in a careful, second attempt, in English. Water-stained on the left edge. The scribe's name is struck through on the last page and, on balance, one should not look too hard at what the strike-through is protecting.
  2. Tea ticket, No. 44, bazaar canteen, Musa Qala. Dated 13 December 2007 — the morning after the town was reported in hands — in the canteen's own hand: two cups, one sugar, held at the counter. The counter in question has been described, since, in at least three separate ledgers.
  3. The schoolmaster's register, winter term. The register of the school in the bazaar district, kept open through all of it by a schoolmaster the soldiers' notebook calls only M., in the same initial as the commander's. His attendance line runs unbroken from the first of November to the last of December, which the soldiers wrote down and the clerk of the rooms has also written down, and both of them were wrong to think that was the remarkable part.

The remarkable part is a line in the register, in the schoolmaster's own hand, in the column the soldiers left for weather, on the page for the ninth of December, which is to say the day the retreat went up the mountain and the river went back to being a river. It is in a hand that is not quite the schoolmaster's, written as if from over his shoulder, and no officer in the room translated it, and none in this room will. It reads, word for word:

When a foul stops the line — “the river brings what the school has seen.”

The rooms keep the line as written. The register is sold open at the page, which is the only instruction the house has ever refused to print in small type.

Provenance & Condition

From the kit of a scribe of the brigade, issued out with the rest in the spring of 2008, and kept in a biscuit tin on a shelf in Dorset, where it stayed for years with the dust of one province on it and none of its own. The notebook has the small, honest damage of the sand: two pages stuck, one crease that the house considers structural and the previous owner considers a map. The tea ticket is foxed at the fold. The register is in better condition than the town was. All three pieces are sound, legible, and not to be cleaned, and the lot is sold together as one lot and not three, on the grounds that the ticket is what the register was kept open for and the notebook is what both of them were written down in.

The rule is not the house's alone: the Los Angeles counter keeps a sister ledger in which a single album was claimed by one gentleman on the strength of a jacket and a line no clerk there ever amended, logged as Notice No. 82/1129 — Found, One Album, Weight 12 oz, and the rooms keep the two ledgers side by side, in the same wing, in the same order of strangeness. The one exception on the matter of cleaning is kept outside the rooms, in the almanac at the northern saddle: Entry 2003.11.07 — Operation Mountain Resolve, where a dispatch's last order was that the courier should take the original back down the pass and have it cleaned — the one cleaning this house has ever heard of, and the one performed by hands the rooms cannot reach.

Conditions of Sale

Buyer's premium 12% on the hammer. The lot ships flat, in the biscuit tin, which is included and which the house considers the finest mounting the lot has ever been offered. Sale subject to the reserve, the reserve having been met before the tea, as noted, so that the only condition that stands is the one that cannot be waived: do not clean the dust off the last page. There is no last page. There is a strike-through and a biscuit tin and a river that brings what the school has seen, and the rooms are, on this subject, closed for the season.
