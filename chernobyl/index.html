Reactor 4, Chernobyl

A diorama in 1:200 scale, entry by entry — the plant, the sarcophagus, the exclusion zone, and one geiger counter that will not stop being correct

Started 3 March · scale 1:200 · base 1,200 × 800 mm · concrete grey, radiator red, trefoil yellow

Entry 1 · Why this bench, and what the numbers said

On the night of 26 April 1986, reactor 4 of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant, which sits near Pripyat in what was then the Ukrainian SSR of the Soviet Union, went through a power test and came out the other side as an explosion. The accident killed a number of people outright — the two shift operators, Valery Ignatenko and Boris Melenikov, first, then the fire crews, then the rest in the hospital wards — and it let a great deal of radioactive material into the sky, which then travelled for weeks and did what it does. A zone was built around the plant. Pripyat, which had 49,000 people, was emptied in a morning and has mostly stood still ever since. The response, the liquidation, involved more than 500,000 personnel; the official figure for the cost of the accident and the zone is 18 billion rubles, which is about 85 billion dollars in 2026 money. It remains the most destructive nuclear accident there has been, rated at the top of the scale — a Level 7 on the International Nuclear Event Scale, the maximum — and it is usually called, on the ledger, the most expensive disaster in history, with an estimated cost running to 700 billion dollars. I have checked these numbers three times. The diorama will not check them for you.

The bench I work at is a long table in a spare room that leans on the print works, and the window looks out over the rim of the plateau. On clear mornings the whole flat horizon shows; the Altiplano runs about 3,650 metres above the window, and I like to think of the model as a small, low, well-behaved piece of country set deliberately below it, so that the two can be told apart at a glance. Latitude is not altitude, and the Naga people say so in their brochure — the visitor's pamphlet for parallel 13° North prints the sentence plainly, and I have pasted the page inside the lid of the baseboard. The plant itself is at roughly 178 metres above the sea. The difference is the whole story of the project.

Entry 2 · The base and the concrete

Baseboard: 1,200 × 800 mm of 18 mm board, sanded to the texture of a yard, painted in four passes of a grey I mixed with 2 parts raw umber and 1 part yellow, then a wash of the same at one part to ten. The reactor building is the big flat-topped box at the north end of the base — VVER-1000, four units in one long hall, and I have built unit 4 as the one whose roof is gone, because the roof is where the story is. The other three units keep their roofs and their little cooling-tower pairs; in the model the towers are a full 28 mm tall, which at this scale is the height of a bottle and exactly the height that makes you look twice.

Entry 3 · The sarcophagus

In April 1986 the broken unit was sealed in a sarcophagus — a concrete and steel box thrown up around it in eleven weeks by hand and crane, the whole thing going up while the plant was still warm and the crews were still rotating in and out. My sarcophagus is sheet steel and putty, 90 mm long, with the six arch ribs on the side facing the camera, and I have scratched it with the point of a bradawl to get the weathering, because a thing that has stood in a forest for forty years does not stay smooth. On top of it I have pencilled, in the scale, the date the new shell — the New Safe Confinement, the big arched cover that slid over the old one in 2016 — came to rest. The arch in the model is a wire bent over the box, and it works.

Entry 4 · The zone and the town

Pripyat gets forty square millimetres of the base: a row of flat apartment blocks in white and blue, the fountain at the central square, the Ferris wheel — the one from the photograph, standing in the field with its carriages still on it. The wheel is a paper disc on a pin and it is the piece I am least ashamed of. Around it the zone opens out: birch and poplar from lichen powder and green ink, a red fire station left in the middle of nothing, and the road that leaves the site with its little barrier. The barrier is 3 mm of plastic with a yellow stripe. The stripe is a feature.

Entry 5 · The geiger counter on the bench

Every bench needs an instrument that cannot lie, and the counter I built for this project is a plastic case, a piezo disc, and a battery, wired so that it ticks — which is the whole of it, and it is enough. On the model it sits on the sarcophagus with its dial facing the camera, and its reading is fixed at a number I found in a ledger and refused to improve. There is a working version of the thing on this page: the counter below keeps its own count. Tap it, or press the trigger, and it will tick at the rate a counter ticks, and it will not stop. The one in the notes for T.T. No. 4, Indian Camp, cut at the boathouse, ends on a paddle and a question, and mine ends on a tick, which is the same shape of answer from a different bench. The other log on this shelf that knows about lamps and long counts — the 1:24 Lotus built turn by turn in the garage — uses forty-seven coats of green where I am using four passes of grey, and we have compared notes on what it costs a thing to be finished.

Entry 6 · Where the log stands

The zone is grown out to the barrier, the sarcophagus is weathered, and the counter is on the bench and correct. What remains is the sky: a sheet of dark blue over the whole base, with the first month of May in it, because in the accounts the accident is a spring story and I am not going to put winter on it. The last number in the exercise book, written at the bottom of the page where the pages turn, is the one the plant keeps: Level 7, the maximum of the scale, the rating the International scale has given to two accidents in its history and to this one first. I have written it three times and left the third one. The log will continue at whatever pace the print works allows.
