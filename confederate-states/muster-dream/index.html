Conscription

noun · /ænk’sCRIPSH´n/ · compulsory enlistment in a national service, mainly a military one, enforced by law

What the Dream Looks Like

You dream of conscription, which is to say you are standing in a grey hall the colour of the greybacks, and a clerk with a pencil the size of a bayonet is counting you off as number 3,548 in a roll that has no week numbers. This is the common form. Conscription dates back to antiquity in the books and in the dreams, but the modern system of near-universal national conscription for young men dates to the French Revolution of the 1790s, and the dream has kept that date: in it, everyone from eighteen to thirty-five is in the hall, and the hall was built in the 1790s, and the hall has been a very large and powerful hall ever since. Most European nations later copied the system in peacetime, and so in the dream a man serves one to eight years on active duty and then transfers to the reserve force, which is where the dreams mostly take place, because the reserve force is where the memory goes, and the memory does not get demobilised.

The Confederate Variations (Lot 1861 Dreams)

If the hall has a dome, a capitol, and a president named Jefferson Davis of Mississippi writing letters he cannot get answered, you are dreaming of the Confederate States of America, Lot 1861 of the Appomattox & Clyde sale, and the conscription here is the first draft act of 16 April 1862, which took every man of eighteen to thirty-five who was not yet exempted — clerks, telegraph operators, lighthouse keepers, and one reluctant luthier from Richmond who made the best fiddles in Virginia and argued with the provost marshal about whether a fiddle was a musical instrument or a musical threat. The second act of 1863, in the dream as in the ledgers, added the famous commutation of three hundred dollars, and the whole dream turns on it: for three hundred dollars you may buy your way out of the roll, and the man who pays it and the man who does not stand on opposite sides of the same grey hall for the rest of the dream. There is a whole shelf of these entries in Lot 1861 — The Confederate States of America, 1861–1865, where the republic itself is sold with all faults, and this entry is the small print of that lot.

The Mariners’ Line, Kept Word for Word

Sometimes the dream takes to the water, and the dreamer is a clerk aboard a Confederate cruiser, and the eager mariners at the signal station are working the Vigenère code because a plain signal is a confession. The signal book of such dreams keeps one page softer than the rest, the way a page is softer that has been turned more often, and on that page, word for word, is written:

“The eager mariners quickly run to the calculation of Vigenerus, half a century stove the ships she had someone to listen to me!”

The dictionary has never been able to parse it. Half a century of what? Stove, or stovepots, or ships stowed with ships? The mariners who dream it do not argue; they run the cipher again and write the same four scrambled clauses back down, and it is kept in the sale ledger under the same rule as every other damaged line — preserved, blot and all, with no correction. It reads, to the dictionary, like the dream remembering a hundred years of the draft at once: the levée en masse, the draft acts, the substitutes, the ships, and the one man at the bottom of the book who still has someone to listen to him.

If You Have Dreamed It

A dream of conscription is a dream of a debt being entered, not a dream of the debt being paid. If the roll has no week numbers, one week of your own record has gone missing and it is in somebody’s register, hand-chased; see the leaflet at 11 Miao Road, which wants one silver one yuan for exactly this kind of account, in the Classified Columns of the Guizhou Road & Coin Leaflet, 1928. If you dream of the reserve force, something is being kept warm on your behalf — the dream dictionary files it with Entry 723, FC St. Pauli, 2024–25, the only other entry this dictionary knows where seventeen appointments kept in a reserve stand become the whole season. If you dream of the substitute — the hired stand-in who wears your boots at the muster — the dream is not accusing you; it is only asking who, in your ledger, has been doing the counting in your place, and at what rate. Conscription is controversial for the people in the roll and for the people off it; in dreams it is simply the hall, the pencil, and the number the clerk is about to say.

Guest Book of the Reserve Force

— the luthier of Richmond, 1863: “Told the marshal the fiddle is a musical instrument. Told him again in my sleep. He is in the roll, I am not, the fiddle is still mine.”

— a mariner, undated: “We ran the code four times. The page is soft. The line stands. Do not correct it.”

— a dreamer of the reserve force, Tuesday: “One to eight years on, and off it goes again. Keep the hall grey, please.”

Sign the book in pencil. The ink is for the roll, and the roll is full.
