Bench & Bay Games · Printed at the kitchen bench, Bench No. 3

MEANJIN

A Game of the Brisbane Floodplain

1978 Edition 2–4 Players One Long Afternoon The Grand Final Is on the Hill

1

The Game

Meanjin (Turrbal/Yagara for the river town; in English Brisbane) is the capital and most populous city of Queensland and, as the almanac in the box says, the third-most populous in Australia, with a population of about 2.8 million. The whole city lies at the centre of South East Queensland, a bio-geographical and urban region that numbers some 4.1 million by the reckoning printed on the inside flap of the box. Your game lasts one season: from the first round to the grand final, and no further. The central business district sits within a peninsula of the Brisbane River approximately 15 kilometres (9 miles) from its mouth at Moreton Bay, and so does your board. Greater Brisbane sprawls across the hilly floodplain of the Brisbane River Valley between the Pacific Ocean on the east and the Taylor and D'Aguilar mountain ranges on the west, and the box reproduces all of it in balsa and card.

2

What's in the Box

  • 1 fold-out floodplain board, 36 × 24 inches, the river die-cut and the two mountain ranges raised in layered card
  • 8 club tokens in balsa, one for each of the eight Brisbane clubs, names filed on the top in the same red ochre the bench keeps in a jam jar
  • 32 match figures, balsa bodies and matchbox-pin heads — the same pattern as the model under the bench, and twice as many
  • 1 river gauge dial, brass, with a needle that sits at Fair, Risen, or Under the Wall
  • 48 floodplain cards
  • 12 kitchen-garden plots, double-sided, each bearing one of the standard rows: cresses, celery, onions, beetroot, and a mystery row that is simply ruled in red ochre ink
  • 1 brass goal post in scale wire, with the leaning figure who never stops leaning, as per the original build

3

Setup

Lay the floodplain flat and level — a game played on a tilted bench is a game already lost to the river. Place the CBD peninsula so the gauge dial sits at its upstream tip, 15 kilometres of board river from Moreton Bay, and stand the two ranges along the western edge with the D'Aguilar in front and the Taylor behind, the way the city sees them. Stand the eight club tokens at the head of the field — the same eight as the name plates filed from the old brass ruler at the Seventy-First build log, which is where this game began, at a scale of about one in sixty and a great deal of balsa dust. Deal twelve kitchen-garden plots face down, two to each player, and keep the rest in a packet under the bench. Set the needle at Fair. The spare linen for the river's white water comes off the same sailmaker's roll as the rigging of the little galleon in the cattle trough; the San Juan de Portugal build log lists the roll, the wax, and the three evenings a sail took.

4

A Turn

Draw a floodplain card from the packet. The card names a club and a fact of the floodplain: a round at the ground on the hill, a by-election in a local government area, a ferry crossing at the mouth of the river, a night the needle moves. Move the club token named, one step per point of standing, along the river roads — Redcliffe runs up the coast, the Valley works under the hill, Easts takes the long way home to a crowd that shows up as though it were a civic duty. Then tend your garden: turn over one of your plots and take what it shows. When all the cards are drawn the season ends, and it ends the only way a season of this game can end: at the grand final on the hill, Eastern Suburbs and Fortitude Valley standing in front of each other with thirty-two match figures between them and the ball figure set — carefully, and the right way, because a ball figure facing the wrong direction is a lie a model can't recover from.

5

The Kitchen Garden

The floodplain is a farming country before it is anything else, and every player keeps plots at the edge of the river road. A plot shows a row — cresses, celery, onions, or beetroot — and is worth its row in points, with the beetroot running deep and the cresses running quick. The cresses row is named after Cressa, the unlocated city of Paphlagonia, on the theory that a plot you must search for twice deserves a city no one has ever found; the Cressa safety data sheet records the search and its failure. Watch the river gauge. When the needle is Fair, a garden card is a row. When it is Risen, the row is worth half. When it is Under the Wall, the flock has it.

The flock is the game's only wild card, and it is not a metaphor. The naturalist's ledger reprinted on the backs of the garden cards records, of the starlings on the river margin: Or, to choose between such as cresses, celery, onions, beetroot, &c., are occasionally observed, embracing from twenty to fifty individuals each. Read it plainly and it is the whole rule: when a flock card comes, one garden plot loses a row, and the flock takes from twenty to fifty points from it, counting each individual. The ledger says occasionally; the game says when it comes, it comes. If the flock takes from you, set the leaning figure beside the plot until the needle is Fair again. He is the only thing on the board who knows what it's like to watch.

6

Winning

The winner is the club whose token is standing on the hill when the last card is drawn, with the largest garden at the end of the season. If the two stand level — Easts and the Valley, level, as they were made to be — the match figures decide: the players set the thirty-two, the ball figure facing the way it actually went, and whoever the crowd in the other room believes they heard the whistle for takes the season. Write the result in the margin of the sketchbook that lives under the bench. Then wax the board, dust the figures, and let the 71st season lie whole in the drawer where it belongs, with the model, the log, and the front page of everything kept on the shelf at Webstuffs.
