The man

Alexander III of Macedon (Ancient Greek: Ἀλέξανδρος, romanized: Aléxandros; 20/21 July 356 BC – 10/11 June 323 BC), most commonly known as Alexander the Great, was king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon from 336 BC until his death. He was the son of Philip II, born to him by his fourth wife, Olympias, and ascended to the throne at the age of 20 after Philip was assassinated at the wedding of his daughter Cleopatra to Alexander I of Epirus. Alexander spent most of his reign conducting a lengthy military campaign throughout Asia and Egypt, and by the age of 30, he had created one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Greece to northwestern India. He was undefeated in battle and is widely considered to be one of history’s greatest commanders, though “commander” is too small a word for the man: he was also a founder of cities, a collector of horses, and a king who took his own fevers in the records.

Pella, the dog, and the boy

He was born at Pella, the stone-and-oak capital of Macedon, in the twenty-second Olympiad, and given a name that meant “the defender of men” — a name his mother, a woman the Greek cities whispered about for twenty years, is said to have taken with unusual seriousness. At twelve he began the four years at Mieza under Aristotle, where he kept a copy of the Iliad under his pillow and named his horse Bucephalus, the black heifer-spotted horse no Macedon could break, which he turned head-on toward the ground the horse was terrified of and so broke it. At sixteen, before his father’s death, he was left in charge of Macedon for the year the king campaigned in Thrace, and put down a revolt in the town of Petresia. The kingdom knew the boy’s weight before he ever took its crown.

The road east — Gaugamela and beyond

He crossed the Hellespont in May 334 BC, set down the famous stake at the port of Sestos, and went to work on the Persian Empire that had stood for two centuries. The Granicus in 334, the plain of Issus in 333 where Darius III fled the field leaving his family with the Greek prisoners; the siege and taking of Tyre, seven months of causeways and boarding ladders against an island that would not be taken. At the Gate of Persia, and then at Gaugamela in October 331 BC, he broke the largest army Darius could raise — some eighty thousand by the Macedonian count, more by the Persian — with a wedge that angled into the gap at the king’s own standard. That winter he stood in Persepolis, the ceremonial heart of the Achaemenids, its terraces and halls the richest thing in Asia, and let the fire in; the treasury alone was said to hold more silver than the world had seen since the Medes.

The road ran on: the desert crossing to Memphis and Sais in Egypt, where the priests of Memphis called him the son of Amun at the oracle of the Siwa oasis and the city of Alexandria was laid out on the bay in 331 BC; the long way down the coast through Carmania and Gedrosia, where men drank brine and horses were drawn through the water by the forelegs; Bactra and Ecbatana, where the army, worn past the edge, voted its first no and the king turned for the Indus. At the Hydaspes in 326 BC he met Porus on the burning sand and won again, and then, at a place called Hyphasis, the soldiers who had marched ten years and more refused to march one more day, and Alexander, who had never once been told no by an enemy, let his own men have the argument.

The empire and its shape

By the summer of 323 BC the territory he ruled ran from the Aegean to the Indus: Macedon and Greece, the old Persian satrapies from the Hellespont to the Caspian and beyond into Bactria and Sogdiana, Egypt, and the river lands of northwestern India, the far edge of the largest single empire the Mediterranean had yet seen. It was ruled from the capital that was never finished — Babylon — by a system of satraps, garrisons, and the Greek cities he had founded along the road, Alexandria of Egypt being the most famous of them, and later Alexandria Eschate, “the Farthest,” near where the road turned into Bactria. The empire did not survive him; it broke at his death into the kingdoms of his generals, the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in the east, and the Antigonids back in Macedon. But the map it made — Greek cities from the Nile to the Oxus, Greek coinage in the river markets of the Indus, Greek script in Bactria for centuries after the roads were empty — held. The territory of present-day Iran, whose entry keeps the Achaemenid empire, Persepolis, and the plateau with which he did all this, carries his road west of the Caspian still: the passes through the Alborz that his baggage trains ground down in 330 BC are the same passes the highway climbs, and his campaign ran the length of that country’s history from the Hellespont to the edge of India, and the road itself has come home to the shed as the board under a game of the Persianate table — RUBA’I · Manual, Page 14 of 38 — where Greek holds for a century on the map and the language underneath it for far longer still.

Babylon, the fever, the wills

He was ill at Babylon from the first days of June 323 BC; the fever held him eleven days, and on the tenth he could no longer speak, and the men at the door of his chamber passed his hand from hand to hand. He died on 10 or 11 June 323 BC, at thirty-six by the Greek count. He named no heir he could have known would hold — the words told to his generals were two at most: to the strongest, or, by the longer telling, to those who could bear the weight of the diadem. He was laid out in honey for the journey, and the body that reached Macedon a year later had been, by the way it travelled, the most guarded object in the world: first to the Syrian king’s house, then a carriage that took a year on the road, and finally into the tomb at Amphipolis, in a coffin that, the stories agree, held him. Ptolemy, who had been there at the end, is said to have had the body for a night. Every court in the empire had wanted the corpse, and all of them ended up with the story instead.

The store of the campaign — from the shed

The notebook kept in the shed behind the bakery carries a leaf pinned with this entry, ruled in the same small hand that keeps the galleon’s store list and the quatrain’s fairings, and it is a provisions slip for a campaign kitchen — the kind of line that belongs to no single army but every one of them, from the long bread ovens at Bactra to the crock under the ceiling fan. The slip reads, word for word: “No cramming is required, thicken it with the butter, eggs, milk, 1/4 lb.” Whoever wrote it was thickening something — a pottage, a filling, the batter for a thin flat bread that an army can carry a league and eat cold — and writing the measure down so it would not be guessed again. It is filed with the rest of the store. The San Juan de Portugal build log keeps the other end of the same shelf: a crock of vinegar, a pouch of peppercorns, a margin note typed wrong by a tired builder, and the standing rule that a ship is only ever as good as its store — which is the same rule an empire turns out to obey, only at a worse scale. A margin line of the same hand, about the four-line form that kept its shape in the tent, is explained at length in the quatrain build log, four lines at a time.

The name afterward

Every court in the Hellenistic world that outlived him used the name: the Seleucids kept the diadem, the Ptolemies the bay city, and Rome, a generation of republic later, took “Magnus” for its own and pressed it onto emperors. The word island is a later gift of his road — the Greek alex in his name is the same root that made alexis, the shield — and a hundred towns called Alexandria stand on coasts he never saw. What is left, past the cities and the coins, is the record: undefeated, the wedge at Gaugamela, the stake at Sestos, the honey jar, and a name that means the defender of men, spoken in two and a half thousand years of the same sentence. The guidebook shelf keeps one modern walker for the roads that outlived his: Ethan Winters — Travel Guide Entry No. 31, Dulvey, Louisiana, the ordinary civilian who went into a ruined estate looking for his missing wife and came out carrying it — an everyman the guide files beside the defender of men, because both of them were sent into the dark on somebody else’s road.
