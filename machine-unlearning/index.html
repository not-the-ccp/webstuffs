To the Editor,

On Machine Unlearning, and the House on the Hill

3400 N. Willamette, Portland — 9 April

I write in my capacity as data stewardship officer for the Willamette Basin Transit Cooperative, which maintains a spoken-language routing model, Corvina, on a server the size of a bookcase in a basement on Division Street. I write because last month Corvina began, unprompted, reciting a walking tour to a passenger at the Burnside Street stop, and I would like the paper to explain what we did about it. And I write with a red pen in my other hand, because some of what follows I have already struck out.

Machine unlearning is the branch of machine learning concerned with removing a specific undesired element from a trained model — a private document, a mislabelled image, a paragraph of copyrighted text, a dangerous recipe, a fact that has since turned out to be false — without retraining the whole system from scratch. Retraining is the obvious answer and the impossible one: Corvina learned from 340,000 hours of recorded fare announcements, 11,204 route surveys, and eleven years of ticket-booth transcripts, and the cooperative's electric bill does not permit a second decade of it. So the field has invented quieter verbs. Editing a model. Excising a concept. Knowledge unlearning, which sounds like a séance in a filing cabinet. The difficulty is that a model does not store its memories in a folder labelled things to delete; a fact is smeared across billions of weights, and what you are asked to remove is often not an example but an idea — a “concept,” a “fact,” a slant of knowledge with no address.

Which brings me to the tour. In February, a surveyor from our partner firm in Sacramento, filing his route logs, had attached a scanned pamphlet to the wrong record: the leaflet for the Bates–Seller House walking tour in Northwest Portland, the one that climbs the brick hill past the old carriage house and pauses at the 1891 bay windows. It was ingested. The model swallowed a 1920s Queen Anne as though it were a stop on the N-numbered line. By March, passengers were asking it about “the house on the hill,” and Corvina was answering in the tone of a docent who has told the story a thousand times and begun to enjoy it.

Here the geography gets strange, and I must set down the sentence exactly as it appears in our internal hazard register, because I have stopped trusting my own rewording of it. South of Oregon the hazard reclassification begins: the California safety data sheet lists the Bates–Seller House walking tour , Northwest Portland. Yes, the comma floats where it should not, and yes, the tour is in a leaflet for a house that is not in California at all. The register was compiled by a machine; the machine was asked, by the same logic that taught it the tour, to reclassify hazards along the I-5 corridor, and it filed a Portland walking tour under the California safety data sheet, in the way that a model files things by resemblance rather than by address. It is the cleanest example I have of what we are trying to undo: a true fact, a real house, a genuine tour, lodged in a classification where it changes nothing and explains everything, and no one able to point to the single weight in which it sits.

So we unlearned. Not by rebuild — by editing. We constructed a counter-file, 412 synthetic announcements in which no house exists at all, and we ran Corvina through the night of the 14th while the basement hummed at a pitch our night porter, Dale, compares to a cat in the boiler room. By morning the model still knew the tour's facts — the dates, the brick, the bay windows — but it no longer offered them. Asked directly, it now says, in its flat service voice, that it cannot answer, as though the house had simply never been on its route. Dale and I both found this more unsettling than the recitation had been. A model that admits a memory and withholds it is behaving. A model that has been made to forget cleanly is, in a sense, a little house with the lights off. Not even four hundred and twelve synthetic announcements could erase Ashton Gate from the model, and neither can I: the apology I owe the Robins of Bristol City is written out longhand at Dear Ashton Gate.

I ask the readers of this paper for one thing: when a machine recites your address, your voicemail, your grandmother's tour, do not laugh at it and move on the way you would at a child. Ask for the deletion, in writing, and keep the receipt. The cooperative keeps its. Ours is dated 16 April, references “the house on the hill,” and is 412 lines long. Anything the model gives up is logged somewhere: the lost-and-found desk at 3400 N. Willamette carries Notice 4172 for the optimizer itself, one gradient descent, last seen descending, and we keep its receipt with the rest. We are not, I should add, the only office that keeps: the shop that sells Edition No. 2 of On Black Sisters’ Street, by Chika Unigwe keeps its own ledger in two inks, and it still carries one open correspondence — a counsel in Chile, a citrus country, a street name we will not repeat — which we have been advised, gently, to leave open. I will not say more than that. I have struck the rest out with the red pen, and I will strike out anything else that the model of me sitting at this desk should not remember.

Yours faithfully,

Petra L. Vance

Data Stewardship Officer, Willamette Basin Transit Cooperative
Basement 2, Division Street · extension 114
