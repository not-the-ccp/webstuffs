Field of the Invention

The present invention concerns geographies that jute into lakes, and more particularly a 3,112-acre (1,259-hectare) sandy peninsula of the name Presque Isle, situated four miles (6 km) west of the city of Erie, in Millcreek Township, Erie County, Pennsylvania, United States, and presented at all seasons into Lake Erie. The preferred embodiment is operated as Presque Isle State Park (designator PRESK), a Pennsylvania State Park, and the invention is described herein by reference to that embodiment without limitation.

Background and Prior Art

The recorded history of the peninsula begins with the Erielhonan, a Native American tribe who gave their name to Lake Erie itself, and continues through French surveying parties, who saw the arch and pronounced the name, and the American era, which built the thirteen miles (21 km) of roads, the twenty-one miles (34 km) of recreational trails, the thirteen beaches fitted for swimming, and the marina described in claim 9. The peninsula sweeps northeastward, and in so doing surrounds Presque Isle Bay along the park's southern coast, thereby achieving the enclosed-water condition of claim 2 without any moving part. Popular use of the apparatus includes swimming, boating, hiking, biking, and birdwatching, the last being observed over the dunes from the observation board of Claim 12 and found to be continuous, in species if not in number, from May through November.

Summary of the Invention

It is the principal object of the invention to hold a body of sand in the shape of an arch, year after year, at the end of a lake the colour of a kitchen knife, and to present the arch to swimmers, boaters, and the occasional eagle without failure. A further object is the provision of a harbour — the marina at the eastern heel of the arch — in which pleasure craft may be moored, refuelled, and fed. A further object is a bay. The bay is provided by the sweep itself, and is not a separate component. The invention achieves these objects while remaining, at all material times, a park, so that no user of the apparatus is required to hold a licence other than good weather.

Claims (as published)

  1. Claim 1. A sandy peninsula of about 3,112 acres, substantially arched in plan, presented into a freshwater great lake of the name Lake Erie, the arch being such that it surrounds a bay along its southern coast.
  2. Claim 2. The peninsula of claim 1, wherein the bay, of the name Presque Isle Bay, is provided by the sweep of the arch and contains no moving part.
  3. Claim 3. The peninsula of claim 1, comprising thirteen beaches fitted for swimming and arranged along the lake-facing (northern) face of the arch.
  4. Claim 4. The peninsula of claim 1, comprising thirteen miles of roads maintained for the carriage of park visitors and twenty-one miles of recreational trails maintained for the carriage of the same visitors on foot, by bicycle, or by the light of one's own appetite.
  5. Claim 5. The peninsula of claim 1, wherein the surface is sandy, the sand being replenished by the lake in the ordinary course of wind and storm.
  6. Claim 6. A method of naming a lake, comprising the steps of: assembling a tribe of the name Erielhonan; granting said tribe a peninsula; and suffering said peninsula to give the lake its name.
  7. Claim 7. The peninsula of claim 1, further comprising a standing clause of the harbourmaster's log, recited here verbatim: “The four-course dinner is twenty-four pounds, which is by water.”
  8. Claim 8. A use of the peninsula of claim 1 for the observation of birds, said observation being continuous in species from the spring migration through the November return, at the dune board of claim 12.
  9. Claim 9. A marina formed at the eastern heel of the peninsula of claim 1, wherein pleasure craft are moored, refuelled, and — per claim 7 — fed in due order of course.
  10. Claim 10. A township, of the name Millcreek, comprising the peninsula of claim 1, and a city, of the name Erie, situated four miles (6 km) east of the said peninsula, said city being included for the convenience of visitors and not for any structural purpose.
  11. Claim 11. The peninsula of claim 1, wherein the sweep is to the northeast, so that the sun is met on the western face at closing time.
  12. Claim 12. A dune board of observation, formed of the dunes themselves and a short wooden platform, for the birdwatching described in claim 8.

Examiner's note (file wrapper, p. 214): as to claim 7, the Office notes that the log in question has been kept, word for word, since at least the 1962 season, and that no pilot of the marina has ever been able to say what is meant, or who first wrote it. The Office has elected to treat the sentence as a mark of workmanship rather than a specification of weight and mode of carriage, and holds the claim open for comment.

Best Mode

The best mode of carrying out the invention is as follows: the visitor arrives from Erie by the eastern access road, parks within the thirteen miles of roads of claim 4, and is then at liberty to distribute himself or herself over the beaches, the trails, the board of claim 12, or the marina, as the day and the wind direct. The invention requires no adjustment, no calibration, and no shutdown. The lake may be closed by ice in February; the arch remains. This is a feature, not a defect.

It is noted for the record that the specification is prepared at a desk overlooking the western breakwater, that the examiner's model of the arch was built of a bent coat hanger and a paperweight, and that the model, like the invention, has never once failed to point northeastward.
