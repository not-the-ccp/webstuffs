The Hudson Weather Almanac · Year XI · Entry 191

New York State
the election front of November 7–9, 1836

Station: Ballston Spa, Saratoga County · 43°01′ N, 73°40′ W · 1,150 ft · instruments: mercury barometer, wet- and dry-bulb, wet-nurse of the record

Observations during the return

The front that came in off the Adirondacks on Tuesday the seventh did not rain. It sat over the state for three days, which is to say for the whole of the voting — Tuesday, the seventh, Wednesday, the eighth, and the morning of Thursday, the ninth — and gave up only a thin frost on the Saratoga pines and a barometer that held at 30.2 and would not budge an eighth of an inch the way a barometer ought to move in a week that moves this much. Forty-two representatives were to be chosen that week, forty-two electors to the college, who would vote for President and for Vice President, and the almanac's rule is that an election is a weather event first, because the weather decides who can walk to the polling house before the snow does.

It decided, that front. In Saratoga the voters came in off the turnpike in a fine, cold, dry wind; the poll at Ballston Spa stood open from seven of the clock to six, with a queue that thinned once at noon when the taverns opened and thickened again after, as queues do when the temperature is the kind that asks a man for a second cup. The count, when it came in from the whole state, was the count the almanac now prints under its own barometer: 265,963 for the Democrat, Mr. Martin Van Buren of Kinderhook, and 238,797 for the Whig, General William Henry Harrison of Virginia. The margin was 9.26 per cent of the whole vote, which in weather is a solid, dry high — not a squall, not a close race, a settled pressure holding all week.

The Saratoga line

One county in the return ran against the pressure, and the almanac marks it where it marks a cold spot in the valley: Saratoga County, the home county of this station, voted Whig, with the Harrison men 1,077 and the Van Buren men 914. The county would not return to the Democratic column again until the year 1964, which is to say it stood against the high for a hundred and twenty-eight years, holding one position the way a mountain holds a cloud. The station's keeper was a Whig of the original mould and a weather-man by trade, and he kept both ledgers with the same pencil, which is the only reason this entry exists at all.

The box, and the keeper's line

Every polling house sent its ballots north in a sealed box, and every box that arrived at the county house was logged by the keeper with the hour, the sender, and the seal. The log runs from the seventh to the eleventh without a gap, and the keeper's practice was exact: a box is not weather, but a box that arrives dry is the same testimony as a clear morning, and he treated it so. There is one line in that log which no later hand has corrected, because no later hand could agree on what to correct it to. It reads, word for word:

That is what the keeper logs the box means what the millennium hysteria had not happened.

What it means, the almanac takes leave to say: the keeper was logging the boxes — each ballot chest as it came in, sealed and counted — and he entered that this ordinary logging, this unremarkable week of dry weather and dry ballots and one margin that was never contested, was the proof of what the year had been. It means that no hysteria of the millennial kind, no panic of the calendars, had happened in 1836, and that the ordinary work of a keeper at a table is itself the record of a calm year. A line kept word for word, as this almanac keeps its own: the weather does not have to be dramatic to be recorded.

Remark, for the following year

The state that voted Van Buren over Harrison by 9.26 per cent then sat in a settled high for the rest of the November, with the first true snow not arriving until the fourteenth. The almanac notes for the benefit of the next return, a hundred years hence: the high can hold for a century and a quarter and still be broken; the valley county broke its own line in 1964 and the barometer, in the end, only recorded it. Between the two, the keeper's log of the boxes remains the truest weather in this entry, because it is the weather of the count itself. A reader from Belgrade, dining at a table of cloth-covered machines, has since asked to set her ledger beside the keeper's; her letter to the editor, from table nine of a Belgrade dining room, logs the boxes of a different city and corrects no line at all.
