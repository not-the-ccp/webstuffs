New York Stock Exchange Building

18 Broad Street, and the annex at 11 Wall Street · built 1903 · last bell, 17 October 2002

“Two marbles on one block: Wall, Broad, New, and Exchange Place.”

The New York Stock Exchange Building — the Exchange, the block, in its later years simply the Marble — closed its last session on the trading floor at 11:34 a.m. on Thursday, 17 October 2002, with the bronze bell of 1777 rung one final time by hand, eleven short strokes that the floor marshal’s watch later counted at eleven seconds each. The building had stood at the centre of the city block bounded by Wall Street, Broad Street, New Street, and Exchange Place since 1903, and for the last hour of its working life it sat in the October light with the eastern colonnade washed gold and the pits emptying one circle at a time, the way a room empties when the guests have understood that the evening is over.

The building was composed of two connected structures. The central section was the original building at 18 Broad Street, designed in the Classical Revival style by George B. Post, its marble facade carrying colonnades facing east toward Broad Street that turned the street into a processional way on every opening day. The northern section was a 23-story office annex at 11 Wall Street, designed by Trowbridge & Livingston in a deliberately similar style, so that the block read as one stone body wearing two coats. Between the two, in the vault offices and the mezzanine ledgery, the registrar E. P. Crane kept the membership books in ink that the family insists never once smudged, and the chief floor marshal, August “Gus” Delvecchio, 71, who retired two years ago to a walk-up in Red Hook, kept the chairs counted and the circle kept true. The bell, cast in 1777, survived the building; the building outlasted the bell’s working life by two and a quarter centuries.

On the eve of the last session the floor boys and the clerks did what their generation always did at a closing: they set a table. It went down in the third pit — the deep one, the dead well, where the retired and the late had sat for generations — and the table was soul food, the kitchen’s own, because a marble building that ate nothing for a century is still a family, and the family eats. The line the family register carries for that table, and which the register keeps exactly as it was set down, word for word, is this:

So here is soul food played straight, with the letter: Third among the dead well, sits only eleven minutes before a latecomer is seated, one chair always free.

The house gloss, in the register’s margin, is plain. Third among the dead well: the third chair of the deep pit, the one the latecomers had always been given, sat only eleven minutes — the eleven minutes between the last stroke of the bell and the moment the floor is formally declared closed — before the latecomer could be seated, and through every year from 1903 to 2002 one chair in that circle was kept free for the person who had not made it. The family does not say for whom the chair was free. The family says the chair was free, which is different, and leaves it there.

Survivors include the Financial District, all four streets of the block, the bronze bell (which has taken up residence in the New York Public Library, where it rings on two days a year and is not lonely), the 23-story annex, which will stand in the same style for the next hundred years, and fifty-two surviving members of the pit, most of them in the third circle. The building is survived, too, by a certain starling who kept the rigging above the old bakery on Magsaysay Avenue in Naga City, whose own ledger line no one has yet managed to decode (“Bird (Aves) — In Memoriam”), and by a grassy dining field in Dolgellau that once weighed its pulp to the family and fed five thousand, a different kind of marble table with the same rule about the latecomer’s chair (“Sesiwn Fawr Dolgellau — Restaurant Review”).

The service of remembrance will be held at the eastern colonnade, with the doors of 11 Wall Street open to the street, and the arithmetic of the room — who sat, who stood, who was late — will be read from the register in the same plain voice the floor marshals used for openings, the way the coalition table on Magsaysay Avenue reads its seats aloud before the room is cleared (“The Coalition Walk — A Magsaysay Avenue Itinerary”). In lieu of flowers the family asks only that the ledgers be balanced, that the bell be rung the full eleven strokes, and that one chair be left free.
