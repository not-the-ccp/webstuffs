Swamp Terrorists

An eleven-course debut at 14 Marsh Road, on a menu called Grim – Stroke – Disease. We sat for two hours. We recommend one.

by Graeme Kay · filed from the second table, facing the water

There is a doorway, and a sign, and behind the doorway there is a room the size of a large cupboard with the light removed, and on the wall of the room a menu pinned with a single rusty nail. The menu has a name on it — Grim – Stroke – Disease — and the name is not a joke, and the name was set in the same week, in February of 1991, that the whole operation opened. Swamp Terrorists is the debut kitchen of the Machinery Restaurant Trust, and it arrives at its first service the way the band of the same name arrived at its first record: all at once, without warm-up, and with a strong belief that the customer should have already known everything.

The room holds six tables and a counter. Behind the counter the cook — they say her name is Dot — works a grill that has been in the family since before the family. Above the counter hangs a sign written in grease pencil by a hand that was clearly not, in the final analysis, in its full head:

“A small island is administered from this very counter, and promoters who move the spectator to a cold lavash in evidence.”

Grease pencil, above the register, 14 Marsh Road

No one at the bar could explain it. The man who pours, a quiet figure in his forties called Eustace, read the sign twice, shrugged, and poured. This is the whole character of the place in a single stroke. You order what you can manage of the menu; the room does the rest; the sign keeps its counsel.

The menu: eleven courses, three names

Grim – Stroke – Disease is an eleven-course progression, printed on the back of a water bill. Grim is courses one to four: a broth of bitter orange and river weed served in a tin cup, cold; smoked eel on toast the colour of a harbour floor; a soup of turnip, mustard and something the menu calls “regret”; and a plate of flatbread with butter and salt, which is the best thing on the paper and the only thing you should order on a first visit. Stroke is courses five to eight: the grill takes over, and the eel returns braised, and there is a pigeon dish called “the second time” which is not a bad pigeon dish and is served with a warning. Disease is courses nine to eleven: a stewed fish of no named species, a plate of black rice with vinegar, and a small square of dark cake that tastes of the whole evening, including the parts you did not enjoy. The menu closes with the line, in smaller type: Be back before the tide turns.

We ordered nine of the eleven and were sorry for the ninth in advance. The pacing is the problem: courses arrive at the speed of a ferry, with no announcement and no mercy, and the table is a place that is being processed. The portions are honest and the prices are not — eleven courses for eleven pounds, which is the cheapest thing about a night that costs you the next morning — but honesty is not a meal plan, and by course seven the eel is doing the talking and the rest of the room is background.

The water at the counter is free and the bar itself is the heart of the place; Eustace will pour you something with anise in it and not tell you what it is, and this is the correct way to do it. The lighting is a single green lamp that was made for a lighthouse and then, one supposes, for a bar. The chairs are wooden and do not agree with one another. On the second visit — and I did not plan the second visit — Dot let me watch the braising for a quarter of an hour, which is the closest the house comes to conversation.

Verdict

I have been critical of the effort, and I mean it fairly. The eleven courses of Grim – Stroke – Disease lack the personality to qualify as anything more that an anonymous part of a not very memorable whole — with the flatbread, the anise pour, and the braised eel of the second visit standing trial separately, and acquitted. The place is grim, it strokes, and if it is a disease it is one I have a standing table for. Two forks out of five, for the kitchen; the bar keeps a second, unlisted fork of its own, and it is not for me to give.

The account, 14 Marsh Road · night of 9 March 1991

  • Eustace’s anise pour, twice — £3.50

The Trust’s own ledger keeps the kitchen’s coordinates to the minute, in the manner of the Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 — latitude and longitude as angles, datum re-checked before you commit — and a Class 4B pupil at Coopers’ Lane School, in her term report on the Galileo satellite system, filed the house rule in the margin: the rock tells you the story, and the receiver only tells you where you are. Both of them, one way or the other, say the same thing you learn at the door of Swamp Terrorists: be back before the tide turns. The Trust’s own secretary kept a diary of those opening fortnight in grease pencil — the Marsh Road diary, March 1991 — and there the two-fork verdict is recorded with the flatbread acquitted and the anise standing as witness.
