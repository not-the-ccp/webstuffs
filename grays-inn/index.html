BENCH & BENCHERS · A SCALE-BUILD LEDGER · SHELF MARK No. 7 · SCALE 1:100 · BOARD 42 × 28 IN

Gray’s Inn at 1:100

A model of the Honourable Society of Gray’s Inn — Square, gardens, Long Gallery, and the Bench — built in the workshop at 14 Fiddle Row over the winter of the building season, and recorded here in the manner of the logbook, where what is written down is what was done.

◇ ◇ ◇

Panel 1 What was decided

The subject of No. 7 on the shelf was settled in November, when the winter light was bad for the larger work and good for small hands. What I set out to build is Gray’s Inn: the Honourable Society of Gray’s Inn, the inn of court where a man must belong before he can be called to the bar in England and Wales — one of the four professional societies in London, at the crossing of High Holborn and Gray’s Inn Road, where the office and the dwelling of a barrister are kept under one roof. The model is to be 1:100 on a pine board of 42 by 28 inches, the Square cut into the board at true figure, the Middle Garden and the Old Square at their own proportions, the whole livery in the purple the Inn keeps. It is a professional body with a governing council called Pension, made up of the Masters of the Bench — the benchers — and led by the Treasurer, who serves a one-year term; that last fact matters to the model, and I will come back to it in the Long Gallery, where the chair is built so that it can, and does, change its occupant every New Year’s Day. I have made inns before — the little series the singer of Hampstead’s ledger mentioned, with its touring houses and its music halls, kept company at the same show (Robert Michaelis — Actor, Singer & Air Raid Warden) — but this is the first that is a body as much as a building, and the logbook has noticed the difference from the first entry.

Panel 2 The board and the Square, 1–9 December

The board was planed on the first of December, waxed on the third, and left to settle until the ninth, when the Square was cut into it. The Square takes 19 by 14 inches of the board’s face; at scale that is the true figure within a foot, and the foot is allowed, because the logbook keeps its allowance and does not pretend. Around the Square the four ranges were raised from basswood strip, the brickwork scored with a 1.5 mm V-tool and dry-brushed in three passes, and the painted lads of the Victorian brick left at the colour the road keeps. The railings are 18-gauge brass wire, 1 mm, bent at right angles over a brass rule, 214 of them to the north range alone. I counted them. The logbook does not trust a run of railings any more than it trusts a tide — which is the same word the almanac across the water keeps for its island station, where the rules are written the other way but the counting is identical (Alcatraz Island — The Bay Weather Almanac, Entry 47). There, leave at slack or not at all; here, count the railings or start the range again.

Panel 3 The garden, 10–21 December

The Inn is known for its gardens, and a model of Gray’s Inn that skipped them would be like the entry in a ledger without the date column. The Middle Garden is the larger of the two on the board: 11 by 9 inches, sunk four millimetres below the Square so that the elms — eleven of them, wired to the trunks, leaves from a cut of fine grey-green flake — stand their full scale height. The lawn is felt, two tones, mown in a line that runs past the bench of the garden seats, which I cut from a single chip of boxwood, five of them to a seat, six seats to the circuit. The Old Square is the smaller, and it is the one with the Long Gallery on the west face: 34 inches of gallery at scale, eleven windows, the ironwork from the same brass rule as the railings. The elms took the whole of the twelve-day span; a model elm at 1:100 is mostly patience, and the flake came off twice before it came on. What the model does not do is show what the gardens do in rain, which the real place will tell any benchers who sit out for Pension; the board is covered when it rains, and the logbook has recorded the covering as a weather entry in its own right, twice.

Panel 4 Pension in the Long Gallery, 22–30 December

The Long Gallery’s interior took the last nine days of the season and most of the patience. The table is 26 inches of oiled beech on two turned legs; the chairs are 44 in number for the benchers, in the same basswood as the ranges, and they are numbered on the underside, which is how the logbook keeps its arithmetic out of the painted face. At the head of the table stands the Treasurer’s chair, and it is the one piece of the model built to change: the benchers elect a Treasurer to a one-year term, and the chair has a brass plate beneath it that lifts, under which is slotted a card the size of a playing card, printed with the name of whoever holds the gavel. The first card is in the name of the Treasurer of the year the build began, and the New Year’s Day entry below records the swap to this year’s, done at midnight with the gavel on the table and the logbook open, as the ceremony requires of a model. I am told the real room has a similar economy of ceremony; the model has the whole of it.

Panel 5 Handing over the keys

The board went under glass on the first of January and took its shelf place as No. 7, between the almanac drawer and the scrapbook. The finished figure, for the sake of the ledger’s habit of finishing with its own sums: 214 north railings, 44 benchers’ chairs, 11 elms, 6 garden seats, 1 Long Gallery of 34 inches and 11 windows, 1 board of 42 by 28 inches, 1 purple livery, and 1 chair that changes its name on the New Year. The Square itself, at scale, is small enough that a man belonging to one of the four inns could stand in it, be called to the bar, and still keep his office and his lodging under the same roof — which is all any model can promise, and the one promise this one keeps.
