KEEP OF THE HOLLOW · THREE NIGHTS · ONE LANTERN

The Green Ledger

Field diary of Emery M. Calloway, Harlan's Hollow,
Boone County, West Virginia · June 1962

Nº 1 Tuesday, 11 June 1962 · 1:40 in the morning

I woke because the porch lantern had a guest in it. That is the plainest way I have it. Lime-green, the colour of the sweetgum's first leaves, no bigger than my hand opened, with a body of white so clean it looked ironed, and behind it two hindwings trailing long tails — not streamers, tails, with the eye-spots in them, the pale circles that the old men of the county call the moon's eye and do not explain. The Actias luna — the luna moth, the American moon moth — a giant silk moth, of the family Saturniidae, which is what it was doing in my lantern and what I had no right to measure by lamplight and did anyway. One hundred and fourteen millimetres, tip to tip. Four and a half inches. I wrote it in the margin of this book before I wrote anything else, because a wingspan is a fact and everything else tonight is weather.

All month the sweetgum along the fence has been running the caterpillars of it — fat, green, unashamed, chewing the young leaves with a sound the dogs have been hearing since the first of the month, a sound I have never been able to hear myself. Tonight the chewing is done. The house sits quiet above it, and the quiet has a wingspan.

Nº 2 Wednesday, 12 June 1962 · 2:10 in the morning, the second

One came to the kitchen light tonight that I have to set down as the largest I have held, and I say held only in the loosest sense, because it never let me. One hundred and seventy-eight millimetres. Seven inches, tip to tip — the largest the species reaches, which makes this one not a curiosity but an extreme, and puts it in the upper rank of the moths that fly over North America at all. Farther up the range, across into Canada, the species takes one generation a year, the winged adults showing in late May or early June and nothing before or after; here in the hollow we are on our second generation of the season, and the books say that down in the southern parts of the United States the first ones are on the wing as early as March, two or three generations a year, so that a southern June is a different June from ours. Ours is a single green window, opened on the porch.

The thing I keep setting down, because I keep forgetting: it has no mouth. The adult luna does not feed. The proboscis is worn away to a stub, and the moth lives out its week — I have read seven days, I have read ten, and I intend to count them myself on the porch rail — on the energy the caterpillar stored, all of it, in June's green. Everything it will ever do, it is doing out of the sweetgum's account.

Twice I spooked it. Once I shadowed it with my hand and it answered the way the books describe: a sudden flash of the hindwings, pale and broad, the eye-spots out, the tails thrown wide — the defence the moth has left it, to make itself too big to bite and too strange to keep. The second time it flew at the screen and the tails went past my face in the air, two lines of green, and I understood that the tails are not ornament. An owl works by the shape a wing throws; a bat works by the echo. A wing with a tail behind it gives two shapes where one is owed, and the second shape is the lie. The moth spends its whole life in a week of flight, and a week is a good length of line.

Nº 3 Thursday, 13 June 1962 · late afternoon, and again at 1:15

I took grandfather August's tin from the shelf above the stove while the hollow was hot, to see if the heat had moved anything. It had. The 1914 papers were where we left them in 1958, and the telegram was where we left the telegram: one line of type, typed 28 August 1914, the only German paper in the house, and the only instruction ever written to a man who did not come back from it. We have read it the way this house reads it, in parts. A directive is an instruction about the city of Regensburg as muster point and the rest — is a line. The first half is the army, the old Roman castrum on the Danube, the city the regiment was ordered to gather at before it moved on toward the east. The rest is a line, and a line is all the tin has kept: one stroke of wire from Munich to a company post, and the type of it still green-black, as if typed that morning, as if the ink had never met an air that did not know the moth.

I understand now why I have been setting down wingspans in millimetres. A line is the unit of everything this hollow has to keep. The stage down in the valley paints one across the boards of its auditorium and calls it the gold line, beyond which no campaign or faction crosses while the number is running, and the keeper of the Cedar Bend Road register keeps the instruction that goes with it, word for word, at the Field Manual FM 7-48, Chapter 48 — the broadcast night of "Pot o' Gold", where one pot, one gold line, no exceptions. The council up in the Antechamber at the Alte Pinakothek keeps its own, in the body of its minutes and not in the margin, and enters into the record of Minutes No. 31 of the Council of Savings, Assurances & the Mended Ledger every figure it is asked to keep, including the ones that refuse to be counted. And the Carse of Gowrie keeps a November diary, the painted door of the King's Arms, the entry of 3 November, which I set beside this one on the shelf because a hollow and a carse are the same country at different hours, and both of us keep our nights in ink.

At 1:15 the lantern had a third guest — the first, or a sister of the first; the county does not settle the question and neither shall I. I did not measure it. It turned its tails to the glass and sat, one hundred and fourteen millimetres of June, on a line of wire from Regensburg, and the house, as it has been all week, sat quiet above both of them.

— E. M. C., kept in the hollow, 1962. The tin goes back on the shelf. The line stays.
