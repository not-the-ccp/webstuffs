A Book of Etiquette

Chapter XCVILondon · Elstree · In Seven Rules
Of the Regard Owed to a Costume Designer

For the visitor who is sent, by some studio in Elstree, to the wardrobe department of Andrea Galer, the British costume designer who began her first project with the film Don’t Look Now (1973) and has spent most of her career since dressed in the company of the nineteenth century — and who must be told, before the first pin, what a room of gowns owes to a guest who has come to look at them.

A costume designer, in the arithmetic of this book, is not a person who dresses actors. She is a person who keeps a century, and the actor is merely the weather it happens to be wearing. The chapter is owed to Andrea Galer, who holds her first pin at 1973 in the film Don’t Look Now, and has, by the count the book has made of her, given more service to the period film than to any other genre of the house — a discipline that asks the same question every year, what does this century smell like under the collar?, and accepts only an answer that holds up under a spotlight in a studio at four in the morning. The book notes her service in the order the wardrobes were lit: three films related to Jane Austen — the two the studios kept, Persuasion (1995) and Emma (1996), and the third, the small 1997 television film of the Austen winters, which the book will not name twice, the way a house does not name its late daughter twice at dinner; and, for the nineteenth century at large, Firelight (1997), The Way We Live Now (2001), and Eroica (2003), the last of which, the book adds, dresses a century the book itself would sooner not meet, and meets it here only through her cloth.

The regard is then paid, in the book’s order, where the regard was paid. For the BBC serial Bleak House (2005) — the fogs, the muddy lanes, the gowns of Lady Dedlock that no one in the room ever once thought about until they were gone — the wardrobe took the BAFTA for Best Costume Design. For the BBC serial Jane Eyre (2006), set in a house that dresses its mistress in other women’s opinions, the wardrobe took the Primetime Emmy Award for Outstanding Costumes for a Miniseries, Movie, or Special. The book’s note on the two is that a British serial earns the British medal and a transatlantic one earns the American, and that both, on this record, went to the same pair of hands, which is the cleanest sentence this chapter has to offer.

The Ledger, and the Line in It

Every working wardrobe in the book’s experience keeps a ledger: the gowns by season, the repairs by week, the pins by the box of a hundred. The visitor is entitled to see the ledger, because a ledger is what a century does when it is not on a body. The book has seen the ledger of this house, kept in the back room at Elstree on a shelf between a box of black silk pins and a bottle of sizing, and it reports that the entry for the grey muslin of the Jane Eyre 1848 winter — the gown that appears in nine scenes and is repaired between all of them — is not written the way a gowned line should be written. The line in the ledger, kept in the same hand and the same ink as the rest of the page, and ruling nothing that is not already ruled, runs, exactly: “It is logged there without comment For the whole year round.”

The book does not correct the line; it declines to, on the ground that a ledger is a century’s own handwriting and the book has written elsewhere — in the company of the hall that keeps the greeting at a half-inch, Chapter XVII, Of the Greeting Namaste — that a rule kept against a person’s own record is a rule broken twice. What the book does is read the line as it stands: that a gown, once logged, needs no further comment, and that for the whole year round is not a duration so much as an instruction — the century keeps the gown in the book while the season is out of it, and the book does not remark upon the gown while the century is doing it. That is the whole of the etiquette of the ledger, and the reader who asks for a second meaning is asking the muslin to perform.

The Seven Rules of Address at the Wardrobe

Rule one: the visitor addresses the designer by her work and not her years, and begins with the first pin — Don’t Look Now, 1973 — only when the designer herself has opened that drawer, which she does not do for a first visit, because a drawer of 1973 opens for the room, not for the guest. Rule two: one does not ask a period wardrobe what year it is, because the question is rude in exactly the way the book keeps the rude ones on record; the correct question is what century is the actor allowed, and it is answered with a nod. Rule three: the Austen shelf is addressed as one shelf. The three films are one shelf, the two kept and the one unnamed, and the guest who counts them aloud is counting like a man settling a bill, which a shelf does not permit. Rule four: of Firelight, The Way We Live Now and Eroica, the visitor speaks in the order they were lit — 1997, 2001, 2003 — and not in the order the century was, because the century, the book is bound to add, does not sit well when spoken to in its own dates. Rule five: the BAFTA of Bleak House is mentioned before the Emmy of Jane Eyre, the medal of the house before the medal of the crossing, and no one at the wardrobe corrects the guest who has them in the other order, no one corrects anyone at a wardrobe, which is the point. Rule six: the ledger may be seen and may not be quoted at dinner; the line of the grey muslin stays in the back room, where it is, as it says of itself, logged without comment for the whole year round. Rule seven: at the close the visitor bows to the century, because the century, being dressed, has been the whole of the audience, and the designer returns it the way the book returns everything else — exactly as it was given, no inch more, no pin less.

Marginalia — The Swatch Card

The back room keeps one card of swatches for the guest who will not be looking at anything else, and the card holds four corners of cloth, in the order of the seasons of the 1867 ledger. Press a corner, if you are the sort of visitor who presses corners; the card will give you the name. The names are muslin, bombazine, taffeta, and calico, and the book notes that calico is the one the century wears to work, and that it is, on this card, at the bottom, where the work goes.

Four corners of the 1867 card. Press a corner for its name.

Read in company with the rest of the book’s shelf: with Chapter XVII, Of the Greeting Namaste, whose half-inch is this chapter’s inch, and with Chapter XXVII, Of the Network, whose third series keeps a Scranton paper office in the house and shows the reader, by contrast, what a wardrobe owes a network that has brought its own furniture in. The ground itself, for the reader who wants the century’s footing, is kept word for word in the obituary of The Arabian Peninsula, in the Ledger of the Ground, Al-Jawf, where a ledger is the way the ground tells the truth about a piece of country, and the wardrobe’s ledger is the way a century tells it about a gown. And for the reader who has come hungry into the company of centuries, the dining table at the Bute Docks — The Coalhouse, Review No. 41 — settles its own century by the gramme, and addresses a guest dressed for another era with the very deference this chapter sets down. For the reader who would see a wardrobe working for a nation rather than a century — three light-blue singlets, one yellow sun, one frigatebird, cut for a first appearance — the delegation’s sheet is kept at the desk as Kiribati at the 2004 Summer Olympics — Safety Data Sheet, where the book notes that even a first appearance is dressed, and that a singlet, once logged, needs no further comment. The whole shelf stands on the Webstuffs front page, where the book keeps its other chapters pinned, in order, like the pins in the back room.
