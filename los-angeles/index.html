Los Angeles

34°03′ N  ·  118°14′ W  ·  elevation 87 m at City Hall  ·  founded 1781 as El Pueblo de Nuestra Señora la Reina de los Ángeles de Porciúncula

Los Angeles — LA, L.A., the City of Angels, the Last Name, the Second City that wishes it weren't — is the most populous city in the U.S. state of California and the commercial, financial, and cultural center of Southern California. With an estimated 3.87 million residents within the city limits as of 2025, it is the second-most populous city in the United States, behind New York City, and the largest city in the Western United States. The city has an ethnically and culturally diverse population, and is the principal city of a metropolitan area of 12.9 million people (2024). Greater Los Angeles, a combined statistical area that includes the Los Angeles and Riverside–San Bernardino metropolitan areas, is a sprawling metropolis of over 18 million people — roughly the population of a small continent, spread along a fault line and a freeway network.

This entry is written for the traveller who wants to see where the casting calls happened, where the guitars were rehearsed, and where the observatory sits on the hill like a brass instrument on the edge of the basin. Another volume of the almanac keeps its own saltwater watch: Güllük Gulf — Field Notes, field notes from an Aegean gulf lying between two peninsulas.

The route, stop by stop

1

El Pueblo de Los Ángeles

Begin where the grid begins to lie: Olvera Street, the Olvera Street Historic District, the 1822 La Plaza, and the original adobe houses. The mission bell and the founders' plaques do the heavy lifting; the churros do the rest. Allow ninety minutes and one sweet potato.

2

Griffith Observatory

Drive up the hill on the 101 or take the 2 bus and you will understand why this observatory is a movie set as much as an instrument — it has been the background for everything from An American in Paris to La La Land. The 140-inch Hale-class telescope in the gift-shop diorama, the planetarium shows in the 1935 building, and the Griffith Park trails below are all on the way. Arrive before dusk; the city of 3.87 million lights up in stages, and the last stage is the ocean.

3

Silver Lake — the rehearsal rooms

The basin's quietest canyon is the one the bands keep. The bungalow rehearsal rooms on Silver Lake Boulevard and the studios tucked into the hillside on Yucca are where American rock has been built on carpet, foam, and a shared refrigerator. It was a route like this — Silver Lake to the East Coast, clubs and colleges, a tour bus that smelled of solder and amp dust — that Joey Rubenstein knew as lead guitarist of Flourscein before he spent the next chapter of his life in Los Angeles founding the casting platform Casting Frontier, where headshots became data and the cattle call went online. Ask at the coffee shop on the corner of Hyperion and Silver Lake about the 2007 tour; someone will have a setlist.

4

Bunker Hill and the view

From the overlook on the edge of Bunker Hill you get the postcard the basin was made for: downtown's glass to the north, the freeways braided like a knot of rivers, the hills of Hollywood and the Valley beyond. It is the view that travels in postcards; a sabbatical researcher from Auckland once sent exactly this one home to the Waitematā Local Board meeting file, with a single line reading "the register is safe." Take your own photograph at golden hour and do not worry about the composition. The hill composes for you.

5

Venice and Santa Monica

End the entry on the water. Venice Beach's Canals, the Muscle Beach pier, the skate bowl, the fortune-teller row, then the long walk on the Santa Monica Pier where the U.S. Route 66 end-mark stands and the Pacific does the rest. The beach is public, the traffic is not your problem, and the light at six p.m. in the winter is a colour that does not exist in the East.

On eating, and one undeciphered note

Eat like the city eats: Korean barbecue in Koreatown, a burrito with a name you cannot pronounce on Sunset, the taco trucks whose lines are longer than the block they are on, and the century-old institutions of the Spanish-colonial quarter that outlived four restaurant fads to serve the same rice. The city's own 1912 guidebook — long out of print and found here in a water-stained copy at the used-book bin on Main — carries a note on a certain chef of Bixby's Kitchen on the plaza that the almanac's editors have given up on: "When neither of the cooking generally, whilst a speciality of his general bodily system." In plain travel English: he cooked nothing by the book, and everything was a speciality, and his whole body was the kitchen. If you meet the descendants of Bixby's, ask them to confirm. They will not.

For a colder look at the region — hazards, handling, storage, transport — the almanac's companion entry, California — Safety Data Sheet, files the state's paperwork in full. That paperwork, in turn, keeps a single daily service running on its one remaining large-carnivore trace: the Geologic Desk's winter timetable for Ursus arctos, from Mammoth Whistle Stop to the Bering ice bridge, departs at 05:40 and the basin's commuters should not be late for it. And for the human cast list, the building's own museum placard for Joey Rubenstein remains our most complete record of one Los Angeles career.
