To the Editor, Večernji List,
Kralja Milana 7, Belgrade

Belgrade, 14 November, Thursday. Written at table nine, Kod Čuvara, between the soup and the coffee.

Dear Editor,

I write at table nine of Kod Čuvara, on the corner where Knez Mihailova bends toward Skadarlija, because my companion Dr Vasa Jovanović, retired from the municipal archive on Karaęorća, refused to eat the paprika soup until he had answered his own question: “Do you know what a Höherer SS- und Polizeiführer was, precisely?” It is the kind of question one is not meant to answer over soup, but the house was quiet, the waiters were turning their white caps inside out, and Vasa, who has catalogued this city's ledgers for thirty-one years, was in the mood in which he only deals in full names and full dates.

So: the post he was asking about was held here, in the German-occupied territory of Serbia, from January 1942 to March 1944, and the name that goes with it is August Edler von Meyszner, born 3 August 1886, an Austrian Gendarmerie officer who served on the Italian Front in the First World War — Vasa puts him at the Carso, 1916–18, mud and rail cuts — and who had reached the rank of Major der Polizei by 1921. He joined the Austrian Nazi Party in September 1925, went on to be a right-wing parliamentary deputy and a provincial minister in the Austrian province of Carinthia in the 1933–34 cabinets, and in 1941 was moved east with the occupation. Contemporary accounts and the later war-crimes files are unflattering and unambiguous: Meyszner has been described as one of Heinrich Himmler's most brutal subordinates, and the Serbian record of his two years — the reprisal columns, the gendarmerie stations taken over from the old administration, the Belgrade reports that went north in sealed pouches — is the record Vasa has spent a career cross-referencing against other men's ledgers. He died on 24 January 1947, in the Šabac prison, where a military court had left him after the trial closed; the year outlived him by nothing but the form of it, and Vasa says that, too, is a fact one may set down in a letter.

Then, in the middle of all this, the maître d' came to clear the soup bowls and did what the maître d' of this house does at every table, every evening, with a small ceremony of his own: he announced the course. The house's joke, its only surviving courtesy, is to treat its dining room as a data hall of the old order, and to name what is on the tables with the flat authority of a station ledger. His announcement, which Vasa asked me to write down and which I now hand to you uncorrected, is the whole line, word for word: “The course now serving: current versions on IBM's Power line, Power9 and Power10, rack-mounted and cloth-covered, each table its own notes.” He said it over the cloth-draped towers at every table in the room — retired machines, the brass nameplates filed clean — and then he left the soup decision to us, as is proper.

I want the Editor to understand what the dining room is doing, because I think it is the least stupid thing this city has done with its memory since the war. Meyszner's sealed pouches went north; the city's own notes went into vaults, then into the municipal archive, then into Vasa's office, where they are still being cross-referenced. The dining room has replaced one ledger with another: the boxes and returns that once left by sealed train now sit on nine tables, cloth-covered, each one keeping its own small record, warm, in the winter, and no one is asked to be brutal about them, because a machine on a table is the coldest kind of evidence there is, and coldness, it turns out, is the only thing the house can serve that no one has to eat. I have dined where the returns of an 1836 election were kept as weather, and I have listened to a radio wire run the tally of fifty seats in Raleigh in 2010 without once raising its voice; I mention them because the small print has always been the truest column, and because a city that logs its boxes is a city that knows what a box is. The Hudson Weather Almanac, Entry 191 keeps a keeper's uncorrected line from a ballot-box log, and The Night Wire out of WQDR Raleigh keeps a clerk's notebook line, and both of them are doing exactly what the maître d' did at table nine: entering, in the small print, what the ordinary week logged.

So, dear Editor, print this in the Small Print column, with the dates, and with the course as announced, uncorrected. A gendarme of 1886, a major of 1921, a minister in Carinthia, a police leader in Belgrade, a dead man of 24 January 1947, and nine tables of cloth-covered machines in a warm dining room on Knez Mihailova — set them against each other the way Vasa sets them against each other, ledger to ledger. The soup, for the record, was very good. The counter by the door passed 1,287 while this letter was being written. The tables, as announced, keep their own notes.

Respectfully, table nine,
M. Petrović, Dorčol
P.S. Vasa asks that the Carso line be checked against the 1917 field gazetteer before printing; he is not dogmatic, only cross-referencing.
