The Night Wire

Election night returns · North Carolina general election · Tuesday, November 2, 2010 · Broadcast-log excerpt

SOUND: Station break. Teletype clatter. A distant telephone bell, ringing once, twice.
ANNOUNCER: This is WQDR Raleigh, the night wire, bringing you the returns from the North Carolina general election of Tuesday, November 2, 2010. All fifty seats of the state Senate are on the ballot tonight, beside the races for the United States Senate, the United States House of Representatives, and the state house. The polls closed at half past seven, and the wire is humming.
REPORTER (MILLER): I'm sitting in the canvass room at the State Board of Elections in Raleigh, and the board has been calling precincts since eight o'clock. Wake, Mecklenburg, Forsyth, Guilford — the tallies go up on the board in chalk, and the chalk runs out. Fifty seats. Tonight the arithmetic of the chamber changes.
ANNOUNCER: The primary was held on May 4, with a runoff on June 22 wherever no candidate cleared the margin. The campaigns have run their course, and now the voters have spoken.
SOUND: Paper shuffling. A gavel, tapped once.
REPORTER (MILLER): In District One, Senator Marc Basnight, who held this seat for a generation and ran the chamber as president pro tempore, concedes to Republican Bill Cook. The last time Republicans won the most seats in the North Carolina Senate, William McKinley was in the White House — 1896, one hundred and fourteen years ago.
CLERK (DEPUTY, PRECINCT 17): Precinct seventeen, Ward Two. Machine count: two thousand four hundred and twelve. Hand count: two thousand four hundred and nine. The difference sits in the notebook under miscellaneous. Cross-referenced with the sugar until the next meeting.
REPORTER (MILLER): She read that aloud, and nobody in the room knew what it meant. The notebook is now with the canvass clerk, and the canvass clerk is not saying.
SOUND: Teletype accelerating. Chalk snapped against a board.
ANNOUNCER: By midnight the call is clear: Republicans hold the most seats in the North Carolina Senate for the first time since 1896. Phil Berger of Eden will be the next president pro tempore. The chamber meets in January under new leadership.
REPORTER (MILLER): Fifty seats. Two thousand precincts. One notebook line that will outlast the returns. This is Miller, at the Board of Elections, back to the wire.
ANNOUNCER: You have been listening to WQDR Raleigh, the night wire. Election-night coverage continues until the last precinct reports.
SOUND: Station break. Fade under the teletype.

Between returns, Miller kept company with two documents on the desk beside the wire: the Field Manual FM-4 chapter on Methodism — circuit riders, class meetings, the whole itinerant apparatus — and the Recalde Press desk's FAQ on the Bank Charter Act 1844, with its Issue and Banking Departments and its one illegible ledger line. Strange what keeps a person awake until the last precinct reports. What kept Hutchins awake was the same desk — and out of that night's Log Book 3 grew the workbench build log The Thirty-Two — A Build Log · The Class 2 Senate Board, 1936, thirty-two lamps wired to answer the desk's line about credit and trade. A third paper lay on the desk, thinner than either, mailed in from Redding, California: Newsletter No. 27 of the Hooptie & Frill Club, on the 2006 tragicomedy Little Miss Sunshine, which also keeps one uncorrected line of the minutes and reads it as a tolerance — the closest thing to a broadcast log the wire had all night. A fourth paper came by mail a week after the canvass closed, from a 31-member club on Turk Street in San Francisco that keeps one unexplained line under a walnut box instead of a note in a notebook: Newsletter No. 47 of the Seer’s Post, on the demon of divination from season seven of a certain Thursday drama, which reads the wire's chalk-and-chalk line as company.
