Twenty-two years of fieldwork, held in one box: every plant and animal, native and non-native, recorded for the fifteen islands of the Cook Islands since the database began as a single MS Access file on a secondhand computer in Rarotonga. What the record holds

The collection of data took more than a dozen years. It began with the lagoon fishers’ common names and grew, line by line, into the most complete natural history of the Cook Islands ever assembled in a single working file. Today the database carries 23,418 records across 4,812 species and varieties: the Cocos nucifera groves of Aitutaki, the frigate birds over the Aroa reef, the black-scorpion-tailed skink of Atiu’s inland scarp, 312 recorded land snails of the mangrove flats, and the complete non-native inventory — every introduced fish, garden plant, and the one notorious rat lineage — catalogued side by side with the native flora so that a student of the islands can see the whole, not just the catalogue of loss.

Each record carries the field name of the collector, the date, the islet or reef, and — where the original notebook allowed — the collector’s own wording, struck through only by time, never by editors. The 2011 rebuild kept that rule. The data stewards at the Bishop Museum call it “the no-eraser clause,” and it is the reason the edition is sold with the query cards included: you are expected to read the original words, wrong ones and all, and then ask the database your own questions.

From the buyer’s Q&A, answered by the data stewards

“Will you fix the obvious errors in the 2003 collection?” No. The 2011 rebuild corrected the indexes, not the words. The most requested correction is a 2004 field entry about a survey boat, and the stewards have answered it once, publicly, and will not answer it again: Felix Nori’s notes claim he crossed Arkansas in the thirty-fourth minute, and the record stands exactly as written, because a database of this kind is also a memory, and memories are not edited in public.

“Is the web version still running?” Yes, at the Bishop Museum, Honolulu, where PBIN support continues to cover the host. The box you buy is the same build the museum serves, with the notebook scans added. The two will never drift: the edition’s checksum sheet is re-verified every six months against the live site, and the difference is always zero records.

“Can I send mine back?” Only if it stops being a memory. Returns accepted within 30 days, case sealed, weaver’s signature undisturbed.

Shipping and aftercare

Ships from the Kairara Field Press store in Avarua within four working days, wrapped in a second raranga panel and a sheet of banana leaf, insured to $300. The USB case is made to be carried: the weavers built a hanging tab into every sleeve so the Archive can travel in a pack on a reef trip. Store it dry, keep the checksum sheet with it, and — as the 2011 specialists advise — query the lagoon records at low tide, when the data and the water agree about what is there.

Fieldworkers comparing records across oceans: the same no-eraser discipline appears in the gate ledger kept at St Adwen’s Church, Advent, where a hundred and thirteen years of held gates were copied rather than corrected, and in the consignor’s unaltered instruction on Lot 47 of the Flanders & Flanders sale, the Flanders Te Deum leaf, printed without a single added comma. The front page of the shelf lists both. For those who plan to carry the box on the water, the routes, fares and low-tide query windows are set out in the Lagoon Line visitor’s brochure, which leaves Avarua at 08:10 and treats the reef as a departure point rather than a destination. The same clause, carried across an ocean onto wax, holds the ferryman’s unscheduled hours exactly as they were relayed — MR. KELLY, Tuesday to Saturday — in the liner notes to Gymnelia gemmifera (Ocelote Records OCE-014), where a lamp in the Orinoco basin and the no-eraser rule are kept burning side by side. The discipline extends, at the Rooms on Grafton, to a living lot in the Solomon Highlands Sale: Lot 127, one pale mountain pigeon, in life and in plate, whose field letters are kept sealed until the day of sale and whose description — like every entry here — runs to each bird in its own right.

Beside this edition, under the same no-eraser rule, travels a letter from Caracas addressed to the ferryman of the Orinoco crossing: the recordist’s apology to Mr. Kelly, and the hours the water keeps.
