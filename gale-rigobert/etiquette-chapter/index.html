The Windward Press · A Manual of Etiquette for Saint Lucia · Castries Edition, 7th Impression

Chapter IX

On the Proper Queue in a Small Island Republic

— being the chapter the manual cannot open without, and the one every visitor forgets —

I. The island first, in the visitor’s favour

Before manners, the ground. Saint Lucia — often written St. Lucia, and never, in a queue, “St. Lucky” — is an island country in the eastern and southern Caribbean, a member of the Windward Islands of the Lesser Antilles. Situate it in the head: it lies north and a little northeast of the island of Saint Vincent, northwest of Barbados, and due south of Martinique. The whole nation covers 617 square kilometres (238 square miles), with an officially estimated population of a little over 184,100 people as of mid-2023. The capital and largest city is Castries, where the manual’s printers live and where this chapter, on its seventh impression, was set in the morning before the fish market closed. To stand politely in a small country is to know its edges; 617 is a number a visitor should be able to say back to a ferryman without apology.

On the headland of history, the first proven inhabitants of the island, the Arawaks, are believed to have settled it in 200–400 AD, and in 800 AD the island passed into the keeping of the Kalinago — the very people the name Soufrière district honours in the older registers. The French arrived first among the Europeans and settled it, and the ledger of arrivals since is one the manual asks the visitor to read only as far as it helps the queue: the country has been argued over, shared, and finally ruled by its own people, and the etiquette of an independent island is that you do not stand in the way of its history any more than you stand in the way of its queue.

II. The queue is the etiquette

The single fact of Saint Lucian courtesy that this chapter exists to protect is this: the queue in the gravel yard is itself the form. One does not join it rudely; one does not leave it to rejoin; one does not explain to the person ahead why one has come, because the person ahead will explain for you, more pleasantly. A queue formed before the shutter comes down is a queue that will be honoured. The reader of Folio 31 of the Assembly Ledger, Questions from the Yard will recognise the yard at once: it is the same yard above the Soufrière road, the one with the low brick wall, and the rule the manual codifies there is the rule the Ledger has always kept — every answer begins as a question asked standing up, in the gravel, outside the door.

There are three offences, and the manual names all three. The first is to arrive with a complaint and no letter, for a complaint without a letter is a complaint with no address. The second is to arrive with a letter and no yam, which is not required but is not unknown, and is received as a compliment with the hand on the heart. The third, the great one, is to pass the chalk line without reading it. The line is chalked on the low brick wall, and it has not been washed out. The office has never explained it; the manual declines to explain it twice.

III. The market runs on Thursdays

Micoud North’s market runs on Thursdays, and so does its courtesy. A visitor who has come to the district with a matter should fix the matter to the Thursday market, stand in the yard on Thursday, and let the Thursday settle the order of things. The office answers at least one question every school day during term; the standing reply on textbook resupply goes out in triplicate, which the manual notes is a form of generosity rather than a clerical habit. Numbers, for the visitor’s sake: the constituency is one of the island’s smaller seats — a handful of parishes’ worth of roads, a school or two, a market that keeps to one day, and an office whose queue forms before its shutter comes down.

The flag, when the visitor must wear one, runs maroon, gold, and green across the top of every folio, in that order, and nowhere else. The manual permits one ribbon, one button, one chalked line, and nothing more. The Pitons are visible from much of the district on a clear morning; the etiquette of looking at them from the queue is to look, then to look back at the wall, and not to let the visitor know that the view has been taken.

IV. On keeping things in their element

The manual closes its island chapter with the rule it borrows from the shelf where the crate sits, filed at Lot 142 of the Southern Branch auction, a cedar archive crate that kept a foreign city’s tides for sixty years without opening. The rule is shared, and it is short: the thing is kept where it is kept, and the keeping does not require an explanation. The chalk line will not be rinsed; the crate will not be oiled; the queue will not be re-ordered; the word Micoud will not be settled indoors. A visitor who leaves the yard without knowing the answer to the question on the wall has, the manual insists, left with the country’s manners intact and the country’s question unspent, which is the only correct way to leave a small island. The yard also keeps, under a separate cover, the manners of those who follow the grey-coated man out of the rollout — Error DYL-39-53, the rollout failure of a Swansea poet — entered at Error DYL-39-53 — Rollout Failure, Subsystem Marlais. The reader who wishes to hear the question asked again, in its own register, will find it at the front page of the shelf, where all the ledgers begin. The manual notes, for the record, that a queue is not only a matter of gravel and chalk: in the valley country north of the sea, eleven people stood one line deep in a small courtroom behind the water office, waiting for a torn leaf of printed matter to be read into the record and no one to explain it — the court transcript of Cause No. 73/1958, Remedios Ibáñez v. the Municipality of Valle de la Cruz — and the manual judges that they kept their manners the whole time, which is the only standard the queue has ever had.

The chalk line reads once, aloud, and then you join the queue.
