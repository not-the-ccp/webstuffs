In the Municipal Court of Aden, Southern Aden

Nori v. The Aden Natural History Society

Civil Case No. 41 of 1964 · Transcript excerpt · second day of trial, 14 November 1964, 9:40 a.m.

Hon. J. F. al-Amrani presiding. S. Dawood, court clerk. Present: the plaintiff in person; the defendant by its secretary, Mr. Q. Basman.

Excerpted for the record
— do not fold —

The Court: We resume. Yesterday I directed that the matter be narrowed to a question of principle, which is this: whether the plaintiff, a customs clerk and not a taxonomist, holds any interest in the naming and classifying of the moth known to both parties as the white-wing, which he says he first observed at the harbour lamp on the night of 12 October 1962, at ten minutes past eight, in the Crater district of Aden. The defendant contends that the naming of a taxon belongs to no one until the thing is properly described, circumscribed, and placed in its hierarchy. The plaintiff is sworn.

The Clerk: Sworn. “Do you swear to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, so help you this Court?” — I do.

The Court: Mr. Nori. Tell the Court your trade.

Plaintiff: Customs clerk at the steamer wharf, Aden, your Honour. Twenty-two years of service. I examine manifests and cargo by day, and, on certain evenings of my own choosing, I examine insects.

The Court: And what is the study of naming and classifying living things called?

Plaintiff: Taxonomy, your Honour. It comes out of the Greek taxis, arrangement, and -nomia, method — arrangement by method. It is the scientific naming, the defining, the circumscribing, and the classifying of groups of biological organisms on the strength of shared characteristics. The modern practice goes a step further and prefers common ancestry: the evolutionary relation between things. One does not merely pin a thing in a box. One places it.

The Court: And how does one place it?

Plaintiff: One gives it a taxonomic rank. A group of one rank may be aggregated into a more inclusive group of higher rank, and so the hierarchy is built up, rung upon rung, the way the wharf is built up, crate upon crate. The principal ranks in modern use are domain, kingdom, phylum — in botany they say division, and I will not quarrel with botanists — class, order, family, genus, and beneath genus, species. The white-wing is, at this moment, a taxon of one. Which is to say it is its own family, its own order, its own kingdom, waiting to be told it is not.

Mr. Basman, for the defendant: The defendant takes no issue with the ladder, your Honour. The defendant takes issue with the plaintiff’s rung on it.

Plaintiff: The rung was given to me at ten minutes past eight, by a moth with forewings the colour of flour and hindwings the colour of a candle seen through a closed fist. I have not moved from it.

Exhibit A — The Harbour-Lamp Table

The Court directs that the harbour-lamp table be re-erected in the courtroom as it stood on the wharf, 1962: one kerosene lamp, one ledger, one glass vial, and the crate of specimens which is Exhibit E. A moth is brought in in a vial. It does not emerge. The plaintiff observes that the vial has been dark for twenty days and the moth has gone into mourning. The Court rules the exhibit received, and the mourning noted.

Exhibit B — The Card

The plaintiff produces a card the size of a postage stamp, browned at the edges, an ingredient list on the face and a single sentence on the reverse, which the Court will come to. Ersilia Nori, sister-in-law of the plaintiff, called from Mirandola, Emilia-Romagna, and sworn by telegraphed affidavit, testifies: “That is my card. I wrote it. The almonds are always 140 grams, because that is the weight of the small specimen box, and the cake is twenty-two minutes, one side of one record, or the length of one story. It is not eaten until something with white wings has come to the lamp. If nothing comes, it is wrapped in linen and left for the next night.”

The Court: And the reverse of the card?

Witness: The players collapsed on the Secchia bank. I do not know. It may be a football notebook. I keep it in the recipe box and I check the pharmacy safe before I bake, though there is no pharmacy safe. The ritual is the point.

Exhibit C — The Canteen Ledger, 12 October 1962

The Court reads from the harbour canteen ledger, entry at 8:10 p.m.: a cook had asked the evening’s guest what to put into the pickle jars for the night, and the guest — the plaintiff, per the defendant’s own concession — had dictated: It should be broken into small round pickling-onions, nasturtiums, capsicums, chilies, &c. The entry is initialed F.N., and the jars were filled by 8:12. The plaintiff testifies: “The moth arrived at 8:10. The pickle was ready at 8:12. Neither is rushable. My sister-in-law still makes it on the nights she bakes the cake. That is how the sentence lives — under the onions, in the brine, in a jar labelled 12·10·62.”

Exhibit D — The Little Rock Chalkboard

The plaintiff produces a photograph of a pie-stand chalkboard, Little Rock, Arkansas, spring 1962: Slices of dry or left to roam at large or 24 small pies. Testifying: “I crossed Arkansas that spring, before Aden, sleeping in the rice-country motels. I copied the board into the family recipe box. It means nothing, and it is all of what the box means. For the state itself I refer the Court to the visitor’s brochure, which the plaintiff has seen and finds honest, in a landlocked way.”

Mr. Basman: Your Honour, Exhibit D is a photograph of a pie sign. It is not evidence of anything in this case, moth or otherwise.

Plaintiff: It is evidence of where a man sleeps before he pins a thing. The Court will take its pick.

The Court: The Court receives Exhibit D, on its merits, which are a merit.

Exhibit E — The Crate

Thirty-six glass vials and one leather net, packed 1964, marked “NORI, F. — do not invert.” The Court orders the crate sealed pending the proper description of its contents, and orders that no taxon herein be named, renamed, or aggregated without a published account in tenths of a millimetre.

The Court, on its finding

The Court holds that the plaintiff holds an interest in the placing of the white-wing, being the first to observe it, but not in the naming of it, which belongs to the discipline. The lamp is returned to the plaintiff, to be kept at the rear porch of the family house in Mirandola, beside the Secchia, and to be lit on summer evenings only. The cake may continue, subject to the condition that it be eaten with black coffee, no plates, and one story that ends with a moth. The pickle, being a jar, is not appealable. Case 41 of 1964 is closed as of the evening of 14 November 1964, at ten minutes past eight.

Clerk’s note, entered later: On 12 March 2002 the taxon in suit was formally described as Scythris albocanella, B. A. Bengtsson, from Wadi Hadramawt and the desert fringe east of Shabwa, Yemen — half a page, in tenths of a millimetres, with a drawing of the genitalia that looks, if you squint, like a piece of architectural drafting. The plaintiff was dead by then. The cake was served that night in Mirandola, on the strength of a moth at the lamp, as is custom. The vials in Exhibit E went to a museum in Helsinki. The jar labelled 12·10·62 was still in the pantry. The two ledgers of the case have since been kept in the same drawer as the sleeve of a Nile leech album, Mucous Membrane (Nile Basin Records NBR-041), whose closing line — a league charter closed at 8:42 pm — sits a half-hour from this case's ten minutes past eight, close enough that no one disputes the pairing. The daytime half of the family's walk, the one that carries the jar from the porch along the Secchia to the hill in eight stops through the mustard beds and the cress, is now written down as an itinerary of its own: The Mustard Walk, which begins at 9:15 in the morning and ends, as all of it does, with the jar back between the flour and the silence. One further drawer is now known: in a property office at Stop III of Washington Park, Portland, Oregon, the founds ledger of the Chiming Fountain carries one unexplained line — Stop III The days and leaves on paper. — set down word for word, as this Court sets down all of its sentences, at Lost & Found Notice No. 891, unclaimed and kept.
