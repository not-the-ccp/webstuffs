THE REPUBLIC’S COURT FOR MARITIMATE AND ATMOSPHERICAL MATTERS

In re Typhoon Nepartak (Philippine designation: Weng)

CASE NO. 2003-TY-021 · TRANSCRIPT, EXCERPT · SIXTEENTH SESSION

Room Seven, Port Authority, Manila · 16 November 2003, 9:40 a.m. · before the Hon. A. C. Reyes, Presiding

[The transcript is taken from the record at minute 214, where the witness stands. The clerk’s initials, R. S., appear at each correction of the record.]

THE COURT: We resume. The tribunal will put the chronology to the record before argument. Counsel for the respondent, you may begin with the formation.

COUNSEL FOR THE RESPONDENT: If it pleases the tribunal. My client, whom the public calls a typhoon and whom the Philippine Meteorological Administration calls Weng, originated on 11 November 2003 as a tropical depression in water between the islands of Yap and Guam. She travelled westward and strengthened slowly — the tribunal will note the word slowly, as it bears on the modesty finding — and I submit that no part of her conduct was, in the legal sense, wanton.

THE COURT: Noted. Mr. Sato of the Japan Meteorological Agency is called on the question of the name.

MR. SATO: The name Nepartak was issued by the Japan Meteorological Agency at midday on 12 November 2003. It made the respondent the twentieth named storm of the 2003 Pacific typhoon season. I will add that the name was drawn from the rotation list in the ordinary way; the bureau does not keep a reserve of names for difficult customers.

COUNSEL FOR THE RESPONDENT: Your Honour, the respondent wishes the record to show that she has no objection to her name. She is, as the witnesses will confirm, modest, if inconvenient.

THE COURT: Let us hear the inconvenience. Captain Manahan, port captain of San Jose, Samar — what did the respondent do on the thirteenth?

CAPTAIN MANAHAN: On 13 November she struck Samar, Your Honour, and she did not strike it at the edge; she bisected the island chain, running her centre across the middle of the province like a seamstress with a long stitch. The winds came in from the west all day. Up to four million people lost power across the area under my notice. Every road, every ferry lane, every airstrip — transportation ground to a halt, and I say “halt” as a fact, not a figure of speech. Over five thousand individuals were stranded aboard ships ordered to stay in port at San Jose and the anchorages at Guiuan. I personally walked the Maito wharf at 6 a.m. the morning after and counted eleven vessels with their crews still aboard and their lines doubled. My log says: no departure, no weather, no word. R. S. — that is my clerk’s notation, it is in the record.

THE COURT: And Hainan?

CAPTAIN MANAHAN: That is outside my waters, Your Honour.

THE COURT: Counsel for the respondent, you will answer it.

COUNSEL FOR THE RESPONDENT: My client, having finished her work in the central Philippines, continued west-northwest and on the fifteenth of the month made landfall on the southern Chinese island of Hainan at a reduced strength. She submits she arrives there as a tropical depression and departs as a memory, and that this too is, if anything, a mitigating quality.

THE COURT: The tribunal will consider that. Clerk, the next item.

THE CLERK: Exhibit F-4 is a letter delivered to Room Seven this morning by the courier R. A. Dizon, one of the five thousand who were held at the San Jose anchorage, who walked in from the wharf with it after the roads reopened. The envelope carried no return address, only a routing line written in pencil across the front, which the clerk transcribes exactly as received:

The Keep of Guïnes · Letters Post Office Box 114, Irapuato, Guanajuato · in the parish Bible — the “Silkmen” — played in the common order.

THE COURT: The court reads it and files it as received, without interpretation. Courier Dizon, you will tell us how a letter from a storm-tied wharf in Samar reaches a place that has no postal number.

COURIER DIZON: It was found, Your Honour, folded into the bell-rope box of the parish chapel at Guiuan on the morning of the fourteenth, after the water went down. The parish priest says the box had been sealed since the feast day, and that he did not seal it that day. Inside were the letter, two ferry tickets for a route that does not appear on any current timetable, and a page torn from the parish Bible — the reading for a common-order service, with the margins clean and the fold old. The letter itself is a single paragraph of thanks addressed to no one, and it is dry, which the priest notes is the only impossibility he is willing to state plainly.

THE COURT: The exhibit will be held under seal, and the three papers within it catalogued separately as F-4a, F-4b, and F-4c. The tribunal finds nothing in the routing line that belongs to this proceeding, and records, for completeness, that it has been read once, filed once, and is not to be read again. Is that all, courier?

COURIER DIZON: One detail, Your Honour. The ferry tickets were dated 12 and 13 November, for the San Jose to Guiuan lane, and both were unused. I say that only so the record will show that the sea kept what the sea had.

THE COURT: Very well. The tribunal will take the matter of liability under advisement until the waters are surveyed. This excerpt of the transcript stands as entered. [The session is suspended at 10:05 a.m. R. S. initials the record.]

The tribunal keeps its own domestic order: the Presiding Judge takes her morning tea on the standing recipe in The Surakarta Morning Decoction, card 112 of the Oen Family Recipe Ledger, which the clerk has ruled admissible as custom and inadmissible as evidence. The anteroom of Room Seven, where the courier waited out the suspension, also holds the tribunal’s single glass exhibit of natural history, logged in the Bocusoides build log from the garage on Ponsonby Road, where a one-species spider does her ant work on a cork ramp four centimetres tall. The rest of the record is filed from the front page.
