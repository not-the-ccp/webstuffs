On the subject of the Marquess

Every number of this paper keeps the same subject at the head, because a club that changes its subject is a family that has lost its keys. The subject is John Paulet, 2nd Marquess of Winchester (c. 1510 – 4 November 1576), an English peer, eldest son of William Paulet, 1st Marquess of Winchester, and of Elizabeth Capel. His styles ran like a staircase, and the club has ruled that they must be read in order: The Honourable John Paulet between 1539 and 1550; Lord St John between 1550 and 1551; Earl of Wiltshire between 1551 and 1572; and then, in 1572, the last step, Marquess of Winchester, which he held for the four years he had left. The house of the family stood at Bishampton in the hills, with the manor gatehouse facing the long field, and the court business was done at Lambeth, and the quiet part of his life at Harefield in Middlesex. He died on 4 November 1576, and the club keeps that date on a slip of card in the tin, under the minutes, where nothing else is allowed to go.

The crate, and what was in it

On 12 March our member Mrs. C. P. Harker went to the cottage by the old mill at Bishampton to collect a chaffing dish, and came back with a crate. The crate was stamped BITTERS — 2 DOZ — PAY DUTY, had once been nailed shut, and held, between two layers of 1953 newspaper, an oak panel thirty-eight inches high. On the panel, in dark red and a green gone grey with age, stands a gatekeeper in a heavy coat, hand on a ring, with the Bishampton gatehouse behind him and no sky at all above it. The painter is not named anywhere, and the club has decided not to name him, because a man who stood at that gate would not have wanted to be named twice. We have examined the panel at three meetings. The ruling of the club, recorded in the minutes of 29 September and carried eleven to two, is one sentence, and it stands word for word: The keeper at the gate is painted in the crate. We mean this exactly. The painting was found in the crate, and it is kept in the crate, and the crate has been moved to the hall of the Crown Inn where it sits on a trestle under a lamp, and members are invited any Thursday to stand in front of it for as long as they like. Mr. Blyth says the face is “right for a man who was never going to be painted,” and the motion to varnish the panel was defeated on the strength of that alone.

Business of the club

Eleven of our fourteen met at The Crown Inn on Thursday 29 September, which is a quorum and a crowd. The treasurer, Mr. T. F. Blyth, reports £41.60 in the tin, up from £38.20, of which £3.40 was spent on lamp oil for the crate and £1.10 on the slip of card for the date 4 November. It was moved that the panel be framed in pine, seconded, and lost to oak, and it was further moved that No. 8 be sent to every member and to two correspondents only. The next meeting is Thursday 13 November, which is a day the club considers unlucky for nothing and lucky for the Marquess, whose anniversary we keep with plain bread, strong tea, and silence at eight o’clock while the lamp burns over the crate.

Correspondence and cross-references

The society keeps two standing cross-references, because a club that reads only itself reads nothing. For the ledger that rules around its one unstamped line and closes the page — the same gesture our minutes make with the crate — see The Wanters (1923) — Error Message Documentation, Lab Ledger No. 9, where the cold’s lost frame is set down as found and the page is ruled. And for a kitchen that keeps a fragment of a ledger with no subject of its own and bakes to it the way we stand before the panel — Camden, Bergen, and the sixth rule — see The Fabian Sunday — Family Recipe No. 11, The Webb–Bivand Kitchens. Both pages are read in this society as found, unamended, and closed, and both are owed a visit from any member with a spare afternoon. Return to the front shelf.

Guestbook of the Gate

If you have stood in front of the crate, leave your mark. The keeper is watching, or is not; the panel does not say.

  • Mr. T. F. Blyth — £3.40 lamp oil, paid, and the face was worth it. 29 Sep
