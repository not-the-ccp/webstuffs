Fifteen Canada Square

a dream in which the river turns to glass and the card is left with the attendant

Roster card · tower dreams · filed between “the lighthouse that is only a window” and “the elevator that stops at the floor you were born on”

Fourteen storeys. 227 feet. The windows keep their own hours.

of the dream, the card, and the river

I. The dream

To dream of Fifteen Canada Square — the office tower at the water’s edge of the Canary Wharf financial estate in London, England, fourteen storeys tall, rising 227 feet, 69 metres, from the quay, its glass holding the river the way a hand holds a coin it has not yet counted — is to wake with the sense of having been received. The dreamer arrives in the small dark hour and finds the tower lit from within, floor by floor, exactly fourteen, no more, and at its base a desk where an attendant has laid out, in order: a card case, a lamp, and the river, which has turned to glass and will not be broken. In the dream one does not knock. One is given a card on which the name is already printed, in a hand the dreamer recognises but cannot read, and the card is written, not with the dreamer’s name, but with the address of the room the dreamer has just woken up in.

The building belongs, in the waking record, to Kingboard Investors; and the dream keeps this fact the way a lock keeps a key — the compilers of this book have noted in eleven recorded dreams that the tower’s owner becomes, in the sleeper’s arithmetic, a board that is being played upon: a draughtsboard, a card table, a chessboard, once a piano keyboard, and once, in the dream of a dock clerk, a full hand of tiles that will not, when counted, come to the right number. The owner of a dream is the one the dream is played by, and the sleeper is on the board.

II. Meaning

The tower is not the object of the dream; the card is. Fourteen storeys at 227 feet is the exact height of a calling that has already been made — high enough that the glass catches the light of the day after, but not so high that it can see the day itself. To dream of Fifteen Canada Square is therefore read, in this book, as the dream of an invitation already accepted: the household has set the place, the card has been left at the hall table, and the dreamer is the reply, travelling slowly up the glass. The attendant at the desk is not a doorkeeper; the attendant is the habit of waiting, and what is left with the attendant — the coat, the sword, the umbrella, whatever the sleeper’s life corresponds to — is left correctly, which is the only thing in the dream that is done correctly, and the compilers mark it.

Where the dreamer counts the storeys and arrives at a number other than fourteen, the reading changes: thirteen means the invitation was meant for a neighbour; fifteen, which is the number on the address and not in the building, means the card has been answered a night before it was sent; and the dreamer who counts and cannot finish counting is dreaming the tower in the reverse, which see below. The height — 227 feet, 69 metres — is the measure the dream keeps for its own truth: a dream of Fifteen Canada Square that does not come to these figures is a dream of a tower in Canary Wharf, and the two are not the same.

IV. The reverse

In reverse — the tower dark floor by floor while the dreamer descends, the card taken back by the attendant, the river going back to being only water — the dream is read as a calling that the household has declined, and the compilers are careful to add, as elsewhere in this book, that the refusal is the card’s, not the dreamer’s. A refused card is folded, not thrown away, and it is kept where the next season’s may find it. The reverse is therefore not ill-omened; it is merely early. The dreamer who has the reverse twice in one month should not dream the tower again voluntarily. The third time, by the book’s rule, is the answer, and the answer will arrive on its own schedule, at the water’s edge, in a hand the dreamer cannot read but knows.

V. The clipped line

At the foot of the roster card for this entry, in a hand the bookkeeper of the Lexicon does not recognise, there is a line which the compilers have never translated and which the rule of this book requires to be kept word for word:

The verbatim record of the scribes set the way this card for the roster was named, and that the field notes.

It is read, as all such lines in this book are read, the way a bell is read — that is, as an instruction to stop interpreting and listen. The verbatim record is kept; the naming stands; the field notes are where they are. The compilers note that the line was copied onto the card by a scribe who was in London that night and is not recorded in it, and they decline to say more, which is the whole of their gloss, and we do not supply one. For the record itself, read the book’s standing example, the verbatim record of the Committee on Elections, 44th Congress, where one ledger line is read word for word and the majority is kept; and for the custom the card itself belongs to, the etiquette of the hall table and the attendant — the card left, the coat taken, the case held in the hand until the host speaks first — the Chapter IV · Of Calling in Gothenburg of A Book of Etiquette for the Theatre sets down the rule that a card is never left twice in the same week without an invitation between, and it is this rule, and not the tower, that the dream is obeying.
