Field Notes of a Naturalist · Volume II · The Built Range

Caltrans

Observations on the California Department of Transportation as a resident species — one headquarters, one statewide range, three rails, and a single monument no walker has yet been able to find. Kept in the field, in place, from 1973 to the present.

Log opened, Sacramento, on a Tuesday that rained in the afternoon · log closed, I-8, near Boulevard, in November dry

Entry I · Sacramento · the headquarters

A Department That Counts as Weather

Any naturalist who has spent a season in California will confirm that the most constant presence in the landscape is not a bird and not a bloom but a department: the California Department of Transportation, branded as Caltrans, an executive department of the state, keeping its headquarters in Sacramento the way a badger keeps a den — unglamorously, and for a very long time. It sits inside the cabinet-level California State Transportation Agency, CalSTA, which is to say it reports to a table where the other chairs are also large. The current individual of the species Caltrans began operations in 1973, succeeding a series of state agencies that came before it, so the lineage is older than the name. I have counted the generations; I have miscounted them; the species does not keep its own pedigree, which is a mark of maturity.

What the specimen does is this: it manages the state's highway system, which is not a metaphor in this log. It also supports the public transportation systems throughout the state, and it provides funding and oversight for the three rails treated under Entry III. The range is the whole of the state. The diet is fuel tax, tolls, and federal apportionment. The plumage is described next.

Entry II · the range · the green shield

On the Feathering

Within the range, one system stands out and carries its own name: the California Freeway and Expressway System, the part of the highway estate that the species maintains at full flight. The plumage is unmistakable — deep green, white lettering, the small printed directionals at the bottom of the shield — and it is the only green in the whole of California that means nothing horticultural. I have walked the range the way you would a coastline, counting mile markers the way a dendrologist counts rings, and I can report that the markers lie as rarely as any animal I know, and that a mile marker in full sun at two in the afternoon is the plainest promise the landscape makes. Where the freeway gives way to surface road the feathering thins to a county ribbon, and the species is at rest between its shoulders.

For the walking record in this shelf, the species' work is best observed on the old ninety-four: the Martin Luther King Jr. Freeway out of downtown San Diego, the last green shield in Spring Valley, the wagon-road miles east through the mountains — all of it kept on the same schedule, stop by stop, in The Ninety-Four — A Walking Tour of California State Route 94. Sixty-three and three-tenths miles, 63.324, and every foot of pavement a feather this department grew.

Entry III · the three rails · intercity

Three Rails of One Branding

Alongside the highway estate the species keeps three intercity rails under funding and oversight, collectively branded Amtrak California: the Capitol Corridor, running up the central valley toward the headquarters itself; the Pacific Surfliner, riding the coast south; and the Gold Runner, the shortest of the three, which I have always suspected is the most punctual, in the way that small things are. A department that can keep a state's freeway system groomed and also keep three rail lines fed and on time is, in the field's phrase, in good husbandry. I note the phrase. It will matter at the end of this log, and it is not by accident that it does.

The riders of the three rails are the species' only true symbionts; everyone else who meets it does so on the shoulder. That is where the record thins into the personal, and where the log picks up the people of the Ninety-Four.

Entry IV · SR 94 · company in the field

The Abell Porch and the Khaki Necktie

I have walked the route with the guide, in season, before the valley fog. We saluted the Abell porch — the one where the rigging wax is still sold, by the ounce, the way it was sold two generations ago to skippers — because the tour's maps refuse to redraw that corner of the valley, and so does, apparently, the weather. At the high point, on the bench, there stood, according to the guide's pencil line and to the dictionary of dreams in its entry Goodman, E. Urner, Entry G-13, a tall, soft-spoken man in a khaki necktie, exactly the right age to be kept on. I did not see him. I saw a green shield and a radio sign above a booth at the far end of a field, and I kept the pace and did not answer the sign. The dictionary transcribes the sign without emendation; this log transcribes the man without emendation, which is to say, not at all. Both records are accepted.

Entry V · the eastern end · I-8, near Boulevard

The Monument in the Ledger

At the end of the Ninety-Four, where the wagon road's repaved successor hands its last mile to the interstate, the walk is over and the naturalist is left with a stone marker, a cold bench, and one open question. The question is this: in the transfer ledger of 1973, the year the current species began operations and took the range from its predecessors, the last line of the old commission's hand is written, and the new department kept it word for word, unemended, in the margin of the route sheet for the interstate near Boulevard. The line reads:

"Cape of Good Husbandry," about the size of the mountains onto the shoulder of Interstate 8 near Boulevard.

Transfer ledger, 1973, last line; route sheet, eastern end of SR 94

Read plainly, the line is a monument report. Good Husbandry is the name the old surveyors gave to the quality this species was hired to keep — the rails fed, the range groomed, the porch where the wax is sold left unredrawn — and the cape is the monument that was supposed to be raised for it, a stone thing, the ledger implies, about the size of the mountains, set onto the shoulder of Interstate 8 near Boulevard, at the very corner where Route 94 runs out and hands its mileage to the interstate. Every walker who has stood on that shoulder has seen the bench, the marker, and the traffic that will never stop. None has seen the cape. The ledger, re-inked like the Abell entry each season, insists it is there. I have checked the drainage line. I have checked the shoulder's stones. A monument about the size of the mountains would be a hard thing to walk past, which is why I file this entry under unobserved, not contradicted — the field's honestest phrase — and I keep the pace out of the wind.

Filed Together

This log is kept in the same drawer as the walking tour it grew out of, The Ninety-Four, and as the dream entry that shares its bench and its radio sign, Entry G-13, and beside them the safety data sheet for the state of California — the whole range as one substance, hazards and all. Everything on this shelf is filed from the Webstuffs front page, where the rest of the ledger is kept. The naturalist closes the volume with the route read out in the handbook's fixed form: 63.324 miles, 101.910 kilometres, from the freeway ramp at I-5 in downtown San Diego to the interstate near Boulevard, where the cape is, if the ledger is to be believed, exactly where the mountains end.
