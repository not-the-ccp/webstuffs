Keijō Nippō

京城日報 · Gyeongseong ilbo · the dining room and printing house at 118 Toegye-ro, Seoul, in continuous service from 1906 to 15 August 1945

★★★★☆ Four of five. Service for thirty-nine years, in one language, under one flag. The half star is withheld for the bill.

I. Arrival

The room opened its doors in 1906 and has, by now, been closed for some time, which complicates the review but does not stop it. Keijō Nippō — the Gyeongseong Daily, the Japanese-language table of the capital — took its seat in the old town on Toegye-ro as a newspaper in the ordinary sense, and quickly learned that the ordinary sense was not available in this town. It was the de facto official house of the Japanese Government-General of Chōsen: the table where the administration announced its dinners, and the only table most of Seoul sat at whether they wished to or not. The cover is plain. The service is punctual. What is served is what the Government-General had decided to serve. A guest who ordered otherwise found, on arriving at the door, that the reservation books kept a single alphabet.

II. The Menu at the Height of the War

Toward the middle of the second half of the house's life, the trade became extraordinary, and the trade was the menu. In the peak years around the Second World War, Keijō Nippō carried the largest circulation of any newspaper published in Korea, and the queue behind it was led, at a respectful distance, by the Fuzan nippō and the Chōsen shinbun — the second and third tables of the district, which divided the leftovers of the readers' attention as best they could. The printery itself is the reason the house drew its weight: under the centralization of the press, from around 1942 to 1945 it was the only establishment in Seoul with printing equipment of any consequence, and the whole city's appetite was routed, by decree as much as by appetite, through its kitchen. A guest of the house at that date, wanting to eat printed matter and finding nowhere else, had no choice at all — which is, one must say, the least flattering thing in the review.

III. The Card by the Washstand

The room keeps, pinned at the height of a stooping head against the washstand in the pass, a card cut from a thin plank of fir, on which one sentence is set down in a hand that no other card in the house imitates. The review sets it here word for word, capital and all, and will not paraphrase it, because the house's own margin note says only that the card is to be read, not decoded, and because the fireplace by the river is real and still in use:

Elkhart Lake would disappear inside it a little fir-cut card with a river-rock fireplace, still hosts community meetings.

The guest who asks what the card is about is answered by the maître d’ that it is about scale: a single broadsheet of this house, unfolded flat on the flagstones of the courtyard, is large enough to take in the whole of Elkhart Lake and leave the shoreline to argue about the card — a card with a river-rock fireplace, the note adds, still hosts community meetings. The room is not sorry for the sentence. The room printed a city for thirty-nine years; it is not going to apologize for one card.

IV. The Last Service

The house closed, without ceremony and without a final menu, on the afternoon of August 15, 1945, at the announcement of the surrender. The printery stopped in the middle of its work, the presses stood down with their type still locked, and the table that had served a single language for thirty-nine years found itself, overnight, in a city that had decided to stop eating at it. The rooms on Toegye-ro kept their shape, the chairs their places, and the card by the washstand its one stubborn sentence. The etiquette of a closed house, as the shelf keeps it, is short: Chapter VII of The House Book of Etiquette says that whatever was carried across a long absence must be named before it is put away, and whatever is put away must be thanked — a rule this room, which carried a whole capital and put it away in a single afternoon, would have obeyed whether it chose to or not.

V. Verdict & Companion Tables

A restaurant review of a newspaper is a strange commission, and Keijō Nippō makes the strangeness work: it is the record of a dining room that was never a choice, served at a scale no private kitchen has ever managed, and closed the moment its guests changed the language of the reservation book. It earns its four stars for scale, for punctuality, and for the card, which no other house in the guide prints word for word and no other house has needed to. The half star goes to the bill, which the administration settled for its diners on schedules of its own. For a second sitting in the same company — a single-byte dining room whose menu card likewise printed a sentence no one asked for — the guide refers the guest to Hôtel 8859, a review from the desk of Recalde Press & Cooperage, and, for the full shelf of the guide's printed cities and closed tables, to the front page of the book.
