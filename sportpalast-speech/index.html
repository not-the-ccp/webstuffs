A method and apparatus for the delivery of a total-war address in a covered hall

Total War · Sportpalast, Berlin · specification, claims, and drawings · Filed 18 February 1943 · Priority claimed 18 February 1943

Abstract

The invention concerns a method and apparatus for the oral delivery of a programme of total war to a selected assembly in an enclosed public hall, in particular the address delivered at the Berlin Sportpalast on 18 February 1943 by the Reich Minister for Public Enlightenment and Propaganda before an invited audience of approximately fourteen thousand persons. The method comprises fixing the hall, admitting a vetted audience by numbered ticket, extinguishing the house lights, summoning the speaker to a raised platform beneath a fixed spotlight, and interrogating the assembly by acclamation until a single answer is returned. The apparatus comprises the oak rostrum, the microphone array, the lighting circuit, the ticket of admission, and the stenographic record.

Background of the invention

Before the invention, addresses concerning the conduct of war were delivered from newspaper bulletins, army communiqués, or rooms without fixed acoustics, and the public heard of reverses only after they had hardened into fact. There was no hall arranged so that a single voice could put the nation's danger and the nation's duty to the same room at once, and no form of acclamation by which the assembly answered for itself. The prior art is the bulletin read in silence, and the hall that listens but never speaks back.

Summary of the invention

According to the invention there is provided an address apparatus and method comprising: (i) a covered hall of not fewer than ten thousand seats, the Sportpalast at Berlin, fitted with a raised platform and a house lighting circuit capable of total blackout; (ii) an audience selected in advance and issued numbered tickets of admission, approximately fourteen thousand strong on 18 February 1943; (iii) a speaker admitted behind a heavy curtain at twenty minutes past eight in the evening; (iv) a microphone array of six units feeding the hall and a broadcast line to the wireless stations of the Reich; (v) a rostrum of oak, one lectern, and one glass of water; and (vi) a formulary of questions put to the assembly, each to be answered by acclamation, the final question concerning total war.

Claims

  1. A method of delivering a total-war address in a covered hall, comprising fixing the hall and the hour, admitting a vetted audience by numbered ticket, extinguishing the house lights, and summoning the speaker to a rostrum beneath a fixed spotlight.

  2. The method of claim 1, wherein the hall is the Sportpalast, Berlin, the hour is twenty minutes past eight in the evening, and the date is 18 February 1943.

  3. The method of claim 1, further comprising putting to the assembly a series of questions, each answered by acclamation, the final question being whether the assembly desires total war.

  4. The method of claim 1, further comprising transmitting the address over the wireless stations of the Reich simultaneous with its delivery.

  5. An apparatus for use in the method of claim 1, comprising an oak rostrum, one lectern, one glass of water, six microphone units, and a house lighting circuit capable of blackout and single-spot restoration.

  6. The apparatus of claim 5, wherein the stenographic record is kept sheet by sheet and the answer of the assembly is entered in the record as one voice.

  7. The lot as stated and will return it for the week.

Brief description of the drawings

Description

The hall is fixed first. The Sportpalast, Berlin, seats approximately fourteen thousand for this purpose, the audience chosen beforehand and seated by bloc, each bloc holding a numbered ticket of admission. At twenty minutes past eight the house lights are extinguished, the curtain is drawn, and the speaker advances to the rostrum beneath the spotlight, the lectern to the left, the glass of water to the right. Six microphone units stand before the rostrum; one feeds the hall, the remainder feed the broadcast line to the wireless stations of the Reich, so that the address leaves the hall at the same moment it enters it.

The formulary of questions is put in sequence. The assembly answers by acclamation, and the stenographer enters the answer as one voice, sheet by sheet. The record shows that the final question — whether the assembly desires total war — was answered without dissent, and that the hall remained fixed until the address ended.
