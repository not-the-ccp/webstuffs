1. What the ship was

The USS Jacob Jones, officially Destroyer No. 61 and later DD-61, was a Tucker-class destroyer built for the United States Navy just before America entered the First World War. The class was one of the larger wartime destroyers of the period, and the ship carried the designation of the day: a little over 315 feet (96 m) in length overall, a beam of just over 30 feet (9.1 m), and a standard displacement of 1,090 long tons (1,110 t). It is the first United States Navy vessel named in honor of Jacob Jones, the blockade runner and Continental naval officer of the American Revolution, and that is the point the report keeps returning to, because a name like that was a promise made before the crew ever stood a watch.

2. Built at Camden

Jacob Jones was laid down in August 1914 by the New York Shipbuilding Company of Camden, New Jersey, on the Delaware opposite Philadelphia. That is a year before the United States declared war, so the hull went into the water as a peacetime construction, and the launch came in May of the following year, May 1915, with the war still a European matter on the other side of the Atlantic. Ships of the class were driven by a pair of steam turbines, and Jacob Jones made up to 30 knots (56 km/h) under them, which for 1915 was a speed that let a destroyer of this size choose her engagements. I have checked the dates against the class notes in the class file, and the August 1914 keel and the May 1915 launch are the ones the yard's own log gives, two years and nine months of building in between.

3. Armament and fit-out

For guns, the ship carried four 4-inch (10 cm) guns, which at the range a destroyer was expected to fight was the right size: fast to train, fast to reload, and good enough against the small craft and coastal targets a 315-foot hull would meet. For the attack that destroyers were really built for, she carried eight 21-inch (533 mm) torpedo tubes, which is the number the class notes list and the number I have written in the margin of my sketch. A 21-inch tube is a big one, and eight of them mean the ship's whole job could be decided in a single minute of a single run, which is exactly the arithmetic a Tucker-class hull is designed around.

4. The name

Jacob Jones himself is the reason the name exists. He was a merchant captain turned naval officer of the Continental Navy in the 1770s, famous for a raid in which his sloop surprised and captured the British sloop St. Johnsbury, and he has been called the father of the American navy by the men who wrote the old histories. Naming the first ship for him in 1914 was not a decoration; it was a claim. The Camden builders were building a warship for a country that was still, officially, at peace, and the name said what the keel said: this hull is for the sea, and the sea is not finished with us.

For the drawing part of the report I sketched the ship from the hull profile in the class file and then held the sheet up against the Westgate crest on the door of Room 214, which has the crown over the wave and is the only nautical thing in the building that I am allowed to copy. I have left both in the report, the hull and the crest, because the crest is wrong and the hull is right, and the difference between them is a keel.

6. Conclusion

The ship holds together as a set of numbers: 315 feet, 30 feet, 1,090 long tons, four 4-inch guns, eight 21-inch tubes, two turbines, 30 knots. Underneath the numbers is the fact of the name, a Revolutionary-era captain put on a 1914 keel in Camden to make a warship that the Navy was building before the war. The report's conclusion is that DD-61 was built in peacetime on a promise, and the numbers above are what the promise weighed, to the ton.
