The Item

LOST, or rather found: one (1) group of nineteen astronauts, selected by the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Administration in April 1966, and known — since one of its own members chose the name in deliberate parody of the original Mercury Seven — as “The Original Nineteen.” The namer was John H. Young, test pilot, who saw in a class of nineteen a fine joke to tell at the expense of a class of seven, and who was right, because the joke turned out to be the truest record of the lot.

The group arrived intact and unaccompanied. It is described as roughly half of one kind of pilot and half of another: nine of the nineteen flew to the Moon in all, and of the six lunar module pilots who walked on the lunar surface, three came from this group — Neil A. Armstrong, Alan L. Bean, and Charles M. Duke Jr. — the other members of the walking club having been raised in earlier classes. The remaining half of the group flew Skylab and then the Space Shuttle, and supplied the core of the Shuttle's early commanders, a fact the flight line still cites whenever a commander signs the board for the seventh time: they were always going to do it again.

Particulars & Distinguishing Marks

One member of the group wore unusual marks: Joseph P. Engle had already earned astronaut wings before the corps ever met him — set in 1962 when, as an X-15 pilot, he flew the rocket plane to Mach 4.52. His was the only case on record of NASA hiring into the corps a man who already held the qualification; the personnel file notes it once, dryly, under “Prior Service,” and has not needed to note it again, because it has never happened to a second member, or to a second group.

Otherwise the group's marks are standard: test pilots, several in the Navy, several with combat sorties, one medical doctor, one who would go on to command the space station's crew for a time he measured in weeks and later in decades. All nineteen answered the same summons in the same April, and the office would be glad to hear from the relatives of any who remain unclaimed.

Where Found

The group was found on 9 July 1966, during the reshelving of the personnel store, in a crate of handwoven cotton shipped in from the coast, the weave turned over by the crate's weight. The nineteen were not in the cotton; the record of them was — two drawers of the manifest, name cards pinned, flight marks pencilled. Both drawers were filed under its own small weaving: in Case No. 14 Kruiskade, Paramaribo, Suriname. The address is not ours to explain; the office notes only that a drawer found in a weave in a case that is not here is still a drawer, still filed, still claimable, and that the record has been kept standing under this notice ever since, which is how the office handles everything that arrives already in order.

To Claim

Claims are received in the personnel store, first floor, between the balsa models and the flight boards. A claimant must present (1) a signed flight roster, (2) two witnesses who were there or saw the record, and (3) an account of what the nineteen did while unclaimed, which the office will compare with the manifest and find, in most cases, to agree word for word. Unclaimed items are not destroyed; the office does not. They are reshelved, one decimal deeper per year, at 623.4, under the thread on what a number owes a thing — the number holds, and the pencil is not required. The sister office, which keeps its own notice on the language found in the sample crate, answers to a different door but the same law, and will confirm the filing practice on request: see Lost & Found Notice 47B, Halls Creek State School office. Inquiries may also be left with the Webstuffs general counter, marked “Nineteen,” where they will be noticed and answered in order.
