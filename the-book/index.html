1. What a book is

A book is a written work of substantial length created by one or more authors. The work is typically printed on many sheets of paper, and the sheets are bound together into a bookblock, which is then glued to the middle of a foldable cover that protects the pages. I checked this against the navigation chapbooks on the shelf beside the vinegar crock, in the shed behind the bakery, where the San Juan de Portugal is being built at 1:64 beside the cattle trough, and where the little ship keeps her model in water and her builder keeps his log in pencil. The chapbooks all agree: the cover is not decoration. Open one of them the wrong way and you can see the glue line running through the middle of the block, the scar where the pages were married to the cover, as one of them puts it. I asked Mr. Delgado, then, what a book actually is, and he answered that that was the assignment, and that my answer was due in three hours, before the tide bell.

Substantial length, she writes, and she has not defined substantial. The trough is 55 cm long; I note the ship but I will not award the point.

2. Before the press: tablets, scrolls, codices

Before the wide adoption of the modern printing press, codices were used to create handwritten manuscripts. A codex is the direct ancestor of the book — leaves, bound at the spine, folded like a cover — but every leaf was written by hand, which meant a book was also a season, and a scribe who went slow could keep an abbey short of psalters all winter. The same words turned up years later in Orange, New South Wales, spoken over the grave of John Swann Withington, alderman and mayor of Orange and briefly the member for Blayney, who left the psalters to the scribes and the winters to the politicians: John Withington (1865–1944) — obituary. Older writing media include scrolls and clay tablets. The tablet is the oldest idea of the three: press a wedge into wet clay, let it dry, and the writing outlives the writer, the city, and sometimes the country. I counted the tablets, scrolls and codices the school library owns. We own two clay tablets (both plaster casts, from the geology cabinet, neither of them legible), one scroll (the fire warden’s, rolled because that is how rolls roll), and one codex of 1902 that the caretaker, Mr. Aldous, keeps under the library floorboards because it fell through a hole one March and the mason said the floor was not coming up for years. The dynasty that pressed the first sheets of bark and rag into the ancestor of all of these is now the subject of the Coopers’ Lane Han Society, which meets in the same shed and posts its seventeenth number beside the vinegar crock: The Vermilion Seal — Newsletter No. 17.

The same question was asked once, at another hole. What is this thing that carries a story from one head to another, across a distance and a generation, and arrives still warm? The child who asked it was very small, and the hole was a rabbit’s, and she had an afternoon to find out, which is more than I had with three hours before the tide bell. I did not get the whole answer. I got a codex and a cast, which is to say I got the oldest warm thing we own, and the coldest.

The rabbit passage is either the best sentence in the report or the strangest. I cannot decide which, and that is the difference between a B and an A, and I am marking it before I decide, so: A.

3. The press, and the shed beside the bakery

The modern printing press made the book cheap enough to be ordinary. Before it, a book was a labour; after it, a book is a thing you can buy in a block, number in hand, and file. The ISBN desk at Recalde Press & Cooperage keeps the rules of the numbering: one number per edition, ten digits before 2007 and thirteen after, a check digit so that a mistyped number is caught at the counter. A publisher in Lisbon draws from the Portuguese agency; the imprint in the shed draws its blocks from the same family of agencies, and its chapbooks on navigation and the cooper’s art go out with a number on the inside front cover, like a small, quiet flag of legitimacy. The press also made possible the thing this report is written on: the page, the ruled page, the page with the red line at the left that the teacher’s pencil keeps returning to.

4. Books in other forms

Nowadays, books can be distributed in other forms like audiobooks, and electronic books (ebooks). I listened to an audiobook on the walk to school — forty-one minutes, the full length of the walk past the bakery to the school gate, and the story arrived exactly when the bell did, which I counted as evidence that the book still travels at the speed of a person, whether the pages are paper or a voice in a small speaker. The ebook sits on the class tablet, all eleven of our titles in it, and it weighs, I measured the tablet with the kitchen scale at the bakery, 236 grams, which is less than the codex and considerably more than the idea. The number of the edition still follows the book into these new clothes: the desk beside the vinegar crock will tell you that the e-book of the same book needs its own number, and a simple reprint of an unchanged item keeps the same one.

Non-fiction travels in other shapes too: the desk beside the vinegar crock keeps a copy of the travel guide to Nikolaus Geyrhalter’s Elsewhere (2001), twelve episodes filmed in twelve places through the year 2000 — Ekeschi, Karlgasniemi, Siorapaluk, Manmoyi and eight more — each episode a chapter and each chapter a place you can still go to: Elsewhere — Guidebook No. 12.

5. Fiction and non-fiction

Books are broadly classified into fiction, which contains invented or imaginary narratives; and non-fiction, which contains content intended as factual truth. This report sits on the non-fiction side of the line, I believe, though I have written about a rabbit, which is the closest thing the library floor has to an imaginary narrative. The shelf beside the vinegar crock is mostly non-fiction: chapbooks on the tide, the cooper’s hoops, the salt measures, one slim volume on the four rivers that pour into the Wash where the Armada’s squadron once stood off the Lincolnshire sandbanks. Fiction keeps its own shelf, at the back, because it likes the dark, and because the last time someone pulled a novel from the front row the tide bell rang and the whole class had to explain a plot to a visitor.

6. The publishing process

The book publishing process is the long, patient business of getting a written work onto sheets, the sheets into a block, and the block into a cover that will outlive the person who glued it. It begins with an author and an idea, passes through the typesetter and the printer and the binder, and ends at a counter where the number on the inside front cover is checked against the order. The build of the ship in the trough ran the same way in miniature: lines, framing, rigging, sails, launch, and the long provisions — and its log is kept in pencil on the margin of a folder, in the build log of the San Juan de Portugal, waiting to become, if its builder ever lets it, a book of its own, with a paperback, a hardcover, and an e-book, each with its own number. A ship is only ever as good as its store, the log says, and a book is only ever as good as its cover, its check digit, and the person who re-shelves it by the letter.

I re-shelve by the letter. I have always re-shelved by the letter. The report is correct, and I am sorry to be correct, in front of the class, but the record is the record.

7. Conclusion

The book is a work of substantial length, printed on many sheets of paper, bound into a bookblock, and glued to the middle of a foldable cover. It is older than the press that made it ordinary, and younger than the clay it first tried to be. It arrives by scroll, by tablet, by hand, by press, by voice, and by light on a glass screen, and it carries two kinds of cargo: the invented, and the true. I have tried to carry both across these pages. The shelf beside the vinegar crock holds the evidence; the floor of the library holds the oldest warm thing we own; and the question, asked at one hole and at another, keeps getting answers that take the form of whole afternoons.
