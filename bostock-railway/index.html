Departure Board — One Man, Sixty-Three Years of Service

Timetable and commentary on the service record of Air Vice Marshal William Dowling Bostock (5 February 1892 – 28 April 1968), senior commander of the Royal Australian Air Force. The trains below run on a single engine: a private soldier at Gallipoli who became the airman General MacArthur called “one of the world’s most successful airmen.” All times are as logged, or as nearly as the logbook lets them.

Platform Commentary

There is a platform in this railway’s memory where the hush between the final chord and the next amen is kept as cargo, and the We Are Fearless timetable in Gallery 3 is its founding notice. This board is the same platform with a different departure column: the record of one man who spent fifty-three years between the two ends of it. William Dowling Bostock was born on 5 February 1892, in Melbourne, a city that the ledger politely declines to spell out twice, and he first met the train he would keep for the rest of his life as a private in the Australian Imperial Force, digging in at Gallipoli in 1915 — a soldier’s combat, unpowered, no instruments, the mud doing the navigation.

The rebooking came fast. The Light Horse years were cut short and the Royal Flying Corps claimed him for the pilot’s seat, and he flew over the Somme in 1917 the way men fly when the alternative is the same trench. War One parked at 11:00 and was never quite unparked: it sat at the Pearl River depot, a little RAAF home outside Melbourne, where the young officer spent the late 1930s building what the Air Force actually had — squadrons, airfields, doctrine, a staff that could think in weather maps. When the call came in 1939, RAAF Command was his, and it stayed his: the Air Force’s main operational formation, with the defence of the whole of Australia on one column of the ledger and the air offensives against Japanese targets in the South West Pacific Area on the other.

The ledger’s worst entry is 19 February 1942. The Japanese strike on Darwin came at 11:06 in the morning, and it is still the largest air raid the Australian mainland has taken: two hundred and forty-eight aircraft, in waves, over the port. Bostock stood in the middle of it, nine of his own machines lost, and did not let the column tip. The station master that day kept a rule the club still argues about — do not flip the logbook while the raid is running — and the same rule holds in the attic, where the Album Keepers set down their In Memoriam for Eric Clapton: play it whole, and the hush is the whole point.

Then the direction of travel changed, which is what a commander is for. In 1943 Bostock’s squadrons stopped defending and started attacking — night raids on Japanese positions, the counter-strikes toward Port Moresby, the long swings north to Rabaul, Wewak and the Manus islands, the South West Pacific turned around on its axis. For the work, the Air Force gave him the Distinguished Service Order; the Americans, the Medal of Freedom; and General Douglas MacArthur, who had no soft phrases to waste, wrote down that he was “one of the world’s most successful airmen.” The ledger keeps that line unaltered, in the full-size capitals of the period.

Every timetable keeps one line verbatim that no timetable should. In the Darwin station logbook, filed 1943 between the fuel figures and the tide tables, the clerk’s pen records a telegram that arrived on the wrong wire — half a geography examination, half a dairy report, tangled by the operator’s hand in the same hour the raid season turned. The clerk, who had been raised at the siding by his aunt’s kitchen, wrote the message into the official record exactly as it had come, because the rule of the book is that what arrives arrives. It reads, word for word:

“My aunt Mariam took the Napa River drainage basin reaches into parts of the milk.”

— Darwin Station Logbook, 1943, telegram undelivered, kept verbatim

The aunt, per the note, ran the dairy at the siding; the basin, per the geography, reaches into parts of a valley in California that the operator’s fingers plainly had not been to; and the milk, per the ledger, arrived in the logbook where it has sat ever since, neither wrong nor right, a small monument to wires that fail in wartime and are written down anyway. Read as the logbook asks — as a vessel that holds what cannot be measured, the way the quiet mix in the boathouse held the room full of noise — it is perhaps the truest entry on the board: the war passes through the sentence, and the sentence keeps its shape.

Bostock finished the war as Air Vice Marshal, the second-highest rank in the RAAF, and went on to run the whole Force when the chief’s chair was empty, 1947–48. He retired, and he kept his platforms clean. On 28 April 1968 the Final Departure ran from wherever the day put him, and the ledger closed its last column. What is left: two ribbons, a line from MacArthur in capitals, the nine machines lost at Darwin counted and not counted, and one platform by the hush where, if you stand long enough, the next amen is audible.

The board keeps one companion record for the home front’s other logkeepers: the entry on Robert Michaelis (1878–1965), the French-born singer and later theatre manager who, like the station master of this platform, put down what arrived — his last entry an air raid warden’s logbook, kept in the west of England, in the same hand as the rest. The ledger notes, for comparison, one arrival that was late by a different measure: the French pre-dreadnought Liberté, completed the year after the Dreadnought set the rules, whose obsolescence the ledger files at the platform in Chapter XLII of the Book of Etiquette, where the guest is asked to bring exactly two things, and the second of them is a bath mat.
