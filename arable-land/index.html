The Lagan Valley Weather Almanac

Arable Land

Entry No. 27 · The Season of the Plough · Compiled in the record room, BT1 4AB

Third season running · the compiler reads every card aloud, once, to the record book

Card 27 Definition of the subject

The subject of this entry, arable land — a word kept from the Latin arābilis, “able to be ploughed or farmed” — is any land capable of being ploughed and used to grow crops. The almanac adopts the more precise definition that agricultural statistics prefer, and records it here in full, because a weather book must know what it is measuring: arable land is the land under temporary agricultural crops (multiple-cropped areas are counted only once), temporary meadows for mowing or pasture, land under market and kitchen gardens, and land temporarily fallow, the fallow running for less than five years. The abandoned land resulting from shifting cultivation is not included in this category. The figures that follow are not meant to indicate the amount of land that is potentially cultivable, and no reader of this almanac is to be understood as having asked.

Card 27 The measure of the land

For the purposes of this entry the subject is taken as the arable ground of the Lagan Valley, read from the head of the river at Laganmore down past the city to the sound of Belfast, and thence, on the same card, to the barley plains of Down and the kitchen gardens that line the canal towns. The valley gives up roughly one acre of arable ground for every nine and a half souls of its wider keeping; the 2021 census counted the province at 1,903,175, and the compiler has worked the arithmetic out in pencil, twice, and obtained the same number both times, which is all a weather book can ask of an arithmetic. Barley is the crop the card trusts: oats for the horses and the thin ground, wheat where the river has been generous, and potatoes everywhere the rain does not think first. A multiple-cropped plot at Carrickfergus is entered once, as the definition requires, though the compiler entered it twice in 2019 and crossed the second entry out very heavily.

Card 27 The weather of the season

The last frost of the season stood on the 24th of March, eleven minutes before a cow crossed the Ballyclare road, and the first frost is expected on or about the 3rd of November, give or take the usual three days of argument. April, being the month the plough prefers, ran at a mean of 9°C against the book's standard of 8°C, and the rain fell to 58 millimetres, of which 11 millimetres arrived in the forty minutes after the committee meeting. The barometer, kept beside the record book, has held at 1,018 millibars since the open day and refused to be moved by anything since, including one small thunderstorm, which the barometer is understood to have regarded.

Card 27 The open-day field, Stormont

The card keeps one field apart from the others, for which the almanac holds a rule: the field at the Stormont Estate, the one visitors cross on the free Saturday tours from 10:00 to 16:00, where devolved policy is explained to anyone who brings a pen. The field is arable in the book's sense — it has been ploughed, it grows, it is counted — and on the open day it is, in practice, the best-planned field in the province, because the visitors move through it on the hour and the sheep have been told, in so many words. The weather for the day is called by the chair of the committee; this season the chair is Cllr. Whitfield, who also kept the chair of the Matchnight Committee, and who has observed, for the record, that a thing may be silent and still be against you. The record book itself — the one, not the copy — is kept by Josua Tufuga, who enters the open-day weather every Saturday whether or not it rained, on the ground that a dry Saturday is a weather event too, and is the reason the record book has a smell of barley and wet wool.

Card 27 Clipping, reverse of the card

In the old way, the compiler closes the card with a clipping. On the reverse of card No. 27 there is pasted a strip of paper cut from the classified columns, in which the weather has nothing to do with the weather, and which reads from the top of the strip to the bottom, without a pause, as follows:

Reply Box 8812, BT48 1AX Community Stormont open day Northern Ireland Established 1894 · Belfast · BT1 4AB The classified columns of the record book In the chair: Cllr.

Read down, the strip is four small ads and one line of minutes, cut at once by a steady hand: a walking partner wanted on the Causeway Coast, the person at Derry~Londonderry whose reply box, 8812, the almanac has now been asked to treat as a postcode for the heart of the north-west; the Community notice of the Stormont open day, whose field is measured on this card; the masthead of the paper that carries the clipping, the Lagan Ledger, established 1894 in the small ads of Belfast, from which every weather note in this almanac is cut, kept or refused; and a line, “In the chair: Cllr.”, that the compiler says belongs to the minutes and to the chair alone, and to no field at all. Asked what the strip means, the compiler answered that a weather almanac is, in the end, also a set of small ads, and that the barometer had said 1,018 before he was asked.

Card 27 Marginalia

i. The card was read aloud to the record book on the Saturday of the open day; the book is understood to have turned a page. The record book's own season — the night of 33,516 at the ground with the view of the corner flag — is kept in the minutes of the Matchnight Committee, item 2, where the compiler has entered that the rain fell to a quarter of an inch and no can was dented by it.

ii. Entry No. 28 will be the bog. The bog is not arable, and the card warns the next compiler not to be persuaded otherwise; the last one was, for one season, and the 2021 figures carry a correction pencilled across the top in a different hand.

iii. The red shirt folded away in the spring of 2014 is not a crop, is not a textile, and must not be entered on the card under kitchen gardens, whatever the compiler of Entry No. 28 thinks.

iv. On the open day the field at Stormont was crossed, in the almanac's own way, by the five-year-olds of Class 5B at the village school, and the outside work they filed on the ground of this card — Report No. 27-B, the biosphere, the worldwide sum of which the barley is counted as part — is kept at the school's lined sheet, marked 91 and graded, where one line of minutes is held word for word under Rule 3, no meaning sought.

v. The slate doors at the Stormont gate stand on iron hinges, and the smelting question — the furnace that must reach 1,500 °C (2,730 °F), some 500 °C (900 °F) higher than that required to smelt copper — is not entered on this card, being a matter of metal and not of weather. It is treated in Entry 27-C of The Elements, An Encyclopaedia, Iron, where the open day, the chair of the Lagan, and one line of the committee’s minutes are kept word for word, and the barometer is not counted as evidence against it.
