The Council of the Hall · Sitting held in the room above the red ink

Minutes No. 58The Matter of Augustine of Hippo

Held in the hall of A Book of Etiquette, this 28th day of August, the hour being the hour at which, in 430, the bay of Hippo Regius went quiet, and the maps of Numidia lying rolled at the north wall as No. 57 directed.

Present The Keeper of Names (chair); the Hon. Saint Ambrose of Milan, excused from the room but present by letter from the Chancery of the Hrazdan, the letter being read at length, as it is long; the Hon. Mar Jacob of Serugh, present by portrait, the door standing on his side, which is where the book keeps it; Mrs. Monica of Thagaste, on behalf of the household, who was heard three times and interrupted twice, both times by the room; the grey bronzeback, present in person and keeping his shelf by the parcel post, which is a presence, and not an argument; and Graham, the parish clerk, as Clerk of the Minute Book, the ink being red, as ordered.

Absent Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis, deceased 28 August 430; his Confessions, ten books, were read into the record in his place, Book X being read twice because the room asked, and his City of God, twenty-two books, was noted as on file and not read, on the ground of weight.

Apologies Ephrem the Syrian, excused on the score of antiquity, which the chair ruled is a score the Hall recognises; Narsai of Nisibis, detained at the last seat of the table; and the Vandals of Gaiseric, whose apology was not requested, and who did not offer it.

1 The man, and his coming

The Clerk read the first item. Augustine of Hippo — in the Latin books, the form in which the bearer himself wrote it, Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis, and in the mouths of the house simply Saint Augustine — was born at Thagaste, in the province of Numidia Cirtensis, on the 13th of November, 354, and died at Hippo Regius, on the sea, on the 28th of August, 430, in the seventy-seventh year, with the city under siege and the door, as this Council has already observed in Chapter IX of the book, on his side. He is generally regarded as one of the most influential philosophers in the history of the Western world, and is viewed as one of the most important Church Fathers of the Latin Church in the Patristic Period; the Clerk read both clauses aloud and the room did not laugh, which the Clerk recorded in the margin in a firm hand.

In his youth he was drawn to the Manichaean faith, in which he kept a desk, the Council was told, for about nine years; and later to the Hellenistic philosophy of Neoplatonism, which the chair described as a second youth, taken up late and returned in good order. After his conversion to Christianity and his baptism in 386 by Saint Ambrose, Augustine developed his own account of the matter, and kept it — the chair added, for the record and against the shelves — until 430, when the Vandals, of whom the house keeps a fence and two villages at a time, knocked at the sea gate, and the city took a long time to answer. The Council noted that a philosopher who keeps one sea for thirty-five years owes that sea a different greeting from one who moves among sees, and that the book of etiquette has always held that the door goes with the bishop.

2 The name, and its two vowels

The chair called the matter, which is the matter. The name is registered in the Hall under three spellings — Augustine, Austin, and Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis — and in two pronunciations, the English mouth having done the work of three councils: aw-GUST-in in one house, and AW-gə-steen in the house across the water, the Latin form, OW-goo-STeen, being admitted at the door as found and set down with the vowels in their original places. The Clerk, having been told the second form twice, pronounced it once, correctly, and the room allowed him the one line in the margin it allows.

It was proposed that one of the two English forms be struck, on the ground that a name should not carry two vowels at once. Mrs. Monica of Thagaste spoke against it, from memory and from habit, and was overruled on the substance and thanked on the manner. The chair then read the same rule No. 57 read for the Korean name, namely: spell the name as the bearer spells it, and only as the bearer spells it. The Council held, without division, that the bearer spelled it Aurelius Augustinus in his own hand, and that both English forms descend from that spelling by the ordinary wear of tongues; that both stand as registered; that the Latin form is noted in the margin of the maps, inked lightly, so that it may be read at the second introduction and kept as a gift; and that no Augustine — the hall keeps doors under the name at Thagaste, at Hippo, and at Milan, and the bronzeback keeps one — is to be corrected before his third drink. The two vowels, the Clerk added, are to be argued no further; No. 57 argued a hyphen to its end, and this Council has ruled that a vowel, being soft, is not to be argued at all.

3 The seat at the table, and the door

It was put to the Council that the book of etiquette seats Mar Jacob between Ephrem and Narsai “with the door on his side,” and that the door, in the house, is the furniture that goes with the man. The Clerk read the count from Chapter IX: well over two hundred mēmrē claimed, fifty-one authenticated, the lower figure kept on the shelf because a number that is hard to reach is a number worth reaching for. The house applies the same count to this man: ten books of the Confessions authenticated, twenty-two of the City on file, and the remainder — the letters, the sermons, the questions and answers — set down in the drawer, uncounted, for the reason that a bishop's correspondence is not a dish with the rim intact and must not be carried in one mouth.

As to the door: the Council was reminded that at Hippo Regius, in the winter of 430, the sea gate stood against Gaiseric and his men, and that the bishop, being the man with the door, kept it open to those who had a question and shut it against those who had a battering ram, and that he died on the night the city's singing stopped. The chair ruled that a door kept in that manner goes on a man's account of age, which is the only piece of furniture the book has ever placed there, and the room assented, being the room that assents to doors.

4 The drawer, and the catalogue in it

The Hall keeps the file on the name in the shared drawer of shelf five, half a shelf below the tortoise's parcel post, and beside the papers of the house's late Transmission — Error TM-2008, Publication Ceased, whose seventeenth and final issue printed a catalogue of the saints' names in three alphabets, one for each sitting of its year, and whose drawer the house has kept on its hinges since. The Clerk read the shelf line as it stands in the minute book, and it is entered here as found, capital and all, and the Clerk was asked to set it down exactly and to stop thinking about drawers:

Some catalogues set him down with its whole care, for a London literary magazine that appeared three times a year and stopped at seventeen issues in 2008.

The room was heard to object that the line is not in order; the Clerk read it a second time; the chair ruled that the line is not out of order but out of its magazine, and that a line out of its magazine is the property of the shelf, and the shelf keeps what it keeps. The bronzeback, whose name was entered in the visitor's book by his own keeping, was noted by the Clerk as having shifted one parcel to make the drawer sit level, and the shift was recorded, without comment, in the same red ink.

5 The table of many languages, and the adjournment

For the etiquette of many languages at one table — many fathers, many tongues, one room, no one rewritten — the Council refers the room to Chapter VII of the desk book of the Recalde Press & Cooperage, of the Common Language Infrastructure, where the rule is that every language waits its turn and every barrel is hooped before it leaves the shed, and where the same rule, it will be noted, was observed at this table: the Latin spoke first, the Syriac by portrait, the English in two vowels, and no one was left standing, which is the whole of the infrastructure in one line. For the sitting that argued a matter of names in this hall before this one, and ruled both forms admitted and neither form argued, see Minutes No. 57 — The Matter of McCune–Reischauer, which stands on record beside this one, hyphen and vowel, the two soft places of a name. For the baptizer, present by letter, and the acclamation of 374 that put a statesman on a see, see Ambrose of Milan — A Letter to the Editor, from the Chancery of the Hrazdan, where the letter this Council read at length is kept with the cross-file. The whole shelf of the book, and the table it seats, stands on the front page of the house, shelf five, and the bronzeback keeps his own shelf beside it, which is a shelf of a kind this Council has never had to rule on and never will.

So ordered, so minuted, and so closed, the door on its side, the ink uncapped, and the room exhaling as it does when a matter of this size is properly finished, on the 28th of August, the day the bay went quiet.

The Keeper of NamesGraham, Clerk of the Minute Book
