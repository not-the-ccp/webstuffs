To the Editor,

Γαλατιαν

The Epistle to the Galatians — a letter to the editor, in answer to the shelf

THE ALBUM KEEPERS RECEIVED AT THE BOATHOUSE
AT THE HOUR OF THE HUSH

Dear Editor,

I am seventy-six, and I am writing from the second chair by the boathouse bench, the one the club has kept for the Greeks, about the small red-cloth book that has ridden on the shelf beside the 1937 Sydney lecture for forty years: a pocket Greek Testament, spine cracked at the third page of the New Testament, which the major kept in the glovebox and in his coat pocket at the same time, the way a soldier keeps a whistle. Tonight I read it to the chair. Not the whole of it — the chair is patient, that is the point — but the Epistle to the Galatians, from its opening greeting to the last mercy, and I am writing because the letter I read aloud is, in a way I did not know until I was doing it, a letter to an editor. Paul the Apostle writes it to a number of Early Christian communities in Galatia, and somewhere in between their doorsteps and the second chair, it has been writing to mine.

It is, in the count the shelf has always kept, the ninth book of the New Testament and the fourth of the Pauline epistles in the traditional arrangement. The scholars — I read them the way we read records, whole and in order — disagree where the letter was addressed, and the disagreement is the first joke of it: Galatia was either the Roman province in southern Anatolia, or the larger country defined by the Galatians, a Celtic people of central Anatolia, who had crossed over there with their kettles and their long hair and their stubbornness, and stayed. The letter was originally written in Koine Greek — the street Greek of the eastern Mediterranean, the Greek of the market and the ship’s cabin — and later translated into other languages, which is to say it was written to be read out loud, the way Paul’s communities would have heard it: somebody standing, somebody sitting, the town listening. In it, Paul is principally concerned with the controversy that was eating the Apostolic Age, the one between Gentile Christians and the Mosaic Law, and he argues the case the way his old friend the argument went in that Sunday class of ours: that the law could not make a person righteous, that faith could, that “a man is not justified by the works of the law,” and that the communities of Galatia were being badgered, by his own word, by troubleshooters from nowhere who would have put them back under the yoke he had just lifted. I do not know if Paul wrote it in haste; the red pencil in the margins of my husband’s copy says he thought he did.

Which brings me to the red pencil, and to why the chair is hearing it. The major ran that class — Sunday, three of the afternoon, the boathouse bench, six of us and a blackboard that had been a window somewhere. I was the worst of the six at the Koine; I was the best at showing up. He never corrected me out loud. He corrected me in the margins of my pocket book, in that small red hand, and in the last winter, the one before the club named the second chair, he marked my last margin, see you on the other side of the text, which is the only sentence in this letter I will not quote, because the chair has it now. In December of 1976 he went west to the prairie ground, and I went with him, because the club’s thread on that winter fight — the one the field board keeps, Prairie Grove, Field Thread No. 191 — had argued the night over for four years and somebody ought to have stood on it. We stood on it. There was snow in the corn stubble and only one of us besides him, and I will say it once, in the clerk’s place, because it is true and because the thread deserved a witness: Powell, on the ridge into mud, and you can see why neither commander claimed victory. He would not say which side of the ridge he had been on. He said, once, both, in the same hour, and we walked back down the prairie road toward the Arkansas the way Blunt had walked his division, in order, and he read the whole of the second chapter of Galatians to the car, quietly, without a book, and I have been unable since to separate the voice from the prairie.

So: the letter is his, it is Paul’s, and it is the chair’s now. It is the one Paul wrote in large hand himself toward the end, where the scribe had been doing the work — Behold, I write to you of my own hand, the big letters, the autograph, the signature in the text — and it is the one that carries the thorn in the flesh, the mercy asked three times, the “neither Jew nor Greek, neither bond nor free, neither male nor female,” and the last line, the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, which is the whole peroration. Read to a chair, it does what it was written to do to a town: it stands up the argument, balances the clauses, and leaves the room holding its own breath. My husband’s entry — the division’s, the long one, the one that ends still in Greek, John Enoch Powell, Biographical Entry No. 48 — does not set down the red pencil. The shelf does. I am setting it down here, in answer to the shelf, in the place the letter belongs: between the editor and the reader, in the Koine, whole, in order, without flipping.

Yours in the Greek,

Helen Vane — Wolverhampton, the second chair

P.S. — The pocket book is where the shelf will have it, between the Pindar and the Sydney lecture. If the division ever reads the whole of the New Testament to the chair, the way we read records, let it take Galatians fourth, in the traditional arrangement, and let it read it the way Paul would have heard it read back: out loud, from somebody standing, to somebody sitting in a bench under a drip, keeping the count. The front of the club’s keeping is at the house, and this letter is filed beneath it.
