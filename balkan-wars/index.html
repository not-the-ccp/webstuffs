The Balkan Wars, 1912–1913

A school report written by Maximilian Baumgartner, Class 9B, on the two wars that redrew every border in our atlas corner in the space of eleven months — and on which of the new borders belongs on the lid of the tin biscuit box.

1What the Balkan Wars are

The Balkan Wars were two conflicts that took place in the Balkan states in 1912 and 1913. They are the kind of history that is very hard to do on two pages, because in the space of one winter the map of southeastern Europe was torn up and re-pasted, and then, the following summer, a different part of the paste was ripped off. The First Balkan War was fought by four states — Greece, Serbia, Montenegro, and Bulgaria — acting as the Balkan League, against the Ottoman Empire. The Second was fought, which is much worse, by Bulgaria against the other four. I have tried to keep them in order, and in one place, where the dates crowd, I have given in and let the atlas decide.

2The First Balkan War, 1912–13

The League declared upon the Ottoman Empire in the autumn of 1912, and the campaign went the way it was going to go. In October the Bulgarian army crossed the Maritsa river and broke through at Lule Burgas and Dospat; in September the Serbian army won at the Battle of Kumanovo, one of the heaviest artillery battles before the Great War, with some 130,000 men on the field. Adrianople — the old Ottoman capital, Edirne on the atlas — held out until 9 December 1912, and its fall was rung in on church bells from Sofia to Athens. By the spring the Ottomans had been stripped of their European provinces: Thessaloniki to Greece, most of Macedonia between the three, and the sandzak of Novipazar to Serbia, until the map of the Balkans in the atlas looked like a quilt made by a very fast tailor. By the Treaty of London of 30 May 1913, only Eastern Thrace remained under Ottoman control, and a strip of land around Constantinople that was never quite finished being discussed.

3The Second Balkan War, summer 1913

Then the argument began, and the argument had a front line. Bulgaria, which had taken the largest share, wanted more — more of Macedonia, the Aegean coast, the road to the sea near Kavala. Serbia and Greece refused, the League broke, and on 29 June 1913 Bulgaria found itself fighting Serbia and Greece on the west, the army of King Constantine I of Greece at the Dardanelles-side passes, and then, on 23 June, the unexpected one: Romania entering from the north, taking the old Dobruja back from the Danube delta to the river. It had been the same four-plus-one in different hats. The war lasted ten weeks and ended with the Treaty of Bucharest on 10 August 1913, in which the four carved the rest out. The Ottoman Empire lost the bulk of its territory in Europe. Bulgaria kept a coast at Burgas and lost a war; I do not count that as a bargain, and the report does not ask me to.

4Where the map went afterwards

Although not involved as a combatant, Austria-Hungary became relatively weaker as a much enlarged Serbia pushed for union of the South Slavs under Belgrade's umbrella, and the great powers spent 1913 quietly pretending this would not matter. It mattered; I have left the rest out on purpose, because it is a whole different report and my two pages were already short.

The part of the report that is mine to be precise about: Bulgaria. The country now has a case of its own in the Carinthian Rooms — Placard No. 0207 in Case 12, Gallery of Borders and Small Crossings — where it is set out at 110,994 square kilometres with its capital Sofia and its cities of Plovdiv, Varna, and Burgas, and where the seven-country tin biscuit box, lost-and-found No. 114, is the lid that stamped it. In our class card file the entry for it is printed, and I copy it exactly because the file is the file: One of the King’s College 1754 expansion card, and the sixteenth-largest country in Europe by area. The expansion card is in the card file beside the atlas, and it shows the post-war borders, so the card is, technically, a picture of the peace the wars made.

5The exchange: 1913 by radio, 21:00

For the exchange element, 9B's pen-friend class in Helena, Arkansas, sent back a note about the beetle, because the beetle was in the room. The pressed tumbler — Mordellistena dalmatica, blue-black, 4 mm — had travelled from my grandmother's script drawer, which was on temporary loan at the time, to Klagenfurt, where it stands in Case 12 next to the placard about the country of its home hedge, collected from the rose hedge at the Ilovo station in 1991 and released, the label says in smaller type, to a hedge. Helena's line, as transcribed and returned by Fräulein Höchsteiner and printed with a note that she was sorry, but the tape was the tape, reads:

“The war had four against one, then one against four, and the beetle kept flipping through all of it, which we have checked against the ledger of 34 windows and it is so.”

— Exchange note, Helena, Arkansas, received 11 November, transcribed by F.H., who added in pencil: “The ledger does not list a beetle. Leave it.”

My understanding, which the teacher accepted: the two wars ran in opposite directions, and the only thing in the file that faced the same way at the start and the end was the beetle, which does not know the difference between a hedge and a border, and the museum — on the point of the coffee stains — holds it not responsible.

6Conclusion

Eleven months, two treaties, one empire left with Eastern Thrace and a city, one kingdom left with a coast and a grievance. I recommend the atlas to any class that wants a history that moves; page 308 is the place to stand. The tumbler is not recommended for history, for the reasons in section 5. My grandmother's archive, with its script drawer, its brooch, and its three-alphabet address book, is the whole reason the beetle was anywhere near a report on the Balkans in the first place, and it was offered, in full, as Lot 314 — Monika Baumgartner, Personal Archive and Working Library; the drawer's own report — the Bavaria report of 70,550 square kilometres, this report's older sibling — is the Class 9B school report on Bavaria, and I keep my two reports in the same folder so the atlas does not get ideas.
