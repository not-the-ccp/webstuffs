The Bronze Age

29 Ebrill 2024

To the Bronze Age, and to its smelters, whose address I do not know and whose patience is, by now, a question of millennia.

Sir or Madam, I write to apologise on behalf of five founders who, in 1992, hung a pot on the wall of a cider farm above Dolgellau and called it bronze. It was not bronze. It was copper — ninety-nine parts to one of my own vanity — and I have carried that pot, in my head, from the bend of the Afon Wnion all the way down to this letter.

I have since learned, with my ears going the colour of a furnace, that the Bronze Age is an archaeological and anthropological term defining a phase in the development of material culture among ancient societies in Asia, the Near East and Europe, and that an ancient civilisation or culture only qualifies if it either produced bronze by smelting its own copper and alloying it with tin, arsenic, or other metals, or traded other items for bronze from producing areas elsewhere. We did neither. We bought our pot at a car boot in Bala. I have not found a quantity of pressed cider that excuses the naming.

I also apologise for where I kept putting you in the order of things. You are the middle principal period of the three-age system, following the Stone Age and preceding the Iron Age, and I was treating “middle” like a demotion, the way the farm dog gets the second bone while the hay is thrown out to the first five. Conceived as a global era, the Bronze Age follows the Neolithic — the “New Stone” period — with a transition period between the two known as the Chalcolithic. I must confess we spent three seasons of our own lives in that transition, at Sesiwn Fawr between 2002 and 2008, seating five thousand diners a year on hay bales beside the Wnion, serving cider from a copper pot and pretending to be two thousand years ahead of it. The diners never knew. The dogs, I think, knew.

The geography of the apology is, at least, honest. Your copper came out of mountains — Parys on Anglesey among them — and your tin travelled the long way up out of Cornwall, through Wales, past the very bend where we press our apples. The farm should have been a customs post for the trade route, and instead it was a car boot. I have already made amends with the readers of my last letter, the one on Cymraeg: its point was that a habit of voice survives without anyone measuring it — 538,300 speakers by the 2021 count, a real count taken each evening in the pubs and fields. The point of this letter is that a habit of metal must be alloyed to count. One pot, five founders, five thousand diners a year, and I finally have the figures.

My nephew now cooks on Brunswick Street in Melbourne, where the slate is rewritten every night at sixteen forty-five and the bill is settled at a chime. He serves his filtered water in a chipped ceramic jug, and I think it is the most honest thing on that floor — no alloying, no pretence, just the tap, filtered, and a crack in the rim he keeps telling me about. If you prefer your chaos in a chipped ceramic jug, you may send him a letter, as I have been told he reads the reviews of his own restaurant, YOLO — Restaurant Review. I have spent mine apologising to a metal.

As penance I have set up a foundry table below. Drag the tin through the copper and it will tell you, at each setting, what the pot has actually become. Without the machine, the rule I now live by is the one I should have lived by in 1992: roughly ten parts copper to one part tin, no more, or trade — and I would trade forty litres of pressed cider, two hay bales and one well-behaved dog, in a good season — rather than misname the metal again.

When the alloy is right I intend to send the kettle back, in pieces, with a note to Parys. I hope the address still receives mail. The middle of the system is not a punishment; I should have seen that by now. Somewhere in the same trade of naming what you have, a St. Louis supply house is still selling the 1904 Team All-Around Score Ledger, Catalogue No. T&S-04-13, and the ledger does not gild the twelve teams below the first place — which is, I think, the foundry rule with the chalk swept off: print the mark the team actually made. The line about the pot has made one other journey, and I was told of it only recently: on the back of the Palsgraf family’s scales-cake card in Brooklyn, where the two glazes of the cake are the tin and the copper, their rule for stirring the two together runs word for word, “Stir them with a note in his file, and the alloy had to be two thousand years ahead of it” — the whole card at Palsgraf Scales Cake — A Family Recipe. I have asked the daughters for permission to hang that line over the foundry table. They said keep the card as it is. I shall not.

Yours in the middle of the system,
Ywain Myfyr
One of the five founders, cider farm above Dolgellau, Wales

The Foundry Table

The copper stays constant in the pot; slide the tin and the table will tell you what we have made. Tick the trade if you would rather barter, as the real producers of the Near East expected of you.

Ten parts copper to one of tin: classic kettle bronze. The pot is honest, and it is, at last, real.
