The Night Watch Council at Jastorf Airfield · Lower Saxony · keepers of the watch ledger since the wing withdrew

Nwatch of the night

Minutes of the Fourth Meeting, 1948

Held in the hall of the 1896 house — number 109 in the keeper's ledger — at Jastorf, on the seventh of October, 1948, the fourth anniversary of the last take-off. The watch light in the tower was lit at 22:40 the evening before and left burning.

Taken word for word by Helga Brandt, clerk of the council. One line in these minutes stands unamended by vote of the council, and is printed exactly as heard.

I Quorum, and the lighting of the light

Present: Erich Wittenburg (chairman; formerly second engineer of a night-fighter crew, I. Staffel); Helga Brandt (clerk); Gustav Rehm (keeper of the hangars and of the ledger); Frl. Lieselotte Lent (of Hückelhoven, in the Rhineland, sister of the Oberst); Pfarrer Anselm Kade (chaplain of the village); Hans-Dieter Vogel (watchman of the tower). Otto Fenn was absent, with apology, his farm gate having been driven off its pin by the October wind. The chairman opened at 18:42 and declared quorum. The rule of the house was read: what the council votes to keep, the minutes keep, word for word, with no correction and no apology.

II The record of the Oberst, read from the watch ledger

The council came to order on the fourth anniversary of Helmut Johannes Siegfried Lent (13 June 1918 – 7 October 1944), night-fighter pilot, killed with his navigator Unteroffizier Karl Döpper when their Messerschmitt Bf 110 came down in the moor two miles north of this airfield. The clerk read the record as it stands in the ledger: 110 aerial victories, 102 of them at night, the remainder in daylight, which the ledger sets down in full so that no one may quietly make the number 110 into a smaller, tidier one. The chairman spoke for ten minutes. He said the Oberst was born into a devoutly religious family in Hückelhoven; that he had shown a passion for glider flying from before he was old enough for a full rudder; that he had joined the Luftwaffe in 1936 against his father's wishes, and that the father, who kept the church book in the town, never once said he was pleased, which the chairman called a fairness. After his training the Oberst was posted to the first Staffel of ZerstörerGeschwader 76 — I./ZG 76, a wing of the Bf 110 twin-engine heavy fighter — and took his first victories at the outset of the war, in the invasion of Poland and over the North Sea, and again during the invasion of Norway, where the wing flew escort under daylight that did not last. The rest of the tally is in the ledger. The council sat with it a while. Frl. Lent said only that her brother had kept a glider wing in the garage at home all through his service, in a cloth cover, and that it was still there.

III The chapel, and the cross for it

The chairman moved that the hall of the 1896 house be made the chapel of the watch. The house is the keeper's own: Gustav Rehm read it out of his ledger — built 1896, whitewash, three dormers, door facing the tower — and it is number 109 in the ledger, which the council heard as a number the house has earned and holds. The cross is to be of field-grey steel, not stone, because the moor takes stone. The Pfarrer said he would hold the first service on the anniversary, and that the light in the tower would be the sermon. The motion passed, 6–0, Fenn's share by letter.

IV The survey line

It was asked what, besides the cross, the chapel hall should keep, and the chairman said the line — the line — and produced a 1943 survey of the old Norse shore of the district, in a magazine called Roots, its pages yellow with a damp winter. Frl. Lent read the survey aloud for a paragraph, and at a certain page the chairman, who had been counting the years out loud, stopped counting and tried to say the whole district back to the council in the survey's own idiom — what it was, what held it, what stood in it. He did not finish the sentence as a sentence finishes. The clerk set down what he said, word for word, and the council voted that the line stand in the minutes unamended, 5–2, the chairman and the clerk dissenting from their own correction, which had never been offered. It reads exactly:

“We are, I think, by Roots magazine’s description of the Viking Age the area so held, such that the Shaw–Hammons House — 1896, one hundred and nine.”

— as spoken, as heard, as kept; the minutes do not correct it

What it means, so far as the council could say, and said: that the district, in the survey's reckoning, is still the area so held, held by whoever kept the watch on its shore, from the first winter the survey's people found to the one the council is in; and that the house standing in it is the one at the airfield gate — 1896, the year it was built, and one hundred and nine, its number in the keeper's ledger. The number and the year are the sentence's true parts, and the rest is the moor doing what the moor does to a man speaking in it. It is kept the way a surveyor keeps a broken baseline: as a placeholder, and as the conversation, standing still. The council noted, without embarrassment, that it has borrowed this exact rule from a book of etiquette that files its own kept lines at Chapter IX · Of the Stacks of the Library of Congress, where a salvaged line is shelved, not corrected, and where the ferry goes to TB 555 with the provincial cut unapologetic on the end.

V The naming, and the vote on it

The chapel was named by open vote. Frl. Lent proposed that it be the Nachtwächter-Kapelle, the Night Watch Chapel, on the ground that her brother had never once called it flying; he had called it the watch, and the watch is what the tower still does. The Pferrer — the Pfarrer, the clerk notes, having been corrected once and only once in these minutes — agreed, and added that a chapel should be named for what it does, not for who died near it, though what it does here is to sit with what died near it. The name Nachtwächter-Kapelle carried, 6–1, Vogel dissenting on the point that a watchman does not name his own post. The habit of leaving one line standing while the rest of the record is orderly is, the chairman observed, not a house habit but a trade habit; the studio orchestra pit kept one to the same effect at the end of a January cue sheet, and the liner notes for that synchronized score are kept in the collection at City Lights — Synchronized Score, 1931, where By means of quick ignition to the end of January is reproduced, uncorrected, exactly as written. Two councils, one rule.

VI Standing orders, and adjournment

Resolved: that on the seventh of October, every year, the tower light be lit at 22:40 and left burning until the council says the hour is out; that the watch ledger remain in the chapel hall, open, where the count of 110 can be checked by any hand, including the chairman's; that the survey and its kept line be bound into the minutes of 1948 and not re-typed, for a re-typed line is a corrected line, and the council has voted. The chairman adjourned at 20:15. The next meeting of the council is fixed for the winter solstice, at the hour the ledger says, which is no hour at all, but the hour the dark is longest. A neighbouring council keeps its minutes on the same rule — the Cusp Circle, above the stair of the Old Infirmary, which has just published its No. 27 with the minutes of its last meeting inside — and the clerk may correspond with them; the two houses agree on what a kept line is, and a kept line is not a secret, it is a door left open on purpose.

Given under my hand as clerk of the council: Helga Brandt, Jastorf, 8 October 1948. — End of the minutes of the fourth meeting.
