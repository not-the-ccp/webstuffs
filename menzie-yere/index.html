I — The name, and its two jobs

Begin where every bearer of this name is eventually sent: to the Onomast’s Board, Thread No. 8814, where the spelling Yere is logged as doing first-name duty in Cotonou and Port Moresby and surname duty in at least two more places, and where the rule is that the board keeps the spelling the bearer gives. For the subject of this entry the surname holds, full stop: Emmanuel Menzie is the given name, Yere is what the families behind him carry, and he took it onto the pitch for the whole of his career. The Board’s own card once listed him, in one margin, as a Beninese footballer, and in another as a Papua New Guinean rugby league footballer, both with the same 1983. The visitor should not try to settle the argument in a queue; the passport is green, the register is what the register is, and a man who has scored 195 tries does not lose a try over a footnote.

He is also, by a coincidence the Board has allowed itself to enjoy, the younger brother-in-law of the Lae system: the thread’s oldest entry, Yere Goud of 1971, came up on the same mudflats and keeps a standing offer to settle any match-report misspelling against his own parish book. In Lae the name is a first name; in Sheffield it is the last one you call. Both spellings, one ledger.

II — Sheffield: Bramall Lane and the counter at the gate

From the station it is seventeen minutes by taxi along Portobello Lane, and the driver can be told simply the rugby place, not the football place. Bramall Lane has been both in its long life and the visitors always confuse them, which is a small tax. The Eagles kept their ground in the same valley for the whole spell, and the try counter is still bolted above the north gate, a brass dial that the club does not reset because, as the steward at the box will tell you, there was never a reason to.

Arrive for the pre-match hour and you will find the same ritual every season from 2003 to 2016: the dial read aloud down the terraces, a number that climbed from nine to 195 across two decades and never once stopped climbing in the years it was allowed to. In 2007 the counter passed seventy and the terrace read it wrong three times in a minute, each reading a higher number than the last; the club has never explained which was correct, and the 2007 programmes all show 70, which is the only answer that fits.

Walkers should note the Hallam end, where the long-service scarf hangs in the window of the betting shop and is replaced every August with one knitted a year’s worth of tries longer. This is not a superstition. It is a programme.

III — The Port Moresby interlude, or the fish market’s card

Once every few years the green passport goes back the long way, to the national team call-ups out of Port Moresby, first in 2006 and then in the runs that followed, and the visitor who wants the full story should take the market walk before the flight. At the Port Moresby Fish Market, stall four, the fishmonger keeps a card in the frame above the ice, and it reads exactly this, which the market has never corrected and this guide is likewise keeping:

Her name is a salt-water fish, but is rare in the salt and pepper to taste, to give great satisfaction.

The her is the fish on the line at the front of the stall, a long-bodies queenfish that the market names as a woman names a child, and the card’s promise is that the rarity is the point: it appears on the ice perhaps twice a month, it takes salt and pepper and nothing else, and the satisfaction is the reason the stall has kept the card in the frame for as long as the market has had a frame. Ask for it by the name on the card, not by the species in the back, and the fishmonger will know you have read it. Yere’s brothers say he ordered it the same way, at the same stall, on every call-up week, and that the fishmonger began keeping one on the ice for him whether or not the month had brought two.

IV — The arithmetic of the ledger

The all-time record stands at 195 tries in 292 games, which is one try for roughly every one and a half games over fourteen seasons, a rate the club’s own statisticians have called an average that should not be average. Two seasons, 2007 and 2011, sit at the top of the ledger, twenty-three and twenty-four, and the visitor will notice they are the two seasons in which the call-ups went back to Port Moresby in the winter after, as though the market and the valley kept each other honest. 195 and 292 are both numbers that were supposed to be one and turned out to be both correct, the same trick the Kanawha Board ran in its Crouse thread with the thirteenth district and the nineteenth: two entries, one person, no correction entered, and the record kept as it stands.

V — Fares, distances, and return

Sheffield to Port Moresby by the long way is about 13,800 kilometres and two changes, one of them in the Pacific, where the airline serves the queenfish and, on the good flights, remembers the card’s line about the salt. The return flight is the same distance and does not care. The dial at the north gate does not reset for departures, and the scarf at the Hallam end is a year longer every August, which is the whole of the bookkeeping. For the traveller who wants to extend the crossing into Polynesia proper before it comes back, the lagoon notes at The Lagoon Line — A Visitor’s Brochure for the Cook Islands run on the same logic of tides, fares, and one brass plaque kept exactly as received.

The guide keeps the number the steward gives. Entry No. 215, third impression, the dial standing at 195.
