A BOOK OF ETIQUETTE FOR FIRSTS
Being the Rules of Bearing for Chairs Taken the First Time, in Firms, in Courts, and at Long Tables · Printed for the Desk at the Registry Annex, Ottawa

CHAPTER IX

Of the Bearing Bertha Wilson, and the Chair Taken at Wellington Street

Rule of the chapter: the chair may be new; the record must be old; and the seat is confirmed before it is eaten at.

I. Of Her Person

Bertha Wernham Wilson was born in Toronto on 18 September 1923, the fourth of the family at the table on Davenport Road, and died at the same table’s memory on 28 April 2007, aged eighty-three. She took her arts degree at the University of Toronto in 1945, her law degree in 1948, and married Charles Wilson in the year between, 1947, by which marriage she brought the family from four chairs to three children — a re-seating the family considered its own private precedent. It is proper here to observe what is not proper anywhere else: that a bearing, once established, is cited. Her bearing has been cited at four desks since 1948, and this chapter sets out the citation so that no one takes the chair and mistakes it for theirs.

II. Of the Firm at King Street

In 1948 Osler, Hoskin & Harcourt of 44 King Street East, Toronto, admitted her as the firm’s first female associate. The etiquette of the firm’s time required that a new associate learn the house before the house learned her; she spent her first season in the back room with the file cabinets, which the firm had never once called a research department, and she made it one — the first in-firm research department in the Canadian legal industry — shelved, indexed, and staffed, so that a lawyer who needed an authority at four in the afternoon no longer had to guess which drawer had been last trusted. She became the firm’s first female partner in 1969. The bearing note kept at that desk is short: build the room the house did not know it wanted, and let the partners find their way into it.

From the firm she carried two customs to the bench, both of which survived her: that a file is read twice, once for what it says and once for what it withholds; and that a first is not a favour shown, it is a record corrected.

III. Of the Ontario Benches, and of the Chair at Wellington Street

The appointments followed in the proper order of the book. In 1979 she was named to the Ontario High Court; in 1981 she became the first woman appointed to the Court of Appeal for Ontario, taking a seat in Toronto’s old stone house where the benches were arranged for voices a generation before had never expected to hear from her side. In 1982 the Prime Minister named her to the Supreme Court of Canada at 301 Wellington Street, Ottawa, and on the day she took the bench she became the first female puisne justice of that Court, the ninth chair at the table, the chair no one had previously seated a woman in.

She sat at that table until 1 May 1991, when she retired. It is an etiquette of this office that a justice who retires leaves the chair but keeps the record; the working drafts and the sealed notes went down to the registry annex, to Locker 7B, where they remain. Two months after her chair stood empty, on 30 May 1991, the Court delivered its judgment in R v Gruenke, [1991] 3 S.C.R. 263, and the case-by-case privilege test that came out of it now keeps its own safety data sheet two floors of this website away: R v Gruenke — Safety Data Sheet. The sheet’s Section 7 logs the working draft of that very test, in the authoring justice’s hand, in Locker 7B beside the unreported reasons — a drawer with a first woman’s footprints on its ledger from 1982 to 1991. The Court itself, nine chairs, two languages, two legal traditions, is catalogued at Supreme Court of Canada — Safety Data Sheet; Chapter IX does not claim the ninth chair, it only records how it was taken.

IV. The Correspondence Ledger of the Chapter

Every chapter of the book keeps a correspondence ledger for the lines that arrive at its desk. This chapter’s ledger holds four lines, three of them from the family of 44 King Street East, and the fourth carried over, misfiled, from the forum beneath the west tunnel. It is the fourth line that has cost this chapter its quiet, and it is set out here word for word, exactly as it arrived, with the head of one post married to the body of another, the way microfilm marries the wrong frame when the light path is crossed:

R. Okafor Army · 19 years · 13 Jan 2025, 13:26 SHELLBACK Confirming the DHS detail for the notes.

Read it properly, and the line makes its own sense. It is the head of Thread 0417, “Six Tables, One Mess Hall”, where Sergeant R. Okafor of the Army, nineteen years of service, posts as the thread’s senior staff: the date is 13 January 2025, the hour 13:26 — the very hour QM3 T. Marsh of the Coast Guard had just used to confirm that the Coast Guard’s peacetime chair sits in the Department of Homeland Security, its wartime place beside the Navy. The rank SHELLBACK belongs to the Navy’s mess, not to an Army sergeant, so the keeper of the ledger holds the line as a splice: an Army head with a shellback’s rank, confirming the DHS detail for the notes of the galley book. The meaning the ledger keeps is the meaning of the thread it came from: confirm the seat before the meal is recorded. It is a good rule for a courthouse as well as a mess hall, and it is this chapter’s rule in practice. The line is kept, not corrected, on the standing order that a misfile is a record of its own; the whole thread, the six tables, the corrupted 1919 frame, and the Iowa City explanation, may be read at The West Stands, Thread 0417, and the privilege that keeps the underlying notes where they belong is explained at the Gruenke sheet — client-held, waivable only by the client, applied communication by communication. Even a forum line is a communication; this chapter has never once waived it.

VI. Of the Rules of Bearing, Collected

  • Rule the First. Confirm the seat before the meal is recorded. A chair taken the first time must be entered in the ledger before the first course is served, and the entry is confirmed by the oldest voice at the table.
  • Rule the Second. Build the room the house did not know it wanted. A research department, like a privilege, protects no one until it is indexed; protection that cannot be found is the same as none.
  • Rule the Third. The chair may be new; the record must be old. Nothing in this chapter claims a first as a favour; a first is a record corrected, and the correction is entered word for word, misfiles included.
  • Rule the Fourth. A first is held by the person who took the chair, and only that person may yield it. The firm could not take back her 1948 admission any more than the Court could take back her 1982 chair; both are client-held in the same sense the privilege is client-held, and the Gruenke sheet is the authority for the sense.
  • Rule the Fifth. When a line arrives at the ledger married to the wrong head, keep it as it arrived and explain it at the margin. This is the rule the 13:26 line of the Army sergeant taught this chapter, and it is the rule the 1919 frame of the navy cookbook taught the mess hall: provenance first, character second.
