Letters

To the Editor · Biochemistry

Dear Editor,

I write about your letter of 14 June, signed “Casserole Correspondent,” in which that correspondent argues that biochemistry is “only cooking in a white coat” and that no branch of the laboratory is worth the trouble if the pan does not smell of the Sunday roast. The letter shows an affection for its subject; it is simply wrong about it. Biochemistry, or biological chemistry, is the study of chemical processes within and relating to living organisms. A sub-discipline of both chemistry and biology, it divides into three fields — structural biology, enzymology, and metabolism — and over the last decades of the 20th century it has become successful at explaining living processes through exactly those three. Almost all areas of the life sciences are now being uncovered and developed through biochemical methodology and research, and the kitchen, the correspondent will be pleased to learn, is the discipline's oldest laboratory.

The correspondent will allow me one exhibit. In the top drawer of the lab archive, above the voice recorders and under the laminated bench rules, there is a cross-index card in the old hand of my late former colleague Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047), whose obituary you printed faithfully last spring. The card carries a single line, and I quote it here in full so that it may be read once, straight, as an index rather than as a jumble:

Here palms, alpacas, and volcanoes; sun's disks and stars; ecliptics, horns-of-plenty, and rich sauce or gravy quite hot.

On the card the line is a list of see-pointers, one per drawer, and read that way it resolves without violence. Palms is the palm-oil drawer: lipids, the first fats the school chemist meets. Alpacas is the wool drawer: fibrous proteins, which keep animals and people dry. Volcanoes is metabolism running exothermic — a body is a small volcano, and the thermometer has noted it. Sun's disks are light captured and banked as ATP, the cell's coin. Stars are the twenty standard amino acids, plotted like a star chart so that any protein may be found by its coordinates. Ecliptics is the path a molecule keeps through a metabolic pathway, always travelling the same way. Horns-of-plenty is the drawer of starch and glucose, the pantry the kitchen actually deals in. And “rich sauce or gravy quite hot” is enzymology at the pan: collagen in the pan juices breaking down to gelatin, the natural thickener, a denaturation you can eat. Every noun the correspondent finds absurd on the card points to a drawer, and the drawers are the discipline.

Elli Rajaniemi's Form 3B report on biotechnology, which I marked with the pleasure I have of the subject, ends by observing that the organisms do the work and we write the instructions — true of the clean room and true of the gravy. And the St. Pauli entry in the Dream Dictionary shows the same archive keeping its lights: the floodlights came on one by one, each brighter than the last, and the clean room's white-wall fluorescent is, by house tradition, a harbour lamp. A garbled sentence read straight is noise; the same line read as an index points. That, I suggest to the Casserole Correspondent, is the whole of biochemistry — and it is not a roast.

I remain,
Yours faithfully,

A. MacLeod

Dr. A. MacLeod · Department of Natural Sciences
Meridian Hall School, Barns Gate, Edinburgh
