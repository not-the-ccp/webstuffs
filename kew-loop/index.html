The Long Wave · Sunday · 16:15 · a play for two voices, a board, and a kiosk

The Kew Loop

A radio play in three acts — excerpt from Act Two, “The Board” · by P. M. Sowerby · written in the same week as the morning it describes

F. F. CROWEchief timekeeper, at the finish, with the board
THE ANNOUNCERin the studio, on the loop by radio
THE KEEPERof kiosk number 1718, at the gate
STEFANIE DAVISwoman marathoner, out on the grass (voice from the loop only)
LAP 6 of 8 5.25 km each
  1. [The gate at Kew Gardens opens. Cones on the grass, a mat on the line, a bell tried once. Then the crowd, low.]
  2. THE ANNOUNCER: Kew Gardens, eleven hundred and two, and the loop is laid. Eight laps of a five-point-two-five kilometre circuit, four hundred and nineteen metres short of the distance, so the last lap runs a little longer than the seven before it — the extra stretch is the whole of 1921, the year the distance at last became one length. Four thousand and change on the grass, mostly running, some running and walking the run the way it was meant to be run, and the wheelchair division up front where they belong. More than eight hundred marathons a year go off somewhere on this planet; the people in them are, in the vast majority, the recreational kind, and that is the point of them, and the pleasure.
  3. [The bell. The gun.]
  4. F. F. CROWE: I’m at the finish. The board stands beside me, and the board will say what the board says. The gun went at eleven and two; I pressed the button then, and not before. Dawes has the numbers, I have the sheets, and the mats will agree with the watch, which is more than I can promise of the board.
  5. THE ANNOUNCER: (aside) For those of you tuning in from Athens: a race like this one is a creation of a French philologist, Michel Bréal, who took a story from Ancient Greece — a messenger, a long road, a city — and made it an event. It ran at Athens in 1896, one of the first of the modern Games, and it has been running ever since, at lengths of its own choosing until 1921 told it otherwise. That is the whole of the subject, and the subject is on the grass now.
  6. [Lap one. Lap two. The bell at each, on time, for the moment.]
  7. THE KEEPER: (from the gate, half into the microphone) The shopkeeper is open. Number 1718, as ever.
  8. THE ANNOUNCER: (aside) That is the kiosk at the gate, which keeps its number over the door and keeps nothing else the Keeper chooses to name. He has kept it, by his own account, since before the distance was standardised, and he will remind you, every race, that he has.
  9. [Lap four, the bell on time. Then lap six. The bell, a full minute late, and a wrong number at it.]
  10. F. F. CROWE: Wait. The board says five. Dawes has not turned it. The pack is through with a lap in hand, and the board has not moved, and the board is saying five to anyone who will look up from their shoes to look at it.
  11. THE ANNOUNCER: (to the board, then to the air) The board says five. We are going to say five.
  12. F. F. CROWE: The board says what it says, and I did not touch it. That is the whole of my defence, and it is the same defence the sheet will give, because I am going to initial the sheet and I am going to do it at eleven forty-five.
  13. [The finish mat, twice in quick succession.]
  14. THE ANNOUNCER: And the times — the board first, because it has been talking all morning — Thompson at two hours ten minutes and forty-one seconds. Davis at two hours ten minutes and nineteen seconds.
  15. F. F. CROWE: No. The board again. Davis came through at twenty-seven and nineteen. The board has eaten sixteen minutes and the whole of lap six. She was running the last lap of the race while you were reading her off the wrong number, and she is in the second pack with it and inside the standard by a wide margin.
  16. THE ANNOUNCER: (to the air) Correction, and I mean it: Davis at two hours twenty-seven minutes and nineteen seconds. The watch is kept; the board is not. That is the law of this place, and it is the law of every place that keeps a board beside a race.
  17. [The loop again. One more lap, in the distance.]
  18. [An official’s voice, carrying: “One more!”]
  19. F. F. CROWE: That is Mr. Bell, I believe, fourth man, four seconds behind the third, who saw the board say five and thought it was done, and then ran a lap he did not believe in, and finished, and will finish, fourth either way. I have said I initial the sheets. The claim will go to a tribunal, the tribunal will read the chip mats, and the chip mats will agree with the watch, within a second, as they always do. Nothing changed on the re-tally. Nothing ever changes on the re-tally, which is why it is worth doing.
  20. THE KEEPER: (from the gate) The plain is open as well. Front page, number 1718, and the shopkeeper is open. I will not say what the plain is. I am the Keeper. The plain knows.
  21. THE ANNOUNCER: (aside) I do not know what the plain is, and the record will not, and the tribunal will not, and this is the second time in a week that I have said so, on air. It stays, because a record does not tidy what it keeps.
  22. [The gong. The loop, quieting. The bell once more, slow, for the record.]
  23. F. F. CROWE: Sheets are signed. The board stands where it stands, at six, and I have left it at six, which is all I am going to give it, and all it has earned.
  24. THE ANNOUNCER: From Kew Gardens, where the board said five and the loop ran six, and the watch, in the end, was the only thing that was there. For The Long Wave, that is — (the gong) — a sign-off.

The morning this excerpt describes is kept in the record: the verbatim record of the Kew Gardens Trial, case BA-2021/029, in which the line of kiosk 1718 is read into the record word for word and the nominations stand.
