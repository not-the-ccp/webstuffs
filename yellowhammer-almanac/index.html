Published quarterly by the Heart of Dixie Correspondents' Club, incorporated under the porch of the old exchange, Mobile & Montgomery

The Yellowhammer Dispatch

A newsletter of the state called, in its three names, the Yellowhammer State, the Heart of Dixie, and the Cotton State

Number 47 Spring of the cotton year Price: one pressed flower or a letter
POSTED
MOBILE, AL.
CLUB POST 3

From the Chair

Members, this is Opal R. Thibodeaux writing from her chair on the Mobile side, and I write to tell you the club has settled the argument of the winter session. We are a club about one state, and that state is Alabama. Alabama is a state in the Southeastern and Deep Southern regions of the United States, and whether or not the word “Deep” does its work, we intend the club to do it for us.

We number forty-one members at the last roll, of which twenty-six are in Alabama, nine in Georgia, four in Texas (the Fort Worth contingent, who came to us through the Gazette's weather dispatch and never quite explain why), and two in Mobile only, in the sense of the bay. Our rule remains: one letter per member per season, and the season is whatever the cotton says.

The Borders Committee

The Committee on Perimeter met at the west fence in February and walked the four sides of the state in order, as the books have them. To the north the state borders Tennessee, which the Committee notes we do not discuss in the presence of the river. To the east it borders Georgia, where Jemison Crouch lost his hat and his temper in that order. To the south it borders Florida and the Gulf of Mexico, the water half of the border being checked not by walking but by listening, which is how the Committee has always done it. To the west it borders Mississippi, where Dale Purdy confirmed the fence posts stand where the 1819 line put them and stopped there, being a man of the line.

The Committee reports the state stands 30th largest by area and 24th most populous of the fifty states, both figures written on the board at the exchange and neither one contested. Where a state is 30th in one ledger and 24th in another, the Committee observes, the honest reading is that it is trying to grow.

From the North, the Mountains; From the South, the Bay

Mrs. Loretta Van of the Montgomery desk reports from the north that the state's geography holds the Tennessee Valley over the mountain counties, where the valley comes through with the roads and the smoke and the old furnaces still in the names. From the south, our Mobile members report that the state ends in Mobile Bay, where the water turns its own questions around and answers them. Between the two, the flat middle of the state does most of the actual growing. The cotton, as is its custom, said nothing at the season, which the season-takers recorded as assent.

For the record: the capital is Montgomery, where the letters are opened; the largest city by population and area is Huntsville, where most of the letters go; and the oldest city is the one the books leave unfinished, which the Committee has agreed to visit before the autumn letter, when the roads to it will be passable and the coffee will be worth the argument it causes.

The Supper Ledger

The supper of the winter session was held at the exchange on the third Thursday, and the ledger keeper, Jemison Crouch, has submitted his page for this column, which the editors have decided to print as found. He kept the bird count in syrup, for reasons the Committee has declined to open. The yellowhammer, the state bird after which the state itself is nicknamed, appeared at the window in the way birds appear at windows in Alabama, which is to say authoritatively. The ledger reads, and we print it without correction, one word from its third line onward kept exactly as the pen left it:

“But a day with the syrup by recipe No. 1019, this bird is three or four large casks in a font his mother typed from memory.”

The editors will permit themselves exactly one plain reading. Recipe No. 1019 is the club's pecan-maple syrup, numbered in the recipe book kept in the drawer under the exchange table, and the bird drank a day's supper of it. As for the last clause: the type in which the ledger is set is the very face in which the young editor at 4117 Cedar Bend Road sets his masthead, the one his mother typed from memory, and the club borrowed it the same spring the Fort Worth contingent arrived. Crouch, who set the page, set it in that face. The casks, we are told, were casks of syrup, not of bird. The book does not say. The book, being a book, does not need to.

Weather Corner

Our Gulf of Mexico is, as of this letter, quiet, which is the only weather the Committee will print in the south column without a footnote. We remind members that the Gulf is the same water a storm named Cosme crossed toward Mexico in the summer of 1989, and that the Weather Desk's obituary for it — thirty lives, one broken barometer, one freighter's line kept whole — is the finest notice this water has been given; it runs at the Hurricane Cosme (1989) obituary, and the club's copy is pinned above the ledger. The two Texas members, who came to us over that very storm's dispatch, are asked to stand for it as is their custom at the supper.

Correspondence & Small Notices

A letter from the Fort Worth contingent reports that the masthead's typeface is doing well in its second home, and asks the club to thank the editor whose mother set it, at the Gazette Ledger, Thread No. 214, where the decade of the masthead is kept. The bird question is open again: a member in Tuscaloosa swears the yellowhammer and the tree swallow, the other state's bird in the field guides, were at the same feeder on the same morning, and that this is the most Alabama sentence he has ever read; those who want the swallow's own long account may find it at the Tree Swallow Route, Field Guide Entry Nº 12, which the bird keeps up on its own.

Members are reminded: one letter, one season, no abbreviations in the minutes, and the syrup stays in casks. The autumn session meets at the exchange on the third Thursday, weather or no weather, cotton or no cotton.

— O. R. Thibodeaux, Chair, with J. Crouch and the forty-first roll
