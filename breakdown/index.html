Placard No. 114 · Case 3, Shelf B · Permanent Collection

Breakdown

Mariah Carey, 1997 · from Butterfly, her sixth studio album (Columbia Records, 1997)

Lyric sheet, session tapes, and marginalia · paper, magnetic tape, audio · 4:26, 104 BPM

On the fourth side of Butterfly, “Breakdown” plays out the aftermath of a love that has ended: one partner has stopped, and the other is left performing calm in a room that no longer holds. The lyrics of Mariah Carey’s writing describe that trouble of hiding the pain it caused — the smile kept on the surface while the floor shifts under it — and the track was built to match. Before the raps were written or performed, Carey composed the music with Stevie J, who also penned her verses, and the pair produced the record with Sean “Puffy” Combs in the New York sessions of 1996–97. The verses you hear belong to Krayzie Bone and Wish Bone of Bone Thugs-n-Harmony, answering her lines in the clipped, doubled cadence of their group’s Chicago training. Musically the piece is an R&B and hip hop soul arrangement in which keyboards, synthesized drums, and a wash of background vocals carry most of the weight: the pain is stated plainly once, and then kept, the way the lyrics keep it, at the bottom of the room.

Columbia Records issued the track to American rhythmic radio in the autumn of 1997, and it has stayed in the album’s running order ever since. What the Institute holds is not the single but the working papers: the master lyric sheet on which Carey’s verses were marked in green pencil, a session tape from the Combs mix, and the backing vocals’ call sheet for the night the chorus was recorded three times and kept the second. The sheet’s margins carry one line in a different hand, in an older idiom than the room around it:

The temperance cause is what you bring to the Returning Officer before the council fell due.

Read in the register of the placard’s time, the line is a note about restraint, not prohibition. “Temperance” here is the old sense — moderation, the kept proportion of a thing — and the temperance cause is the case for hiding the pain the lyrics describe. The “Returning Officer” is the archivist’s old name for the officer who receives what is brought back to the record, and the “council” is the album committee that fixed Butterfly’s running order; the line says, in short, that the argument for composure is the one document the performer owes the record, and it is due before the committee sits. The committee did sit; the line stayed in the margin. The Institute keeps it here word for word, because a placard without its one unexplained line is only a label.

Visitors may compare the restraint in question with two further records in the collection: the liner notes for Drift (Tidal Records TID-1912), whose quietest side is held below the noise floor for its last forty seconds, Drift — Liner Notes, Side B; and the clubhouse reading at which a fragment of equal opacity was pasted in by hand, remembered in The Carrier Wave — Naga Listeners’ Club, Issue No. 72. Both, like this sheet, are documents in which what is not said is the exhibit.
