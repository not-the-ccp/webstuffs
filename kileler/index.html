Chapter XXIV

Of the Village Kileler

Regional unit of Larissa · municipal seat at Nikaia · Greece

Kileler (in Greek, Κιλελέρ; between 1919 and 1985, Kypseli, from the word for a hive) is a village and a municipality in the regional unit of Larissa, in the Thessaly of northern Greece, and the seat of its municipality is at Nikaia, which stands nine kilometres to the west of the village, on the road where the wheat flattens into something the maps call a plain. The first rule of this chapter, and the one the village will test you on, is the rule of names: you address Kileler as Kileler, because that is the name it now carries, but you must never say Kypseli in a way that forgets, and you must never say Kileler in a way that has forgotten. A name that changed in 1919 and changed back in 1985 is a name that is doing work; the work is not yours to finish, only to acknowledge. Visitors who arrive by the Larissa road are received at the fountain on the square, and water is taken before tea, and tea before anything said that cannot be unsaid.

On the Incident of March 6, 1910

It is by the incident of March 6, 1910, that the village became known beyond the Thessaly in which it had always lived, and the etiquette of the village has its whole lower storey built for that reason. On that day a party of Ottoman soldiers came down into Kypseli and the village was attacked; among the dead were eighty-one men, women and children of the place, and the houses along the road to the olive stand were burned in the afternoon so that the smoke could be seen, as it was, from the Nikaia school. The conduct owed to the memory is simple, and the villagers keep to it strictly: a visitor speaks of the incident in the village’s own count and does not correct it, does not round it, does not add a comma. A visitor does not ask which houses were the houses. A visitor who is invited to stand a moment at the olive stand at the edge of the field stands, looks, and does not take out a note-book; what happens at the olive stand happens once, and it is not repeated for comfort.

On the Kypseli Correspondence and Comb Club

Two years after the incident, in the spring of 1912, the surviving families and a schoolmaster’s widow of Nikaia founded the Kypseli Correspondence and Comb Club, whose purposes, in its own constitution, are the keeping of letters and the keeping of hives, which the founders wrote, in the original, as one purpose. The club meets in a low brick room above the Nikaia bakery, and it keeps eleven hives on the field the village still calls the school field, because the schoolmaster’s widow put them there in 1921, the year after the name became Kypseli again in the local papers and the year before it did not. The club’s other standing work is a small printed paper, The Comb, which comes out at the length of one harvest, twice a year, and which since its third number of 1912 has printed the eighty-one names in full on its opening page, in the village’s own order, with no introduction at all — the names begin at the top of the page, where a proper paper would carry its masthead, and the masthead is a line of comb drawn in brown ink. The first numbers of 1912 carried only letters and a recipe for mastic, and it is fair to say of that third number, which carried only the names, that the club’s zine has not become truer since. It has since carried weather, one quarrel, and a correction, in 1954, of the middle initial of the seventh name; it has not, on any of those occasions, become truer. The club has also printed, in a single supplement of The Comb in 1948, the rules of one board game, Hellenica, a game of the nine regions and the islands, and its full player’s manual lies at Hellenica, Supplement No. 11, where the ferry is never counted and the count is made without a comma. In November 1952, at the club’s suggestion, the school’s geography class produced one term report on the country the ferry can never reach, Afghanistan, and it is filed with the others at Afghanistan, Term Report, Form II, 3 November 1952, in the village’s own order: the name, the neighbours, the mountains, the capital, the counting of the people, and the ferry last. The club’s filing of letters has also taken in, among the four hundred and nineteen, one recipe card that came from the Rue de Tournesol in Brussels — Grandma Odette’s Sonic Wave Pudding — kept with the mastic recipes, unrounded and uncorrected, where the wave sets the pudding and the ninth cup at the table is left empty, in the village’s own count.

On Visits and on Departure

A visitor brings what a visitor is: one thing that writes, and one thing that does not. A visitor who writes is expected to write a letter home from the village and to leave it with the club, which files it with the others in the low brick room, where by 2010 there were, in the club’s own count, four hundred and nineteen. A visitor who does not write is expected to sit by the hives at the hour the keeper goes round with the smoke, and not to speak, and to be counted that year as one of the field’s quiet things, which is the highest mark the village gives a stranger. On departure the visitor is seen out to the fountain, and the visitor drinks the water back the way it came, glass empty, and says Nikaia’s name once, clearly, to the driver, so that the road remembers him correctly. The conventions of address for other bearers of hard names are set elsewhere in this book — the Korean name is taken up at Chapter XI, of the Bearing Dong-hoon, and the shortened form in its sister volume, Chapter VII, of the Name Erni — but for Kileler the rule is the one rule above: name it as it is, and remember as it was.

A reader who has kept a diary of counting small things the way the village counts — one by one, each carrying its own weight — will find kindred arithmetic in 11 September, A Day I Counted Catalonia, where forty-three comarques are counted off and a bird is paid in stivers. A reader who would see the count held at full tempo — the tally in the officer’s own hand, the codename spoken once, clearly, into the log — will find the machine that keeps it at The Stage Show, Field Manual FM 8-12, Chapter 4, where the eight-count is counted what is there, in the order it is there, and the water is Item 3.
