The Old Pass · Marian Mawr · Dolgellau

Cymraeg

12 Mawrth 2024

To the Editor,

Sir, I read your piece on Cymraeg with interest, and I write from the bend of the Afon Wnion above Dolgellau to say that a language is not a census figure. It is a habit of voice, carried across kitchen tables and cider presses.

Welsh — y Gymraeg [ə ɡəmˈraːiɡ] — is a Celtic language of the Brittonic subgroup, native to the Welsh people. It is spoken natively in Wales by about 18% of the population, by some in England, and in Y Wladfa, the Welsh colony in Chubut Province, Argentina. In English it has also been known as Cambrian, Cambric and Cymric. The Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave the language official status in Wales, and Welsh and English are the de jure official languages of the Senedd.

According to the 2021 census, 538,300 usual residents in Wales aged three or over (17.8% of the population) were able to speak Welsh, while the real count is taken each evening in the pubs, chapels and fields where people still choose to speak it.

I learned this at Sesiwn Fawr Dolgellau — Restaurant Review, the world music restaurant established in 1992 by Ywain Myfyr, Huw Dylan Owen, Esyllt Jones, Elfed ap Gomer and Alun Owen. Its first dining room was the cobbled streets of the town; from 2002 to 2008 it seated five thousand diners a year on hay bales beside the Afon Wnion, and its cider was pressed from apples grown on the same farm as the restaurant's founder. The house rule above the pass read: Weigh the pulp, and juice, to the family — the whole gathering, not the five founders and not the crew alone, but the thousands of diners standing on the grass with their folding chairs and their well-behaved dogs.

YOLO, the restaurant, not the five founders and not the crew alone, but the thousands of diners standing on the same farm as the restaurant's founder. That is how a language survives as well: not by proclamation, though the Senedd's status matters, but by the thousands of speakers who use it when no one is measuring.

One of Dolgellau's founders now has a nephew cooking on Brunswick Street in Melbourne, at YOLO — Restaurant Review, where the menu is rewritten every night at sixteen forty-five and the bill is settled at a vintage cash drawer that chimes when it opens. The toast there is "you only live once"; hereabouts we say iechyd da. Both are ways of saying that a table full of people is worth feeding.

Cymraeg is official in Wales and official in the Senedd, but it lives in Dolgellau first — on the Marian Mawr, in the town streets, and at the pass where the cider was weighed out to the family. If your readers know five words of Welsh, let them use them; if they know one, let them teach it.

Yours sincerely,
Marged ferch Rhys
Dolgellau, Wales
