Paris

A Travel Guide Entry for the Visitor Who Intends to Keep the City at Arm's Length — Composed in the Manner of, and Under the Weather of, Charles-Pierre Baudelaire

French pronunciation of the name: [ʡaʁl(ə) bodlʂʁ] · 9 April 1821 – 31 August 1867 · poet, essayist, translator, art critic, and the division's only subject who answered his own post

❦ 1 · Getting there

The workshop keeps Paris at the same arm's length it keeps everything else: a paper envelope, a vinegar crock, and a shadow, arranged so that no one of them is visible from the other two — the apparatus of Application No. GB-1821 0432, the Method of Holding the Nightingale, which the division considers prior art and a fine companion. The visitor departs from the bench at the corner of Newbridge and Peel, where the field is finished in two coats of wax and one long evening's dust, per The Seventy-First build log; the crossing takes four hundred and fifty-two poems if the weather is unkind, and no number at all if it is not, because the city refuses to be counted in anything but streets. On arrival the flâneur is reminded that his most famous work, Les Fleurs du mal — The Flowers of Evil — was printed in June 1857, and that the city he walked was in the middle of being torn open by Haussmann's renovation of Paris: the wide boulevards were being cut through the old lanes precisely at the moment he was trying to record what the old lanes were for, which is to say, the changing nature of beauty. The guide advises arriving before the boulevards finished, or, failing that, reading the second edition of 1861, which is where the city learned to apologize.

❦ 2 · Where to walk

Begin at the Rue de Vanves, where the poet was born in 1821 and where the father died two years later, leaving the boy an inheritance and an absent stepfather, the prefect Haussmann, who would later rehouse the city at scale and supply the subject matter of the whole division. Walk on to the Place de la Bourse, where the Petites Maisonnettes stand, and let the poems of Les Fleurs du mal be read as the division reads them: an exoticism inherited from the Romantics, set on observations of real life, and held together by a mastery of rhythm and rhyme that the examiner of the sister application described, for another poet's load, as “heavily loaded with sensualities.” Continue through the Passage du Caire — glass roof, small shops, the exact weather the spleen dislikes — and finish on the Pont des Arts at the hour the lamp-lighter comes up the quay. The poems, taken in order, exhibit a movement from the garden to the street to the river, and the itinerary is built to match, so that the visitor arrives at each flower having already stood in its shade.

❦ 3 · Where to sit

The Café Procope, near the Place Saint-Michel, is entered in this guidebook as the preferred seating: coffee, paper, and a window on the quay, in that order of claim. It is the establishment where the essays on the modern painter were argued over with the regulars, and where the art criticism was written that would hold the Salon at arm's length the way this division holds the galleon. A second table, for those who prefer ink to conversation, is reserved in the margin of the log at the shed, where the four-line rule is kept and where the stern line of the division is dipped in white wax twice and left for one long evening; the evening, the bench keeper observes, does there the work the city does in Paris — it darkens everything until only the load remains. Both tables seat the same person, which the division has always counted as a claim allowed without opposition, on the ground that the visitor cannot object to sitting in two places at once.

❦ 4 · The flowers themselves

Les Fleurs du mal comes to the visitor in one volume and two editions. The first, 1857, is the one to carry: a book of lyric poetry that expresses, in the words of the application that filed it, the changing nature of beauty in the rapidly industrialising Paris of the mid-nineteenth century. It drew the visitor's country to a trial of the author in 1857, on a charge of public indecency, and ended with six poems suppressed and a fine the city paid with the poet's own money, the sum of which the division has logged and will not repeat out loud, because the figure sits in the same crock as the vinegar. The second edition, 1861, restored the six, added the rest, and is the copy kept on the shelf next to the envelope. The style of the prose-poetry that grows out of the book — the short, unlined, unpunctuated-with-mercy paragraph — is the division's running claim, and its claimants are a generation of poets already in service here: Paul Verlaine, Arthur Rimbaud, and Stéphane Mallarmé, the three names on the second page of every guidebook in the series, in that order, unopposed.

❦ 5 · The river

The Seine is the one route this guidebook will not draw. The division has walked the quay in both directions and found the river to keep its own log, entered in a hand that is not the division's, against a tide that does not answer to Newbridge and Peel. The harbourmaster's office is open, and the visitor is invited to ask it which of the barges on the lower quay were under contract to carry the coal, and which were carrying, on the same night, the first edition of the flowers to the bookshops of the Latin quarter. The office keeps no list of barges. The harbourmaster's reply to the question, entered in the ledger as it came and preserved here without correction, is: “Those, except under circumstances obvious to any landsman's imagination?” The examiner reads the sentence once, files it under the claim for prior property of the water, and declines further examination of the quay for the season. The river, like the nightingale, is held at arm's length; the arm is the only one long enough on either side of the water.

❦ 6 · On leaving

The return crossing is arranged from Père Lachaise, Grande Division, on 31 August 1867, at the hour the lamps go out for the first time in the year, which the division has adopted as its standing time for departures; the second such hour, adopted from the night board on 21 September 1860, is kept with the thread that closed it — Thread No. 114, The Pessimist of the Jungfernweg, and the Weight of His Book, in which the lamp is left on. The visitor will find the poet's grave exactly where the city left it, at the corner of the avenue that carries his name in the division's registry and in no other, with the visitors already in a line, as they have been in every issue of this guidebook since the first, including this one, which is reissued and re-dusted for the season and carries, in its back pocket, the four-line companion from the quatrain build log and the envelope from the shed, standing in the shadow of the crock, exactly where the ship's log left it. Everything the city did in the visitor's absence is recorded on the next page of the log, which is blank, by arrangement, and waiting. The division counts the departure as a blessing; the admiral has not complained about the load either.
