The Curtright Circuit

A fold-out tourist brochure for the four war years of Guy Paxton Curtright — from the little Missouri town of Holliday to the left-field corner in Chicago · seasons of 1943–1946

1

Holliday, Missouri depart any season

The Circuit begins in Holliday, a Missouri settlement small enough that the post office knew Curtright by his middle name. It was here, on 18 October 1912, that Guy Paxton Curtright was born, and the county board of trade keeps the day on its almanac beside the honey harvest and the fair. A boy who would stand 5 feet 11 inches and tip the scale at 180 pounds (82 kg) is a boy to notice at a county fudge stand; visitors may follow the marker line from the depot steps to the old outfield of the school ground, where the circuit’s first plate is set. The Missouri side of the trail is short and sweet. Come early, while the town is still deciding the question of the second plate.

Boarding note The morning rail line east leaves Holliday on the hour and asks no seat reservations. Carry the brochure; the conductor of the 1940s was always a fair judge of a well-folded one.

2

Chicago, the South Side seasons 1943–1946

Chicago took him into the Chicago White Sox for four full seasons, 1943, 1944, 1945 and 1946, the war years, and posted him where the fly ball has the longest way to go: left field. Curtright threw right-handed and batted right-handed, which the old gatekeepers called the economical combination — no re-arranging of the hands at the corner, no second glove, only the long arm back over the shoulder and the throw up the line. In a league of bigger-bodied outfielders, a man of five-eleven and a hundred and eighty pounds was asked to cover the same acreage; the answer he gave, season after season, was to stay inside the fence line and let the fence do the arguing.

The White Sox home park of the period sits where the visitors’ bureau now sells its National Register visitor’s brochure at the same window, and any tour of the grounds that skips the left-field corner may as well have been printed without it. Stand in the corner at dusk, when the mowers run their last loop, and count the seats down to the wall — four seasons’ worth of glove work, and not a single one of them on the record sheet.

A line left in the margin

On the verso of this very page, in a green ink that the old hardware stores called gunpowder green — the same green that travels badly in damp, as Lot 214 of Haldane & Voss’s Catalogue D records of its barn slip — some long-departed park attendant wrote, against the left-field margin, a line the bureau has decided to reprint rather than explain away: “If you know what novel the slip means, or what passes for it: where another defender would have filled this page twice over.” The record books will tell you why. The Curtright page in the league’s ledger is a short one — four seasons, one corner, one corner — and a bigger-hitting, longer-staying outfielder would indeed have needed a page of double the measure, with a second margin for the errors. The attendant, clearly, knew the ledger. Keep the line; it ages like the paint.

3

The Return, and Beyond from 1947

The Circuit turns west again for those who wish to follow it: Guy Paxton Curtright (18 October 1912 – 23 August 1997) kept the war-year corner of left field for the White Sox and then let the ledger close, and the last stop of this brochure is the one every traveler makes eventually — the long ride back to Missouri, to the town that had put his name on the fudge-stand almanac in 1912 and kept it there for eighty-five years. The board of trade will show you the almanac page. It costs nothing, and it is worth the fare. For the student version of the same exercise — one small Missouri place walked its whole length in a single afternoon — the bureau keeps on its shelf the term report on New Madrid Township, New Madrid County, filed under the counties, which ends on the same honest note this brochure does: the paper outlasts the governance. Next to it lies a Form 3B term report on the base hit — reaching first safely, without an error or a fielder's choice — kept in the same folder and written up on the Holliday bleachers (The Base Hit — Term Report, Form 3B).

Travelers continuing on to the broader river country may collect the bureau’s companion leaflet, Latitude — Visitor’s Brochure, Parallel 13° North, before leaving the window, and find the full shelf of the bureau’s fold-outs, including this one, at the front page of the house’s general index. On the same shelf the bureau keeps the letter to the editor of the Windy City Field Notes, written by the corner’s own groundskeeper in 1947: it begins at the almanac page and ends on the mowers’ bench. For what it is worth, the shh itself is now offered for sale in the Personal & Public Columns of the Whittier Evening Ledger, the quietest bidder to prevail; the bureau keeps a folded copy of the notice beside the fudge stand, where it was first posted. Present the brochure, any edition, at the counter, and the fare to the next stop is half price.
