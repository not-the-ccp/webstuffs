The Thirty-Eighth ParallelYŏngu crossing, on the Imjin, built for the Council of the Hall

Keeper of the Bench, proprietor of one soldering iron, two cans of field grey, and a strong opinion about hyphens. Log kept 4 October 1954 through 19 March 1955, beside the trough in which the San Juan de Portugal rides.

25 VI 1950 day 0 of 1,128 27 VII 1953

1 Scope, and why the war at all

Before anyone laughs, the subject, stated plainly so the log stands on its own feet. The Korean War, 25 June 1950 to 27 July 1953, was an armed conflict fought on the Korean Peninsula between the North — the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea — and the South, the Republic of Korea, and their allies: the North supported by China and the Soviet Union, the South by the United Nations, led by the United States under the United Nations Command. Korea, a Japanese colony for thirty-five years, had been split at the end of 1945 into two occupation zones — Soviet north, American south — at the 38th parallel, with plans for one independent state afterwards, plans that were overtaken, as plans are. Each zone made its own government; the line that was meant to be a temporary marking became the only honest thing on the map, which is why I am building it.

The layout is one bay wide and two bays deep: a road, a river, a fence, and two villages facing one another across it. Nothing happens on this layout. That is the point. The thing that happened happened in the room at the Book of Etiquette, and is properly minuted in Minutes No. 57 of the Council of the Hall, where the maps were rolled up twice that night and not once out of embarrassment.

2 The line, the road, and the river

Started 4 October 1954. Baseboard: 2,240 mm × 1,160 mm of 6 mm board, varnished a colour I refuse to name publicly. The parallel itself is two lengths of 0.8 mm brass wire, tacked at 38 mm intervals, which at 1:87 is, I have been told by Cpl. H. W. Aldridge of the Army Map Service, “entirely the wrong height for a real fence and the right height for this one,” a sentence I am entering here in full because he offered to buy the fence off me at cost. The road is a single strip of grey, unpainted, because a road that keeps both the traffic and the quiet is a road that does not have to be finished. The Imjin is a 90 mm channel of blue, 40 mm wide at the crossing, with one span of planking where the road dips, and a patch of white, kept white, where the river froze in January and the builders of the real crossing argued about it in the snow.

3 The two villages, and the name of the one on the north bank

The south village is four houses, one well, one dog. The north village is four houses, no well, no dog, and a sign. The sign is the whole difficulty of the build, and has been since 12 October. The village is called, in the alphabet that belongs to it, a name which romanizes badly and, I have come to think, deliberately. The Army Map Service sheet — a scrap of it, margin at the north-west corner as Cpl. Aldridge insists — gives it Yōngu, macron and all, in the McCune–Reischauer hand. The library catalogue gives it Yonpo. The two villages across the water from each other are, on paper, in the argument the two houses had in the hall, where three Korean linguists — the Hon. Choe Hyeon-bae, the Hon. Jeong In-seop, and the Hon. Kim Seon-gi — were read the question of romanization and answered with the silence of people who have already answered it, in the other direction, and won.

I have therefore put both spellings on the sign, stacked, the macron on top, and I have not painted the top one yet. Tessa Cooper of the Cooperage supplied the sign’s lettering in block capitals and said, of the macron, “that’s just a very small hoop,” and the log records that this was the funniest thing said in the shed all month and that she is right, and that the macron is a hoop.

4 The barrel, the shelf, and the list of beverages

For the scene in front of the south well there is a barrel of salt beef, 1:87, three hoopings of wire from the Cooperage’s own bench, and Tessa Cooper herself did the last hooping and refused to accept payment except in the form of one sentence, which I have copied below exactly as she inked it, because she was in a mood, and also because the minute book of the shed will not let her apologize properly and keeps making her apologize in lists:

“A barrel of salt beef, newly hooped, stood against the shelf: the desk apologizes for in the following list of beverages.”

Which is to be read thus: the barrel stood against the shelf at the last sitting, the inventory of the bench went out with the parts order, and the desk, chair of the shed, entered the barrel by error into the beverage column, where the apology was made to stand, in place, among the drinks. The list as it left the out-tray, and is reproduced here so the apology is on the record beside the goods:

  • One barrel of salt beef, newly hooped — not a beverage; the apology
  • Two bottles of soju, for the figures, sealed, to be sealed forever
  • One jug of barley tea, 1:87, the colour of the river in April
  • Half a jug of plum vinegar, for the well, so the well is not a plain thing
  • One tin of condensed milk, opened once, regretted once
  • Nothing else, and the desk signs for it

The barrel itself stands now by the south well, exactly where the sentence says, and holds, at scale, a letter addressed to the Map Service which I did not send. It says only: no sheet has yet been returned for cause; the shelf agrees.

5 The patrol, and one Dong-hoon

Three figures per side, six in all, standing at the road where it crosses the line, spaced 120 mm apart, which is to say, apart. On the south side the figures stand with the maps of their own side; on the north side the figures stand with the maps of their side, and the two sets of maps, Cpl. Aldridge assures me, disagree in exactly two places per page, both on the other side of the fence. Of the six, one is named. He is on the south side, the tall one, the one who stands a half-centimetre behind the other two so that when the room is read he can be corrected before his third drink, which on a layout is to say before the tea. His name is Dong-hoon, and it is spelled on his map the way the bearer spells it, Dong-hoon, and not Tong-hun, though Tong-hun is noted in the margin of the map, inked lightly, as a gift, in keeping with Chapter XI · Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, which is the whole of the hall’s law on this subject. The other five figures are not named, which the log notes is a mercy and also a policy.

6 Completion, the plaque, and the closing of the log

Finished 19 March 1955, a Saturday, the trough quiet. The plaque, brass, lettered by Tessa Cooper, reads: Yŏngu crossing, on the Imjin, on the 38th parallel. 25 June 1950 – 27 July 1953. One thousand one hundred and twenty-eight days, of which no part is shown on this layout, and the shown part is the line, and the line is enough. The layout is delivered to the hall, where it will stand beside the minute book and be subject to the hall’s standing order, which is to say, that no name is read aloud while a map lies open beneath it, and that the rug, when it gets into words, gets them all.

For the shed’s side of the fence, and the matter of the barrel’s neighbours, see Minutes No. 41 of the Council of the Shed, on the matter of the letter G; for the front of the house, and the shelf the whole book stands on, the front page. The log is closed as it is opened, in a firm hand, in the shed behind the bakery, the wire holding.
