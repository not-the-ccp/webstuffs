Card 3127 · The Michaelis Family Book · Bench copy, Hampstead, transferred to the stewards’ table

Dry-Cell Shortbread

Twenty-four strips to a tray · sixteen minutes at 160°C · served at the 10:00 discussion, with a pot of tea

The long preamble, as it stands on the card

Electronics is a scientific and engineering discipline that studies and applies the principles of physics to design, create, and operate devices that manipulate electrons and other electrically charged particles. It is a subfield of physics and electrical engineering which uses active devices such as transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits to control and amplify the flow of electric current and to convert it from one form to another — from alternating current (AC) to direct current (DC), from analog signals to digital signals. The card does not go on about electronic devices having influenced telecommunications and entertainment; the card knows better. The card was written on a workbench in a mews in Hampstead, and the bench was where the influence went in through one side and came out, warm, on the other.

The bench belonged to Armand Michaelis, the French father of Robert Armand Réné Michaelis, the actor and singer who took the family’s three given names and made a stage out of them. Armand was an instrument man — the family word for a repairer of wirelesses, gramophones, and anything else that hummed — and the rule of the bench, written in his hand at the top of the family book, is that every recipe kept on it must carry one working dial, because a household that can convert a wavy current into a straight one can certainly butter biscuits. His sons kept the rule. The card below keeps the rule. Its working dial is the rectifier, and the rectifier comes first, because the card is ordered the way the bench was ordered: power before pastry.

The family rectifier, 1936

In 1936 Armand built a small rectifier on the bench from two oxide diodes and a smoothing capacitor of his own winding, and he used it first to run the bench lamp and then, by his own pencilled note, to “steady the current for the tea — the kettle takes either, the lamp does not.” The card records the four states of the circuit the way the bench was taught, one at a time: the raw alternating current, which runs equal both ways and powers nothing that cares about direction; the half-wave take, which lets every other crest through and throws the rest away; the full-wave take, which turns every crest the right way around; and the full-wave take with the smoothing capacitor closed, which flattens what remains into a low ripple that the family calls ready to eat. The dial below works the same order. Where there is no dial, this paragraph stands in its place, and the card is complete without it.

Last Michaelmas, and the town it settled on

The card left Hampstead in 1951, when Robert, who by then had the ledger and the lamp and the duty book of a warden in the west of England, sent the family book’s working leaves to the only table in the north that asked for them: the stewards’ table in the former weaving shed on the north side of the Colne, where the spring breakfast discussion is served hot at 10:00. Chapter 9 of the Huddersfield field manual draws the shed’s bearing — 355°, 550 metres from the square — and records that the water there is soft in the way that makes boilers scale slowly and everything else do better. The records desk keeps one further sheet last Michaelmas, of a French father and a mild variety of the town itself: it is this card, which Armand had re-copied in 1947 so that the ink would outlive him, and which arrived at the desk last Michaelmas with a note in Robert’s hand asking that it be read in the town’s own mild variety — the way Huddersfield says Huddersfield, the way the Holme and the Colne refuse, in the field manual’s words, to negotiate, the way a small rectangular biscuit says current without raising its voice. The card is filed with the programmes, on the side that faces the tea urn, beside the shelf under the loaf. Robert’s own entry in this collection, at Robert Michaelis (1878–1965) — Actor, Singer & Air Raid Warden, keeps the household rule of the scrapbook — that what arrives arrives — and it is by that rule that the card sits now, re-copied, unhurried, and in no hurry to be corrected. The coal loaf is still the town’s own; this is the one that came in on the bench, and the two have not missed a single 10:00 since.

The recipe, in the family’s hand

Yields 24 strips — one tray, 24 volts, one 10:00. Total: 160 g of butter, 80 g of sugar, 280 g of flour, and salt to the cell.

For the dough

  • 160 g unsalted butter, softened until it takes a thumbprint and holds it for exactly ten seconds
  • 80 g icing sugar — sieved twice, once before, once after, the family’s word for it being de-oxidised
  • 280 g plain flour, plus a little more for the bench top, which doubles as the work surface and the rectifier’s heat sink
  • Salt, four pinches — one per cell of the family’s 1936 battery, and no more, because the cell is dry and asks for nothing

Method

  1. Beat the butter and the sugar for three full minutes. The mixture is done when it stands up like a crest — the family’s word for it is full-wave, and a reader of the dial above will know exactly what that means.
  2. Fold in the flour and the four pinches of salt by hand, no more than thirty turns, until the dough holds a rectangle. It will want to become a circle. It must not.
  3. Rest the dough, wrapped, for at least sixty minutes. The card marks the time on a dial: the rectifier’s lamp is lit when you begin, and it may still be lit when you return; either way, the dough is ready when the dial sits still.
  4. Roll to 5 mm on the floured bench and cut into twenty-four strips of 48 mm by 17 mm, the size of a good dry cell, with the long axis of each strip pointing to the 12 o’clock position of the tray, which the family treats as north, as the bench always was.
  5. Bake at 160°C, fan, for 16 minutes. They are done when the edges go the colour of a filament and the middle still reads slightly analog — the family’s word for soft at the centre.
  6. Close the capacitor. That is: cool on the tray, undisturbed, until the ripple dies. They keep, in a tin by the tea urn, until the next 10:00, and not one minute beyond it.

Card notes

  • The 24 is not decorative. It is the number of strips a tray takes and the number of volts the bench ran on, and Armand insisted on the coincidence every time the tin was opened.
  • Do not substitute the icing sugar for caster. Caster is the half-wave take: it gets across, but it throws away half the sugar and shows it on the biscuit.
  • The four pinches of salt are the dry cells. A fifth pinch is the card’s only recorded failure, and it is not to be re-run.
  • For the stewards’ table, the tray is set square to the tea urn so that a reader walking in from the Colne side can count the strips without turning the head. The head should not have to turn.
