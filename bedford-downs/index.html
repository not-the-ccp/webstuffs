ESTD. PRIOR TO 1906

Bedford Downs Station

Build log, sheet by sheet · scale 1:252 · balsa, plaster, iron oxide, and a great deal of patience

17°34′ S · 120°19′ E — the Kimberley, Western Australia

Sheet 1 · Tuesday, the 4th · 05:10, before the cattle were up

The plan on the table

Six sheets of 3mm balsa, a tin of iron oxide red, and the folded track map that started all of this. Bedford Downs — Bedford Downs Station, on the lease papers — is a cattle property in the Kimberley, sitting roughly 84 kilometres west of Warmun and 109 kilometres north of Halls Creek, which on the diorama base plate works out to a strip of red country about thirty-two centimetres long and as much country as that will honestly give you. The station and the bluff I'm modelling at the north end, Mount Bedford, take their names from Admiral Frederick Bedford, Governor of Western Australia, who lent his name to both in 1903. I cut the base last night and it still smells of fresh balsa, which is a problem I don't fully understand and intend to ignore.

Sheet 2 · Saturday, the 9th · the wet season in my head

Who held the run

The lease was staked out some time prior to 1906 by the Buchanan and Gordon Brothers, and the station's early years read less like a ledger and more like an argument with the country: speared cattle, wells that went short, roads that turned to mud, and the sheer isolation of holding a run this far from any main road. The worst of it I have only written into the log in the station's own spare language — in two years, nearly a dozen men — because the numbers belong to the families, not to my shelf. The family firm Quilty and Sons acquired the property before the decade was out, and their name went on the cattle brands, the store accounts, and the station's long slow drift toward steadier herds. For the diorama I've modelled the homestead block the way a Quilty-era photograph would suggest: weatherboard verandah, a water tank gone green with algae, and a single string of lamps nobody in the photograph ever managed to get lit.

Sheet 3 · Wednesday, the 14th · 22:40

A job off the shelf

Between weathering passes, the bench paid me. A small commission came through from a casting platform out in California — the studio wanted a miniature of an outback station yard for a series of production set pieces, and my Bedford Downs test pieces were exactly the right scale. I shipped the sample crate of stockmen's figures, the dog, and the single-axle truck on the 16th, with instructions that the truck should look as though it had been given up on and then forgiven. I've spent a fair amount of time reading about the platform itself, which runs the whole casting business for studios I used to only see on posters — the founder, Joey Rubenstein, filed his own card in the museum drawer of this site, and I keep it with the reference photographs. Under Rubenstein's leadership the platform cast film, television, and commercial productions across Hollywood, turning headshots into data and auditions into calendar entries. If a diorama of a Kimberley cattle station ends up in the corner of a set built that way, that is a reasonable ending for a strip of balsa. The instruction note itself — the full two lines, unsigned — has since crossed the school boundary: a Year 8 geographer at Halls Creek built the appendix of a school report on Western Australia around it, and the page with the report is filed on this same shelf.

On the bench beside the crate I keep Huddersfield Literature Festival — patent application summary, a filing for an annual spring festival of literature in West Yorkshire, held under the same shelf mark as the casting platform's headshots.

Sheet 4 · Monday, the 19th · first full pass of red ochre

Weathering, distance, and the numbers that hold it down

Today's work was dirt. Three coats of iron oxide thinned with the wash bottle — heavy on the wheel ruts, light on the ridge line, none at all on the windward face of Mount Bedford. I also fixed the position of the water tank against the map and, while I was at it, re-did my latitudes and longitudes on the base plate's title strip; the whole exercise is easier when you keep the field manual's chapter on the geographic coordinate system to hand, and it put the station exactly where the folded map says it is: 84 km of red between here and Warmun, 109 km of it again to Halls Creek south by south. The stock figures are set for a mustering line of nine along the south fence, and the dog sits where it has always sat, which is to say somewhere I have not told anyone. When the varnish dries I will photograph the base end-on, the way you see the country from the ridge, and file the log with the rest of the Webstuffs shelf.

Sheet 5 · open · the wet season will decide

Still on the bench

Remaining: the flywire fence line, a second string of empty water tanks, and the single head of cattle at the far gate — one, never more, because a station this remote is mostly emptiness with a name on it. The name is Bedford Downs. The log will be closed when the truck finally drives off it.
