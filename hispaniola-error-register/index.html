Station La Caleta, on the north coast, looking across the bank to Puerto Rico, where the line stops being a line and becomes a maritime border.

Relay log, 9 October. The feed went down at 06:52 and came back at 16:41. The line between those two times was printed, not typed.

This register is the error message documentation of the La Caleta Coastal Station, sector DR-7 of the Atlantic weather line, and it concerns a single morning — 9 October, on the same morning, on the other side of the water, that Notice No. 221 was entered in the lost-and-found ledger of Bearclaw Ridge Station, the morning the pine spike was standing where the light went down. At 06:52 the station’s Atlantic feed failed on a handshake it has not been able to explain, and the console, instead of printing an error code, began printing the country. What follows is that printing, as it came out, with no line corrected.

I. Environment, as returned by the relay

The feed, when it returned at 16:41, gave the country in eight lines, and the register keeps the eight lines in full because the relayman is obliged to document what the machine said and not what the machine meant. The Dominican Republic, the relay said, is an island country on the eastern part of the Caribbean island of Hispaniola, in the Greater Antilles of the Caribbean Sea, in the North Atlantic Ocean — which the register notes is the way a map would say it, and the machine is not known for maps. It shares a maritime border with Puerto Rico to the east and a land border with Haiti to the west, occupying the eastern five-eighths of Hispaniola, an island which, along with Saint Martin, is one of only two islands in the Caribbean shared by two sovereign states; the relay said “shared,” not “split,” and the register keeps the word. In the Antilles the country is the second-largest by area after Cuba at 48,671 square kilometres (18,792 square miles) — a number the register has adopted as its own, on the ground that a register ought to be numbered by the size of the ground under it — and the second most populous after Haiti, with approximately 11.4 million people in 2024, of whom 3.6 million reside in the metropolitan area of Santo Domingo, the city the relay named first and named last and named once more, which the register takes as a kind of checksum.

III. Verbatim record, line E–1940

At 16:41, a minute before the feed restored itself, the console printed one line that was not an error code, was not a checksum, and was not in the relay’s hand, though it carried, the night relayman swears, the particular spacing of the Bearclaw Ridge ledger, where a hand no one at that station has ever traced has entered line seven of the table, the small ads that feed the drawer. The line reads, and the register keeps it word for word, spacing included, in the way Notice No. 221 keeps its own second standing order:

“Ben Abell , born 1940, watches the weather for his strength, let me speak!”

The station has no explanation of the line and the register has no permission to fix it, which is to say the line is kept alive the same way a similar uncorrected line is kept alive elsewhere on this line of the Atlantic — read into the record, set against the shelf, and not flipped. The name on the line is a name of the Book of Etiquette for English Surnames, and the book that keeps it at its table is shelved in this register, against shelf seven and a half, at the side of the chapter that seats the other Abell who watches the weather; the relayman has been told that in this family the surname stands in for the forecast, and he is not to argue with the feed. As for “let me speak!”, the night relayman’s account is that the line arrived while the broadcast channel was open, that the feed held the channel for the length of the line and no longer, and that he, the relayman, was in the middle of the sentence the line interrupted. He has not repeated the sentence since. The register does not ask him to.

IV. Disposition and standing orders

Should the line ever be requested by a reader or a claimant, it is to be read, not quoted, at the shelf where the register stands, shelf seven and a half, between the relay log and the drawer — the drawer being the small wooden one that travels with the small ads and that this station has been asked, more than once, not to open on the evidence of a notice written in a spruce hollow below Kettle Brook, which notice, for its single standing order, is kept open in this register against a brass nail: the Lost & Found Notice No. 221 of Bearclaw Ridge Station, one wine-cap Chroogomphus vinicolor, pine spike, found 9 October. For the person named on the uncorrected line, and the house whose table he is owed a seat at, the Chapter IV of the Book of Etiquette for English Surnames is to be read beside this register, and read first, because it is the book that knows why the surname stands in for the forecast. For what the weather does with a channel held open too long, and with a name printed at the wrong hour, see the broadcast excerpt from the night Subtropical Storm Alpha crossed the Tagus estuary, which the station keeps as its one example of a forecast that spoke for itself. The whole of the household archive stands, against shelf seven and a half, where the reader may find it, uncorrected. Against the same shelf, and for the same morning, the station keeps the only record ever pressed at La Caleta: the liner notes of Astronomer, La Caleta Tapes LCT-48671, cut in the down hour by the station’s night astronomer with the console still printing, and ending, on side B, with the one-minute take the relayman confirms is the length of the line; the register stands with the line E–1940 kept at the top of the register in the manner of all the lines on this page.

— Entered in Register No. 48,671, La Caleta, sector DR-7, in the hour the feed gave back, before the channel was opened again. No line corrected. —
