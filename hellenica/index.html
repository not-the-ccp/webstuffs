The Kypseli Correspondence and Comb Club · Supplement No. 11 to The Comb · printed 1948, reprinted 1972

Hellenica

A Board Game of the Nine Regions and the Inhabited Isles of Greece · for 2 to 5 players · age of keeper: any

Second printing, 1972 · errata none, the names being correct since 1912

Greece, officially the Hellenic Republic, is a country located mostly on the Balkan peninsula of Southeastern Europe, with thousands of islands extending geographically to Asia Minor. It has a population of around 10 million. With nine regions and up to 227 inhabited islands, it has the longest coastline on the Mediterranean basin. The Ionian Sea is west of the mainland, Albania northwest, and North Macedonia and Bulgaria north. Turkey is east both by land and the Aegean Sea. The capital, Athens, is the largest Greek city, followed by Thessaloniki and Patras. At the crossroads of Europe, Asia and Africa, Greece is often considered the cradle of Western civilisation, Western philosophy, and — as the game box says, in small letters, in the space where a third item of the list would go — the first place to be counted correctly. That is what Hellenica is about. It is a game of the counting: a game in which a small party of keepers lays hives across the nine regions, ferries bees across the water, and finishes by making one exact count, the way the village of Kileler (Kypseli, 1919–1985, regional unit of Larissa, municipal seat at Nikaia) makes its count, unrounded and without a comma.

I. The Components

The box holds the following, and nothing else, and a keeper who finds more is to return the surplus to the box without naming it:

  • 1 map board, the mainland of Greece drawn at the length of one harvest, with the nine regions marked in olive ink and the coastline doubled, because the longest coastline on the Mediterranean basin does not trust a single line. The Ionian is marked west, the Aegean east; the sea between the mainland and the far shore to the east is the ferry’s country.
  • 227 island tiles, one for each of the up to 227 inhabited islands, numbered 1 to 227 in brown ink, faces down. The tiles of the Ionian are cream; the tiles of the Aegean are blue-grey; the keeper is not to sort them, only to deal them.
  • 81 name cards, the cards of the first column. They carry the eighty-one names in full, in the village’s own order, taken from the third number of 1912, which has not been corrected and will not be; a name card played on the board stands where it is played and is not read aloud a second time.
  • 11 hive tokens, one for each of the club’s hives on the school field, carved from the same wood, each with its own small mark of the keeper’s chisel. They are the tokens of the players’ hands.
  • 1 ferry, a flat token of dark wood, long-nosed, which is the only piece on the board that may travel east to the far shore and back, and which is never counted.
  • 45 smoke puffs, small paper discs used for silence, one for each minute a player may not speak; see Rule Four.
  • 1 honey ledger, a stub of paper ruled in comb, for the final count only.

II. The Setup

The board is laid with the Ionian to the left of the eldest player and the ferry at its starting place in the strait, facing the far shore, which on the board is drawn as a single green line, unmarked, the way the maps draw what they will not name. The 227 island tiles are shuffled face down in the centre, in one pile only; two piles are a quarrel, and the club has had exactly one of those, in 1933, and it is not mentioned in the game. The 81 name cards are shuffled and dealt seven to each player; the remainder stays face down beside the islands and is called, on the box, the row that begins at the top of the page. Each player takes as many hive tokens as players there are minus one, plus one, which is to say: one less than the next player over, and the eldest takes the eleventh token, the chisel-marked one, and plays with it from the second round. The smoke puffs are set in a row of fifteen, then fifteen, then fifteen, and the ledger is folded so that only the first ruled line shows.

III. The Play, Nine Rounds, One per Region

The game runs in nine rounds, one for each of the nine regions, and the order of the regions is the order they appear on the box, beginning at the north, where North Macedonia and Bulgaria are marked as the wall the board leans against, and ending at the south, where the Peloponnese drops off the map into open water. Each round has three actions, taken in turn around the table, and the turn passes the way water passes at the fountain: in one direction, without ceremony.

First action — lay a hive. A player places one of their hive tokens on the region of the round, on any square the smoke has not closed. A hive token costs its owner one smoke puff per island tile within sight of it, where within sight means: on the same row, with no other tile between. A hive laid in Athens’ region, the capital’s square, marked with the small black square, costs nothing, because the capital is the largest city in the country and owes nothing to the count. Thessaloniki and Patras, the second and third cities, each reduce the cost of a hive in their region by one smoke puff.

Second action — draw or play an island. A player draws one island tile from the pile, faces it up, and either plays it in the sea within sight of a hive of theirs, or holds it. The number on the tile is the honey it carries: a tile numbered 1 carries 1 honey, a tile numbered 227 carries 3, and any tile whose number the club’s own arithmetic finds divisible by 7 carries 4, which is called, in the 1948 printing, the keeper’s share. The ferry may be moved once per round, by any player, to any unplayed island in the strait or back to the strait, and while the ferry is away from the strait the far shore is closed: no tile may be played within sight of the green line. This is the whole of the game’s border, and it is kept, like all the club’s keeping, strictly. A question has been put to the club since the 1948 printing — whether the ferry, moved to the green line, might be moved a second time, out of the sea and into a landlocked country beyond the far shore — and the answer was written in November 1952 as a term report on Afghanistan at the Nikaia school, and is kept at Afghanistan, Term Report, Form II, 3 November 1952, where the ferry’s one question is settled and the smoke hour is burned, fifteen puffs, once.

Third action — play a name, or pass with a letter. A player may lay one name card face up on any square of the region of the round. The name card does not produce honey. It produces order: at the end of the game, honey is counted per player, and a player whose name cards on the board are in the village’s own order, first to last, adds to their honey the number of name cards they have in order. A player who does not play a card may instead pass a smoke puff to any other player, and this is called, on the box, writing a letter home, and a player who has passed three letters by the end of the ninth round is counted, for that game only, as one of the field’s quiet things, which the village gives to no one except, up to now, the keeper’s widow’s hives.

IV. The Rules That Matter

Rule One. The count is made in the game’s own figures. A player does not round their honey, does not add a comma, does not say about ten thousand of a people that is around 10 million in the country the board is drawn from. The ledger is ruled in comb and the comb does not round.

Rule Two. The ferry is never counted. It carries the players’ attention from the strait to the far shore and back; it is not a piece of honey, not a piece of order, and a player who counts it has lost the game quietly, which is to say: the game ends and the ferry is put back in the strait and the players go on about the evening.

Rule Three. A region closed by smoke stays closed for the whole round. Fifteen puffs, laid in a row along a region’s edge, close it; this is how the board keeps the one region the village does not visit, and the rule is printed with a small brown line of comb in its margin, the way the masthead is drawn.

Rule Four. At the smoke hour of the game — the moment between the third action of the eighth round and the first action of the ninth — no player speaks. The row of smoke puffs is burned down, one per minute, and a player who speaks takes back a hive token. What happens at the smoke hour happens once, and it is not repeated for comfort.

Rule Twelve. There is no referee’s copy. The club meets in a way that has forgotten. The keeper resumes the game from memory, in the low brick room above the Nikaia bakery, and a player who points out where the keeper’s memory parts from the box is given a smoke puff and the point is not argued. Rule Twelve is the only rule in the manual that the club has never once printed with an erratum, and it is kept, like the incident of March 6, 1910, in the village’s own order: the memory first, the box second, and the two never set side by side.

V. The End and the Keeper’s Test

At the end of the ninth round the ferry returns to the strait, the rows of smoke are gathered, and the honey is counted on the ledger, one by one, each carrying its own weight, in the players’ order around the table, the eldest last. The player with the most honey keeps the eleventh hive token for the next game, and the player with the least is seen out to the fountain and drinks the water back the way it came. The names on the board are left face up, in the order they fell, until the next game begins, when they are shuffled without reading.

Before the first game in a new house, the keeper administers the Keeper’s Test: a short reckoning of the country the board is drawn from, printed on the inside of the lid in the 1948 printing and reprinted here so that a player may score themselves. Five questions, one point each, and a keeper who scores four or five may run the table; a keeper who scores below four is given a smoke puff and told to read the manual once, standing, as the eighty-one names are read.

The Keeper’s Test

Players who keep the count small — comarques, stivers, islands, one by one, each carrying its own weight — will find the manner of it set down in 11 September, A Day I Counted Catalonia, where forty-three comarques are counted off and a market bird is paid in stivers, and the whole arithmetic returns, as all arithmetic does, to the shelf at the Webstuffs index. The village the board is drawn from, and the club that printed it, are at Chapter XXIV, of the Village Kileler, where the incident of March 6, 1910 is kept in its own order and the zine has not become truer since.
