BLUE · GREY

Notes written in the second floor listening room, Haldum · a week before the record's date line · two swatches on the bench, a kettle, the album on repeat

1. The record

The record is Be, the fifth Korean-language studio album by the South Korean boy band BTS, and the note is written for the song the label put third. “Blue & Grey” opened the second half of the run as the album settled from its brighter material into weather: a slow, tender pop ballad, three minutes and forty-eight seconds long, in a key the engineers in the second floor listening room agreed to call “the key of a street at 4 a.m. in winter.” It was released with the album on the 20th of November 2020, into the middle of a pandemic that had made the song's subject matter — depression, burnout, anxiety, the particular loneliness of being kept safely apart from other people — the shared vocabulary of the year. The song speaks that vocabulary in two colors only. Blue is the cold side of the feeling; grey is the tired side. Nothing in the song is green, nothing is gold, and the notes here are written in the same discipline.

Who put the song together: the writing credit runs to Suga, RM, J-Hope, and Metaphor, and the production to Park Ji-soo, Levi, V, and Hiss Noise — a long list by the album's standards, which is itself information. A ballad this small usually has fewer hands on it. The song arrived at the session already carrying a history: it was originally intended to be included on V's solo mixtape, and it was the mixtape's intended centerpiece. It left that project the way a passenger leaves one train for another — with its luggage, its seat worn to shape, and the label's blessing — and boarded Be as track 3 instead, where it has stayed.

2. What the colors mean

The lyric holds two colors against each other the way the cover art does. The notes heard in the listening room, taken in the order the song runs them: blue is the color of the night the songwriter could not sleep, of the sea seen from a window he was not allowed to open; grey is the color of the Monday after, of the uniform, of the ceiling, of a phone screen with no new names on it. The song does not resolve the two colors into a third one, and that is its whole honesty. It is a song about being unable to leave the room and unable to stop being in it. Critics who found it a standout track on Be mostly found it for the same reason the notes agree: its vulnerability is unedited. The vocal performance was praised track by track in the reviews — the breath before the second verse, the way the top note of the bridge is almost dropped and then kept — and the production, thin and deliberate, was praised for refusing to cushion the low notes with strings it did not need.

From the session margin, in the producer's hand, above the last take of the final verse:

Do not chase them past the palisade the guide The departure is the truest sentence I have not worn since.

The margin line sits under two swatches, blue over grey, and the notes read it as the song's own rule: the departure — the leaving of the mixtape for the album, the leaving of the room the song keeps describing — is the truest thing the record says. It is a sentence the songwriter has not worn since the session, and the label's practice is to print such margins rather than explain them. The palisade, for the record, was the studio's garden fence; the guide, the night engineer, who was told not to follow the members out onto the lane and had the sense to stay inside it.

3. The mixing bench

The bench below is where the two colors were measured. The slider sets the ratio of blue to grey; the swatch shows the result, and the bench's ledger names it. At 0 the bench is pure blue — the cold side, the unopened window. At 100 it is pure grey — the tired side, the ceiling. The song, the producers agreed, lives somewhere in the middle third, closer to grey, and the reading they marked on the bench was 34 parts blue to 66 parts grey, which the ledger names “wet slate.” Set the bench where the song sits and read the name; it is the same name on the back of the CD case.

34 parts blue to 66 parts grey — wet slate

The bench is a small instrument and the song is a small song; that is the only comparison the notes will make. Without the bench, the record still stands: it is the third track, three minutes and forty-eight seconds, two colors, one departure.

Track 3 earns its position: it is the hinge the album turns on, the place where the year's bright face stops speaking for everyone and starts speaking in the singular. The reviews that picked it as a standout picked it for the hinge, and the notes agree with the reviews, which is rarer than either side would admit.

5. Credits and shelf-mates

Written by Suga, RM, J-Hope, Metaphor. Produced by Park Ji-soo, Levi, V, Hiss Noise. Recorded in the winter of 2020, across the year the calendars stopped. Issued as track 3 on Be, 20 November 2020. Preceded in the label's history by the solo mixtape it left behind. Followed in the room by track 4 and the kettle.

For the shelf: the song's company sits in the same drawer as the conglomerate's music ledger, where the catalogues run longer than the company and the four left-over lines sit unexplained — the field chapter that keeps that ledger is Sony — Field Manual FM-21, Chapter 9. The listening bench for the note was first tested on the island side, in the back room above the bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, where the Listeners' Club traded the ballad through its speakers one Thursday and copied the margin line into its commonplace book — the account of that province is kept in Camarines Sur — Geography Report, Naga National High School, Grade 10. The whole drawer is filed in Webstuffs. And the departure itself has its water charted: the wharf office at Gloucester, Massachusetts keeps a 1941 season brochure for the rock that gave the third quartet its name, where the boat does the actual going, the bell keeps the hour, and the white scarf is still issued at the gangway — The Dry Salvages — Visitor's Brochure, Cape Ann.
