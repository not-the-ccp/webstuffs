Hôtel 8859

The Lane Behind the Bakery, on the shelf between the vinegar crock and the salt beef · Open Thursday–Sunday, 5 p.m. to the last code point

★★★★★ — Four stars, withheld half a point for the last menu card

There is a dining room at the back of the lane behind the bakery that seats exactly 191 characters and no more, and it is the most orderly table I have ever been served at. The house is Hôtel 8859, and its name is a standard: ISO/IEC 8859-1, though the menu does not say so, and the waiter does not say so, and the room behaves as if the number were simply the house’s. It is an 8-bit room. Every plate, every garnish, every name in the cellar is one byte wide, carried in with the quiet authority of an ASCII-based standard character encoding, and the first 256 seats of the house are exactly the first 256 code points of Unicode — walk in at 5 and the front of the room is already your neighbour’s. The house is the basis for a great many popular 8-bit sets, the way a good small room is the basis for a great many larger ones; I have eaten better in places that inherited from here, but I have eaten nowhere so neatly.

Of the room

Tables of twelve, linen the colour of a page that has never been re-encoded, and a place-card at every setting. The room is used throughout the Americas, Western Europe, Oceania, and much of Africa — the passport stamps on the door are the proof — and it is the most declared single-byte character encoding for the web of any dining room I know, which is to say: everyone who arrives has written their address down as this house’s. What the card does not tell you, and what I will tell you, is that the guests themselves do not notice. They expect a superset. They expect Windows-1252, and the room, being a modern one and knowing its HTML5, serves it as such: the seat at 137, vacant in the standard and long empty, holds a per mille sign; the seat at 145 holds a masculine ordinal; the window seat holds a broken heart. You are a character from the wider set by the time you have finished the bread, and no one at the table will mention it, because it is exactly how the standard is observed in practice. The house does not pretend to be 8-bit when it is not.

Of the courses

We were served, in the order the kitchen keeps, and the kitchen keeps it strictly: first, an amuse of cédille à la française, U+00E7, shaved paper-thin over a warm crêpe, the cedilla falling where the grave accent stands; then a velouté with the œ floated whole on top — U+009C, the only letter in the room that is two letters wearing one coat, and the only one the kitchen prints twice as wide when it sets the card — and the soup was excellent; then the seña de Castilla, U+00F1, the n with its tilde pressed firm, served cold over sherry vinegar, and I should add that the vinegar comes from the crock behind the bakery, the same crock that keeps the desk’s chapter of etiquette, so that the salt beef and the house rules are seasoned by the same bottle — this is not a coincidence, this is infrastructure; then, as the main, the salt beef itself, hooped, cured, and cut by weight and not by favour, which is the room’s one rule and it is kept with the seriousness of a binding once made; and finally a tarte à l’umlaut for the northern guests, ö and ü set like two small round windows, and a little ß, U+00DF, the sharp s, which is on the menu as a dessert of its own and which I found, like all the sharpest things, best taken small.

The cellar is 256 references long and 32 of them will not be opened to you, but nobody minds; they are the control characters, and control characters are never opened to guests. The sommelier is a Mr. Rook, who works the concertina between courses and reads the vintages by ear. The melodic interlude is by a Ms. Vale, on the melodica, and she plays the house’s own tune, which is nine notes long and gives eight of them away and takes eight in, each in its proper place — the desk will know the piece at once.

Of the last menu card

The fault of the evening was the last card, and it was a fault of encoding, and I must set it down whole: the kitchen printed it in a page of its own and, having agreed nothing with the table, the words drifted — the final line of the card ran—

“Do _you_ want a kick’? By the Divorce and Matrimonial Causes Act, 1857, a wife may now be ascertained by taking the stern.”

and no one at the table had asked for divorce law, and no one is ascertained by any stern. I took it to the desk, which is what desks are for. The maître, without looking up, said that the card had been set in a code page that had retired in 1999, and that a retired page is served the same as a retired language: read it as it was recorded, and do not rewrite it at the table. He said further, which I repeat exactly, that the line was a reference to the model galleon rising in the galvanized cattle trough beside the desk — the San Juan de Portugal, built to the 1:64 line drawings — and that taking the stern was what the room calls turning a table so the sea comes through the door. I am not certain this is true. I am certain the card was correct as printed, which is more than can be said for most reviews. The build log of that ship is kept two shelves down, and the desk will let you see it.

Of the bill and the verdict

The bill came to 191 lines, one per character present, and the tip was rounded down because the room does not bill for control. I would return for the salt beef alone. I would not return for the last card, and that is the half point withheld: a dining room this exact should know that a page it cannot agree with its guests is not a page. Four stars. The name is Latin-1, as every guest in the room will tell you, and the number is for the house, and the house is for everyone, in 256 languages at once, served one byte at a time.
