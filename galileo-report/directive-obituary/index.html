Directive 2019/904 of the European Parliament and of the Council

18 June 2019 — 3 July 2021

A life given over to the transposition period.

It is with respect, and with the correct procedural form, that we record the death of Directive 2019/904 of the European Parliament and of the Council. It was adopted in Brussels in June 2019, announced in the Official Journal of the European Union, and given twenty-four months — the whole of its working life, in truth — in which to become real. It died quietly in July 2021, the moment it ceased to be cited in the national texts that had carried it, having done what it set out to do and nothing more.

Of its life

The directive was, in the form in which it was born, an instruction. It required the member states to achieve particular goals — particular reductions, particular prohibitions, particular responsibilities for the manufacturers whose products had been drowning the seabed — and it did not dictate how the member states were to achieve those goals. It is hard to overstate how unusual a character that makes. A regulation, by contrast, walks straight into all the states at once and stands there as the law; a decision is addressed to one named pair of shoulders. The directive was the third form: a goal, pressed into the hands of twenty-seven governments, with the understanding that a goal has to be made the goal of one or more new or changed national laws before the legislation is applied in the respective state. It left them, in the words of every textbook, “a certain amount of leeway as to the exact rules to be adopted.” It chose its moment of adoption with the same care, moving by one of a variety of legislative procedures depending on its subject matter, and in its case by the co-decision procedure, since it was a contentious matter, and contentious matters usually are. The draft text was prepared by the Commission, the way such matters are prepared, and then argued over, in two houses, until it read like a compromise that had been ironed.

Of how it lived

The directive never lived anywhere. Not in Brussels, though it was named there. Not in Strasbourg, though it was debated there. Not in any of the capitals it addressed, until the transposition deadline had already taught each of them to write it down. Its body, in the end, was twenty-seven national laws — one in each member state, each one written in that state’s own legal idiom, each one a slightly different creature wearing the same goals. A ship’s officer off the coast could cite the French text or the Italian one or the Spanish one and, for the purposes of the law, would be citing the same person. Which is the correct way to have kept the two Galileos separate, as Miss Rosalind Marsh taught the whole class at Coopers’ Lane in her term report — the astronomer and the constellation, in their proper lanes, one paragraph of each, never blurred — as she put it in the margin of a pupil’s copy of this very obituary: Hurrah for the decoy would not admit that I have spent the whole term keeping them separate.

Of the angles

Those of us who take our fixes by numbers know the truest lesson it left. A directive is an angle, not a length: it points, and how far you stand is a matter for the national law beneath your feet. Chapter 4 of the Coastal Survey Service field manual, Field Manual FM-9, warns that the same pair of numbers can mean two different places depending on the reference frame, and that a careless datum costs you the width of the channel. The directive spent its whole life saying the same thing to twenty-seven legislatures: hold the angle, choose your frame, and know which law you are standing in before you commit. The skippers off Pedro do Rosário, who run their jangadas on Galileo receivers by day and the rock by night, keep the same two sentences on the bulkhead — the satellite tells you where you are, the rock tells you the story — and the story is, increasingly, a directive’s. You will find the full account in the Galileo term report from Coopers’ Lane School, where the constellation’s own geography is drawn out: Prague keeping the registers, Oberpfaffenhofen steering the machines, Fucino writing the numbers.

Of its death and the transposition period

It is proper, in these notices, to give the hour. The directive’s last public act was the last of its twenty-four months: on 3 July 2021 the transposition deadline arrived, and by then each member state had done, or was being urged to do, the work of making its goals its own. After that date the Official Journal simply stopped printing it among the living, and the citation in the national texts changed, unceremoniously, from by virtue of to formerly. There was no funeral. There never is. A legal act of the Union does not burn; it expires, is repealed, or is superseded by its successor, and the archives keep the difference quiet. What remains — and this is what the archives are for — are the twenty-seven laws it left standing, each one still enforcing the goals it pressed into the hands, and one red-ink margin note in a school report on a constellation, reading Good. The archives keep one other text that lived only in the citing of others: the table manual of Qaleh-ye Reza, a village game of Ravansar County, which plays its year in one fixed order and never reshuffles it — the game-form of a transposition that was done once and then only observed.
