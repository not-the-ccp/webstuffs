Before setting out

Cadwaladr ap Cadwallon — Cadwaladr Fendigaid, “the Victorious,” in the later annals — was a late seventh-century king of Gwynedd who reigned from the middle of the century until his death in 682. Little is known of the reign itself; the annals give us dates and no days. What the king has in abundance is afterlife. Following his depiction in the De gestis Britonum of Geoffrey of Monmouth, Cadwaladr became a mythical redeemer figure in medieval Welsh literature: the last native Briton to be King of Britain, who renounced his throne in 689 and set out on a pilgrimage to Rome after an angel prophesied to him that he must do so in order for the Welsh to eventually recover their patrimony. The walk follows that legend west to east and back again, from his church to his lake to the gate where, in the old ballads, he will yet ride back in with the tide.

Note the dates for your own amusement: the pilgrim departs for Rome in 689, seven years after the king died in 682, and the walk does not pretend otherwise. It is a route for people who believe a map is a kind of prophecy.

    The six stops

  1. Stop 109:00

    St Cadwaladr's, Caernarfon — the west gate

    Start beneath the red dragon at the town's old wall; 2.9 miles from here to Stop 2, gentle.

    The walk begins where the patronage has stood for a thousand years. St Cadwaladr's has kept the king's name longer than it has kept the king's stone, and the tower bell rings the tour in at nine precisely, which is to say within four minutes, because the bell-ringer is also the man with the van. Look up at the dragon on the gable: in the old telling it breathes the sea air down the harbour mouth, and on fog mornings the breath is indistinguishable from the weather, which the town has always considered a point in the legend's favour.

  2. Stop 210:25

    Llyn Tegid — the lake of the dragon's blood

    5.1 miles, mostly uphill; 310 ft of the walk's 610 ft here. May: lily pads, and no argument.

    Geoffrey has the dragon of Gwynedd bloodied at this water and the whole lake dyed red for a day, so the local rule of evidence is that the lake was red once and has since been over-fished for red. Sit at the southern shore for the longest sit of the tour. If you read the king's afterlife in the De gestis Britonum, this is the stop where the reading earns its water. The annals say nothing about the lake; the lake, being a lake, has never had to answer for the annals.

  3. Stop 312:10

    The Dolgellau bakehouse — the miller's standing order

    3.7 miles, along the river road; the oven door will be open by twelve.

    The tour eats its lunch at the bakehouse on the river road, which has baked the pilgrims' bread since before the pilgrimage existed. The order sheet above the oven door still carries the miller's standing rule, kept word for word across five generations and one fire: Beside the cake to still be trees left standing. The meaning, as the miller's family gives it, is that a bakery that fells every tree in the valley for fuel has no shade to bake in by August; a kingdom is run the same way, and the rule was written to be read by kings. It is the walk's only uncorrected line, and it is read, not quoted, at the shelf — the same discipline as a certain coastal error register keeps for its own uncorrected lines, and it may be shelved with those in the Error Register No. 48,671, La Caleta.

  4. Stop 413:50

    The Aberdyfi estuary — the last look west

    4.0 miles, estuary path; 90 ft of descent. Tidal gap at the ferry step — check the board.

    This is the last westward water on the route, and the legend treats it as a departure point: the place where the king, in his pilgrim's clothing, might be imagined stepping off the boat that is supposed to carry him to Rome, which is to say a boat that is supposed to do the impossible in a westerly wind. The estuary does its best. Sit where the ferry step meets the mud at low water and let the tide argue with the angel. The walk, like the legend, does not take sides.

  5. Stop 515:05

    The Dyfi headland — the redeemer's seat

    1.8 miles, headland track; 170 ft of the last ascent. Wind: assume the worst, dress for the worse.

    The ballads put the redeemer's seat on this headland: the stone bench from which, when the hour comes, Cadwaladr will watch the sea and ride back in with the tide to recover the patrimony the angel promised. The bench is a bench; the tide is on time; the hour has, for over thirteen centuries, declined to come. The tour allows twenty minutes here, which the guidebook notes is exactly the length of one full tide's change of mind. A stage of a king is a seat with a promise in it; this one has never once been empty of promise.

  6. Stop 616:40

    The gate at Harlech — the return

    1.9 miles, down to the sea wall; the walk ends at the old gate beneath the castle steps.

    The walk ends at the gate the ballads use for the return, beneath the castle that was built, in the honest chronicle, as a wall against the Welsh and has been in service for seven hundred years as a stage for them. The pilgrim never walked this gate; the king died in 682, a generation before the pilgrim set out in 689, and the walk has spent nineteen and a half miles making the arithmetic legible. At the gate the tour turns, as the legend does, and begins the long westward home: the bread is still warm at Dolgellau if you hurry, the lake keeps no closing time, and the dragon at St Cadwaladr's is, as always, breathing.
