Council of the Village of Mestia · Upper Svaneti · Republic of Georgia

Minutes of the 14th Session of the Autumn Assembly

Held in the upper room of the Church of the Holy Trinity, Mestia, on Thursday, 9 October 1961, and begun at 10:15 in the morning. Session No. 14 of the year 1961, the four-hundred-and-second year of the minutes.

Subject of the session: Dali (also written Daal, Dæl; Georgian: დალი), the huntress of the high places, and the keeping of her taboos for the coming winter.

Members present

Bakur Jimerinidze, chairman of the council; Ketevan Amiranashvili, secretary; the hunters Zurab Lominadze, Lasha Khizanishvili and Otar Chkhikvadze; the shepherd’s representative, Giga Guliashvili; the herder’s elder, Mikheil Chubinishvili; Nino Beridze, present for the first time on the council’s bench at her own request; the tanner of the lower quarter, Revaz Jibladze; and two elders of the valley who took no part in the count. Eleven in all, of the thirteen who hold benches. Absent: Tsimil Chagelishvili, at the pastures above the Enguri, and his cousin Dato, at the mill.

The quorum was called at 10:16 and found full. The chairman declared the session open in the name of the valley, and the secretary opened the book at the first blank leaf.

Item 1 — The state of Dali, and her office

The chairman opened the session by reading the standing recital, which is read at every autumn session: that Dali is the goddess of the hunting places of this people of the Caucasus; that she holds office as patron of the hoofed wild animals of the mountain, first the ibex with its black horns, and next the red deer, the chamois, and all the rest that run on the scree; that the hunters who kept her taboos had their success in the hunt assured to them, and the hunters who broke them were punished by her hand, which has been observed to work on the eye, the hand, the gun, and the heart of a man, in that order, and not always in that order. She is most clearly attested, the recital continues, in the stories of the Svan people of this northwest corner of Georgia, and the council took the recital into the record as read. A trader from Sogavjishvili of Mingrelia, who has sat in this very room in a past session, reports that the people of the western lowlands keep a figure of the same office whom they call Tkashi-Mapa (Mingrelian: ტყაში-მაფა), the mistress of the wood, and holds the two offices for one desk with two names. The council does not contest the report; the herds do not keep to the map.

The chairman added, for the record, the shape in which she is usually described in the telling: a beautiful nude woman of the high places, standing in the rock where the ibex stand, which is to say that the hunters see her only when the weather is right and the man’s account with her is clean.

Item 2 — The seven taboos, ratified

The secretary read the list of the taboos as amended for the winter. Mr. Lominadze moved, Mr. Khizanishvili seconded, and the council carried the motion by eleven to none that the seven taboos stand as follows:

  1. One shall not take a doe that carries, in the season of carrying, on pain of the eye.
  2. One shall not shoot across the water of a running stream; the water carries the word.
  3. The skull of the first kill shall not be left to the weather; it shall be set upon the stone at the head of the pass, face to the north, before the second kill.
  4. No knife that has tasted the offering meat shall cut bread in the house for nine days.
  5. One shall not say the word for the antler on the morning of the hunt; the word goes before the man.
  6. One shall not follow a wounded animal into the dark of a gorge after the last light.
  7. One shall not speak the hunt of another man, good or bad, to a third person before the meat is in the larder.

Mr. Chkhikvadze asked what the punishment is for the sixth, and was answered that the sixth has no announced punishment, which is why the hunters observe it. The council agreed and moved on. It was noted that the counts of the season to date stand at forty-two ibex, nineteen red deer, and six chamois, against the forty-one and twenty-one of the season before, and that the difference is ascribed, in the presence of everyone, to the keeping of the first taboo.

Item 3 — The question of the tape and the benches of Arzl

Ms. Beridze, who had spent the summer at the limestone benches above the Imst road and who has kept her own small record of what the stone there demands, reported that a keeper of a station diary at Arzl im Pitztal had found a coil of red tape wound about a knife-edge crimp on the long traverse of Problem 14, laid smooth and older than the polish on the rock, and had concluded, in print, that a line that holds, holds. The full account stands in the station’s diary for 14 July 2017, Station No. 714, the traverse at the Arzl boulders. The council discussed for eleven minutes and judged the tape, at such distance, for what it is: a boundary marker left by some keeper of some other god, at the edge of some other range, in the same hand that leaves them here. No action beyond the record was required.

Item 4 — On the keeping of a peace

The herder’s elder observed that a hunting taboo is the same instrument as a peace treaty, only with one party, and that the taboos in Item 2 are the valley’s way of signing with a god who signs no paper. For a study of the instrument in its other hand, the council was shown the obituary of the Treaty of Salynas (1398), which the keeper of the book keeps in the second drawer: a peace of thirteen months, signed on an islet of the Neman, broken in the open field at the Blue Waters. The elder’s whole address was that Salynas promised a gift and then built machinery for the gift, while Dali’s machinery is the hunter himself, and the council took the address into the record with no amendment, the difference between the two kinds of peace being that the Neman could be crossed again and the mountain could not.

Item 5 — Exhibit C, from the storeroom

A page was brought up from the storeroom beneath the church, found between two bundles of sealed letters on the night of the 4th, and laid upon the table. The hand is Latin and not Georgian; the paper is a brochure leaf, the kind the rail posts at the station at Mestia put out for the lowland visitors, cut down and ruled over in a careful hand. The secretary was directed to read the page into the record word for word, as it stands, without correction, and read:

Provenance: the storeroom night is logged — to generate visual feedback from a card in his own account, “polite and wrong,” and he says the brochure at all.

The council was asked to interpret, and no one did. Ms. Beridze offered that the page is a ledger entry kept by a man who wrote his trade into his god’s books, and that the phrase “polite and wrong” is the truest line in it, which the council accepted without voting. The page was directed to be returned to the storeroom between the same two bundles, and the bundle on the bottom, which was the heavier one, was directed to be left on top, on the ground that the storeroom keeps its own order and the council does not hold a commission over it.

Closing of the session

At 16:40 the chairman declared the 14th session closed. The minutes were read back in full, corrected in two places by the secretary at the chairman’s word, and taken for signature. The next session is fixed for the first full moon of November, for the division of the winter pastures, and any member who has found a marker on the rock, a page in a bundle, or a god in a lowland name, is directed to bring it up.

Bakur Jimerinidze Chairman of the Council
Ketevan Amiranashvili Secretary
