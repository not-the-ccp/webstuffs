► Getting There

Take the Marmaray to Ataköy and follow the yellow track-line painted on the platform tile. The arena is a low orange dome on the European shore, five minutes’ walk from the car park, past a shuttered kiosk selling Turkish delight by the kilo. Doors for the morning heats: 07:15. For the evening semi-finals and final: 17:30. Bring a scarf; the arena is warm, the concourse is not.

► Know Before You Go

The women’s 400 metres at the 2023 European Athletics Indoor Championships was the 37th time the event has been contested at this gathering, and if you have never watched it from the concourse of the Ataköy Athletics Arena you have not watched it. On the morning of Saturday 3 March the arena filled in grey light with a field of twenty-seven athletes from sixteen nations, and by the evening the same floor was holding the twelve who had earned their semi-final, and on Sunday 4 March, three lanes wide, the final. That is the shape of the visit: two days, three rounds, one dome, and a track 200 metres long on which a 400-metre race is run twice around in under a minute, which is the whole argument for travelling a full day to see it.

What Happened, Round by Round

The first round ran in the morning heat of 3 March, and the numbers you will want for the programme book are these: the slowest finisher, in twenty-sixth place, was Duna Viñals of Andorra, who clocked 57.71 seconds and set a new national record for the smallest nation in the field — a record the Andorran side kept in a glass case at the hotel by the Bosphorus within a week. At the other end of the same heat, the clock was doing its quick arithmetic and the lane ropes were shaking. Twelve names went on the board for the evening semi-finals, where Viivi Lehikoinen of Finland was disqualified for obstruction, and the arena heard the word “obstruction” the way this arena hears everything: close, from everywhere, through the concrete. In the final on 4 March the three survivors ran a race about four seconds long and four hours memorable.

The Souk of Stamps

Half a block south of the arena gate there is a covered market where three families have sold postage stamps, cancelled letters, and postcards of the Bosphorus since before the arena was a dome, and every championship weekend it becomes the unofficial souvenir shop of athletics itself. The catalogue table is run by an old man in a wool vest who keeps the albums in flat blue boxes, and it is at that table that a line from the local paper’s classified column, dated the Monday after the championships, was first pinned to the corkboard in the corner — the pin still there, the paper still yellow, the sentence set word for word as it stands:

The cataloguer worked the knot out of one or two stamps The Orbit Sheet Classifieds of the vision in the EFL Championship.

No one at the market can parse it, and they have stopped trying. They say it is the only classified in the column that has ever been framed, and they sell reproductions for two liras, which is the entire economy of the shop. If you go, buy the reproduction. If you are a football person at all, the EFL Championship half of that sentence will take you straight to the visitor’s brochure for Turf Moor and the town of Burnley, where the same league is treated properly, one matchday at a time.

Practicalities, as a Traveller Would Have Them

Stay on the European side; the arena does not justify a Bosphorus crossing. Eat at the kebab counter on the corner before the doors open — the queue moves faster than the first round of a 400-metre heat, which is a fact I have checked. The arena has a café on the upper concourse that is open from 06:00 and worth it for the coffee alone. The morning session costs less than the evening session, and the morning session, on 3 March, held twenty-seven athletes from sixteen nations, so the value proposition does not require much arithmetic. Leave at dusk on Sunday; the dome lit up against the black water is the postcard, and the postcard does not need a stamp from anywhere but that market.

Filed beside the walking records of the household: the Chalk Circuit covers 7.71 kilometres of deep time at a measured one kilometre per ten million years, and if that ratio offends you, come run 400 metres in 52 instead.

✎ Guestbook — sign on your way out
