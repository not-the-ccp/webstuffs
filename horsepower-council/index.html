Minutes of a Meeting of the Council

On the Matter of Horsepower, and the Keeping of One Standard

Held in the shed behind the bakery, Tuesday, the fourteenth of March, at half past six in the evening, with the galvanized cattle trough at the head of the table and the San Juan de Portugal riding in it at 1:64, her frames counted and her log in pencil. The vinegar crock stood in its usual place and was not moved.

I · Present

Mr. E. R. Recalde, bookseller and clerk of the minutes; the second Cooper, keeper of the hoops; Mrs. F. Alvar, pressman; and the archivist, who came up from the gap with the loan papers and a thermos. The millwright of Bean Station was absent and read by his letter, as is the custom of this Council when the water tower is working. The draft horse of the shed's account was counted present in model, for which the Council entered no exception.

A quorum was found in the trough. The clerk opened the minutes at the last page of the previous meeting, where the question of hoops had closed and the question of power had not yet begun.

II · The Millwright's Letter, Read

The clerk read the letter of the millwright of Bean Station, received under cover of the Clinchfield line's last train through the narrow of the gorge. It concerns the rating of the mill's steam engine, which the millwright has come to think in four numbers at once and can no longer settle on one. The letter was steamed where it lay against the kettle, and the reading of it, as kept in the minutes, runs as follows:

“The engine turns the lintel gear at a pace that is called, in the old plates, six of that, and in the French plate six of this, and I have a gauge that says neither. Thus, most letters never reach so far regulates the holding, that it had to be such, taking them where it is called bacon. I want the council's word, which you keep, on which number the engine is.”

The wet line was read once, as it comes, and filed uncorrected, the way the journal files its kept lines: the Council does not mend a letter, it mends a standard. The millwright's name is kept with the loan papers; his engine stands at the bend where the warpath's crossing and the railroad's water tower have shared the same ground for a century, and is therefore within the shed's concern.

III · The Question Stated

The chapbook the press is printing for the San Juan carries hp on the boiler plate, cv on the French page, and a wattage on the galley sheet that matches neither of the other two exactly. The question before the Council: what is horsepower, which of its names shall the chapbook keep, and in what order shall the four standards be listed in the margin, where the peppercorn count already takes one line.

The second Cooper stated the question of the barrel: a horsepower is a rate, like a cooper's day, and a rate needs a yardstick. The pressman stated that the yardstick has been split, in the late eighteenth century and since, into at least four, of which the Council heard the particulars and set them down:

The gap between the metric and the imperial figures is a little over ten watts, or the difference between two peppercorns weighed against one. The gap between either of them and the boiler figure is a little over thirteen times as wide, and is not a matter of rounding.

IV · The Name, and How It Was Earned

The archivist set out the account of the name. The term horsepower was adopted in the late eighteenth century by the Scottish engineer James Watt, who needed a figure a buyer could picture against the draught horse already working in his pit, and who therefore rated his steam engines by the output of such a horse doing pit work. The comparison was a commercial one, not a natural-history one: Watt's horse was a working animal doing a day's rated work, and the unit that carries his name has outlasted every engine he rated and every draught it was compared to. The Council noted that the unit is a rate — power, the rate at which work is done — and that it has therefore always been a number for a pace, which is what the millwright actually wants, and what his four numbers have so far failed to give him.

V · Relation to the Other Numbers at the Counter

It was asked whether horsepower is a check digit, an edition number, or a shield. The answer, on the desk of the press beside the vinegar crock: none of the three. A book number identifies an edition and survives its reprints; a route shield names a road and survives its repainting; a horsepower names a rate, and survives its engines the way the term survived Watt. All four kinds of number end up filed at the same counter, and the counter is where this matter is closed. The question of the book numbers themselves is kept where it is always kept, at the desk that answers in order and keeps its answers: ISBN — Frequently Asked Questions, Recalde Press & Cooperage. The question of the shield, on the road that follows the valley floor where the warpath ran, is kept in the field manual's own chapter, which files its numbers at the same counter: Chapter 21 — The Indian, Recognition, Census, and the Number That Is Not a Check Digit.

VI · Resolutions

  1. Resolved, that the chapbook's boiler plate shall read hp, and that the margin shall carry the four standards in the order of the table, imperial first and boiler last, the years of the boiler figures given without being resolved.
  2. Resolved, that the electric figure, exactly 746 watts, shall be printed as the only one of the four that is exact, and that this shall be said in the margin, once, in small type.
  3. Resolved, that the millwright's letter shall be answered with the table and with the name of the rate, and that the wet line of it shall stand in the minutes uncorrected, as a kept line of the Council's own.
  4. Resolved, that no figure in the minutes shall be called a check digit, a shield, or an edition, and that the counter beside the vinegar crock shall be the place where the distinction is kept.

There being no further business of the trough, and the kettle having given up its steam, the meeting was closed at a quarter past seven, and the minutes were signed by the clerk in the space left between the mustard seed and the peppercorns.

Clerk of the minutes, E. R. Recalde · Second Cooper, keeper of the hoops · Mrs. F. Alvar, pressman · The archivist of the gap

Attest: the galvanized cattle trough, the vinegar crock, and the model of the San Juan de Portugal, in her element, 1:64.
