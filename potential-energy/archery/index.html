Thread No. 17 · The Bluff Range — on archery, for the record · six posts · pinned by the Gate 3 crew

Marla of Eureka Springs 14 Oct, 21:12 Post 1

I finished Edna's letter on potential energy in the Gazette sitting on the bench behind the Brine Bath, and I had to write this one, because I have been standing at the back of the Gate 3 line for six seasons and I still cannot tell you what the bluff range actually is. The archers have been up there since last month — eight of them, faces bolted to the rock above the dynamo room — and the only instruction I have ever been given about it is “stand down and mind the quivers.”

For the record, then: archery is the sport, practice, or skill of using a bow to shoot arrows at a target. The word comes from the Latin arcus, meaning bow — same family as the arc the arrow makes, which seems like a fair amount of information for one word. Historically it has been used for hunting and combat, and in modern times it is mainly a competitive sport and a recreational activity, which is exactly how the range runs it: a competition at Friday night, and anybody who stands around it the rest of the week is doing it for the air up on the bluff. A person who practises it is typically called an archer, a bowman, or — the word the historian uses when she thinks nobody is listening — a toxophilite. So: what is the range, what do you shoot at, and why does the crew get cross about it?

Fenwick Gate 3 crew, third from the front 14 Oct, 22:03 Post 2

What it is: forty metres of rock, cut level, eight lanes, eight faces. The faces are the standard five-ring kind — ten, eight, six, four, two — painted on targets the size of the boiler's old manhole cover, which is why the manhole cover is no longer on the boiler. Two lanes are marked for compound bows, six for recurve; draw weights run 60 pounds for the compounds and 40 for the recurves, and the park's house arrow is 420 grains, same for everyone, because the park does not do “my arrow.” You shoot an end of eighteen arrows, you call your rings out loud, and the face-keeper writes them down in the same ledger the dynamo room keeps, one column to the left of the volts. It is the only place in the park where the ledger is written by hand, and that is the whole reason the crew gets cross about it: nobody types, nobody erases, a ring that is not called is a ring that did not happen.

C. D. Rucker park historian 15 Oct, 07:41 Post 3

May I. Archery is older than this park by some centuries, and the bluff it sits on is older than both of them. The old travelling fair carried a bow in the brine barrel — a short hunting bow, the kind that does not mind wool and salt air — and it is in the housekeeping ledger, not on page 47 but on page 48, directly under the lettuce line from Edna's letter, in the same canteen hand, transcription kept exactly, punctuation as found:

One bow, one quiver, one black speck, to be counted at the turn of the barrel.

Whoever kept the canteen was doing the same housekeeping the ledger does on the lift hill: storing things, counting them, and refusing to let a single rust speck into the inventory. The range runs on the same principle. You store the work in the limbs, you count it back as rings, and the black specks — the misses — are not failures so much as entries.

T. of the Brine Works 15 Oct, 08:15 Post 4

For the energy side, since it is the letters page's fault we are having this conversation. A drawn bow is the elastic kind of potential energy — stored work in a deformed thing, the same spring the launch springs out back are made of — and a loose arrow is that ledger being spent in a straight line instead of along track. I asked Rucker last week how she describes the release to the new archers, because I run the water and I want to know what she tells the people who stand in my queue. She said, “You draw to the anchor and the arrow is already going; you only decide where. It is fast, and it is always fast.” I have been thinking about that ever since. She goes on too rapidly in it the way the energy is always under construction, the way the lift hill does.

Edna R. Calk season pass, row of six 15 Oct, 19:58 Post 5

Numbers, since I will be cross if they do not get printed. The house setup — a 60-pound compound, 30 centimetres of draw, 420 grains of arrow — stores about 80 joules in the limbs before the bow spends its share of inefficiency, and leaves the arrow travelling roughly 71 metres a second when it leaves the string. Over the 40-metre line that is a flight of about 0.57 seconds, and in that time gravity takes about 1.6 metres if you shoot level — which is the entire reason a sight is set a few degrees high, and the entire reason the face-keeper keeps a level on the target rail. The 4.2 g at the crest of The Boiler Room is what the park does with stored work when it gets 48 metres of it; the range is what it does with 80 joules and one straight line. The state they both sit in — Ozarks, bluffs, brine, pie — is still described to the decimal in the Arkansas visitor's brochure, and the brochure has never once described a target face, which is an omission I note for the next printing.

Gate 3 crew pinned 16 Oct, 06:00 Post 6

For the record and the queue: target nights are Friday, 19:00, on the bluff, weather or brine; the pie stand moves its counter to the foot of the range that night and will sell you the same pies the barrel has been packing for six seasons. Bring the season pass, mind the quivers, and if you are new, stand at the back of the line until somebody with the ledger tells you otherwise. The barrel has been open since 2006. The face has been up since last month. Both are keeping their accounts.
