Description

OFFERED, and the house is proud to offer it whole: the order Coleoptera, the beetles, in that superorder of the completely metamorphosing, the Holometabola, from which the beetle takes its name and its wings. The distinguishing mark is visible to the unaided eye: the front pair of wings is hardened into wing-cases, the elytra, a small roof of lacquered armour which no other order of insects can imitate without losing its temper. About 400,000 species are described; the Coleoptera is the largest of all orders, constituting almost 40% of described arthropods and 25% of all known animal species, and the count moves while this catalogue is still warm, new species turning up in the margins of other people’s hedges. The honest estimate of the total stands between 0.9 and 2.1 million species, which is to say the lot contains, at the outside, about two million more beetles than the lot number promises, and the house has decided to treat the difference as interest.

Found in almost every habitat except the sea and the polar regions, the order interacts with its ecosystems in several ways: beetles often feed on plants and fungi, break down animal and plant debris, and eat other insects, including, in certain lots, the house’s own staff during long viewings. The consignment is offered exactly as consigned, elytra closed, tumblers in their corner, no two turning the same way, which is a condition of the order and not a flaw of this sale.

Provenance

Consigned by the Velden Customs & Lost Property office, from a tin biscuit box, lid stamped with a diagram of a beetle tumbling, recovered at the customs hall on a bench where it had been, by all accounts, patient. Held inside the tin box, at the position of lot 399,999 but not sold with it, is drawer 11 of the late druggist’s press at Am Hof 9, Klagenfurt — the tumbling flower beetle drawer, the vial of three Mordellistena dalmatica collected 1956–1959 from the linden hedge behind the apothecary, the subject of a running advertisement in the small-ads column of our friends across the border; see Column 3, The Carinthian Entomologist, 14 June, where the vial is offered at 420 pf., the drawer kept separate, and the rule stated that the person who releases a tumbler does not turn the same way either. The drawer is held in reserve inside this lot for the buyer of the tin box; the house considers this a natural inheritance, and so, we are told, does the beetle.

Estimate & Conditions

Estimate: more than the building. The lot will not be split; the house has asked and the elytra have declined. Bidders must register a paddle by 17:00 that day; bidders arriving later may still bid, but the record will say “late.” Our colleagues to the west run the same discipline over their retired packages and declare their package managers before raising a hand — see the Bergen Posten, Saturday classifieds, column 11, where the auction sat at NHH lecture hall B with a NOK 30 catalogue and a stubborn third column of wine prices. A species key will be accepted in lieu of a package manager, provided it is honest.

Copy closes at five; the beetles do not. This is the house’s standing note, set at the foot of every sitting for thirty years: the pencil stops at 17:00, the telephones stop at 17:00, the hall closes at 17:00, and the order Coleoptera, which does not read the catalogue and has never once been late, continues.

Remarks of the Valuer

Valued in person, the tin box by Mr. Färber, the elytra by nobody, because the elytra are the price, not the thing priced. A buyer should know that the lot feeds on plants and fungi, breaks down what other lots leave behind, and will, within a generation of purchase, have eaten at least one previous buyer’s curiosity. The house is not liable for interest. All else, as per the small print, which is long and honest and mostly about the weather.
