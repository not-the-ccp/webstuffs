LOT 41 To be sold without reserve, 14 March 1975, 10 a.m. sharp

The Waikiki Biltmore Hotel

2130 Kalākaua Avenue, Waikīkī, Honolulu, Hawaiʻi · nineteen stories · 640 rooms · 1955–1974

Condition Report

The Biltmore stood on the corner of Kalākaua Avenue and the beach road for nineteen years and one day. Opened in 1955 as the first high-rise hotel in Waikīkī, it rose nineteen storeys above a shoreline where, until its arrival, the tallest buildings had kept their heads below the coconut palms. The condition report is therefore of a place already in disposal: at the time of this catalogue the west facade is furred with scaffolding, the last desk bell has been weighed, and the demolition crew from Kamehameha Contractors has been given keys to eleven of the fifteen elevators. The hotel that stands for the hammer is a hotel that will not stand much longer. It is offered as the whole — structure, habit, and view — in the same parcel, and the hammer will fall once.

Interior: 640 guest rooms, the Lagoon Pool, the Tiki Bar, the ballroom which seated nine hundred, and the lobby with its twelve-foot teak front desk, where the bellboys were paid in tips and in stories. The view lot — that famous first-floor view of the whole white crescent of the beach, taken from a promontory no building had dared before — is included at no charge, as views are and always have been.

Provenance

Built by the Waikiki Biltmore Company, opened with a dinner of 1,200 at which the mayor spoke for eleven minutes and the band played “Aloha Oe” twice. Occupied continuously 1955–1974: governors and film crews, the 1967 Miss America pageant, four hurricane-season typhoon shutters, and the nightly parade of outrigger canoes past the windows of room 1901. Demolition began in the autumn; by spring the site was a level brown field, and the lot on this address — the Hyatt Regency, a taller and later cousin — now stands in the same soil. The Biltmore's own records, 78 boxes, are sold separately as Lot 42.

Exhibits Accompanying the Lot

  • Ex. 1. One brass desk bell, L-shaped, with the hotel's palm-leaf crest cast in the base; rings true.
  • Ex. 2. One key tag, room 1901, laminated, sun-faded; the highest room in the building, kept by the night auditor.
  • Ex. 3. One 16mm reel, Kalākaua Nocturne (1959), shot in the lobby and on the beach by the house cameraman, E. Poepoe.
  • Ex. 4. One photograph of the demolition, west facade, first tower down, taken by a guest who had not yet checked out.

Queries to the Auctioneer

Q.1 Is the lot sold with or without the Lagoon?

A.1 The lagoon is dry as of 1 February and goes with the land at the Hyatt Regency site. The lot is sold without water, with salt.

Q.2 What had he done before the base plate's title strip — two scenes, on the place-cards.

A.2 The “he” is Poepoe, and the base plate is the one from Ex. 3. Before the title strip rolled on the first reel he had filmed, for eleven seconds, the bellboy winding the desk bell on the night shift, and before the two place-carded scenes — the cards read LOBBY, 4:05 PM and BEACH, 4:40 PM — he had walked the whole length of the lobby in one take, past the teak desk and the palm-leaf crest, with the door closed. The place-cards are in the lot; the reel is in the lot; the eleven seconds are in the lot.

Q.3 Will you ring the bell?

A.3 Yes. The auctioneer rings the lot's bell once before the hammer and once after, as is the custom at this house for lots that ring.

Estimate and Hammer

Estimate: US$40,000–60,000. Note: lots comprising buildings of this age have sold at Malia & Co. at roughly three years of rent per story, the last such lot (a seven-storey office on Beretani) having fallen at twenty-one years of rent. Hammer: US$52,000 to the telephone in San Francisco, at 10:14 a.m., buyer's premium of ten per cent added at the desk. The bell rang once before and once after. The Biltmore, as this catalogue closes, has one tower left standing.
