Chapter 7
PRECEDENT

7-1. Purpose. This chapter prescribes the way a decision already made is to be carried into the next decision at a meeting of the Flanders Street Revenue Reading Society, and sets the rules by which the small print binds its successors. The subject is precedent: a judicial decision that serves as an authority for courts when deciding subsequent identical or similar cases. Precedent is fundamental to common law legal systems, which operate under the principle of stare decisis — “to stand by things decided” — in which past judicial decisions serve as case law to guide future rulings, thus promoting consistency and predictability in the law. It is the defining feature that sets common law systems apart from civil law systems. A reader who has finished this chapter will be able to hold the line when the same account is offered up a second time.

7-2. Definitions. The following terms are used in this chapter as follows:

a. Precedent is the earlier decision, once entered in the minutes, that governs the later, identical or similar, question.

b. Stare decisis is the standing order to stand by it: the court, the committee, or the club does not reopen what it has already ruled, for the sake of consistency and predictability.

c. Binding precedent is a decision of this society or of a higher bench, on point, which the reader must follow. The docent’s shh at Stop A on Flanders Street is binding precedent of the first order; the rule has been in continuous practice since before 1966.

d. Persuasive precedent is a decision the reader may consider but is not bound to follow — the ruling of a sister desk, a neighbour’s code, a decision handed down four thousand miles north of the arrowhead. The class at the Shaw–Hammons House, Anoka, has a three-simultaneous-shh in its own docket, reported in its Form 7C; this society treats it as persuasive, which is to say, with deep respect and no obligation.

e. Ratio decidendi is the part of the decision that did the work — the ground on which the nickel was fined.

f. Obiter dictum is what the reader said on the way out of the hearing. Obiter dicta are welcome in the margin, as in the margin of Newsletter No. 47, The Assessor’s Arrowhead, and bind no one.

7-3. Application. Precedent operates in a common law system where the case law is built by decisions, one ruling resting on the last. The civil law system, in contrast, is characterized by comprehensive codes and detailed statutes, in which the written code comes first and the decisions illustrate it. The society works in common law for its own affairs — the rules grow by decision, the way the minutes grow in the drawer marked Ledgers — but keeps a civil law temper toward the statutes it reads at the table: the revenue code, the 1966 Act, the 25 percent rehabilitation credit clause, is read as written, and a decision of the club may never amend a statute. The division of labour is the same Mr. Ellison states for rates: the law is the machinery, the politics is the load. Precedent is the record of how the machinery moved when the load changed.

7-4. Field procedure. When a question is put at a meeting, the reader proceeds as follows:

a. Search the minutes, fourth shelf, Pittock Block room, beside Dr. Seller’s old library catalogue, for a prior decision on the identical or similar question.

b. If the prior decision is on point and is the society’s own, it is binding. State the number, stand by it, and move the meeting on.

c. If the prior decision is that of a sister desk or a foreign bench, it is persuasive. Weigh it, say so in the margin, and rule fresh if the society rules fresh.

d. If no prior decision exists, the present ruling is entered in the minutes and becomes precedent for the next meeting, which is the whole machinery working as intended.

e. Distinguish the present case from the prior one only on the facts, and say where the facts differ, in the margin, in the reader’s own hand.

f. When in doubt, and the question is one of rates, stop: rates are politics, and a rate quoted from memory as if it were statute carries a fine of one nickel, as assessed on the occasion of the 1913 income tax.

Entry 12 of the minute book is transcribed above exactly as the clerk set it down: one line, two estimates fused, the cabinet labels trailing on behind the drawer the way the fish-scale shingles trail down the tower at Flanders Street. The society’s rule for the minute book is the same rule the Kitchen Column lives by — set down as received, no corrections, no commentary — and a clerk who corrects a precedent before it is dry has not corrected it; he has destroyed the evidence of how the bench first stood. The line therefore stands, fused and trailing, as the chapter’s own precedent: that some readings of the small print are authoritative exactly because they were never tidied.

CAUTION

Precedent does not stop a private owner from hanging curtains, hosting a party, or serving beets. Stare decisis binds the reader, not the house. If the docent has shhed, the shh is binding even where it appears to have no visible logic, and the correct field response is silence, not an appeal. Appeals belong in the margin.
