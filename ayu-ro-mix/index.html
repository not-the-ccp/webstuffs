Kantō Plain · Tokyo & the Edo River · February 2000

Ayu-ro mix

Tue 15 February 2000 · 09:40 · The record shop, east side of the street

The specimen is not yet out — it is due tomorrow, 16 February — but the shop already holds a stack of its dust jackets in the window, white and black, waiting like eggs. I had come for a tide chart and stayed for the case. The clerk, who keeps the chart office ledgers better than the fishery does, tells me this is the second remix album from Hamasaki Ayumi, and the first did well enough that the label has reared a second generation, the way a successful nest prompts a second clutch. I ask how many he has of this one, and he turns the stack. I do not count out loud in shops. I write it down here instead: the stack was not small, and tomorrow I will know exactly how the population behaves, because the clerk keeps a notebook of releases and says, and I quote, seven figures will make the year.

Wed 16 February 2000 · 05:55 · Bank of the Edo, at the confluence, before dawn

Went down to the river the morning of release to watch the water do what it does, which is to carry everything. The Edo here takes in a smaller arm, and the two streams meet without any ceremony at all — no marker, no line — the dyke simply ends and the water argues itself out. I had the harbour chart open at 1:25,000, and on the chart the meeting is a neat little V, but on the bank at that hour it is a slow, wide braid, grey on grey. If you go yourself and you arrive before the light, keep the chart flat in your hand, count yourself in at the confluence and down toward the entrance to the light at that scale. At 1:25,000 the river is a thread long enough to hold this whole year's music, and I have decided that is the correct scale at which to measure an album that intends to stay on the chart for a long time. It does, by the way. I will write that down properly on the last page.

Wed 16 February 2000 · 11:15 · Same shop, first sales

First sales of the day were done by eleven, and the window stack is down. The specimen: twelve tracks, the artist's own singles returned to the studio and rebuilt — slowed, sped, broken at the chorus, reassembled with the house beat that the Super Eurobeat series is named for, a beat I can now feel in my watch when I stop looking at it. The first press is black vinyl-sleeved card, jewel case at the front, and I have filed it beside my tide chart in the same drawer, which is a mistake I will correct in the spring. The clerk's notebook now shows the day's figure. I will not put a shop's day against a year's total in these notes. What I will put is this: the second generation nested well, and the year — 2000, the round year — will rank it.

Spring 2000 · Chart office, second floor, on the way to a tide table

The figures are in, and the clerk's notebook was right to the shape of it. The album is the second highest selling remix album in Japan of 2000, with 700,000 copies sold — a population I had to write down twice to believe, because the first number on my own paper came out wrong and I had to scratch it out like a miscounted gull. It is also, by the chart office's own count, Hamasaki's longest charting remix album: over 31 weeks on the chart, which is what I said on the river bank before I knew it, at 1:25,000, when the thread of the Edo looked long enough to hold it. The all-time ledger places it fourth among remix albums in Japan and seventh among remix albums worldwide, a wider range than the Edo's catchment, and I have marked the second and the seventh in pencil on the ledger so the next reader knows where the specimen lives.

For the shape of a case that holds a record — hard cover, spine, the block inside — the report on the book that a class of seven-year-olds' elders filed at Coopers’ Lane is the clearest I have: The Book — Report, Class 7B, section 3 on the press, which made a case like this cheap enough to be ordinary. And for the habit of walking a family of things along a river bank in eight counted stops, counting the flowers rather than guessing, the itinerary my companion kept beside the Secchia is the model I am copying: The Mustard Walk, with its rule that the code is counted, not guessed. My own rules for this notebook are the same in spirit: the numbers on this page are counted, not guessed, and if a number comes out wrong it is scratched out and counted again.

Late entry · Pencil, on the back of the tide chart

The specimen has outlived its winter and is holding the chart at numbers I have not had to write down since the gull year. A second remix album, by one singer, in the round year, moving 700,000 bodies' worth of sleeves from the shelf to the hand: in my experience of rivers, that is a flood that behaves. I keep the jewel case in the drawer with the tide chart and the clerk's notebook, and on the sleeve, in pencil, in the margin where a naturalist keeps the small true things, I have written the date, the number, and the rank — 16·02·2000 · 700,000 · 2nd / 4th / 7th — which is the whole of the species in one line, and I am satisfied. The companion's house keeps the same discipline for smaller counts: it catalogues the jar's brine as Lot 62 of its autumn sale — Lot 62, The Secchia Sale — and prints, for 1,342 bases, the rule I print for 700,000 copies. The rest is water, and the water keeps its own notes.

— R. Okafor, naturalist on secondment, Kantō Plain. See also the companion's notes from a different plain and a different century, on the marble country of the Green Mountains: Ethan Allen — Field Notes. Field notes answer to no office; the pencil is the signature.
