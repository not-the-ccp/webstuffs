Obituary · Entered in the Mezzanine Book, Folio 8

East River

Tidal strait, sixteen miles · also known as the Sound River · died in the small hours, 19 March 1992, at the change of the tide

Long Island City, Queens. The notice is entered by Miriam Feldstein, Clerk of the Minutes, for the Borough Committee of the 63rd Street Line, 21st Street–Queensbridge Ward, whose minutes of 3 March of this year are kept in the overhead mezzanine of the station six and a third miles south, at Minutes of a Meeting of the Borough Committee — 21st Street–Queensbridge Station.

East River, saltwater tidal estuary of New York City, and by long-usage misnomer a river though it never was one, in fact or in law, died in the small hours between one and two in the morning on the night of 19 March 1992, at a change of the tide so mild that the mezzanine clock went past it without marking the hour. It was, at the moment of passing, a waterway of sixteen miles, twenty-six of them counted in the metric measure, navigable for its entire length, and the committee heard the news only because the F train that night ran four minutes early and Dr. R. K. Whitcomb, the station master, who had been walking the platforms, came up the stairs with salt on his cap and said, it turned and did not come back.

Of the Life

The strait had connected Upper New York Bay, at its southern end, to Long Island Sound, at its northern, and had spent its life holding the boroughs of Brooklyn and Queens, on Long Island, apart from the island of Manhattan and the Bronx, on the mainland — a division it performed with the steady indifference of a line between two properties. For the greater part of its existence it answered to a truer name, the Sound River, after the sound to which it is connected, and it wore that name so long that several of its neighbours still use it in print, which the Clerk has ruled is not slander but affection. The current was its whole character: the waterway changed its direction of flow with the tide, running south one way, north the next, and was subject to strong fluctuations in its current, accentuated by its narrowness and by the variety of its depths, which are not a matter of record but of weather.

The Chairman of the committee, Harold Okafor, who had known the water only from photographs in the mezzanine office and from the M train window on the bridges, described it, on being asked, as a tide, not a temperament, and the Clerk entered those words as the committee’s tribute. Dr. Whitcomb added, from the platform evidence, that the strait kept its own little territory in the way that a room does, and that the committee, per the standing rule, had never entered it unannounced.

Of the Last Words

The last words set down in the log came to the mezzanine by radio, tuned between stations, from a studio on match day across the harbour, and the Clerk entered them in the log book word for word, as the log requires: “But Mitchell has started eleven of twelve, and the inside is sufficiently brown, cover them as close packed in small pieces; bake about 20 minutes.” The full record of that broadcast is preserved elsewhere, at the James Mitchell — Radio Play Excerpt, from which the Clerk has taken leave, without the studio’s knowledge, of the eleven of twelve.

The committee read the line as a farewell, and the reading stands in the record. Eleven of twelve was taken as the strait’s account of its own hours: it had still run in eleven of the twelve of the tidal turn, and it kept the twelfth for itself, the slack, in which no one will find it. The inside sufficiently brown was taken as the colour of the water at its banks, which the committee is satisfied is correct. Close packed in small pieces the Clerk read as the ferries, the barges, and one haunch of venison lying on the lost-property shelf wrapped in a flattened Federative Line timetable, of which the full notice is at Lost & Found Notice BC-63-LF-221 — 21st Street–Queensbridge Mezzanine Shelf. Bake about 20 minutes was ruled the length of the slack water, measured at the west end of the mezzanine where the little brass plate reading Redcliffe has hung since 1989, and which is the only thing in the committee’s charge older than the station.

Of the Burial

By resolution, the committee declined the usual burial, on the ground that a strait cannot be removed, and has entered the death instead in the register of conditions, where terminal is a condition, not a character has already been recorded and where the death now stands beside it, in the same hand, as a condition likewise. The M train will pass the waterway by day, and the F train by night and weekend, in each direction, and the committee has directed that the passing be noted by M or by F in the log, as the Clerk marks the meetings. When the Queens Bypass resumes and the mezzanine becomes an express stop, the file is to pass, with the register, to the next committee, with the instruction that the twelfth hour be left where it is.

The committee is survived by two boroughs on one side of it, two on the other, one bay, one sound, sixteen miles of navigation, and the 9:41 weekday M, which the Chairman allowed, for this year only, to stand as a monument.
