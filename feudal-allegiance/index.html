A Handbook of Courtly Usage, being a Manual for Houses and Their Guests in the Western Lands · Third Edition, revised at Minas Tirith

Of Fealty and Its Observances

IV

“A king without men is a fiddle without strings; and a man without a king is a plough without a horse. The usage binds the two, and the binding is an oath.” — Proverb of the North Road, recorded in the Grey Archives

§1. The Swearing

Fealty, as observed in the kingdoms of the West, is a debt of service sworn to a lord by a subject, and of protection owed back again. It is one of the older institutions, older than coin, and it is for this reason that the handbook treats it first among the social debts. A house that has a lord, and a lord who has men, is a house and a lord; a house that has neither is merely a roof.

The form of the oath is not to be trifled with, and no one in the last age understood this better than the captains of the Rohirrim. On 15 March in the year 3019 of the Reckoning, at the Ford of Bruinen in the glen of the Hoarwell, seven hundred and seventeen horsemen of the Mark — such as could ride — rode up from the south with the white horse-banner of Déor, and the oath of their fathers was called to mind. The debt had stood unfulfilled for nearly three centuries; the captain Éomer, speaking for them, took it up again with a single word: “Our word is not yet broken.” The usages require that a sworn debt be honoured in person, or by a representative of rank no lower than the one who first received it; the Rohirrim observed this to the letter, riding two days with little rest, and it is the standing judgment of this handbook that they observed it to the letter of the letter as well.

The observance was repaid, and repaid with interest. On 15 April, in the Pelennor Fields, the horses of the Mark came down the Hill of the Guard with the sun behind them — of which more in §3 — and the three kingdoms that held, held for the swearing made at the ford. It is the first rule of fealty: an oath kept costs less than an oath bought.

The Tally of Oaths

Keep your own column. Press the seal for each oath sworn, kept, or broken. The clerk of the house may countersign.

The column is as yet empty. This is not, in itself, a breach.

§2. The Ranks of Observance

  1. i. Answer the call in person where the call admits a rider; if the call admits no rider, answer it by one of your household whom the lord would not refuse. The rider of the Mark who answered at the Bruinen did so with a spear and no saddle cloth, and was the better for it.
  2. ii. Do not take the oath twice. One lord, one debt. The hand stretched to a second while the first is warm is a hand that will be questioned; if it is questioned, it is seldom in the swearer's favour.
  3. iii. Keep the debt in trust, not in the ledger. Fealty entered into account books curdles. The service of the Shire-folk to their own land was never entered in any ledger, which is perhaps why, when the Shire itself was called upon in the autumn of 3019, six thousand of them went without a single entry being made.
  4. iv. Let the lord's house receive you when the debt is done. He who has paid his man is a man to be sat with. This is the courtesy that is most commonly forgotten in the West, and its forgetting costs more than the debt itself.
  5. v. Part, if you must part, by release and not by flight. A fealty may be lawfully released by the lord; it may not be lawfully dropped by the subject. To drop it is the one act for which no apology has ever been framed, and this handbook declines to frame one.

§3. Of Those Who Were Never Sworn

Every institution is best taught by its absence, and the usages of fealty by the one creature of the age who was its master's servant by habit rather than by oath. The wretch of the Gladden, called by the names he had and the names he had been, served one master for five hundred and ninety-one years, and in all that time the master's hand was never stretched to him, nor his to it. He wanted the thing he wanted more than he loved the one who gave it, and on the last evening of the War he proved, at the foot of the fire-mountain, that this is what an un-sworn bond comes to: it fails in the one place a sworn one would hold.

Set beside him, the contrast the age itself drew. The little gardener of the Shire, sworn to no one but his master's door, walked the road as far as the road would carry him, and when the road would carry him no farther, he took up the burden and carried it. It is not in this handbook's style to flatter; but the reader is asked to notice that the two men of the tale who kept their word to the last step of it were, both of them, servants. The rank was not in the hand. It was in the keeping.

§4. The Stained Oath

A fealty is a document as well as a custom, and documents, like all wet things, may be stained. In the winter of 3020, when the waters of the Andúin stood high and the lower halls of the Great Library of the White City were ankle-deep, the oath rolls — the rolls on which the words of the captains were recorded, in ink, three centuries running — were lifted from their cases and carried to the upper court. Some of them came back clean. Others came back with a tide-line, and with the words of the last three years read off the parchment like a map of nothing.

The conservator's note for the treatment survives in the margin of the restoration book, and this handbook sets it down uncorrected, because the usage is that a restorer's hand stand as written. The note reads:

The reagent strips recent loans from the centre of the column.

Let the reader weigh that for himself. The loans are the last items entered against the rolls — the oaths most lately sworn, and therefore, by the nature of the reagent, the first to go; the column is the central pillar of the court in which the rolls were laid, and the tide-line was found only at its foot. The etiquette of the matter is simple, and is the same as the rest of this chapter: what is borrowed is to be returned, and what is recorded is to be kept. A debt recorded nowhere is a debt that can be denied anywhere, and a house that keeps its oath rolls dry keeps, for three hundred years, the one thing no army has ever taken back.

§5. The Parting on Good Terms

The last usage, and the least practised, is the release. When the War was done and the White City had its king again — a king of the old line, thirty-fourth in the reckoning from the northern house, called by the name of the stars as well as the name of the earth — the fealties of the age were not broken, but they were recast: the old debts were taken up into the new house, and every lord of the West found himself, by an arithmetic nobody objected to, the steward's guest rather than the steward's subject.

It is the model of the matter. Fealty well observed does not end in a quarrel; it ends in a door left open. The handbook's last word on the subject is the last word of the age on the subject, and it belongs to the man who rode out of the city to ride into the West: he had served his debt, and the debt had served him, and both of them were done.
