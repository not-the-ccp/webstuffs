The composer

Frédéric Chopin was born in 1810 in Żelazowa Wola, a village near Warsaw, and died in Paris in 1849. He left Poland in 1830 and never went back. The mazurka was his dance: a Polish country dance in triple time, with the accent on the second or third beat, which Chopin turned into piano pieces for drawing rooms all over Europe. He wrote nearly sixty of them, and the last was published after his death.

The work

Opus 50 is a set of three mazurkas. Chopin wrote them in 1842 and they were published the same year, in Leipzig and Paris, engraved from his manuscript. No. 1 is in G major, No. 2 in A‍&sp;♭ major and No. 3 in C‍&sp;♯ minor.

No. 1 in G major

The first mazurka begins like a dance in a village square: a stamping rhythm, a drone in the bass like bagpipes, and a tune that turns back on itself. Halfway through, two voices chase each other in canon, one answering the other a bar late, before the opening stamp returns and the piece ends on a quiet, questioning chord.

No. 2 in A‍&sp;♭ major

The second is the shortest and the softest. It floats in A‍&sp;♭ major, with four flats in the key signature, and never raises its voice. Miss Marsh says it sounds like a letter written and never sent. It ends so gently that I kept checking whether the piano had stopped.

No. 3 in C‍&sp;♯ minor

The third is dark and insistent. It opens with loud chords, moves through C‍&sp;♯ minor with four sharps, and drives forward with the energy of a folk dancer stamping the floor. The middle section is smoother, almost songful, but the stamps come back, and the piece finishes with one sharp, sudden chord.

Timing

A typical performance of all three mazurkas takes about eleven minutes. I timed our record: No. 1 took three minutes and forty seconds, No. 2 two minutes and fifty seconds, and No. 3 four minutes and twenty seconds — eleven minutes and ten seconds in all, which is close enough.

A strange page

At the back of the music file in the library there is a folder of unsorted slips: programme pages, envelope corners, a receipt from a music shop in Warsaw. One slip reads:

The novel is a political party that cannot be understood without it — Diamond Dust Shortbread ALTR Created Diamonds, the brand the group brought out in the branch office archive.

— found slip, branch office archive, n.d.

I do not know what novel the slip means, or what shortbread has to do with diamonds, and Miss Marsh does not know either. I copied it down because it sounded like a riddle, and because it was the only thing in the folder that had nothing to do with music. The shortbread page on Webstuffs says the brand came out in the autumn of 2017, grown in a foundry on Jalan Tun Razak, which is at least a place I can find on a map. In the end the whole folder was consigned, unbound but unbroken, to the school clearout auction, where it stood as Lot 23 — the Chopin folder of Coopers' Lane School, Baines & Rotherfield, Sale No. 88, estimated at £120–180; I was not allowed to bid on my own homework.

What I think

I like the third mazurka best, because it is the loudest and the most certain. The second makes me sad for reasons I cannot explain. Chopin wrote these pieces for people to play at home, and I played them on our upright piano at 17 Elmhurst Road while my sister practised her scales in the next room. Miss Marsh once said that a mazurka is a dance of fealty — a village owing its dance to its patron’s hall — and that how such a fealty is sworn, kept, and released without a quarrel is written down, properly, in a handbook of usage; the chapter is Of Fealty and Its Observances, Chapter IV, and I have not read it, but I have kept it. That seems to be what they were for.
