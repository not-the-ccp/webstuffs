The Base Hit

A term report on baseball statistics — kept beside the Curtright Circuit and the Missouri by Foot notebook

ByCarla M. Villafuerte, Form 3B
DateTuesday, 6 May 1947
Kept withThe Curtright Circuit · Missouri by Foot

This report is about the hit. In baseball statistics a hit is denoted by the letter H. It is also called a base hit. A hit is credited to a batter when the batter safely reaches or passes first base after hitting the ball into fair territory with neither the benefit of an error nor a fielder's choice. That is the rule, and I wrote it down word for word because the rule is the whole of the exercise.

What the scorers count

A hit is one of four things. A single puts the batter on first base. A double puts him on second. A triple puts him on third. A home run circles all the bases and scores. The scorekeeper counts total bases — one for a single, two for a double, three for a triple, four for a home run — but the column headed H counts each of them only once.

What the rule does not count

What the rule does not count matters as much as what it does. If the fielder drops the ball, throws wild, or muffs the catch, that is an error, not a hit. If the fielder puts another runner out and the batter reaches first because of it, that is a fielder's choice, not a hit. Neither one earns an H in the box score. The batter may stand on first base all the same, but the ledger says nothing beside his name.

Batting average

The batting average divides hits by at-bats: H divided by AB. If a batter finishes the season with eighty-seven hits in three hundred and ten at-bats, his average is .281, read as "two-eighty-one." Three decimals, no more. A player who hits safely three times out of ten is doing well. A player who does it four times out of ten is in the ledger for keeps.

Left field, 1943 to 1946

This report began in the left-field corner at Comiskey Park, where Guy Paxton Curtright kept his station for the Chicago White Sox across the war years — 1943, 1944, 1945 and 1946 — batting and throwing right-handed, five feet eleven inches tall and 180 pounds, the same boy born on 18 October 1912 in Holliday, Missouri, where the post office knew him by his middle name. An outfielder's job in that corner is to let hits fall or keep them in, and the long throw back over the shoulder up the line counts for nothing unless the batter is out. Curtright's corner kept the ball from the stands for four seasons; the league's ledger gives him a short page. A bigger-hitting outfielder would have needed two pages, with a second margin for the errors. I keep both pages folded inside this report.

The walking tour

I wrote the middle of this report on the van between Rocheport and Jefferson City, during Mr. Okafor's walking tour of Missouri, which keeps company with the same Form 3B shelf as the term report on New Madrid Township. The day's notebook line reads exactly this way:

Day 4 Tuesday, 08:00–15:30 · 4.0 miles Jefferson City (30 miles).

Mr. Okafor's rule is the scorer's rule: keep the strange line word for word, never tidy it up. The capitol dome finished at 238 feet in 1917, and the Missouri River bluff walk carried the keelboats west. Between the governor's garden and the Supreme Court building I counted hits instead of miles: how many batters reached first safely, and how many got there only because someone else made a mistake. That difference — error or hit, fielder's choice or base hit — is nearly the whole of the game.

Conclusion

So a hit is not merely reaching first. It is reaching first by the batter's own doing, with the ball fair in play, and no one else's mistake to thank for it. From Holliday to Comiskey Park, and from the Katy Trail to the capitol steps at Jefferson City, the ledger keeps the same column. H stands for hit, and the hit stands alone.
