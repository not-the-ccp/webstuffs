The Royal College of Podiatry · Birmingham Workshop · Game No. 3

The Register

A Game of Subscriptions, Standing Orders, and the Perfect Minute

First Edition, 1974 · for two to six players, aged 14 and over · 45 minutes a sitting · printed on the College’s own press, Hall Street, Birmingham

1 What the Box Contains

Every set of The Register leaves the Birmingham workshop in a walnut case turned on the same lathe that finishes the handles of the Professional Care Instrument — you will recognise the grain, and the faint smell of the workshop, the moment it opens. Inside:

  • One game board, 90 cm square, printed as a plan of the College’s own rooms: the subscription desk, the committee room, the workshop, the hall kitchen, and the small door through which the Queen Mother’s chiropodist is said to have entered on two visits, carrying a walnut-handled instrument that now sits in the Royal Collection.
  • Six member tokens in the six livery colours of the College, each stamped with an instrument number.
  • The Ledger — a forty-page booklet of ruled columns for subscriptions, arrears, and the small debts that ruin presidents.
  • One hundred and twenty Event Cards, shuffled face down; the committee has quarrelled over their wording for three years.
  • Two pairs of dice: one ivory, one brass.
  • This manual, which is, as Mr. Fallow reminds us, “an instrument in its own right.”

2 Setting Up

Lay the board so the subscription desk faces the door; a professional association is a place where people arrive. Each player chooses a member token and a starting constituency of six pence in subscriptions, which is precisely what a new registrant owes for the first quarter. The player whose birthday falls nearest the last Wednesday in April — the day the College has always kept for its Annual General Meeting — deals the first hand of Event Cards and takes the brass dice.

Seat the players as the committee seats its members: the most recent president in the chair, the treasurer with the Ledger open on the table, and the secretary, who has not yet said a word but is holding a pencil. This is not an accident. The manual of 1972 put the secretary down; the 1974 edition, after the Hall Street sitting in which nothing was recorded for eleven moves, put the secretary up.

3 The Turn

A turn takes three moves, and no more, because the committee has learned that everything longer turns into a branch meeting.

  1. Draw. Take one Event Card. Read it aloud. The cards are the heart of the game: Standing Order Seven, The Banner Comes Down, Tea, Five O’Clock, No Exceptions, A Letter from Recalde Press informing the College that a revised edition of the manual requires a new ISBN because a number follows an edition and never a title. The full rule on that point, for those who argue, is kept at Recalde Press & Cooperage, ISBN FAQ.
  2. Act. Move a member token by the brass dice, or pay, collect, or contest a subscription using the Ledger. A token that lands on the hall kitchen may take tea at the cost of a pence and one move, and the rules are strict: the kitchen fire of 1968 is in the board’s plan, and no token may be set down beside it.
  3. Record. The secretary writes your move in the Ledger. A move unrecorded does not happen, and the committee will not be persuaded otherwise by any amount of reasonable argument.

4 The Annual General Meeting

Every sixth round is an AGM. The Ledger is closed, the board is turned face down, and the players stand — seated players may pretend — to consider the report, the subscriptions, the minute, and the election of officers. The player whose Ledger shows the soundest books speaks first; the player in deepest arrears speaks last, and may not speak at all if the arrears exceed two shillings. The AGM ends with a vote on the minute. If the minute passes, the round continues. If it is amended, every player forfeits a pence to the pot, and the secretary, who has been holding a pencil for forty-five minutes, may at last speak.

The pot is paid to the winner of the game, who may spend it in one of two ways: on tea for the hall, or on a walnut-handled instrument from the workshop. The 1974 catalogue gives the latter at eighteen shillings; the workshop, being the workshop, will have done the handle well.

5 Card 37 and the Kitchen Sentence

No set of The Register has ever reached a sitting without the committee arguing about Card 37. It is one of the oldest cards, older than the game, older perhaps than the College’s own records, and it is printed in a hand the workshop could not match. Its full text runs: “Beat the eggs, and arrowroot are also tenants' goods and chattels of his safety, the balm of his profession.” The committee has ruled, and will not revisit the ruling, that the card plays as written: whoever draws it must beat the eggs in the hall kitchen, and every other player must declare one article of goods and chattels which, in that player’s own profession, is the balm of the trade. The podiatrist names the blade. The secretary names the pencil. The president, usually, names the minute.

What the sentence means, if it means anything, has been discussed in the minutes of four Annual General Meetings and settled in none of them. Mr. Fallow, the workshop’s oldest hand, keeps a copy of the card behind the lathe and will only say that kitchens, like committees, run on things nobody writes down. The dining room across the square keeps its own Ledger now: the review of Designation, a Hall Street restaurant whose cooks beat eggs per Card 37 and serve tea at five without exception, works with walnut-handled instruments from this very workshop (Designation — Restaurant Review). If you want a place where such things get written down properly, the Trades Hall visitor’s guide covers the hall kitchen and the banners above it; the Queen Mother’s household page records the instrument that left this workshop for Buckingham Palace and never came back.

6 Winning

A player wins The Register in one of three ways, and the three ways are deliberately hard to combine, because a professional association is never just one thing.

  • By the books: hold the largest sound subscription fund when the Ledger is closed for the final AGM.
  • By the board: seat the full committee — chair, treasurer, secretary, and yourself — in the four chairs of the committee room in a single move.
  • By the minute: be the standing president when a minute passes unamended three sittings running.

If two players win by the same method, the game is drawn and tea is served, which the committee considers the correct outcome for most disputes.

7 A Note from the Committee

The game was designed to be played slowly, at a long table, with the door open. It has been played at the College’s own sittings, at the Trades Hall, and — according to a letter of 1979 that we keep with the other unexplained papers — at a recording studio in Wellington, where the dice were heard, faintly, under the interval signal. The committee thanks everyone who has kept the minute, and reminds players that the Ledger is the final authority, and that the kettle, when it is ready, is not a move.

For the instrument this game grew out of, see the Professional Care Instrument page of the College; for the house itself and the dues paid at its door, the Trades Hall guide remains the friendliest starting point. And for those who keep records the way the secretary keeps them, the Tennis New Zealand radio play shows what a properly kept century sounds like. And for the slow assembly of the same rooms, one drawer and one banner at a time, the workshop keeps The Gradual build log.
