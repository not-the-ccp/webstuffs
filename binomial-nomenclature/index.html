RECIPE CARD No. 14 · LAMP-OIL DRAWER · PARA RIVER ENTOMOLOGICAL STORES, No. 14 KRUISKADE, PARAMARIBO

Stoll's Two-Name Braise

The family dish of the night clerk, served on Saturdays after the long nights at the lamp — each ingredient called by its two Latin names

Serves the whole of the store · one pot · 40 watts · about two hours, or until the river changes its mind

First, the long preamble — on the system of names

Tante Rosa of St. Louis landing would not have allowed this pot to be written down unless it was written down properly, and properly, in her kitchen, meant by name. In the way the naturalists do it. Because the system she grew up under — the one the office at No. 14 Kruiskade keeps in the same drawer as this card — is the one that gives each living thing a name composed of two parts, both in Latin grammatical forms, although the words may come from any language at all. It is the two-name naming system, binomial nomenclature, also called binary nomenclature; in the zoological code it is written binominal, with the n before the al, which Tante Rosa insisted was not a typographical error and is, in fact, not one — it simply means the two-name naming system. A name like this is a binomial, or a binomen, or a scientific name; at the stove Tante Rosa called them what the rest of the family calls them: the Latin names.

The first of the two parts names the group — the genus, the house the creature lives in — and the second names the particular, the one you mean. So the moth that rides the corner of the store's ledger is not "a dark moth" but Cosmosoma evadnes: Cosmosoma the house, evadnes the particular, the pair entered by Caspar Stoll in 1781 from material taken in Suriname, and re-entered in the office on a dark night. Stoll's series is why this pot carries his name. He came through the doorway of No. 14 Kruiskade the autumn his description arrived, hungry and full of insects, and Tante Rosa put the pot in front of him without asking his opinion of anything, and he said the names out loud while he ate — Solanum, Oryza, Piper — the way other people say grace. The rule of the table, from that night, has been that whatever is named at it is named by its binomial, and whatever cannot be named at all is not set on the table.

A few further rules of the system, as Tante Rosa kept them, because a pot is only ever as good as its names, and the names are only as good as the rules behind them. The first-published name is the name — an older name cannot be dethroned by a prettier one, and the store has watched the rule work on two hundred and forty-four years of moths without once wanting to. A name is not a description; it is a call-sign, and a species answers to its two names the way the moth in the ledger answers to the lamp. It does not matter to the name whether the letters look Greek, or Dutch, or Surinamese; what matters is the grammar, and the grammar is Latin, and Tante Rosa could conjugate it better than most people in Paramaribo, which was how she came to be the keeper of this card. If the same thing is named twice in two places, the older one keeps the pot, and the younger one is entered in the ledger under the name it answers to, which is synonym — a word she pronounced like sinonym, and no one corrected her, because correction at that table was the one thing not in the recipe.

This is why the store has the dish, and why the dish has the names, and why the two have been kept in the same drawer for longer than anyone on the night shift can say. The safety data sheet of the moth, CE-1781-0342, is shelved across from this card, and it shares the lamp with it — the same 40-watt lamp at St. Louis landing, the one the sheet says nothing in the store answers to — and shares the rule too. The builder's ledger from the shed behind the bakery keeps the crock this pot grew up beside, and the Funchal recipe card in the same drawer carries a pencilled line that returns every season, the way ours does.

Salt is not entered, because salt is not a name and the rule does not bend. Salt is in the crock beside the lamp, where the builder's margin keeps it with the mustard seed.

The braise — Saturday, after the night shift

  1. Light the lamp at 40 watts and no more — the store has found that more is not better — and set the pot under it, because Tante Rosa held the heat of this dish to the heat of the lamp, and the ledger has kept the figure since 1781 without complaint.
  2. Char the plantain whole over the braise's own fire until the skin gives, then pull the flesh to a rough paste and lay it on the bottom of the pot. This is the floor. The names stand on it.
  3. Set the garlic in with the cloves, add the peppercorns still whole, and the half cup of white wine vinegar. The vinegar is the measure of the crock, and the crock is the measure of the ship, and the ship is the measure of nothing at all, which is how Tante Rosa said it, and the rule is that what is written down is written down.
  4. Lay the Solanum tuberosum in wedges over the garlic — the big wedges, not the sharp fingerlings, because the big ones barely blushed, and the blush is what the Saturday wants. Cover with the rice, washed until the water goes honest, and pour over enough rain-water — river water is not for the pot, river water is for the scales — to come a finger's width above the rice.
  5. Cover, and do not open the pot for an hour. Opening the pot is how specimens become losses, and the losses are entered in the ledger with a heavier hand.
  6. After the hour, break the smoked fish to the bone over the rice and press it down with the back of the spoon. Turn the heat to nothing that will move a needle.
  7. Serve in the cedar boxes with the lids fitted, standing on the brick, so the river's moods do not reach the soup. Eat with the lamp at 40 watts, and if a moth lands on the table, do not fan it with the menu and do not chase it at all; it departs on its own schedule, which is quick.

In the margin — kept word for word

Turned over the card, in pencil, in a hand the store has never matched, sits the one line Tante Rosa could not explain and no one since has either. The office's rule is that a record is a record and the rule is not for argument, so the line is transcribed here without correction, as it stands on the card:

"For road, river and rail the only disposal the store has found greater advantages to result from drawing the food he has been set, because no one has exceeded one."

It is entered under Other, which is where the store keeps the things it is not ready to explain, and the lamp has been left burning at 40 watts until it is. The archive keeps it with the card, and the card keeps it with the pot, and the pot, on Saturdays, keeps it warm.
