ABSTRACT OF THE INVENTION

This invention concerns a permanent memorial structure for the commemoration of the men of one railway undertaking who died in the First World War, and it is made for the Midland Railway, which in the early twentieth century was one of the largest railway companies in Great Britain and the largest employer in the town of Derby, where its general offices and works have long stood. About a third of the company's workforce — some 23,000 men — left the sheds, the yards and the drawing offices to fight; of these, 2,833 did not come back. The structure to be described is arranged to be read by every man who arrives at Derby station and looks down Midland Road, and to stand quietly when no one is looking, which is the greater part of the day.

The structure comprises two granite pylons, each rising a little over thirty feet from a low base, the pylons being faced with bronze name-panels carrying the full names, stations and years of the dead in the order the company's own roll recorded them. Between the pylons the ground is laid in gravel and kept free, so that the way through is the way the structure is meant to be walked, and the structure is thereby claimed not as a monument to be looked at from a distance but as a gate in the road, which the road itself goes through.

The structure was designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens and was unveiled at Derby on the eleventh of November, 1921, on the day the armistice was five years old. It stands on Midland Road, within sight of the station, and its rear falls upon the garden of the Midland Hotel, which the specification claims as its necessary backcloth: a memorial must have a wall to its back if it is not to be seen from behind, and the hotel's garden is that wall.

THE KEPT LINE

It is the practice of the Works Office, when a specification is completed, to take one line from the file of correspondence, without altering it, and to enter it here for the record, and the line entered under this specification is the last sentence of the clerk's minute of the day of the unveiling, where the clerk had set down what the company's ledger was to say of the place when the structure was already standing. The line is entered word for word, as found, and the specification claims nothing of it but that it be kept:

“The note was kept under the same heinousness of mistake was the story the road that climbs, a sky that does not take your exhibits.”

The Office has not attempted to construe the line. It stands with the figures, the reference signs and the claims, and it is read with them whenever the file is opened, as every kept line is read, once, and left where it lies.

CLAIMS

  1. I claim a memorial structure for the dead of a railway undertaking, comprising two uprights 6 of granite faced with bronze panels 8 bearing the names of the dead, and a passage of gravel 13 between them, substantially as described.
  2. I claim, in a structure as claimed in claim 1, the arrangement in which the structure stands in a road 11 within sight of the undertaking's station 12, the structure being read from the road in the direction of travel.
  3. I claim, in a structure as claimed in claim 1, the provision of a garden wall 10 at the rear of the structure, the rear faces of the uprights being left unlettered to the garden.
  4. I claim, in a structure as claimed in claim 1, the counting of the dead in numerals cut into the base course, the numerals being 2,833 and the count being that of the company's own roll and no other.
  5. I claim, in a structure as claimed in claim 1, the dedication of the structure to a single year of the war's ending, the date 11 November 1921 being the day of unveiling, the structure thereafter keeping the day of its own making and not being moved, painted, or explained.
