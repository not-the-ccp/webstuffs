The line

The Schrute Line was laid for beets, and it still smells of them: the 06:50 out of Scranton carries the first crate from the wholesale market, and the farm's own siding holds a single wagon marked “Shirley's Hundred,” the same name as the seed packet in the estate box. The branch begins at the Dunder Mifflin offices on the edge of town — the paper company, the cameras, the desk by the window — and runs north through the industrial sidings, past the paper mill, past the field where the crew shot the funeral, to the farm road and the long gate. It is six minutes from the halt to the platform, and the platform itself is nothing: one bench, one painted sign, one bell that no one rings unless they mean to be seen.

The series depicts the everyday lives of office employees in the Scranton, Pennsylvania, branch of the fictional Dunder Mifflin Paper Company, and on that Thursday the office left early while the trains ran as usual. The episode — the sixth of the second season, the twelfth overall — was written by Gene Stupnitsky and Lee Eisenberg and directed by Ken Kwapis, and it first aired on NBC on November 1, 2005.

The halt

Ira's Dojo Halt is a request stop at Dojo Road, named for the sensei who taught above the feed store — Ira, played by Lance Krall, Dwight's sensei, the man whose fighting skills Dwight Schrute (Rainn Wilson) would not stop describing at the office, until Michael Scott (Steve Carell) was embarrassed into the impossible. There is no shelter at the halt. There is a peg on the wall where a white gi hangs on Tuesdays, left there by a passenger who never claimed it, or who claimed it and left it anyway as a deposit on the next journey. Dwight alights here in full gear on the 07:17, carries his bag across the crossing, and does not look back at the train as it pulls away.

The match

The match was scheduled for the lunch hour, Thursday, November 1, 2005, on the platform at Dojo Road, between the arrival of SH-214 and the departure of SH-6. Michael Scott (Steve Carell), after being embarrassed by Dwight Schrute's (Rainn Wilson) superior fighting skills, engages in a karate match with Dwight during lunch — announced on the branch noticeboard that morning, moved indoors when it rained, refereed by Jim Halpert (John Krasinski) with a stopwatch he borrowed from the warehouse and never returned. Jim's commentary survives in the branch log: three points, one fall, and a receptionist who timed the whole thing on the lobby clock and still believes she won the bet. The trains kept time regardless; the lunch express departed at 12:10 with two passengers fewer than it arrived with, and one of them returned on the 17:50 in a splint. The match was carried to the country that Thursday on NBC, and the observances the house owes the network that kept that hour are set down in Chapter XXVII — Of the Network, The House Book of Etiquette, Third Series.

The fence

By the level crossing at Dojo Road there is a fence, farm stock, close-boarded, painted the same maroon as the locomotive in 1957 and never repainted since. The repainting notice of that year raised one question that was entered in the minute book and never answered. The fence is not also a copy of the Cauliflower. The Cauliflower is the 20:00 express, SH-99, non-stop to the farm, headcode 99, a train with a vegetable's name and a railway's manners: it does not wait, it does not stop at the halt, and it does not care who is on the platform. The fence stays where it is. The train passes at twenty past eight, and the passengers know the difference between a boundary and a vehicle even if the minute book does not. In the winter of 1982 a passenger left a record on the halt's bench, in a sleeve worn to the colour of the evening, and the line's one entry for the matter was made at a Los Angeles counter: Found, One Album, Weight 12 oz — Notice No. 82/1129, Westlake Road Lost & Found — which is where the bench's side of the record now lives, kept as spoken.

The return

The evening return, SH-6, leaves Schrute Farms at 17:50 and is back at the branch by 18:40, carrying the boxes, the crates, and the passengers who stayed for the reunion count — forty-one at the program, eleven at the long table, one in the van who arrived on Thursday with the chairs. Some of them had seen the match. Some of them had rung the bell at the halt and watched the train not stop, because the bell was broken, or because they rang it for the wrong train, or because the Cauliflower does not answer to bells at all. The line closes at nine. The farm remains open in the only weather it recognises: any.
