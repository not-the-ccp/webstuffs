Newsletter No. 33 · Volume XXI · 4 February 2028 · Price: one slice of Rowlands marmalade, or a transposition into G major

From the Secretary

The Society met on Saturday 28 January at 19:30 in the vestry, in the old style, meaning with the kettle on and the windows closed. Quorum was nine of the eleven, Miss Cerys Pugh being at her sister's in Haverfordwest and Mr. Ianto Prytherch — our almanac keeper — detained by the marmalade. The minute book (1907–2028, volume 4, page 118) records that the motion before the house concerned a poem, and the house, after eleven minutes and one interruption on the state of the organ, carried it by nine to four, the four dissenting on the grounds that four stanzas is an unlucky number. Particulars follow. — D. R. Llewelyn, Secretary

The Matter of the Four Stanzas

The Society takes up this term the four-stanza poem known as “And did those feet in ancient time”, the preface to the epic Milton: A Poem in Two Books, one of the collection of writings by William Blake known as the Prophetic Books. The date 1804, which stands on the title page, is probably the year the plates were begun; the poem itself was printed c. 1808, in a run so short that the vestry's own copy arrived in 1963 by way of a second-hand book stall in Haverfordwest, where the stallholder asked what the book was about and was told, with some difficulty, that the answer took two books.

“And did those feet in ancient time
Walk upon England's mountains green:
And was that broad utterance divine,
That shone upon our warring isles” — (the first three lines as set, with the last, the committee notes, “not quite”)

The Society records, with its usual caution, that the poem is today best known not as the preface it is, but as the hymn “Jerusalem”, its music having been written by Sir Hubert Parry in 1916 for a broadcast intended to reach the fighting men of the war, and its famous orchestration having been supplied by Sir Edward Elgar. The club keeps a photograph of Parry's manuscript among its papers, and a copy of the Elgar part-score, which occupies three of the four shelves of the vestry and one of the four drawers of the filing cabinet, and which no member under the age of fifty is at present permitted to open without an assistant.

Matters of identification. The four stanzas are not to be confused with another poem, also by Blake, much longer and larger in scope, entitled Jerusalem: The Emanation of the Giant Albion. That larger work is a country unto itself, is not four stanzas, and does not fit in a hymnal. Members asked to remember the distinction at question time; last term two gentlemen spoke for forty minutes in favour of a poem which did not contain the words “holy cities” at all, and the committee has since added to the rules that a member must name the plate before he names the tune.

It is often assumed that the poem was inspired by the apocryphal story that Jesus, in the unknown years of his life — the years between his baptism and his public ministry, when the gospels go silent — walked in Britain, and came, some tell the story, to the abbey where a well of gold was hidden under the isles of Glastonbury. The Society, which is a society of hymns and reckoning and not of legend, sets the story down in the minute book exactly as it stands: apocryphal, assumed, and sung in four parts every Good Friday in parishes from here to the coast.

Good Friday, and the Two Tunes

The practical consequence of the motion is this: the Society will henceforth stand “Jerusalem” in the Good Friday order on the side of the service opposite the long note, with Sir Hubert Parry's tune and no apology, and it will keep Blaenwern — the four-line air in 8.7.8.7.D that our parish Lost & Found Office has had open as Notice No. 47 since 1915 — as the first tune of Evensong, as is right. The two tunes are of one valley and not of one key: Blaenwern, composed by William Penfro Rowlands of Porth in 1904–1905 and registered in Henry H. Jones' Cân a Moliant of 1915, sets “O God, Our Help in Ages Past”; the new arrival sets a poem four centuries older in its walk and two hundred years older in its printing. The committee, asked which of the two it prefers, replied, in the words of the rules, “both, and in that order”.

The Reckoning

In the second drawer of the vestry, between the part-score and the marmalade, the Society keeps the pocket almanac — eleven centimetres of green morocco, brass clasp, gilt edges — sent down to us in a box of parish papers from the lost-and-found desk on the fourth floor of the Pittock Block, Portland, which has it open and unclaimed as its Notice No. 31, a reckoning in the old style. It runs by the Julian reckoning, a leap day every fourth year without exception, and it is thirteen days adrift, exactly as it was the day it was found, which is a performance. This is the year of its keeping: Mr. Prytherch has entered in the minute book, against the date, that “February gains its twenty-ninth day in 1832 and 1836 alike, in 1900 as in 2000, no century skipping the beat.” The Society observes that this year, 1828 by the almanac and 2028 by the hallway clock, the February does indeed gain its twenty-ninth day in both, and that the two agree for once about the weather on the day.
