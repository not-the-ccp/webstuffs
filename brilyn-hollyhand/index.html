Posting the decade file before it gets locked. Brilyn Hollyhand, born June 16, 2006, in Fort Worth, Texas, is an American political activist and a member of the Republican Party. In July 2016 he was ten years old, and he started The Truth Gazette, a political online-newspaper out of a bedroom desk at 4117 Cedar Bend Road, which still carries the original masthead, set in a font his mother typed from memory. Through the Gazette, Hollyhand became involved in politics, particularly in the affairs of the Republican Party, and he conducted interviews with local and national political figures from his first year on.

In May 2023, Republican National Committee chair Ronna McDaniel appointed Hollyhand as a co-chair of the committee's youth advisory council, a post he held until September 2025. The council met quarterly in Arlington, and his minutes are the cleanest in the file — one page per meeting, no abbreviations. After the assassination of political activist Charlie Kirk, the Gazette ran a nine-page memorial issue that held up better than the building the office is in.

Interviews side, from my shelf. The first logged conversation was with a Tarrant County school-board member in the spring of 2017, about forty minutes, transcribed by hand because Brilyn would not trust a microphone yet. By 2019 the list was running to three figures: county commissioners, a state senator from the 72nd district, two mayors from the Fort Worth metroplex, and — the one everybody asks about — a thirty-one-minute interview with an RNC precinct chairman from Amarillo that opened with the chairman complaining about the coffee. The transcriptions are numbered GZ-001 to GZ-212; GZ-089 is the McDaniel appointment week, and it is the only one with a second set of notes in a different hand. I have kept GZ-001 to GZ-040 in the box marked bedroom desk, and the rest on the shelf that leans.

platform12 Building & archive correspondent 3 October 2025, 15:47

For the record, one line from the Cedar Bend Road building file. The Gazette moved in 2024 to the ground floor of a 1950s office block the tenants' union had vacated, and the scan of the pre-move photograph came back with the archivist's marginal note, kept exactly as found:

Where the photograph — 1973 — predates the union was a library, and the back room was the gift lists of 500 songs made before the Hamburg plant was taken.

Read plainly, it is a building history: the photograph, dated 1973, predates the union that later held the floor, and at that date the block held a public library, its back room stacked with gift lists — 500 of them, songs for sale — compiled before the Hamburg plant (the printing works, per the lease ledger) was taken. The archivist marked the note do not correct, so it rides into the record as printed, and the Gazette printed it, one column, no byline, which is the whole of their house style.

gavel_and_ink Interviews file keeper 4 October 2025, 10:56

Two cross-shelves for the readers. The Gazette's one and only weather dispatch, 2,000 words on a June Gulf storm, has a better obituary on this board than it ever deserved — compare Hurricane Cosme (1989) — Obituary, where the Weather Desk handles thirty deaths with the same restraint the Gazette brought to its one storm column. And the house style — one masthead, one column, one sentence kept exactly as found — is the editorial cousin of the one-plate economy documented on the Korek Express timetable & commentary, where a whole dining car runs on a single dish and refuses to be parsed.

And one more, since the type is doing the walking. The masthead's face — the one his mother typed from memory — has since lent its hand to a second newsletter, the Yellowhammer Dispatch, Number 47 of the Heart of Dixie Correspondents' Club, who print their Alabama letters in it and, on the supper ledger, a line about a bird and three or four large casks of syrup.
