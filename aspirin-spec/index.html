Abstract of the Disclosure

The present invention relates to acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), the acetylated derivative of salicylic acid, and to a compressed tablet composition containing it, together with a method of use for the reduction of pain, fever, and inflammation, and for the prevention of thromboembolic events in persons considered to be at high risk of such events. Salicylic acid, obtained from the bark of the willow (Salix) and from the flower tops of meadowsweet (Spiraea ulmaria), has long been used in the febrile chamber, but the free acid irritates the gastric mucous membrane and the patient's account is often one of an upset stomach. The present invention acetylates the phenolic hydroxyl of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride under controlled heat, yielding a stable, crystalline acetylsalicylate that is kinder to the lining of the stomach and that, in addition to its analgesic and antipyretic operation, suppresses the normal functioning of the platelets by acetylating cyclooxygenase and reducing thromboxane. Effects for pain or fever typically begin within thirty minutes of administration. A low dose taken each day reduces the chance of heart attack, ischaemic stroke, and blood clots.

Background of the Invention

The art is old. The Reverend Edward Stone, of Chipping Norton in Oxfordshire, reported in 1763 that the bark of the willow, dried and powdered, dispelled agues; Raffaele Piria of Salerno isolated salicylic acid in 1838; Charles Frédéric Gerhardt of Strasbourg first acetylated it in 1853, though his preparation was impure and unstable and was never put to use. The problem the present invention addresses is twofold: to produce acetylsalicylic acid in a pure and stable state, and to deliver it in a measured dose that a household may keep on a shelf and take without a physician's visit. Prior preparations of the salicylates relieved the fever but burned the throat and the stomach alike, and the art had no answer to the dose.

On 10 August 1897 the inventor, working in the laboratory of Farbenfabriken vorm. Friedr. Bayer & Co. at Elberfeld, acetylated salicylic acid with acetic anhydride and a trace of sulfuric acid, kept the mixture below seventy degrees Celsius, and isolated the product by precipitation into cold water, filtration, and recrystallisation from water, yielding needle-shaped crystals melting at about one hundred and thirty-five degrees Celsius. A name was required: “Aspirin”, from the acetyl group (—a-) and from Spiraea, the meadowsweet in which the salicylates occur, with the usual medicinal ending (—in). The name is claimed as a trade designation and not as part of the invention.

Description of the Preferred Embodiment

Composition. Each tablet comprises acetylsalicylic acid, five hundred milligrams, with starch as binder, talc as lubricant, and a trace of starch paste; optionally an enteric coating of cellulose acetate passes the tablet unchanged through the stomach so that the acid is released in the intestine, for patients in whom an upset stomach is a recurring complaint.

Manufacture. Salicylic acid is charged to a glazed earthenware vessel with acetic anhydride and a catalyst, heated to between fifty and sixty degrees Celsius until acetylation is complete, poured into cold water, and the precipitated acid is filtered, washed, and dried at forty degrees. The dried acid is blended, granulated, and compressed on a rotary press at eight tons pressure into tablets of five hundred milligrams, then counted into amber glass bottles of one hundred and sealed with a cork liner; the lid is stamped with the batch number and the date.

Method of use. For pain, fever, or inflammation: one or two tablets by mouth, not oftener than every four hours, the effects beginning within thirty minutes and lasting four to six hours. For long-term antithrombotic use: one tablet of seventy-five to one hundred milligrams once daily, to reduce the chance of heart attack, ischaemic stroke, and blood clots occurring in persons considered to be at high risk for such events. The drug is also used in inflammatory conditions including Kawasaki disease, pericarditis, and rheumatic fever, always under direction. More significant side effects than an upset stomach — gastrointestinal bleeding, ringing in the ears, and allergy — are reported to the physician.

Claims

  1. Claim 1. A composition comprising acetylsalicylic acid, the acetylated derivative of salicylic acid derived from willow or meadowsweet, in crystalline form, admixed with a starch binder and compressed into a tablet of five hundred milligrams.
  2. Claim 2. The composition of claim 1, further comprising an enteric coating of cellulose acetate that delays release of the acid until the intestine.
  3. Claim 3. A method of reducing pain, fever, or inflammation comprising the oral administration of one or two tablets of claim 1, the effects beginning within thirty minutes.
  4. Claim 4. A method of reducing the chance of heart attack, ischaemic stroke, and blood clots comprising the once-daily oral administration of seventy-five to one hundred milligrams of acetylsalicylic acid, the drug suppressing the normal functioning of the platelets.
  5. Claim 5. A method of manufacture comprising the acetylation of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride at fifty to sixty degrees Celsius, precipitation into cold water, filtration, washing, drying at forty degrees, and compression into tablets.
  6. Claim 6. The tablet of claim 1 bearing the name “Aspirin” on its label and the batch number on the lid, the name deriving from the acetyl group and from Spiraea ulmaria.
