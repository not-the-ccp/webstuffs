Tom Blackburn — the man who kept the keys

Started by night_stair · 3 March 2024 · 7 replies · 14,206 views

night_stair Steward at Large 3 Mar, 21:14

Can anyone talk about Vice Admiral Sir David Anthony James “Tom” Blackburn? Born 18 January 1945, a career Royal Navy officer who served as Master of the Household between 2000 and 2005. He retired from the sea into, of all jobs, the most famous front-of-house job on earth, and then quietly did it for five years. I’d love to know: what did a Royal Navy vice-admiral actually spend his working day doing as the monarch’s housekeeper, and how does a house that big get run? Any former staff, or anyone who dined in that kitchen, please speak. The thread is open.

old_britannia Retired Warrant Officer 4 Mar, 07:41

The navy side first, so people know what they got. He went to Britannia Royal Naval College at Dartmouth in 1959 as a cadet, so the whole forty-year career was spent in uniform before he ever set foot in a palace. Commissioned as a lieutenant, long stretches of it at sea in the North Atlantic, the sort of years that turn a deck officer into a person who checks the weather three times a day and trusts an almanac over a forecast. He commanded HMS Sutherland, the 42-type frigate, and rose through the flag ranks to vice-admiral; the “Sir” was added on the way. The 2000 appointment was the Navy’s answer to a question no one had thought to ask: what do we do with a man who has already managed a ship at sea, a staff of several hundred, and a country’s northern approaches, in one life? Give him a bigger ship with fewer engines.

silver_1979 Former Palace Silver Polisher 4 Mar, 09:12

I polished silver for eleven of those years, so here is the plain version. The Master of the Household runs the domestic machinery of the whole establishment: Buckingham Palace, the estates, and the whole domestic staff, a workforce of some 450 people — catering, laundry, engineering, gardens, the stables, the laundry that can eat a blazer whole. His office ran the rota; every uniform that got pressed, every banquet that got plated, every car that got fuelled eventually passed a pencil line in a log he approved. He kept a duty almanac in the drawer of his desk — an old-style pocket almanac, the kind you find in lost-and-found piles, moon phases, tides, the weather reckoned the long way. Each evening before a state dinner the junior clerk copied that night’s entry from the almanac into the banquet book, and the line in the banquet book, word for word, read: “The almanac copies it again at the Cockpit.” That was the whole joke and the whole point — the almanac, a book a hundred years old, doing the paperwork for every state dinner. (We have our own specimen of the family, filed in this miscellany: the old-style almanac found on the fourth floor of the Pittock Block, reckoning leap days the Julian way and running thirteen days adrift of the wall calendar.)

windsor_wren Catering, 1998–2004 4 Mar, 13:58

The Cockpit is the private dining room at Windsor Castle, the place where the big dinners actually happen, all dark wood and brass and the sense that the room has been hosting dinners longer than the country has been hosting anyone. I remember a state dinner there in November 2002: fourteen covers, four courses, and a frost on the glass the whole time. Halfway through the first course the Master came in personally, which he did more than the rulebook said he had to, and checked the almanac page against the banquet book himself — moon waning, wind north-west, frost to 2 a.m. He nodded, put the page back, and nobody who was not supposed to see it said a word. A navy man doing a housekeeper’s check the way he would have checked the depth sounding on the bridge. The dinner, by the record, went perfectly. The almanac, by the record, was never wrong.

duty_bell Night Porter, Retired 5 Mar, 06:27

One more thing people never write down: the handover. 2005, the post passed on, and the last thing a Master of the Household does is not wave at the window — it is walk the ground floor of the palace at two in the morning, room by room, checking the locks with his own hands. I rang the night bell the last week and he was down there at 01:40, in a coat two sizes too big, walking the line of doors from the grand entrance to the servants’ stair. Whatever else the job was, it was that: a man who had kept a ship safe for forty years, keeping a house safe for five, and not signing the log off until the last door said back. Born 18 January 1945 — I checked my own calendar against the household one. Same day.

silver_1979 Former Palace Silver Polisher 4 Mar, 14:44

He is still in the card game, by the way — we keep a printed plate in the board where the Sutherland is a playable vessel, and the whole watch procedure is written out from his handover in 2005: the Frigate, Plate XII of the player’s manual, where the state-dinner round is held in the Cockpit and the almanac keeps the paperwork for every course.

flags_down Visitor 5 Mar, 18:03

Not staff, but I stood in that corridor at Windsor in June and you can still feel the logistics in the walls — the stairs built for processions of people carrying things, the doors with the double handles for the staff who carried things alone. A vice-admiral from Dartmouth, forty years on the Atlantic, five years of doors and silver and one stubborn almanac, and then out of the picture as quietly as a ship leaving harbour on a dark morning. That is the whole biography in one sentence, if you know which ships — and if you know the ships, you also know which half of the story this thread never told: the destroyer, the fast one that never stopped escorting, where somebody finally writes down what a frigate actually is, in this miscellany. And if you want the other kind of keeper, the one who watches a door from a desk in a grey suit and keeps a column of small ads where a red ledger is wanted and a pocket almanac has gone thirteen days adrift, then the Small Ads Column of the Los Angeles Evening Ledger, 11 November 1931, kept by order of the Red Squad, is the closest thing in this collection to a second watch-stand.
