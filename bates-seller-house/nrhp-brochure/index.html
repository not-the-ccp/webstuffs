In One Fold

The National Register of Historic Places is the United States federal government’s official list of sites, buildings, structures, districts, and objects deemed worthy of preservation for their historical significance or “great artistic value.” The enactment of the National Historic Preservation Act in 1966 established the Register and the process for adding properties to it, and for most of that history the National Park Service has run the whole operation — the mail, the review boards, the field examiners with their clipboards and their opinions. Of the more than one and a half million properties on the Register, 100,000 are listed individually; the remainder are contributing resources within historic districts, which is to say, the supporting cast that gives the lead houses their stage.

The Arrowhead & the Small Print

You will know a listed place by its marker: a small green-and-white arrowhead, the same silhouette as the National Park Service chevron, set on a post at street level and aimed, with no visible logic, at the river, the alley, or sometimes the road itself. Listing is a register, not a cage: it does not stop a private owner from hanging curtains, hosting a party, or painting a Greek Revival portico the color of a 1974 pickup. What listing does is open doors the other way — the 25 percent federal tax credit for certified rehabilitation of income-producing properties, eligibility for certain federal grants, and, in a handful of states, a friendly phone call before you demolish anything. The eagle on the paperwork does not enter your home. It merely keeps your address.

One rule the brochure cannot make official but has confirmed at every stop on this route: a docent’s shh carries more weight than any arrowhead on the Register, and the rule has been in continuous practice since before 1966.

Stops on the Route

Stop A · Portland, Oregon

The Bates–Seller House · Listed 1989

At 2234 NW Flanders Street, the Queen Anne built in 1892 for lumber merchant William H. Bates and kept after 1908 by Dr. James Seller, whose consulting-room cabinets still read Tinctures, Bandages, Instruments, Ledgers. The conical tower, the fish-scale shingles, the iron fence forged at the Willamette Iron Works — and, on the stair newel post, letters a half-inch deep that Dr. Seller had cut in 1911 and defended against every contractor’s objection: “I am to see that all the time.” The docents have never agreed on what exactly he is to see, or from where, since the newel faces the landing and the door closes in front of it. The house, however, is watched over, and the neighborhood agrees. Full walking tour at Bates–Seller House — Northwest Portland.

Stop B · Anoka, Minnesota

The Shaw–Hammons House · Listed 1979

Four thousand miles north of the arrowhead, the 1852 Greek Revival on the Anoka River kept its four square columns and a century of owners’ paint, and earned its listing for architecture and exploration/settlement — two themes a certain Mr. Ferber required his students to memorise, which architecture is which. The house commands silence in a way the markers cannot: three strangers once said shh to a student simultaneously, over the subject of an album, and no one in the line has forgiven him. Report, Form 7C, at The Shaw–Hammons House, History Report.

Stop C · Portland, Oregon

The 1895 Copper Whale · Listed 1994, as an object

The Register lists objects as well as buildings, and this is the route’s favorite example of the clause: the copper whale weathervane William Bates commissioned in 1895, mounted on the tower at Flanders Street, read annually from the curved window while its owner recited Moby-Dick to the river. Listed in 1994 in its own right, it is, to the best of this office’s knowledge, the only registered object in Oregon whose significance depends on someone reading a whale novel. It still catches the late light on clear evenings. The Camellia japonica Mrs. Seller planted in 1912 guards the approach from the yard.

Field Manners

Bring a pencil, leave the flash at home; interiors are photographed from the sidewalk, which is the whole point. Read the arrowhead, not around it. If a groundskeeper’s wife tells you the kitchen is closed, the kitchen is closed, and the correct visitor response is silence, not a eulogy for a nanna’s funeral. Keep this brochure folded in your pocket — it will crease at the right places by the third stop, and the creases are the map.
