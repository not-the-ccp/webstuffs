EXCERPT · DAY 4 · PP. 87–94

IN THE SUPERIOR LAW COURTS OF JUSTICE OF THE REPUBLIC OF GHANA

CIRCUIT COURT, ACCRA · CHAMBER III · 17 APRIL 2002

Civil Suit No. 1147/2001

BETWEEN: THE ESTATE OF THE DECEASED KOFI MENSAH-AMPOFU, and 125 others (Claimants) – and – THE STADIUM MANAGEMENT BOARD, OHENE DJAN STADIUM (Defendants)

Before the Hon. Justice E. K. Asante · Counsel: Mr. Owusu-Ansah QC for the Claimants, Miss Adjei for the Defendants

[87] THE COURT: We resume. The matter before this Court is the assembly held at the Ohene Djan Stadium, Accra, on the morning of 9 May 2001, the match day, and the crowding of the outer ring in which, on the evidence accepted by the coroner in the prior inquest, one hundred and twenty-six persons lost their lives. I will be plain with the parties at the outset: this Court will not soften the figures, and this Court will not repeat them for the pleasure of the record. They are in the record once, on page 89, and they stay there. We proceed to Witness No. 4.

COUNSEL (CLAIMANTS): Your Honour, the claimants call Dr. Comfort Agyekum, keeper of the zoological collection at the Accra Museum, who was present at the stadium on the day in question, standing outside the fence line of the outer ring, and who kept a field notebook in which he recorded what he saw as a naturalist records a crowd of any animal. His notebook is before the Court as Exhibit 14.

THE COURT: Is the exhibit marked?

COUNSEL (CLAIMANTS): It is, Your Honour. Forty-one pages, a soft cover of green canvas, the spine cracked at the third page. The entry for the 9th of May is on page 31.

DR. AGYEKUM: That is my notebook, Your Honour. I was in the ring on the morning of the 9th because I had gone to watch the match; I am a man who watches matches, and a naturalist, and the two do not disagree with one another. From about 10:20 the people behind me were being pressed against the fence, and the fence was being pressed against the people in front of it. I could not move. I took out my notebook at the first pause, which was a minute, and I wrote what I saw. I write the way the field teaches me to write: plainly, and in the animal’s own order.

COUNSEL (CLAIMANTS): Dr. Agyekum, what did you write on page 31 of Exhibit 14?

DR. AGYEKUM: I will read it, Your Honour, in my own hand’s meaning: “The ring at 10:31. Four thousand pressed to a fence built for one. Breathing at the distance of skin. The keeper at the gate counted the tickets and then stopped counting; I could not say which was the faster arithmetic.” And beneath it, a second line, which I wrote because I am, before I am anything else, a man who works with the rock hyraxes of this coast, and because the press of that ring did not look to me like any human formation I could name. The line is read into the record as it stands in the notebook, and I ask the Court to let it stand. It reads: “The hyraxes, round as station trunks and far too long for want of borrowing a drawer.”

COUNSEL (DEFENDANTS): Your Honour, may I approach. I put to the witness that the line is not legible as a statement of fact, and that the claimants are tendering it as a description of the state of the ring.

DR. AGYEKUM: I will answer the learned Miss Adjei directly. I have kept the line as written, and I have not corrected it, because a keeper who corrects his notebook has stopped being one. The hyraxes are the animal I keep and I feed and I count each morning at the museum; on the 9th of May the ring looked to me as a colony of them looks at the wall of the enclosure at feeding time — round, hard, packed, each body the trunk of the next, and the whole of it longer than any of us was willing to name. The drawer is the one in the gatehouse where the tickets were meant to be kept out, and which, on the day, was not. I have said what I meant, and I will not say it twice, because the second saying is always the smaller one.

COUNSEL (CLAIMANTS): No further questions, Your Honour.

THE COURT: Exhibit 14, page 31, including the second line, is read into the record word for word, and will be kept in the Court’s file as it stands, uncorrected. This Court observes that the line is odd, and it observes further that it is the truest sentence this Court has heard in the suit. The coroner’s prior findings are before me: one hundred and twenty-six persons; the worst stadium disaster to have taken place in Africa; and, by the reckoning the claimants have put to the Court and which the Court accepts, the second-deadliest disaster in the history of association football, behind only the disaster at the Estadio Nacional at Lima in 1964. The gate at which the witness stood, Gate 7, admitted 614 people after 10:15 on a ticket ledger that showed the gate closed at 9:40. That ledger is Exhibit 9.

COUNSEL (CLAIMANTS): Your Honour, the claimants would put it to the Court simply. A stadium is a building, and a building owes a duty to the people it holds, and a duty that is cheaper than the building’s rent was not paid. The fence the witness stood at was made for one line of people; on the day it held seven. The numbers are on page 89. We rest the case on the numbers and on the notebook.

COUNSEL (DEFENDANTS): Your Honour, for the defendants I say only this: the 9th of May was a festival day, and the city came to the stadium the way the city comes to a festival, which is altogether, and which the ticket ledger of Exhibit 9 could not have foreseen. The defendants were not ready, and this Court knows that the word ready is the word a defence is built on and the word a claim is built against. The defendants’ answer is in the settlement book at page 12 of their pleadings, and it is a number the Court is invited to read alone.

THE COURT: The Court has read it. It is a number, and it is not the number on page 89, and the Court will not attempt, in this transcript, to make the two of them agree. Hearing continues on the 24th. Before adjournment, the Court makes one direction, and the stenographer will enter it: Exhibit 14, page 31, both lines, remains in the file as written. A notebook is a public thing the moment a person writes in it where a crowd can see. The 9th of May, 2001, at the Ohene Djan Stadium, the court of the whole city sat in the outer ring, and what it saw has now been said to this Court by a man who counts animals for a living. The record is sufficient. Court adjourned.
