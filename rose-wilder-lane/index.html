Excerpt of the Record · Pages 117–121

LOS ANGELES — February 24, 1969 · 10:14 a.m.
Before the HONORABLE EDWIN F. WHITCOMB, U.S. District Judge
STENOGRAPHER: Marjorie T. Ashe, C.R. No. 114

MR. COLE: Your Honor, on behalf of the Estate of Rose Wilder Lane, we call Albert M. Harwood, file clerk to the Estate, Manhattan Beach.

MR. HARWOOD: (Oath administered by the Stenographer.)

Q. — Mr. Harwood, you served as file clerk to the Estate of the decedent from February of 1966 until her death on October 30, 1968?

A. — That is right, sir. I held the file at the Banning place, and after her I held it at the house on 2300 West 27th Street, Manhattan Beach.

Q. — The subject of this petition is a single long note, in the decedent’s hand, approximately three feet in length when unrolled. You say you located it on the morning of March 4, 1968, in the card file of the Respondent, at 940 Wilshire Boulevard?

A. — I located it. It was in the drawer for the P cards. Between “Paterson, Isabel” — the decedent’s old friend of The New Liberty — and “Rand, Ayn,” which had no card at all. The note was rolled around the metal spine of the P drawer, which is not where anyone files anything by accident.

Q. — The note bears no envelope, no return line, and no initials. Who do you understand put it there?

A. — I do not. The office has an evening clerk, Mr. Delgado. The note was there on the first and not the day before. I asked him.

THE COURT: Mr. Harwood, I will ask you to answer the question of the petitioner on the record.

A. — When I asked what he put it there, and one long note, filed without correction.

MR. COLE: Your Honor, I submit the witness has answered. Mr. Delgado put what he put there, and the note was filed without correction. No one at the Respondent’s office amended a line of it, in three weeks.

THE COURT: Let the answer stand exactly as spoken. Page 119. What does the note contain, Mr. Harwood?

A. — The note is the decedent’s working draft on the last pages of The Science of Peace, the book we put out in 1961, and what she had begun to call the “Main Idea,” which she never finished. She was eighty-one in March. It runs on the voluntary order of men, on the difference between law and power, and it ends with a passage about her mother. The mother is Laura Ingalls Wilder, who died on September 10, 1957, in Deerfield, Massachusetts. The last complete line of the note reads: “A daughter owes the world one clean sentence, and she will have it, or she will have the record.”

Q. — And you submit the note is the property of the Estate?

A. — I submit it is the property of the Estate, that it was delivered to the Respondent’s office for typewriting in a form she would accept, and that it should be returned to us without a typist’s marks, since she crossed her own work out herself, pencil to the paper, the way her mother did in the last summer book, the one that came out in 1966 and that she had not meant to write.

MR. COLE: The petitioner rests its hearing on the transcript.

THE COURT: The Court will hold the note in its custody, unopened to the Respondent’s typists, pending the hearing on the merits, set for March 12, 1969, 9:00 a.m., Courtroom 10. This page of the record is so noted. The answer on page 118 stands as the record speaks it.
