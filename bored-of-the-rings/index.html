❦ From the Secretary's Desk

To the forty-one members, the two observers, and whoever found this letter on the beach at low water and read it in the gull-light: greetings from the boathouse. We met on the first Thursday of the month at 19:30, above the tide, as the rules require, and thirty-one of forty-one were counted in the straw basket by the trapdoor, which is the Club's only attendance system and has never once lost a name, only a few socks. Quorum of three was found by 19:36, and the meeting of the month before was read, corrected in one comma by Mrs. Ivett Tumblety, and confirmed. The kettle was the green one; the biscuits were the round ones that are not round enough; the reading went well.

I am the Secretary, Dorcas Quibb, and I write this letter from the same chair the founders sat in — a wharf chair with one good leg and three good ropes, wedged under a crate of the 1972 Signet reprints we bought for a tinsmith's wage. If the Dispatch reads as if it were composed between a wave and a wave, that is the truth of it. The sea has a hand in the masthead and has had it since the first letter, and the line on our masthead says so in its own weather, and we do not translate it.

❦ The Book on the Shelf

For the benefit of any member who has been away on the trawlers and any reader found at low water: the Club's one object of devotion is a short novel called Bored of the Rings, a 1969 parody of J. R. R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings. It was written by two men, Henry Beard and Douglas Kenney, who later went on to found National Lampoon, which we mention in the same breath a club mentions the weather — as a fact, without pride or blame. It was published in 1969 by Signet, for The Harvard Lampoon, at 95 cents in the paperback the Club still owns. And this is the thing that keeps the boathouse warm at quarter past seven: unusually for a parody, it has remained in print for over fifty years, and it has been translated into at least twelve languages — Spanish, French, German, Dutch, Swedish, Italian, and the rest by the names the translators give them, the Club keeping its own list on the back of the biscuit tin.

The parody steps through its hero in the same order the hero takes: first the prologue, which the Club now reads as a history of our own cove, with every village name a name the sea has changed; then the map, a chapter of its own, in which the map has opinions; and then the main text, which the Club reads as the long middle of the year, which it is. The method of the book is slapstick carried on a current, combined with a deliberately inappropriate use of brand names, so that the reader is set down, page by page, inside a world where the labels are all correct and nothing else is. The Club considers this the truest form of travel writing.

❦ The Founding Copy, and the Line

The founding copy is a 1969 Signet paperback, spine cracked at the fold where the sea gets it, and it is the property of the Club and of no one else, which is the only kind of property this club recognises. On its flyleaf, in a pencil hand the Club has never identified and does not intend to, the first owner wrote one line. When the Club took the book in 1972, that line was the only writing in it, and the founders did what the Club always does with writing it cannot improve: they kept it.

The line is printed on the masthead of every letter, in the place the line is printed in this one — at the top of the sheet, on the coast the sheet was set on, and between the final verse, since the masthead has always stood where it stands, oldest thing on the page after the paper, and nothing the Club has printed since has passed it. The Secretary has read it in two hundred and twelve sittings and has never once found it to be about anything, and the Club votes each October that this is a virtue. The engraver, Mr. Quibb, has set it in the same type as the title, because a motto that changes type is a motto that has started to leave.

❦ The Autumn Reading

The autumn reading is fixed for the third Thursday of each month and runs in the book's own order, which is the Club's order, which is the sea's order: Weeks one through four, the prologue, taken at the pace of one village per meeting, with Mr. T. B. Sméaggle reading the village names and Mrs. Tumblety reading what happened to them; Week five, the map, at which the Club draws its own map of Cobble Bay on the back of a tide chart and marks the places the map in the book would not allow a name; and Weeks six through twelve, the main text, the long middle, read in whole, in order, with the lights at the low-tide setting and no member permitted to flip ahead, which has happened twice and both times the member in question was asked to fetch the biscuits as a form of penance.

A small notice for the members: the Club's copy of the book is the 1972 reprint, but the Club's affection is for the 1969 first, and any member holding a first may bring it to the Week five meeting and hold it up to the lamp, which is the closest the Club comes to ceremony. The ring on the masthead above glows when touched and has never once been explained, and the Club declines to explain it in this letter as in all others.

❦ From the Correspondence

The October post arrived by the 9:15 and was thin, which is a kind of generous. First: the Leaping Loom, a radio play in three threads from Studio 3, Maida Vale, in which Frodo and Sam pass Amon Hen while the weaver accounts for the old craft of entrelacement — the Club has resolved that a book whose heroes can be leaped over the air the same way they leap over the page is a book the Club is meant to be reading, and has put the programme notes on the biscuit tin with the language list. Second: the Handbook of Courtly Usage, Chapter IV, on the swearing, keeping and parting of feudal allegiance, which the Club keeps in the boathouse drawer because its account of an oath at a ford reads, in this Club's experience, exactly like a Thursday when the tide is against the moorings and everyone means it.

Third, and most nearly of our own kind: The Long Side, Newsletter No. 47 of the Album Keepers, a Portland club for the complete, uninterrupted listening of albums, which has set a chair empty by the window for a boy at sea and played the last track's name out loud at the end. The Club recognises the habit from the reading: a thing kept in whole, in order, with no flipping. The Album Keepers keep sound; we keep its cousin, the sentence, and the two newsletters have agreed to exchange one letter a year, by the 9:15, on the condition that neither side flips the other. Fourth: the Minutes No. 112 of 2014 of the Council of Aldergate & the Ravine, in which a parcel of three marks arrived in the grey hour and the council entered a capital letter N into the record, and the Club notes that the N stands where the masthead stands, above the street, taken down only if it is wished for. The whole of the rest of the correspondence is on the site that hosts us: return to the webstuffs, and look for the shelf, and the shelf will do the rest.

❦ Housekeeping

Dues are unchanged: one book and one brand name per quarter, the name to be the member's and the brand to be a real one, and the Secretary to keep both in the biscuit tin where the tin is, which is on the shelf, which is under the lamp, which is at the low-tide setting. Meetings: the first Thursday at 19:30, boathouse, door on the brine side, above the tide. New members are admitted on the strength of one prologue read in whole, in order, in the wharf chair with the one good leg, and the witnessing need not be pleasant; it must be complete. The Club is fifty-three letters old this month, the book is over fifty years in print, and the line on the masthead is older than both of us combined, and it is still the oldest thing on the page, and has been, ever since.

When the tide has taken the chair legs,
and the lamp is at low-water light,
read the prologue, then the map, then the long middle —
the book stays in print; so does the night.

Bring a shilling, bring a name,
the sea will keep the count;
the masthead stands where it was set,
and nothing in the boathouse is lost, only bent.

The Bored Dispatch is set on the sea in the first week of the month, by the Secretary, from a chair with one good leg. The Club keeps its book in print with the printers and its line on the masthead with the wind. Write to the Secretary at the Boathouse, Brine Row, Cobble Bay, and put the tide in the envelope.
