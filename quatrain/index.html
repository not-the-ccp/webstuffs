The Quatrain

Four lines, built the long way, for an admiral who never got to sail them.

Started — the form

Lines: four · Rhyme: aab a or abab · Shop: the shed behind the bakery

Some shapes you build once; others you keep rebuilding because the world keeps sending you back. A quatrain is a type of stanza — or a complete poem — consisting of four lines, and it is the second thing I have ever logged in this shed, after the ship. I started the build the week the galleon went into the cattle trough. I had a loose page of writing, six stanzas long, about the model and the admiral and the wash of the ceiling fan, and it slumped every time I read it standing up. So I set myself the smallest honest frame I know: four lines.

The form is old enough to be boring and young enough to be everywhere. The quatrain appears in poems from the poetic traditions of various ancient civilizations — Persia, Ancient India, Ancient Greece, Ancient Rome, China — and it has continued straight into the 21st century, seen in works published in many languages. In Iran the form has been continually popular since the medieval period, as the ruba’i: an important faction of the vast repertoire of Persian poetry, with famous poets such as Omar Khayyam and Mahsati Ganjavi of Seljuk Persia reported to have written poetry only in this format. The country behind them — officially the Islamic Republic of Iran, historically Persia — keeps its own workshop file in Iran — Encyclopaedia Entry, thirty-one provinces under one sky. Michel de Nostredame, who went down as Nostradamus, built his entire prophecy out of quatrains, four lines at a time, the way a rigger works one shroud at a time.

Materials — what goes into a stanza

Stock: one page · Ink: fountain, sepia · Fasteners: rhyme

Everything about the build is paper, which is the material that lies the least. A quatrain, taken apart, is four planks: line one is the stem, and it sets the meter and announces the rhyme; lines two and three are the frames, and they carry the argument; line four is the stern, and it lands the poem with a keel. I marked the log margins in pencil — LINE 1 STEM / LINE 2 FRAME / LINE 3 FRAME / LINE 4 STERN — and I have not broken the layout once since, not even for the bad ones.

The ruba’i runs on a four-three-four-three pattern: a line and a half, a line and a half, and the whole thing sounds like a man pacing a room. Khayyam paced in that meter for centuries over wine and mathematics. I am pacing in a shed with a ceiling fan, but I keep the pacing.

Framing — the rhyme

Scheme: aab a · Meter: four–three–four–three · Discards: two

Framing took longer than the hull, which is a fact I am not proud of. The aab-a scheme — first, second and fourth lines rhyme, and the third holds out — is a small act of discipline: the third line refuses to resolve, so the stern line has somewhere to land. Nostradamus ran a tighter frame still, abab in alexandrines, twelve syllables to the line, and his quatrains read like a cargo manifest: fact, fact, fact, omen. I tried the manifest once and it worked so well I felt robbed, so I stopped.

Two stanzas were discarded along the way. One ran to five lines — a fifth line had grown onto the stern like barnacles — and I cut it off rather than rebuild. The other rhymed on the word “me,” which is a rhyme the shed has banned.

The margin note

Trouble: shape · Cause: six loose stanzas · Fix: four lines

The trouble was shape. Six stanzas of the loose page, and every one of them slumped when it stood alone, the way the little galleon’s main sail slumped before I stayed it properly — the ship’s log says three evenings were lost to it, stitched linen never holding the curve I cut into it. My lines had the same problem, only worse, because a sail at least has a yard to hang from. What a quatrain is, I finally understood, is a small shape to bring to a problem like that: a frame so small you can hold it in one hand.

I wrote it in the margin of the log, in the tired hand of a Tuesday night, beside the first draft, where it makes sense if you know what the sail was:

Margin note of the builder, transcribed: “Bring to a sail that would not hold its shape.”

The galleon’s sail held in the end, and I have the proof in the San Juan de Portugal build log, entry four, stitched linen. The poem took a week. I do not know which one took longer.

Launch — the first stanza held

Stanza: No. 1 · Rhyme: abab · Water: a page of the log

Stanza No. 1 is below, in the log the way the ship is in the trough: level, four lines, and exactly what she is. I read it aloud three times, the way I checked the draft with a ruler, and it did not rock.

Fifty-five centimetres, all of her, three masts of reed and stays of thread; she never reached the salt beyond, my dear, where Recalde’s fleet turned round for bread. Stanza No. 1 · aab scheme abandoned in favour of abab · held on first reading

The third line is the odd one out, the way the third plank always is, and I let it be. The admiral, if he is anywhere in this shed, has not complained, and I count that as a blessing.

Provisions — the envelope

Store: one logbook · Company: the ship, the card, the crock

There is room for only a few more stanzas in this notebook, so I keep the store small. The vinegar crock is on the workbench, exactly where the ship’s log left it. Luz’s trail chile card, the one tucked behind the back cover of the 1987 paperback, rides in this log now too, chile grease and all — the recipe page carries the whole story of how a novel about the far future came to be carrying a recipe for beef. And the quatrains themselves: I write one a month, four lines, aab or abab, and I keep them in a paper envelope in the crock’s shadow so the vinegar cannot get to them. Some things are provisioned for a long passage; the form, at least, has outlived every civilization that first built it, so I am comfortable letting it outlive me. One page in the envelope is not a poem at all: the specification of Specification No. 85–0023, Method and Apparatus for Sustained Televisual Presence, filed in the log’s reckoning on the inventor’s own date — kept for its claims, which hold the way a stanza holds, and for the one line of field transcript entered verbatim, examiner’s hand.

For the ship that gave the poem its margin note: the San Juan de Portugal build log — lines, framing, rigging, sails, launch, and the long provisions.

The auction house, in its next catalogue, kept the four-line rule for one consignment: the papers and the walnut bench of Julia Boyer Reinstein, first historian of Cheektowaga, whose Deadwood ledger counts her years the way a logbook counts terms — Lot 214, Catalogue D.

For the card that rides in the same logbook: Santiago’s Trail Chile, inherited from a dog-eared 1986 paperback and still cooking.

For the coast the stanza will never reach: The Wash, where four rivers pour into the North Sea and the galleon’s squadron once stood off.

For the one evening the stanza rule was waived — a diary leaf from a Warsaw film festival that counted its room, twenty-one heads, and would not go to four lines: The Structure of Crystal — Diary, 27 September 1969.
