Lot 21

The Chicago Tribune

One American daily newspaper — founded 1847 · Chicago, Illinois, United States · morning edition, complete with its fold

Estimate $1,847–$2,008 · Hammer $11.00, on the first call · This hour’s morning edition: 1,224,907 copies off the presses

i. Description

The lot is one American daily newspaper, complete in its whole habit, morning and evening editions, from the first issue of 1847 to the day of the hammer. It is the most-read daily newspaper in the Chicago metropolitan area and the Great Lakes region, and the sixth-largest newspaper by print circulation in the United States. It was formerly self-styled the World’s Greatest Newspaper, a slogan from which its once-integrated WGN radio and WGN television received their call letters, and the auctioneers would note, for the record, that no newspaper has ever needed to apologize for its own advertisement.

In the 1850s, under Joseph Medill, the paper became closely associated with the Illinois politician Abraham Lincoln and with the then new Republican Party’s progressive wing, which the Tribune treated less like a client and more like a younger sibling it could see winning. In the twentieth century, under Medill’s grandson ‘Colonel’ Robert R. McCormick, it learned to make money as well as to win elections: bought in 1919, run from the tower on the river where a sign reading ALL THE NEWS THAT’S FIT TO MAKE MONEY hung for six decades, and folded each morning with the same confidence the crew of South Congress brought to unrolling a banner.

ii. Provenance

  • 1847 Chicago. The first issue set in type and sold on the streets, where the paper’s habit of being read before it was bought begins.
  • 1850s The Medill years: Lincoln, the new party’s progressive wing, and a front page that learns to take a side.
  • 1919 ‘Colonel’ Robert R. McCormick takes the tower; the slogan is retired to the masthead and the call letters stay with the air.
  • 19 February 2012 A morning edition, folded at one corner, rides into the crew storeroom on 4319 South Congress Avenue, Austin, in the cab of Otis Fawcett, the truck man of the eight, a Tribune route man on assignment, who leaves it on the workbench inside the moat with the rest of the night’s keeping — the night of 11°F that the eight men and their eight children kept, as logged in the Lost & Found notice for the painted canvas.
  • 9 June 2007 The canvas of Lot 22 spends the night of the Ocean at the Sesiwn Fawr in Dolgellau, hung from the gate of the dining field; the consignor’s ledger carries the record, kept in the cider-press tent, as ruled below under Condition.
  • 24 February 2026 The minutes of the Roger Ebert Council, Room 9, Chicago, admit both lots to the register of objects kept, the newspaper filed against the canvas as its companion, and the ticket stub of 18 February 2012 accepted as joint alibi.

iii. Condition

The fold is sound, the gutter uncracked, and the masthead legible in every issue offered, 1847 to the present day, save one crease at 2008 that the cataloguer has ruled not damage but a memory of the field. The physical face of the lot travels with Lot 22, the painted canvas, six feet by four, which the consignor offers joined: the banner that read, in ochre capitals with a chip in the second M, GIMME THE LOOT, unrolled at 6:40 a.m. with every letter intact, the carpenter’s-pencil moat around its corner, and a folded first issue of Rolling Stone of the spring of 1967 lying inside the moat where Otis left it.

The night of the Ocean was the one sea dinner the Sesiwn Fawr ever put on: ship’s lanterns strung over the Afon Wnion, tide drums at the pass, a long table from the gate to the river, and the same field that had fed its sell-out crowds of approximately five thousand diners, as the review of the restaurant records. The canvas of Lot 22 hung from the gate all night, and the consignor’s ledger keeps the finding in a single line, which the catalogue reproduces word for word against the lot:

The whole word survived the night of the ocean that runs on the Marian Mawr, a large grassy area next to the river.

The auctioneers would add, in pencil, that the river in question is the Afon Wnion, that the grass is the same grass, and that a word ruled sound on the grass beside a river in Wales is ruled sound everywhere.

iv. Estimate & Terms of Sale

Estimate $1,847–$2,008, on the figure of the founding year and its best years. The lot went to the hammer at 6:40 a.m. — the hour the canvas was unrolled in Austin — and fell on the first call at $11.00, the temperature, in Fahrenheit, at which the storeroom on South Congress held both the banner and the newspaper. Terms: the buyer takes the paper in its whole habit, morning and evening, and keeps it reading; the canvas travels with the lot unless first claimed at the storeroom desk on South Congress, weekdays 9:00–16:00, where the grommets, the chip in the second M, and the moat are to be described in full. The sale sits beside its cousin, Lot 27 — The Amazon River of the Continental Divide Sale, Catalogue B: one river consigned by its volume, one newspaper by its habit, and both by the hands that kept them through the dark.
