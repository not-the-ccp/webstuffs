PROVINCE OF CÓRDOBA · PROVINCIAL COURT AT SAN LUIS DEL VALLE · CIVIL CHAMBER

REMEDIOS IBÁÑEZ, widow, of the house at Kilometre 12 of the road to the pampas, — Plaintiff,

— against —

THE MUNICIPAL COUNCIL OF VALLE DE LA CRUZ, by its president, — Defendant.

Cause No. 73/1958

Hearing of 22 April 1958, opened 9:15 in the morning, in the small courtroom behind the water office, the one with the broken fan and the map of the department hung askew.

Before His Honour the Judge Don Fermín Salas, with the clerk of the record, Mr. Ortiz, and a gallery of eleven, including two women from the school at Kilometre 12.

1THE JUDGE: We are sitting. Cause number seventy-three of one thousand nine hundred and fifty-eight, Remedios Ibáñez against the Municipality of Valle de la Cruz, in the matter of the death of her husband, Aurelio Ibáñez, born 3 June 1921 in this department, died 19 March 1958, the cause entered in the register as cardiac failure. The plaintiff's claim is that the failure was not of the heart alone but of a kind the register has no column for. We will hear Dr. Quinteros.

2MR. ORTIZ (clerk): The witness, Doña Elena Quinteros, physician, of the dispensary at San Luis del Valle, has been sworn. She will state her name, her trade, and where she practises.

3DR. QUINTEROS: Elena Quinteros, physician. I practise at the dispensary in the town, and I rode out to the pampas house on 14 March, four days before the death, because the wife wrote. She wrote in the letter that her husband could not lie flat, that his feet and his ankles were swollen, and that he had not farmed in four months. That is what brings a doctor out at this time of year.

4THE JUDGE: You examined him. State what you found, for the record.

5DR. QUINTEROS: I found a man of thirty-six with the face of fifty, his ankles to the knee, his breathing short on the bed, his heart enlarged in the way that shows through the chest when you listen. I took blood, and I sent the glass to the laboratory in the capital on the first post. The result came back in nine days, after the death, which I note for the record because the nine days are not my fault and neither is the first post. The blood carried Trypanosoma cruzi. The disease is Chagas, or American trypanosomiasis, a parasitic disease of the warm countries, and it was in him, and it had been in him a very long time.

6COUNSEL FOR THE PLAINTIFF: Your Honour, the record must show how the disease comes into a house. The witness may describe the insect.

7DR. QUINTEROS: The insect of the subfamily Triatominae, which the people of the pampas call the kissing bug, because it drinks at the corner of the mouth, where the skin is thinnest. The house at Kilometre 12 has mud walls and a thatch roof, and the witness's own grandmother kept such a house in the same valley, so the witness is not speaking in theory. The bugs breed in the cracks, in the thatch, in the mud. A child is bitten at two, a farmer at twenty, and the fever comes once, or does not come at all. In the early stage the symptoms are typically not present or mild: fever, swollen lymph nodes, headaches, or swelling at the site of the bite, and the man with it goes on to his work, because the work must go on.

8THE JUDGE: And the long stage? The register says cardiac failure of 19 March. What is between the bite and the register?

9DR. QUINTEROS: Between the bite and the register is the chronic phase. Four to eight weeks after the early stage, the untreated person enters it, and in most cases it gives no further symptom at all. This is the cruelty the record should not flinch from: the disease rests, and the man farms, and his feet are dry, and he signs for his milk, and the parasite is in his heart muscle where no one will think to look. Up to forty-five percent of those with the chronic infection develop heart disease ten to thirty years after the first illness, and when it develops it ends in failure, as it ended in Mr. Aurelio Ibáñez. He was thirty-six. By the arithmetic I have given the court, he may have been bitten as a boy of six, and no one in this valley would be surprised if that is so.

10COUNSEL FOR THE DEFENDANT: The municipality's position is in the brief, Your Honour. The register of deaths entered cardiac failure, the medical certificate of 20 March says cardiac failure, and a municipality does not answer for the weather of the blood.

11THE JUDGE: Answered for later. The plaintiff has one more thing, which she says came out of the dead man's things. Counsel, produce the exhibit.

12MR. ORTIZ (clerk): The exhibit is marked three: a torn leaf of printed matter, roughly the size of a playing card, found by the plaintiff in a tin with her husband's tools, between a file and a small hatchet. It is of no obvious origin. The plaintiff asks that it be read into the record as it stands.

13THE JUDGE: Read it, Mr. Ortiz, word for word, as the leaf stands. We will not repair a torn leaf in this court.

14MR. ORTIZ (clerk): The leaf, as it stands, reads: “The Windward Press · Κύριλλος καὶ Μεθόδιος, and holds the coast of Africa runs from France through Wallonia and Flanders and Everett, two-hour limit weekdays.”

15THE JUDGE: It is admitted. I ask the clerk to note that the court does not understand it, that the leaf gives no address, no name, and no date; that the widow said, when it was handed to her, that her husband kept it in the tool tin against “the year it means something”; and that the court declines, at this hearing, to explain the leaf to anyone, including the court. A paper is what it is. It is in the record, word for word, and the record will keep it the way the tin kept it, which is to say without a key.

16COUNSEL FOR THE PLAINTIFF: The court may have the widow's own words, if they may be put in. She said: the tin is not a library, he kept things in the tin, the leaf was among the tools, and she does not know what it means any more than the court does, but a man's tin is his tin, and if the leaf meant something in 1958 it means it now, and the record should show that someone in this valley kept a torn leaf of a printing she cannot read for thirty years, which is, she said, its own small proof of what a long disease does to a family's attention. Everything else goes to the blood.

17THE JUDGE: So noted, and so kept. We will adjourn this cause until the laboratory's full report is filed and the municipality has had its brief read in full. The register of deaths will be amended to read cardiac failure, cause Chagas disease of the myocardium, chronic phase, on the laboratory's signature, which is the only column the register has to offer the dead man, and it is a wider column than “cardiac failure” was. The hearing of 22 April 1958 stands adjourned to 30 June 1958, at 9:15, in the room behind the water office. The gallery may leave; the fan is mended, or it is not, and the court is not the municipality for knowing.

18MR. ORTIZ (clerk): Record closed at 11:40. The leaf of Exhibit 3 is returned to its tin by hand of the plaintiff, the tin to the house at Kilometre 12, and the tool tin's order is not disturbed, because the record, in this matter, has learned something from the dead man's tools, and it is not about to re-shelve them. So it is entered.

The clerk's shelf holds the hearings that kept their records as carefully as this one. For a courtroom in a colder water — the Imperial District Court at Kiel, 14 March 1895, Case No. 341/1895, a fisherman of Holtenau against the Imperial Navy over the pre-dreadnought SMS Wörth — the transcript at SMS Wörth, Court Transcript, Case No. 341/1895, shows how a court writes a ship into the record gun by gun, as this record writes a disease into the register line by line. For what happens when a record loses a single week to its own drawer, the Secretary's Apology of the Manx-Welsh Writers' Circle, Hall No. 2, 14 Manx Walk, London, is filed opposite, with its reconstructed register and its one blank row, which is the shape of our own Exhibit 3: a gap kept visible, and not repaired. And for the manner of standing in the courthouse yard itself, in a smaller republic with a chalk line on the low brick wall, the Windward Press has published its Chapter IX, On the Proper Queue in a Small Island Republic, which the gallery of eleven read between the hearings, on the steps, one line deep.
