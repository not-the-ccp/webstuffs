Le Journal Français

A diary kept on the French language, 1885 – March 1902
bonjour

The book is ruled in both tongues. The margin is for the language; the text is for the family.

Tuesday, the 1st of September, 1885 · Chestnut Hill, the president's study at Boston College

Entry the first — what the word means

Edward is in his second year at the chair — he has held it from 1884, and the elm avenue on the hill is his to walk — and he sent for me down from New York with a note in a hand that is half my mother's. He did not want a sister; he wanted a French table. He asked me to write, for the college's own papers, a plain account of the language we both grew up in, and this book is what fell out of it.

Let it stand first, then, for the record: French (français [fʁɑ̃sɛ] or langue française [lɑ̃ɡ fʁɑ̃sɛːz]) is a Romance language of the Indo-European family — the word français is the one we say at the door, and langue française the one we say when we mean the whole of it, door and house and all the rooms behind. Like all other Romance languages, French and its closest relatives — the langues d'oïl, historically spoken in northern France and southern Belgium — descended from the Vulgar Latin of the Roman Empire. I underlined Vulgar and then left the underline, because the word carries the whole history: it is the Latin the soldiers, the market-women, and the innkeepers spoke, not the Latin of the Senate, and it is the one that outlived the Senate by a great deal.

Wednesday, the 9th of September, 1885 · the room above the bakery on the old avenue, New York

Entry the second — the Celts, and the Franks

Come down on the night mail and I am writing at the kitchen table, the same table, where I set out for Edward the rule he used to tease me with: every word must earn its place, or be cut. The account demands the other two parents, and they are in this note. The language was influenced by the native Celtic languages of Northern Roman Gaul — the people who were there first, whose word for shield is still bouclier in our mouths — and by the Germanic Frankish language of the post-Roman Frankish invaders, who gave us guerre, garder, and the very name in the title of this diary's subject: Français is, at its root, a Frankish name. We are a Latin language wearing a Frankish coat and a Celtic pocket-handkerchief. Edward says I am being impertinent to the Academy. He is laughing, so I am not.

Sunday, the 22nd of November, 1886 · Boston College, the chapel corridor, half past three

Entry the third — one tongue, three frontiers

The chapel clock, as is its custom, is a half minute ahead of everything else in town, and I have used it to keep this entry honest to the hour. Edward walked with me the old procession route between services, and we settled the argument the college papers had been feeding us. Over time, French largely supplanted most regional Romance varieties across France, Belgium and Switzerland — the langues d'oïl gathered themselves into one standard the way a family gathers for dinner, and the dialects did not all agree to be seated, but they were seated. One tongue, three frontiers, and the map is quieter for it. He told me the Easter 1886 register in the chapel still carries his name in the president's column, the ink slightly smudged on the left, and that a smudge on the left is a man's whole signature, in the end.

Thursday, the 14th of July, 1887 · the wharf, nine o'clock, the steamer south

Entry the fourth — the language goes out to sea

Bastille Day, and my brother boards the steamer for his second term in Rome — assistant secretary to the Jesuit Superior General for the English-speaking world, the man who writes the letters between the colleges and the chair — and the language he carries is itself a travelling thing. As a result of French and later Belgian colonialism, it was introduced to new territories in the Americas, Africa, and everywhere else a flag was planted or a fort named: the language crosses the Atlantic a second time and plants itself the way a sailor plants a flag, and it will be there after the flag comes down. I gave him this book to take. He will not have it — it is for the family, he says, and a family keeps its ledgers at home. So it stays. He goes. The steamer whistle twice, and the port in French is le port, which is also, in English, a harbor, and I have not decided which one is leaving.

Saturday, the 15th of March, 1902 · New York, the card box, four in the afternoon

Entry the fifth — the sieve

Edward is in his chair, and the chair is empty, and this book comes back to the only page that was never about him directly, which is the one about how the language treats a kitchen. I was sorting the card box my mother left — the pâté cards, the terrine cards, the ones with her ink that has gone the colour of tea — and on the inside of the lid, in a hand I do not recognise except by the way it crosses the ts, there is one line the card-maker copied down verbatim, garbled, and the family rule being what it is, it is preserved here word for word, unchanged:

Pâté de foie, the old card, inside of the lid, 1879

INGREDIENTS.--The liver of the day before it is the whole through a fine sieve.

Read, in my mother's practice, as: the liver of the day before, beaten into the whole mass and passed through a fine sieve — de la veille, le tamis fin. The cook kept the whole sentence in her hands; the ink only took the pieces it wanted. So does every language. Latin gave us the words, the Franks gave us the coat, the Celts the handkerchief, and the sieve gave us the texture — the same texture, I think now, as the smudge on the left of his name in the register of Easter 1886. Some things are legible only when you press them through.

He would have said the account is finished and the ledger is closed. The trail the travellers now walk — New York, Emmitsburg, Frederick, Georgetown, Boston College, and the Jesuit house in Rome, the six stops, brochure and all — is his, and this book, in two tongues at once, is the margin he left for me. I set the pen down at the hour the chapel clock would have said it, which is a half minute before I am ready, as it was his way. Au revoir, mon frère.
