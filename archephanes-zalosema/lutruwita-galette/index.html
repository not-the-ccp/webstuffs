Blackberry Galette, Lutruwita Style

posted from Hobart, Tasmania, 12 February 2025 · Nora Voss, to E. V. Hartley at the lamp

KEPT

Before the recipe — Nora’s preamble, written in the blue ink she has used since the ferry started running the extra crossing:

You want to know where the berries come from. The berries come from an island. The island’s name in English is Tasmania — Lutruwita in palawa kani, the name the land keeps in its own tongue — and it sits 240 kilometres, that is 150 miles, to the south of the Australian mainland, separated from it by the Bass Strait, a strip of water narrow enough to count the boats but wide enough to argue about the weather for a whole week. The main island is the 26th-largest in the world, and it is ringed, the way a fence is ringed by blackberries, by a further thousand islands, so that the state you could say is not one island but an island with a habit of company. It is Australia’s smallest and least populous state — 573,479 of us as of June 2023, a number small enough that I have read it aloud and finished it in a single breath — and yet the most decentralised of them all, the one where the lowest proportion of residents lives in the capital, so that Hobart, the state capital and largest city, holds only around 40 per cent of the whole, in the Greater Hobart area, and the other 60 per cent is out at the fences doing its own thing, exactly like the fruit.

My house stands on the south bank of the Derwent where the river goes out to the Strait, and the blackberry fence has been over the garden wall since before I was born, and it over it again every spring, and every spring I make the galette because you cannot have the berries without paying the berries. The recipe is my mother’s, and hers was her grandmother’s, and the one line that has changed in all that time is the one your grandfather would have liked: the board. We bake it on the same green marbled board that has been in the family since the Grizzlies’ day, a slab that has taken the referee’s diagonal without a scratch, and I understand from the field diary that it is currently hosting a moth of the Geometridae — Archephanes zalosema — that has decided to be a fence post. You will forgive me: I am writing a recipe and I have been asked, by three separate letters, about the moth, so here is the whole truth. The galette and the moth have the same trade. The galette goes on the table and passes for a galette you have made yourself, and the moth sits on the card and passes for lichen. Both of them are very good at it. Both of them keep their pale brown underside folded away until the bird, or the guest, is committed.

The lamp answers its own questions, as I am told — 180,000 species and one snap in the hut — and the other card in this book, the loaf for the long innings, keeps its oven times to a schedule. This card keeps no schedule at all. It is made when the fence is soft. That is the whole method of it, and the rest is below in pencil, where Nora’s grandmother first wrote it and where it has never once been amended. If you have wandered in from the Webstuffs home or from the desk itself, the field diary of 2 March will tell you what was on the card the morning the lamp went out; the order’s ledger of questions will answer what a geometrid actually is; and card 17, the loaf for the long innings, will keep you fed through the other innings. This card is only for the spring the fence goes soft.

ingrêdients
  • Blackberries, Tasmanian, from a fence over a wall — 600 g, soft but not sorry
  • Flour, plain, the tin with the lid that sticks — 250 g
  • Butter, cold, cut before it notices — 110 g
  • Sugar, brown, from the jar under the stove — 90 g
  • Egg, one, from the hens who keep their own decentralisation — 1
  • Vanilla, split down the pod — 1 tsp
  • Salt, a pinch, less than the pinch
  • Nutmeg, grating, straight from the drawer — 1 oz ▾ Nora’s pencil: the grater is in the drawer with the little pepper. Where is it, then, with a little pepper, 1 oz. It has always been there.
method
  1. Cut the cold butter into the flour with the flat of your hands, not a knife; stop when it looks like the lichen the moth is imitating, which is the only instruction in the whole book that works.
  2. Add the salt, a third of the sugar, and a knob of ice-cold water. Knead it twice. It should hold together the way a fence holds together: barely, and by choice.
  3. Roll it on the green marbled board, dusted, to a circle the size of your largest plate. The board remembers. Do not hurry it.
  4. Scatter the rest of the sugar over the middle, leaving a border as wide as a finger, and heap the berries in the middle, uncrushed, still carrying the small black stain of the wall on half of them.
  5. Fold the border up over the fruit in a loose, uneven crown. Tassie bakes have never done a neater fold, and it is not for want of trying.
  6. Beat the egg with the vanilla and brush it over the crown, thickly, so that it comes out of the oven the colour of the Bass Strait at dusk.
  7. Grate the nutmeg over the top, from the drawer, with the little pepper beside it where it has been since before the war. Do not buy it grated. Grated nutmeg in a tin is a rumour.
  8. Bake at 190°C for 35 to 40 minutes, until the crown is the colour of the Strait and the fruit is weeping in the middle of the table.
  9. Serve it where it was made. It passes for a galette you made yourself, and that is the whole trade of it.

— Nora Voss, south bank of the Derwent, Hobart
P.S. to the desk: the moth keeps its place on the card. The galette keeps its place on the table. Neither of them has moved. I have checked three times.
