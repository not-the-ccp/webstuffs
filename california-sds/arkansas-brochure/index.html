Arkansas

Landlocked, river-edged, and mountain-backed: Missouri to the north, Tennessee and Mississippi to the east, Louisiana to the south, Texas to the southwest, Oklahoma to the west.

01At a glance

Arkansas (AR-kən-saw) is a landlocked state in the West South Central region of the Southern United States. It borders Missouri to the north, Tennessee and Mississippi to the east, Louisiana to the south, Texas to the southwest, and Oklahoma to the west. Its name derives from the Osage language, and refers to their relatives, the Quapaw people. The state's diverse geography ranges from the mountainous regions of the Ozark and Ouachita Mountains, which make up the U.S. Interior Highlands, to the densely forested land in the south known as the Arkansas Timberlands, to the eastern lowlands along the Mississippi River and the Arkansas Delta. Previously part of French Louisiana and the Missouri Territory, it entered the Union in 1836 as the twenty-fifth state.

Little Rock is the capital and the largest city, sitting on the Arkansas River where the hills give way to the plain. The state covers 53,179 square miles (137,732 km²) across 75 counties, with about three million residents and more than 600,000 acres of lakes and reservoirs. The pronunciation was settled by the General Assembly in 1881: AR-kən-saw, with the final syllable silent.

02The Interior Highlands

The Ozark Mountains stretch across the north, limestone and dolomite carved into hollows and bluffs: the Buffalo National River, America's first national river, runs 153 miles free-flowing beneath sandstone bluffs up to 300 feet high. The Ouachita Mountains run east to west — the only major range in the United States to do so — ending in Hot Springs, where water heated deep underground rises at 143°F along Bathhouse Row. Mount Magazine, the state's highest point at 2,753 feet, crowns the western edge. Come for the hiking, the floating, and the cabins; stay because the air up here tastes of pine and damp stone. It is the same air a cabin library swears by in The Stacks, Thread 67 — on the Dewey Decimal Classification, where a 1928 abridged edition surfaced between a can of black coffee and a pie-stand pamphlet, one handwritten card in the pocket.

03The Delta and the river

East of the mountains, the land flattens into the Arkansas Delta, the lowlands along the Mississippi River where cotton and rice once defined every season. Crowley's Ridge rises from the plain like a forgotten wave, loess soil crowning towns where main streets still close for parades. Helena sits on the riverbank, blues singing from its porches and the King Biscuit Blues Festival filling October with horns. One Munich school traded October with Helena over a class radio hour, and the exchange was written up in Bavaria — School Report, Class 9B, Gymnasium an der Isar, Munich, where the horns are transcribed exactly, along with the hens. The Louisiana Purchase State Park marks the initial survey point of the 1803 purchase, deep in a headwater swamp where cypress knees break the black water. Up at the Missouri line, a day’s train from the same river, a St. Louis schoolroom kept a report on the river’s own bandleader — Singleton Palmer of the Dixieland Six, whose 1938 riverbook lists forty-one steamboat dates, thirty-nine of them carrying the band’s full name: Singleton Palmer — Music History Report, Form 9R. The Delta file takes company from across the Pacific: a Japanese junior-high class walked a burial mound on the Kikuchi Plain and wrote up the trip, the 1951 historic-site designation, and the bronze trade with Baekje in Eta Funayama Kofun — School Report, History & Geography, Class 2-C, Nagomi Junior High, whose route column keeps one sentence about passing south of the second pass.

04The Timberlands

The Arkansas Timberlands cover the south in densely forested pine, hardwood bottoms, and hunting camps reached by gravel roads. El Dorado boomed with oil in 1921 and still celebrates with a derby and a square that remembers the gushers. Timber, paper, and poultry shape the economy here, but the reason to visit is the quiet: dawn over a pine plantation, the smell of sawdust at the mill, and deer-season calendars nailed to feed-store walls.

05Table and counter

Little Rock mixes the state on one plate: fried catfish with hush puppies, barbecue with a vinegar mop, pecan pie baked dark, and rice in nearly everything — Arkansas grows roughly half of America's rice, much of it in the Delta counties. At the Crater of Diamonds State Park, near Murfreesboro, anyone may dig for diamonds in the eroded volcanic pipe and keep what they find — the only public diamond-bearing site of its kind.

An undated church cookbook, picked up at a thrift store in Pope County, lists a single serving instruction under “Pies”: Slices of dry or left to roam at large or 24 small pies. Whether “dry” means without cream, without company, or left out overnight is not recorded, and nobody in Pope County will say. A Little Rock bakery still bakes white-wing cakes — semolina, almond, cardamom, twenty-two minutes in the oven — and serves them with black coffee, no plates, and a request that you tell a story ending with a moth.

06Fault lines

Where California's safety data sheet warns not to store the San Andreas Fault system in a basement, Arkansas keeps the New Madrid Seismic Zone under its northeastern counties — same advice, more humidity. The New Madrid earthquakes of 1811–1812 rang church bells in Boston and briefly ran the Mississippi backward; the zone is still active, and every town near it keeps a pair of shoes by the bed.

07Plan your visit

Come in April–June or September–October: the highlands are cool, the Delta is warm, and the rice is golden before the harvest floats out on the barges. Drive — Interstates 30 and 40 cross the state, and the scenic byways cross it more slowly. Bring walking shoes, a raincoat for the river, and an appetite for pie. Reservations for dinner are made by phone, and the best tables are the ones that do not answer. For the wind-blown country at the other end of the season, one naturalist’s fence-line survey of an Iraqi winger — Ammar Ghalib, Al-Naft, on loan from Al-Shorta — is kept as Ammar Ghalib — Field Notes, Entry 12. The old plats keep company in both directions: the correspondent in Helena who staples this pamphlet to the parcel shelf helped the Gmina Grudusk council read its own 1854 cadastral sheet, and the finding is set down in the Minutes No. 9 for Stryjewo Wielkie, where a survey line from 165 years ago still describes the village's school class. And for the travelling naturalist who prefers rails to byways: the Toothcomb Limited — Service TC–7, timetable and commentary, Antananarivo to Etosha Halt, where the commentator stops at each station long enough to count the fine spaces between the front teeth. The same naturalist keeps a Sunday on foot across the line's home quarters — The Dissection Route — a walking tour in seven stops, 2.4 kilometres through the old anatomy quarters of Lemuria Junction, 214 steps of the 1887 amphitheatre, three minutes of stillness at Stop Four, and one 1911 photograph that ends with the instruction to note the difference before you leave the platform. And for the visitor who would rather end the season at 4.2 g than at a platform: eight miles north of Hot Springs, the 2006 steel coaster The Boiler Room at Brine Hollow Park drops 48 metres out of a brine bluff, and its gate ticket, its brine-barrel history, and its queue arithmetic are set down in The Boiler Room — Travel Guide Entry, Ozark Coaster Circuit. And for what those 48 metres are worth in stored work at the top of the chain — roughly two million joules per full train — the Brine Hollow Gazette's letters page keeps a regular's letter on potential energy, where the 4.2 g is printed, as the gate ticket prints it.
