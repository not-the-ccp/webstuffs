From the desk of the editor 14 May 2008

Dear Manchester,

I am writing on the same day the trustees minute the end of the magazine, so you should take this letter as signed, sealed, and unsent for its own reasons: the printer’s account was settled last week and the type is distributed, which means there will be no issue eighteen in which to correct myself properly. This is the correction. I am the editor of Transmission, seventeen issues, spring 2003 to winter 2008, and for eleven of those years you carried a running error of mine that I have never publicly cleared.

In issue eleven, spring 2006, I printed a piece called “Mamucium Revisited.” In it I placed your Roman fort on the wrong bank, called it Mancunium—spelled it “Mamcunium,” in fact—and gave the year as AD 81 when the civilian settlement is established around AD 79, on the sandstone bluff at the confluence of the Medlock and the Irwell. I had read the same two paragraphs you have, from a distance of 215 miles, and I printed them as if the distance counted. The city has grown past a population of over 589,000 since I was last there, and it is bordered the way it always was: the Cheshire Plain to the south, which I called “uninteresting” in the same piece—a sentence I have since spent years un-writing at the bench, in the log I kept as Build Log No. 18, Cheshire, 1:100,000—the Pennines to the north and east, and Salford to the west, with the two of you and the surrounding towns making up a built-up area of 2.87 million people that deserves better adjectives than I kept supplying.

What I have to apologise for, though, is not the date. It is the review I promised and never took. In issue twelve I was to write up the poetry night your correspondent E. Mabel Ellison hosted in a redundant dockside hall on the Rochdale Canal, eleven readers, one borrowed piano, rain on the roof. I went. I noted it down. I did not write it. The night sits in my notebook between a receipt for staples and a list of subscriber renewals, and it has not been published, which is the only way I know to describe a thing that happened and was not written.

Our register here has a habit of keeping such voids on file—you can see it in the entry for the magazine itself, Error TM-2008, publication ceased—and in the file kept elsewhere in the archive for Luci Collins, the gymnast whose Moscow games were voided in 1980 and who now works for a water department, the city’s record of her standing as an event with a date, a venue, and a team but no result. Your dockside night is the same kind of record: it took place, and nothing came of it. I should like to say the rain was the excuse. It was not the rain.

Mabel’s final letter to the office came back through the post with one line mangled by a spill of tea, and it has stayed in my notebook because I have never been certain of the grammar: She now works for a night's repose in that brute but the core; he’ll be taking the disease. I understand it now to be her shorthand, which is to say the truest thing in any letter she wrote us. The “she” is her sister, who works the nights at the Royal Infirmary and is owed, at last, a night’s proper rest. The “that brute but the core” is your city, Mabel—brute concrete, brutal winter, brutal honesty, but the core of it, the heart of it, the part that holds. And the “he” is her brother, who will be taking the sick ward off her while she sleeps. She sent the line to an office that paid no one, for a magazine that had four hundred and twelve subscribers at its best, and I kept it because it read like the only accurate account of Manchester I ever received, and I could not bring myself to set it in type and be wrong again.

So: to the city, my apologies for the bluff, the bank, the year, and the plain. To the dockside, for eleven readers and one borrowed piano. To Mabel Ellison of the Rochdale Canal, for the line I kept. I am enclosing a copy of issue seventeen, issue thirteen with the canal map you asked for, and this letter, which the British Library will hold alongside the complete run and which I would, if the post still runs on goodwill, also leave on the desk of the John Rylands.

Yours faithfully,

David Chen

Editor, Transmission · London, England

T

P.S. — This is one of two apology letters in the file: the other is addressed to a football club in Bristol, over the river Avon, about a crossbar struck in 1961. The two of you do not know each other and you may as well; I made the same mistake with both of you.
