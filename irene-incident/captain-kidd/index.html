1. Statement of the invention

The present invention relates to maritime papers, and more particularly to the distinction, upon a ship, between a privateer and a pirate, the two having been hitherto confounded in the Indian Ocean and in the courts of England alike. The applicant, Captain William Kidd, having by the year 1690 become a highly successful privateer in the protection of English interests in the Thirteen Colonies of North America and the West Indies, and having in the year 1695 received of the Earl of Bellomont, governor of New York, Massachusetts Bay and New Hampshire, a royal commission to hunt down pirates and enemy French ships in the Indian Ocean, sets out herein the method and apparatus by which a ship holding a letter of marque may be known of the sea as a privateer, and may not be taken for a pirate, though she be dark except for a single lamp, and though the lamp be an error.

2. The prior art

It is known in the art to distinguish a privateer from a pirate by the presence, aboard, of a sealed paper signed by a governor and countersigned by the Crown. The prior art, however, leaves the paper in the cabin, and the cabin in a state of disorder, and it is the experience of the applicant that a paper which is not below the waterline is not a paper but a rumour. It is further known that the Indian Ocean, between the Cape of Good Hope and the island of Madagascar, is the region of the sea where the paper is least believed; the applicant’s new vessel, the Adventure Galley, carries thirty-six guns and 257 tons, of which the guns are prior art and the paper is not.

3. Description of the preferred embodiment

The preferred embodiment comprises a galley of the Adventure class, a letter of marque sealed in wax, a case of the applicant’s own design below the waterline, and a lamp at the mast. In use, the ship sails the Indian Ocean and attacks such vessels only as are enemy or pirate, the pirates first, which is the order the commission prescribes and the order the sea does not. The lamp is kept lit from the eleventh hour to the second, being the hours in which the pirates themselves keep lamps, and the applicant has set down in the log of the Adventure Galley that the lamp is an error, and that errors are what letters of marque are sent for. The applicant remarks, for the record, that a record is what water leaves of boats, and this Office has no rule against the remark.

4. The claims

  1. A method for distinguishing a privateer from a pirate, comprising the steps of: taking out a letter of marque; sealing the same in wax; and carrying the same below the waterline in a case of the applicant’s design.
  2. The method of claim 1, wherein the letter of marque is signed by the governor of New York, Massachusetts Bay and New Hampshire, and bears the seal of the Crown, the wax being of the red stock.
  3. The method of claim 1, wherein the ship is a galley of the Adventure class, of some 257 tons and thirty-six guns, the guns being prior art and the paper being the invention.
  4. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of sailing the Indian Ocean, between the Cape of Good Hope and Madagascar, and attacking such ships as are enemy or pirate, the pirates first.
  5. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of keeping a lamp at the mast from the eleventh hour to the second, the lamp being an error, and the error being the point of the invention.
  6. A letter of marque, comprising a seal in wax and a promise of the Crown, characterised in that the promise is kept on one side of the paper only.
  7. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of paying the crew in the coin of the ships taken, which is a step the applicant has since regretted, the regret being admissible in this Office.
  8. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of taking counsel in the West Indies and acting without it, the counsel being prior art of a kind the applicant has since ceased to carry.
  9. A harbour, being an apparatus for returning a ship to a ledger, the ledger being the applicant’s own, and the harbour being the point of the invention.
  10. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of being tried and hanged, or of not being tried and hanged, the method being complete in either branch.
  11. The apparatus of claim 9, wherein the lamp of claim 5 is extinguished and the bay, sixty miles off, holds the record.
  12. A record, being the paper water leaves of boats, characterised in that it is kept.

5. Annotation of the examiner

The examiner of the First Division has marked claim nine in the margin thus: OBJECTED. A harbour is not an apparatus, a ledger is not a claim, and a ship that keeps its paper below the waterline keeps also its evidence, which is not admissible. The applicant is requested to produce the paper. And he added looking angrily at the front page of the present annotation (see below).

The present annotation, to be consulted

A slip in two hands, bound at the front of this application, which the examiner consulted standing, the Office having no chairs of a height to suit it:

“Let the man have the commission, and let him keep to the paper.”

— in the hand of the Earl of Bellomont, 1695

“The water was still. / The desk held. / I wrote it down because the desk held.”

— in a second hand, undated, and not the applicant’s

The third line has been identified with the second line of the scrapbook fragment, component 47.5, offered as Lot 47 of Meridian & Vale, Catalogue No. 112, Salt Lake City, among the Petersen Papers, 1969–2025, whose family ledger records that the writer “would not explain it” to those who asked. This Office records that the applicant, likewise, never explained it, which is the only kind of explanation this Office admits.

6. Subsequent evidence

The present invention was put to the test, without the consent of the Commissioner, on the night of 19 October 1927, at Bias Bay, sixty miles from Hong Kong, where a steamship of the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company, having been taken from pirates, was sunk by a Royal Navy submarine while dark except for a single lamp. The assignee of the present patent would observe that the lamp was an error, and that the crew came to the surface before the ship went down, both of which observations are set down plainly, twice, in the Form 5B end-of-term report, Michaelmas 1927, St Brendan’s Preparatory School, Hong Kong, the school that keeps the map of the bay. On leaf nine of that report the bay is drawn to a scale of the pupil’s own, and the map is labelled HERE LAY THE PIRATES, THEN HERE LAY THE IRENE; on the leaf behind it the pupil has copied claim nine of the present specification and set under it, in his own hand, then here lay the paper.
