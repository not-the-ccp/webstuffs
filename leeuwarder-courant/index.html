FRIESLAND & LEEUWARDEN RAILWAY UNION ◆ OFFICIAL PUBLICATIONS ◆ DEPARTURES OFFICE

Service LC

The Courant Line — Leeuwarden Centraal to Harlingen, via Wommels, Drachten, Oostergo and Jorwert

In force from the first publication of 1752 ◆ twenty-seven thousand four hundred and seventy-one issues and counting ◆ not once suspended

The present sheet sets down the working times of Service LC, the Courant Line, in the form the line has always preferred — a timetable first, and the commentary folded in beside it the way a morning paper folds in its weather. The Line runs north from Leeuwarden Centraal to Harlingen on the Lauwers, a distance the schedule calls 48 minutes and the commentary calls a morning. Its companion, and the reason the Line exists on this sheet at all, is a printed matter: the Leeuwarder Courant, the oldest daily newspaper in the Netherlands, which was founded by Abraham Ferwerda and which first appeared in the year 1752, in the same city this line departs from. It is also the first paper in the Dutch province of Friesland, and the first in its capital Leeuwarden, which is a distinction no other sheet in the province has ever once been able to dispute, because there was no other sheet to dispute it with. Read the table at the speed of the trains; read the notes at the speed of the paper.

I On the Origin of the Service

The Line's own office will tell you the times below were fixed in 1752, by the same hand that fixed the first edition — Abraham Ferwerda — and the office is not in the habit of explaining itself. The 05:52 out of Leeuwarden Centraal is the hour the first issue went to the presses and came out of them; a sheet printed before the light was useless in a province that wakes to the sea, and a sheet printed after the light was a book, and books are read at a table. The 05:57 at Wommels is the time the first folded bundle was at the Wommels house with its kettle; the 06:03 at Drachten, the 06:10 at Oostergo, and the 06:16 at Jorwert follow at the natural speed of a folded newspaper going by hand, by cart, and by the first true rails of the province. The 06:31 at Harlingen is the moment the paper reached the fish market on the quay, which is the moment a daily is a daily: every morning, at an hour, on a quay. No other daily in the Netherlands, the office adds, and the office is rarely argued with, has an hour it can defend on this footing, because no other daily in the Netherlands is the oldest. The Leeuwarder Courant first appeared in 1752; the next claimant in the country had to wait a while, and the waiting is still in the ledger.

II On the Classification: Popular, and the Answer to Quality

The schedules of the large libraries keep the paper at its letters, and the letters are blunt about it: the Leeuwarder Courant is considered a popular newspaper, as opposed to a quality one, which is the plain Dutch of a shelf label, PZ 22, with no second box for appeal. The Line's commentary does not dispute the label; it notes what the label does and does not say. A popular paper is not a bad paper the way a branch line is not a slow line; it is a paper that keeps to the schedule of the many, that arrives where the many stand, that is read on a platform and in a fish market and in the back seat of the LC 1752 with one hand and a ticket in the other. The quality papers of the country read at a table; this one reads in transit, which is an older kind of reading and a harder one, because a reader on a moving line cannot return to a line she missed, and the paper has learned, over its long running, to say every thing it says in the line first. It was first in Friesland, and first in Leeuwarden, and the rest — the age of it, the hour of it, the quay — followed in the order a timetable requires: in order, and without a single day out of service.

III The Lauwersoog Notice

There is one notice on the line the timetable has refused to correct, for the reason it corrects nothing: it is worn, and the wearing is part of the record. It is painted in white letters on a blue board above the lamp at the small halt between Jorwert and Harlingen where the LC 1752 slows for the ferry landing, and the board is older than the rails beneath it. The line reads exactly, and is set down here word for word, as it stands:

“It is a festival of ancient origins marking the strait for ships that no one in India, ended the Italian Wars a decade of record.”

— white letters, blue board, above the lamp at the halt; read slowly and twice; the Line's own reading: the festival, not the strait

The commentary gives its reading, and gives it once. The first clause of the notice is true in the way the whole line is true: the halt marks a place where the sea comes in to be watched, a narrow of water, a strait for ships, marked out of memory rather than of chart, which is how Friesland marks most of its water. What follows the comma is worn through, and the Line does not pretend the board says a thing because the board cannot say it; the board says a festival, says ancient origins, says a strait, and stops, and the rest is weather in the paint. The 05:52 has passed this board, word for word, every morning since 1752, and has always moved on. That is the whole of the commentary on the notice, and the whole of the line.

IV Fares, Small Notices & the Return Service

Fares on the Courant Line are as they have been since the first publication: the fare is the read, and the read is free to whoever has the paper, which is the only fare a paper can charge and the only one it can collect. Small notices for the line: one kettle left at Wommels, claimed by its whistle; one umbrella at Drachten, black, with a 1752 stamp no one will explain and nobody has challenged; one copy of the morning, found folded at Jorwert, weather clear, sea calm, return 18:44; and one founds line of a different office, across the water at Washington Park in Portland, Oregon, kept word for word opposite the entry for a cast iron fountain of 1891 — see Lost & Found Notice No. 891, the Chiming Fountain, which the Line's commentary has read once, slowly, and twice. The return service runs in the same order it came, because a paper that has run since 1752 has run both directions, and the commentary, which is this sheet, is folded back in and kept with the table for the morning, the way the line is kept: in order, in Friesland, on the hour, and not once suspended.
