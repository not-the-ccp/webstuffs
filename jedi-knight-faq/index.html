JEDI ACADEMY RECORDS · FOREST MOON OF ENDOR · TRANSMISSION 7·7

The Academy's Question Register

Star Wars: Jedi Knight — frequently asked, answered in the old way: a question on one card, an answer on the next, filed in the order the trainees asked them. Instructor's desk, second floor, where the E-NAS sleeve lives in the bottom drawer.

Every answer below is written to stand alone, because the trainees read the cards shuffled, the way the archive was always going to be read — by someone who was not there and had to be told anyway.

Q·01 What exactly is the Jedi Knight series?

A series of first- and third-person shooter video games set in the fictional Star Wars expanded universe. The series began in 1995 with the release of Star Wars: Dark Forces for MS-DOS, Classic Mac OS and PlayStation, followed in 1997 by Star Wars Jedi Knight: Dark Forces II for Windows, then Jedi Knight in 1998, Jedi Knight II: Jedi Outcast in 2002, and Jedi Academy in 2003. Five games, one long argument about what it feels like to stand in a corridor with a plasma rifle and a lightsaber at the same time.

Q·02 Who is Kyle Katarn, and why does a shooting game follow him?

The series focuses primarily on Kyle Katarn, a former Imperial officer who becomes a mercenary working for the Rebel Alliance, and later a Jedi and instructor at Luke Skywalker's Jedi Academy. The first game meets him at the mercenary stage — a soldier who has already lost the Empire's version of the war and is selling what is left of his skills by the contract. The sequels follow him into the robes. That is the whole through-line of the series: a man who starts with a rifle and ends with the Force, one trigger pull at a time.

Q·03 When do the games take place?

The first game is set a year after the events of A New Hope, so the Imperial war machine in Dark Forces is still in full step and Katarn's defection lands in the middle of it. The sequels take place in the decade following Return of the Jedi, which is why the later games feel different: the Empire is no longer a government, only a rumour with a fleet, and the Jedi are back as a species rather than a ghost. Jedi Outcast and Jedi Academy sit on either side of the Battle of Yavin's tenth anniversary.

Q·04 What happens in Dark Forces II, the game Katarn "learns the ways of"?

Katarn is taken on as a bodyguard for his cousin Princess Leia and is captured by the Dark Jedi Jerec, whose cult is building a weapon — the Dark Tower — on the frozen station Voss. Katarn learns the ways of the Force under the tutelage of a hooded figure, escapes the cult, and burns the Tower down. It is the hinge of the series: the soldier from Dark Forces enters the game with a blaster rifle and exits it able to push doors with his mind. Westwood's build for Windows in 1997 is the version the archive keeps, because it is the version the trainees first asked about.

Q·05 What does the academy itself do in the later games?

In Jedi Outcast, the trainee is a padawan at Luke Skywalker's Jedi Academy on the forest moon of Endor — the same moon as the Rebel base in the film — when the apprentice of the fallen Jedi Darth Caedus burns a section of the campus and kills an entire class. In Jedi Academy, the trainee is the padawan, walking the same campus, and the game is built around the argument that the Force can be trained the way a rifle can be aimed: drills, repetition, and a teacher who keeps saying again. The two games are the same place from inside and outside the fire.

Q·06 The first Jedi Knight (1998) — where does it fit?

It fits between the soldier games and the Force games. Katarn, now a Jedi, takes a mercenary contract to recover an Imperial data vault on a planet he has not seen before, and the game's famous trick is that you can solve nearly every obstacle two ways: gun, or Force. The 1998 release also gave the series its multiplayer identity — Force Wars mode, where a team of Jedi plays against a team of soldiers — which is the mode the academy still runs on practice nights, and the reason the range on the third floor has a lightsaber rack where the spare magazines used to hang.

Q·07 There is a line in the bottom drawer of the instructor's desk. What is it?

The drawer holds the E-NAS sleeve — the survey file the archive keeps unopened on principle, because the last person who opened it to the end did not write anything down about what they saw. The trainees ask about it every season, and the answer is the file's own closing line, transcribed exactly as it sits in the sleeve, because the register's rule is that a line, once ruled, keeps its line:

Closed — sleeve E-NAS-347 19 Feb 1986 Atlantic surveyor, uncredited Harem ladder drawn in the same way, which the tribunal ruled admissible as custom and inadmissible as evidence.

That is all the sleeve has left in it. The 1986 date predates the series by nine years and reaches no planet on any map the academy owns; the surveyor is uncredited, as the line insists, and the Harem ladder — whatever it is — is drawn "in the same way" as something else that is no longer in the drawer. What survives is the tribunal's ruling, which is also the archive's ruling: the line may stand as a matter of custom, but it may not be offered as evidence of anything. So the register files it under kept, not proved, exactly as it files the kiosk plate at the Adelaide Oval matchday fault library, Grand Final 17 April 2021, where an unexplained record is kept rather than erased.

Q·08 Why keep a shooting-game register at all?

Because the series is one long lesson in what a ledger keeps: every game records a state of the war — the Empire at full step in 1995's Dark Forces, the cult on Voss in 1997's Dark Forces II, the academy in fire in 2002, the academy rebuilt in 2003. A reading, once ruled, keeps its line. The register is indexed from the front page of the shelf, and a keeper two desks over, at the Correspondents' Board, Thread No. 54, files the same principle under a different dynasty: the one who was excluded, who kept the ledger anyway.
