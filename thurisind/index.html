SIRMIA HYDROMETIC & METEORIC OFFICE · GEPIDIA · FOLIO 12, SHELF C, ROOM OF THE FOUR RIVERS

Thurisind

Weather Almanac Entry 560/044 · Winter Observation, 3–9 February 560 · Sirmium Station, on the Sava

Latin form Turisindus · King of the Gepids c. 548–560 · Station elevation 61 m · 44°57′ N, 19°20′ E

The Observation

The season is written under the name of Thurisind, for he was the king of the land the Sava runs through when this winter came down on it, and the almanac keeps its winters under their kings, as the Sava keeps its water under its banks. Thurisind is the penultimate king of the Gepids, and the last winter of his reign fell on this station in a shape we have not seen since the coup: cold early, cold long, and the river high from the second night. He took the kingdom from King Elemund by force of arms about the year 548 and drove the king's son into exile, and a throne won that way shows up in the weather book, we have always said: the record for his years is full of hard winters and full of corrections.

From the second of February the barometer sat at 978 mm and would not lift. On the third, north-northwest at a constant gale from dusk to the fourth watch; snow to nine inches on the river road by morning, and the Sava at 3.4 m above ordinary, which is high but not yet a word we print. On the fifth the wind veered to the east and the temperature took nine degrees out of the day, and that is the day the whole station stood at the bank with its pots. By the seventh the Sava had reached 5.1 m, the lowest stone line of the old Roman quay was under, and the fish were up at the mill gate as if invited. On the ninth the wind dropped, the frost set, and the river stood at 4.7 m with the ice coming in from upstream in slabs we counted by the fire — twenty-three slabs — which is a count we keep because some year the count will matter.

The king's people came down to the quay the fifth night, when the wind was east and the ice was starting. They said nothing about the weather; they were not there for the weather. The scribe notes only that the lamps along the Roman street burned all night, and that a man with the mark of the crown was seen going upriver at dawn, toward the exile road, and the record is closed on that line.

The Lombard Margins

Twice in the fortnight the drum was stopped to write something that is not weather, which the office permits when the hand is steady. The first: from the north the Lombard scouts, the men of King Audoin, who crossed into Pannonia and have kept their camps on our frontier the whole reign — every winter since the crossing has been a winter of fires on the north horizon, and the observer marks them in the sky column with a single dot, as one marks a planet one does not mean to name. The second: the Byzantine post from the south, the empire that sits resentful of the king and his people and writes us letters with the weather in them — the rains have broken the passes, your roads are mud, the army is where the army is — letters the office files under NOT WEATHER because it has to file them somewhere.

Both margins are kept in the folio. A winter is a winter; what walks through it is recorded at the edge of the page.

Instrument Note, Shelf C

The office's barograph shelf is old Roman joinery, taken up from the quay warehouse in the second year of the reign, and it is the oldest piece of furniture in the Room of the Four Rivers. It holds the month drums of the pressure record, one drum per month, the paper wound and the pens fed by the same clock that runs eleven seconds slow — a clock the office has no intention of repairing, being the only clock in Gepidia that has never once contradicted the water. The six drawers open on a rack and keep their own drums. When a month is done the drum is pulled, the paper cut off at the last line, and the drum kept in its drawer until the folio calls for it, which for February of this year was the third of March, when the scribe took the drum down to find, on the back edge of the paper, that the fifth-night pressure had been written as 980 and corrected to 981 with a hand that was not the observer's. The correction stands. The hand is not named in the folio. The drawer was shut the way it was opened, which is all the office says about it.

Kept at Sirmium, formerly Sirmium, now the town on the Sava that keeps our station; the entry is entered under Thurisind and will stay under Thurisind, penultimate of the Gepid kings, the last line of the folio reading: the river keeps its water, the shelf keeps its drums, and the winter goes where the winter goes.
