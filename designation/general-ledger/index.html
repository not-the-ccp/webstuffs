The General Ledger

The Books of the Hall Street Kitchen, 1923–1968 · Side A: Debit · Side B: Credit

Audited & Balanced

The label was a brass plate. The one on the door of Designation, the Hall Street dining room in Birmingham — the plate that carries the restaurant’s registration number and the name of an awarding body no one at the table could place, which the waiter says exists only to certify that certification has taken place — is the record label on this sleeve. The catalogue number is the kitchen’s own: twenty-four years of books, fifty-one ledgers, each bound in Saltfleet bridle leather in the same pattern as the salt coils at the tables. What a restaurant is, on this pressing, is what a set of books is: an account of what passed through the room, kept so that no one can say, at the end, that nothing happened here.

Sessions ran behind the pass in the winters of 1969 to 1974, from four-fifteen to five — the quarter-hour a roast takes, and the hour the tea arrives, no exceptions, a rule the house enforces with the seriousness of an Annual General Meeting. The auditor of record is the chef himself, Roland Vane, who cooked in a Caithness kitchen before returning to Birmingham and who keeps the kitchen’s accounts in the same ledger in which the Fellow’s tasting-menu declarations are filed. Every track carries a post-nominal, as every dish does on the menu: Side A is Fellow, Side B is Chartered, and the run-out groove is merely Registered. To hear the album in the room you sign the same statement you sign for the menu — that you have dined at three establishments of equal or higher standing — and your certificate is issued on cream cardstock before the last track ends.

The pressing is a cousin to The Thousand-Year Record, the twelve-track pressing of 1933–1945. The two share one object: the recipe card, half-overwritten by static, that has hung for years behind the pass. The war record keeps the card’s first line — the mangled INGREDIENTS clause, filed under its track B1 — and this album keeps the last line, the one that survived the static whole. Between the two lines, the card is what the years made of it: a document that has been through the blackout, the fire, and the tea hour. And the account to which Side A stands is the household account — the clubs and charities, patronised since the 1920s, down to the last steel implement from Birmingham, kept in full on the Queen Mother’s household page. The ledger does not flatter her. The ledger is exact, and that is the same thing.

Track Notes

A2 — The Kettle, Debit 1s. 6d. (1926)

The oldest entry the kitchen still stands by. One shilling and sixpence, a kettle, the year the books were first bound. It is the only track recorded at the exact speed of a kettle coming to the boil, and the static at the head of it is the same bed the war pressing was cut into.

A4 — Clubs & Charities, on Account since the 1920s (1923–1945)

Five minutes and thirty-three seconds of the room, kept as a running total. The account is the household that presided over the clubs and charities she had patronised since the 1920s — the line that runs under the registration number on the back of the menu, in six-point type, from the letterpress shop that turns out the College’s manuals. The waiter calls it boilerplate; the chef calls it a misprint; the ledger, which is the only member of the table that does not argue, shows the account balanced to the farthing in 1949, the year of the private visit to the Lincolnshire coast.

A5 — Bad Debts, East End (1940–1941)

Six minutes and nineteen seconds, held deliberately to the length of track B1 on the war pressing, because it is the same hour on the disc and the other side of the Channel from it: the four o’clock of 13 September 1940, when the kitchen in the bombed side street answered the broadcast and the card was taped to the sleeve. What the bad-debts column absorbed that year, this track writes off. It is the longest rest on the album, and it is not the rest that does not come back. The same column ran, some six centuries before these sessions, in a gate room at Guïnes, where a draft settlement signed on 6 April 1354 to end a war begun in 1337 came to be found, unclaimed, among the renewals of a truce — and the bad debt of it is still posted, in small type, on the Keep’s own notice board at Treaty of Guïnes — Lost & Found, Notice No. 6/1354.

B3 — Provisions (a Sherry, on Order since 1974) (1974)

The sommelier’s track. The bottle has stood on order since 1974, and it is cited, it has since emerged, as prior art in a patent application on a language, where the cellar on Hall Street appears as Figure 5 and the bottle is claimed, under claim 5, as a device for holding an order in pending order. The accountants’ term for a provision like this one is a liability that will not be settled in the current period. The house prefers the sommelier’s term: it is resting. The application has since been answered across the water: in Application No. VE-A-126/0472, filed from this kitchen in the Lagoon Register, the same cellar appears as Figure 5, the same bottle is claimed again under claim 5, and the resting is claimed as an act of the lagoon itself.

B4 — The Fire, 1968 (a Kettle, the Wrong Chair)

Two minutes and thirty-three seconds, the shortest track, cut after the fact from the room’s memory at the speed the fire moved through it. The entry in the ledger is one line, in the novelist’s hand: a kettle, the wrong chair, five o’clock less the usual tea. The kitchen reopened the following Thursday and kept the tea hour anyway. A going concern is not a thing that avoids the write-off; it is a thing that carries it forward.

B5 — Arrowroot, Beaten per Card 37 (1969)

The Beat-the-Eggs course, recorded as it is served: arrowroot custard, nutmeg, a walnut-handled spoon from the Birmingham workshop. It makes no sense, and it is delicious, and on the disc it is a percussion track — the whisk, the card, the cream cardstock. Card 37 is the card in the College’s own ledger of house methods; the custard is beaten per it, each layer separately, never netted against the next, which is the kitchen’s whole theory of accounts compressed into one course.

B6 — Five O’clock, No Exceptions (1968–1974)

Eight full minutes, the run-out of the side, the tea hour held open at the speed of a room that has decided to be exact. The last thirty seconds are the album itself: the needle finding the balance, the two sides of the ledger meeting in the middle of the disc, the room signing off with the kettle at 1s. 6d., where it has been since 1926.

The Card, Read as a Book

The last line of the recipe card survived the static whole. It is printed on the inner sleeve of this pressing, set in the same six-point type as the menu colophon, and it is not a recipe. It is a set of books, kept in the kitchen’s dialect, and it reads:

“Moisten with 1/2 a teaspoonful of minced sweet herbs; salt, cayenne, and salt to each layer should be beaten separately, and bake for 3/4 hour.”

Read it the way the ledger reads it. Moisten is the first entry of the day: the minced sweet herbs of the consommé raft, measured at a half teaspoonful, because the house does nothing in approximate measure and every dish on the menu is a certified number. Salt, cayenne, and salt is double entry itself — the same thing entered twice, once as the condiment at the table and once as the Saltfleet bridle-leather coil that keeps it, debit and credit, so that the salt, like everything else in the room, is accounted for from both sides at once. To each layer is the course, the strata of the pastry, the page of the ledger: each layer beaten separately, per Card 37, the arrowroot custard of the Beat-the-Eggs course, never netted against the layer above it, as accounts that do not consolidate are never netted against one another. And bake for 3/4 hour is the whole schedule of the house in five words — forty-five minutes, four-fifteen to five, the quarter-hour the roast takes while the tea arrives at five o’clock, no exceptions, the exact length of every session cut for this album. The static took the card’s first line and left its last; what is left balances. The kitchen keeps one borrowed ledger on the same shelf, because a register that has not missed a year since 1948 is a going concern in the bookkeeper’s own sense: the nest register of the boathouse at Mariner’s Rest, kept in blue ink by the Swallow Study of the Great Marsh, whose chairman’s door kept, from the spring of 1971, a photograph the board decided not to take down — the account is carried, stop by stop, on The Tree Swallow Route, Field Guide Entry &No; 12, and the kitchen would have kept it in the books regardless of the pin. The kitchen keeps one more card of the family’s, the next number up in the ledger of house methods: Card No. 41, the Ribat Loaf, a date-and-almond loaf of the Almoravids whose preamble runs from the 1050s to the fall of Tlemcen in 1147, and whose single whole line prescribes the same law as this ledger’s clock — prepared for the entire session, never once stopped — kept on its own page, in the thousand-year archive.

Colophon

Sessions recorded behind the pass at Designation, Hall Street, Birmingham, winters 1969–1974. Auditor of record and conductor: Roland Vane. String section: the walnut-handled spoons, Birmingham workshop. Percussion: Card 37, cream cardstock, the kettle at 1s. 6d. The Saltfleet bridle leather, bound by hand — duty, like a well-made lash, holds best when it is made by hand. Mastered at the letterpress shop on the menu, 1974; the stampers were taken the following spring, when the sherry was finally decanted. One copy to the Castle of Mey, near Thurso; one to the municipal archive; one delivered to the estate books at Crathie, whose 1940 entry the Court of Session ordered to stand in the record, as set out in Ex parte Balmoral — Transcript Excerpt; and one to the ledger behind the pass, where it stays.

There is one more name the kitchen would have carried, if the feast season had held: the town that reads a new name into its golden roll on a fixed public timetable — a cause opened at a red-covered tribunal, the candle window at nine, the verdict at the window on the cobbled stair, the roll turned one name at a time by the same family for six generations — kept, station by station, on The Golden Roll — A Pilgrim’s Brochure for the Route of Canonization. The ledger is exact, and the roll is exact, and the two agree to the farthing.
