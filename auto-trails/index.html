Motors & Routes Gallery · Case 4 · Placard No. 31

The Red Band on the Pole

Auto trail marker — painted utility-pole segment, hand-cut trail sign, brush and tin of vermilion, c. 1913

Accession No.1961.44.7
CreditGift of the Huish family, Saint Louis, 1961
Gallery4, east wall, between the gas lantern and the 1919 speed chart

On display: the original pine pole segment from the Lincoln Highway at the Little Miami crossing, its red-and-white band painted in the summer of 1913; the cut-wood sign reading KEENEY TRAIL — EAST, still smelling faintly of shellac; a hogs-hair brush with a bent handle; and the dented tin of vermilion the marker carried between poles. The flyleaf is mounted beneath, on its original nail.

The system of auto trails was an informal network of marked routes that existed in the United States and Canada in the early part of the twentieth century. Marked with colored bands on utility poles, the trails were intended to help travellers in the early days of the automobile. Auto trails were usually marked and sometimes maintained by organizations of private individuals — motor clubs, boosters' societies, and in several states women's clubs — rather than by government. At the 1913 convention in Saint Louis, the good-roads men of four clubs proposed a marked route from New York to San Francisco; it opened with a parade in 1914 and was named, at a dinner at a small Ohio hotel, for the president who had once been an engineer on the Pacific Railroad. Others multiplied fast: the New York–Chicago Trail, the Great Northern Auto Trail from Toronto to Chicago, the All-America Highway of 1920, and hundreds of regionals whose promoters vanished with the paint money, to the point that anyone with enough paint and the will to do so could set up a trail. Trails were not usually linked to road improvements, although the Lincoln Highway Association pushed, pole by pole and lawsuit by lawsuit, until Congress and the states could no longer look away.

The flyleaf nailed beneath the sign was printed by a booster of the Keeney Trail and is not in any hand, which is why we keep it. It promises the motorist “surety, and the road beyond the last town,” and its last line, in the small type nobody has ever explained, reads: “That is to say, the bow, and the lamp oil, and the sea behind you is completed in your hand.” The gallery has kept it word for word since 1961. No keeper has settled it; the placard is not meant to.

When the numbered highways of the 1920s took over the job, the bands stayed on the poles until the poles fell. This segment fell in the 1930s and went to a barn in Hamilton County, Ohio, where a family kept it because a red band is a red band and somebody painted it by hand in the evening. The trail's own route can be followed, county by county and odd marker and all, in the thread on The Scioto Ledger, Franklin County Edition, and the habit of reading a painted road for its small unexplained lines continues in the thread on North Carolina Highway 157, from Guess Road to Hurdle Mills Road. The case itself sits between the gas lantern and the speed chart; the whole collection returns to the gallery index.

Case 4 is on display year-round. Do not touch the tin; the vermilion is still wet in it, in the way things stay wet in this gallery.
