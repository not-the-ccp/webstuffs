The Cinema of China

Official tourist brochure — three threads, two of the world's great studios, and one box office that has grown four digits past the silver dollar.

Market rank 2012: No. 2 in the world Gross 2016: CN¥45.71 billion (US$6.58 billion) Threads: three Largest production complex on earth

Welcome to the Three-Thread Circuit

The cinema of China is the filmmaking and film industry of mainland China, and it is only the first of three distinct historical threads of Chinese-language cinema, this one running alongside the cinema of Hong Kong and the cinema of Taiwan — three threads, the circuit's keepers like to say, that share the same ink and pull on it in different directions. What the traveller is invited to see on this circuit is the thread that became, quite simply, the largest: China is the home of the largest movie and drama production complex and film studios in the world, the Oriental Movie Metropolis and Hengdian World Studios, and in 2012 the country became the second-largest market in the world by box office receipts behind only the United States. The ledger figures for the circuit's current season are printed on every ticket stub: in 2016 the gross box office in China was CN¥45.71 billion (US$6.58 billion), which the circuit office converts, for the traveller's comfort, into “about nine million silver dollars of the old district”s year, at the old exchange, which the old district keeps for sentiment.” Hollywood has noticed. China has become a major hub of business for the Hollywood studios: sound stages in the Metropolis carry American names on their booking sheets, the release dates are planned across the Pacific, and the circuit treats this not as an invasion but as a weather pattern. The circuit runs the whole length of the thread, from the old quarter where the star was first set on a roof to the studio cities at its far end.

Thread I: The Old Film Quarter, Shanghai — Where the Thread Started

Every thread on this circuit is walked backwards to its beginning, and the beginning is the quarter where, in 1922, Zheng Zhegu and Ren Jinping co-founded the Mingxing Film Company at 39 Hengshan Road. The circuit's first day is the walk the district has kept for a century — the Zheng Zhegu Trail, official tourist brochure, six stages and one premiere that never quite ends — from the whitewashed house where he was born in 1880 down to the studio gate with the brass floorboard worn smooth by one heel. The star on the roof that Mingxing set above its own gate is still the circuit's welcome mark, and the 19:45 premiere lanterns still come on in the old order: first the gate, last the roof.

The heart of Day One is the Xinmin Library, Stop IV of the old trail, where the reading room is preserved exactly as the publisher's hand left it. The library's standing notice hangs by the east wall in four rows, and the three you will expect are the old ones: no telegraphing from the window seat, no sleeping in the east wall, and no film until the ink has dried. The fourth row was added in the winter the district lost its man, and the librarians keep it word for word, with no explanation and no footnote: “The fourth row of the library's standing notice: no telegraphing from the head of the province.” No one who has read the district's records can say for certain what it was forbidding — messages to the head of the province, messages from it, or the head of the province's own telegraphing, which is a matter of grammar and not of evidence. The librarians' ruling matches the one the old studio ledger applied to its own inexplicable line: a notice that arrives complete and explains nothing is kept exactly as it arrived. The keepers at Glasnevin, in the Four-Day Ireland, will tell you the fourth row is always the row that stands on its own — they say it about their refectory floorboards — and the librarians here have taken the saying into the house rules without credit and without apology.

Thread II: Hengdian World Studios, Zhejiang — A Country of Sets

Ninety minutes by circuit shuttle from Hangzhou, on the bank of a river that was renamed for the occasion in the local gazetteer, stands Hengdian World Studios, one half of the largest production complex and studio group in the world. The official count for the 2017 season is 81.7 acres of standing sets, 82 principal constructions, and one weather department. The Western Street runs a full mile and keeps its own clock half an hour behind the town, on the theory that drama runs late; the Water Village has its own fishing fleet, and the fish are, the guides confirm, real, because the close-ups demanded it; and the Imperial City of China — gates, palace axes, a moat of genuine river water — was raised in a single season for a dynasty epic and kept, as all the best sets are kept, because the next dynasty was already in the script. Overnight rates inside the Imperial City run by the gate: the outer ward is 480 yuan a night, the inner ward 1,900, and the emperor's bedchamber is not let, which the booking office explains is “maintained for the weather.” A thousand and forty crew work the complex on a production day, and the circuit allows two full days: one for the sets, one for the shooting of one of your own, which is offered, at any length you can bear, to the traveller who arrives with a story.

Thread III: The Oriental Movie Metropolis, Pudong — A Studio Built as a City

The circuit ends where the thread now runs: the Oriental Movie Metropolis in Pudong, Shanghai, a studio city of 1.18 square kilometres in which the studio was planned first and the city around it — a place of sound stages, backlots, a river wall five hundred metres long, and a train platform that appears in films but also, on Tuesdays, carries the staff. This is the hub where the Hollywood studios do their China business: the booking sheets list American productions alongside the domestic epics, the dailies travel both ways across the Pacific, and the Metropolis keeps a glass case in its visitor hall with the single cable that opened the era — a studio in the United States, asking for a stage, winter season, no preference on sun. The case has no label beyond “first,” which the curators consider sufficient. From the Metropolis roof, on a clear day, the traveller can see the old quarter's river road, and on the roof at its end, if the day is very clear, the star.

Tickets, Rates & Provisions

The full five-day circuit, all three threads plus the 19:45 lanterns on Day One, is 8,800 yuan; Thread I alone is walked at the old trail's own rates, which have not changed and are quoted in the old silver for sentiment; the Imperial City inner ward is 1,900 yuan a night and not to be argued. The circuit office reminds travellers that the numbers on the ticket stub — CN¥45.71 billion, US$6.58 billion, rank No. 2 — are the gross figures of 2016, kept on the stub because the district believes a traveller should carry the weight of the market they are walking through. Provisions on the circuit are the old provisions plus the new: plum cake and the grey tea at the library, the real fish at the Water Village, and one rule carried unchanged from the library's fourth row — no telegraphing from the head of the province — which the circuit now extends, without footnote, to all messaging, of every kind, from the emperor's bedchamber.

In the Company of Other Long Roads

The circuit's gate office keeps the itineraries it trusts, in the same flat type as this brochure. The first and the oldest is the Zheng Zhegu Trail through the Shanghai Film District, third studio edition, which this circuit prints as Thread I and which the circuit's keepers will not allow to be re-paginated, because a trail that starts a thread gets the first page. The second is the Glasnevin leaf of the Four-Day Ireland walking guide — the field of four acres where an abbot named Berchán set one row of refectory floorboards out on its own and never walked away; the circuit's librarians consult the Glasnevin keepers' ruling on fourth rows before every season, and the two houses agree on everything except the spelling of the notice, which is a matter they have learned to leave alone. And for the traveller who wants the whole country behind the circuit — the tea, the Yellow Road, the river, and the proper ten-person table — the office keeps the Celestial Empire visitor's brochure, Canton & the Great Road, which this circuit's welcome mark has hung beside since the first edition and which, the office notes, has never once been out of print at the gate.

Departure

Exit is by the river road at the end of Day V, the way the thread runs out: the way it began. The sign there reads what the ledger already says: the star was set, not painted; the market is No. 2 and moving; the notice keeps four rows, and the fourth one keeps itself. The circuit thanks you for walking all three threads, wishes you clear weather for the roof, and reminds you that the lanterns come on again at 19:45, every night, in the old order — first the gate, last the roof.
