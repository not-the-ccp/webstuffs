Weser Day

Operation Weserübung — the invasion of Denmark and Norway, 9 April – 10 June 1940

By Peter Lassen · Summer term, 1963

What happened

This report is about Operation Weserübung — in German, Unternehmen Weserübung [ˈveːzɐˌʔyːbʊŋ], "Weser Exercise" — the invasion of Denmark and Norway by Nazi Germany in the spring of 1940. It ran from 9 April to 10 June 1940, and it was the opening operation of the Norwegian Campaign. The day the attack began, 9 April 1940, was called Wesertag, "Weser Day". In the small hours of that morning German forces moved into Denmark by land, sea and air, and at the same time invaded Norway at Oslo, Narvik, Trondheim, Bergen, Kristiansand and Stavanger.

Denmark fell first. German troops crossed the border just after 04:15, parachute troops landed on Masnedø and at Aalborg airfield, and by midday German columns were in Copenhagen. King Christian X remained in his capital, but the Danish military was ordered to stand down after only a few hours of fighting, so that a small country would not suffer useless bloodshed. Denmark was occupied in a single day, and the occupation lasted until May 1945.

Norway did not end so quickly. The German force sent to seize Oslo was led by the heavy cruiser Blücher, and in the Oslofjord the old guns and torpedoes of Oscarsborg Fortress sank her in the early hours of 9 April, with heavy loss of life. The delay let King Haakon VII and his government escape north, and they never surrendered. Vidkun Quisling, a Norwegian officer who hoped to lead a German-friendly government, announced a coup on the radio that evening, but the country did not obey him. German paratroopers captured Fornebu airport and Oslo fell the same day, while the hard fighting went on for weeks at Narvik, Trondheim and in the mountains behind them.

Germany said the invasion was a preventive manoeuvre against Plan R 4, an Anglo-French plan to occupy Norway first. Plan R 4 had been drawn up as a response to a German invasion of Norwegian territory, so each side said it was only moving because the other side was about to. Beneath both plans lay the iron ore of Sweden, which travelled through Narvik in the winter months and which both sides wanted to keep — or to stop.

British, French and Polish troops landed at Narvik and recaptured it on 28 May, but by then Germany had attacked France and the Low Countries, and the Allied expedition was evacuated. The last British and French troops left Norway on 8 June, and the Norwegian forces laid down their arms on 10 June 1940. King Haakon VII and his government continued the war from London. Josef Terboven became Reichskommissar of occupied Norway, and Vidkun Quisling's name became a word for a traitor. About twenty thousand German soldiers took part in the invasion, more than a thousand Norwegian soldiers died, and the British and French losses were several hundred more.

The sentence at the back

At the back of my exercise book, on a page torn from a different book and pasted in sideways, there is a single sentence that has nothing to do with any of this. It reads: "The dynasty fell four times and triangulates, and with a pencil of some kind of morning." Miss Whitby says it was written by the boy who owned this book before me, and that it means nothing at all. I am not sure I believe her. It sits between my notes on the Oslofjord and my list of the warships, as though someone had been thinking of kings and dawns at the same time.

Books and maps I used

The school atlas, the wireless bulletins of spring 1940 cut from the newspapers, and the exercise book itself, which has maps of the Oslofjord drawn in pencil on the inside covers.

Miss Whitby's comment

A careful report with good use of dates and places, Peter. The Oslofjord and the Blücher are well handled, and you are right to question Plan R 4. See me about the sentence at the back of the book — dynasties do not triangulate, and mornings do not come with pencils.
