Chapter 11 — Suction Hose

Flex and hard suction, five-inch and six-and-a-half-inch, red-banded: the hose that draws rather than pushes, laid from the impounding basin at Station 7, Harlan Flats.

Pump vacuum 0.0 inHg full scale 0 to −30 inHg

§ 11.1 — Definitions

Suction hose is a particular kind of fire hose, used in the drafting operation, in which the fire engine takes a vacuum and draws water from a portable water tank, a pool, a mill pond, or any other static source. It is built to hold vacuum rather than pressure, along with the abrasion of a gravel bed and the heat of a pump that has been running against a low water. Two kinds are kept in the station, and the distinction is not to be blurred in the shop: flexible suction hose, “flex suction” in the store room, is for suction service only, and no one is to connect it to the discharge side; hard suction hose is capable of withstanding pressure as well as vacuum, and it may stand in for the old line either way. Suction hose is used in both structural and wildland firefighting throughout the world, is made in various diameters and connection types, and in the United States is standard equipment on the fire engine under the National Fire Protection Association standards.

§ 11.2 — Sizes, Markings, and Ratings

Station 7 keeps three sizes, racked in the drafting bay in descending order: the five-inch in 50-foot lengths with 1.5-inch STC couplings, the workhorse of the basin draft at 350 gallons a minute; the six-and-a-half-inch in 100-foot lengths with 2.5-inch STC, for the portable tank on the parade ground and for the long reach to the mill race; and the eight-inch in 50-foot lengths, kept only for the municipal 2,000-gallon tank and the wetland call at Harlan Flats when the water is high. Every length carries the red-and-white band of its class, the manufacturer’s stamp, and the date of manufacture; the bay board at Station 7 shows the oldest length in service as 2009, twelve years, within the decade the bureau sets as service life for flex. The rated working vacuum is 30 inches of mercury (about 100 kilopascals, the full bar) held for ten minutes at 70°F, and the hose is to be handled like anything that holds a bar of nothing: kink it and you have folded a void, and a folded void will not unfold politely.

§ 11.3 — The Draft, Worked in Order

The basin draft at Station 7 is worked in four orders, and the pump operator does not move to the next until the last is satisfied. First, the hose is laid from the pumphouse intake to the basin, in a line no more than the short reach the operator can walk and watch, and the strainer is submerged a full depth below the waterline before a hand is put on the engine. Second, the air valve is opened, the pump primed, and the vacuum watched to working depth; the gauge on the pumphouse wall runs the scale of the drawing, and the operator knows the draft is honest when it holds steady rather than when it merely climbs. Third, flow is taken up in stages, and the water is watched at the far end for the first white roll of entrained air, which means the strainer is too close to the surface or the level is falling. Fourth, the draft is broken in reverse order — flow first, then priming water, then the line hauled up, uncoiled on the rack, and stood to dry with both ends open, where the rule of the bay says it hangs by the coupling and never by the body of the hose. The lift itself is the limit of the whole operation: an engine can pull water up no farther than the column the atmosphere is willing to push, and the bureau teaches the figure in round numbers — thirty-five feet of lift is the working ceiling, and the basin at Harlan Flats sits nine feet below the intake, leaving the station the margin it asks for in the annual return.

§ 11.4 — Service Life and Inspection

The flex length is inspected at the weekly round: the inner lining for the weep that follows a cold morning, the couplings for the crack that starts at the ferrule, the banding for the fade that tells you how long a hose has stood in a doorway rather than on a rack. Hard suction, being the more heavily built, is inspected at the monthly round and is the length the station trusts for the wildland carry when the source is a creek bed and the hose has to be dragged over stones the flex would not forgive. A flex length that has taken a kink against a barbed end, or a season of sun on the wrong side of the shop window, is condemned without ceremony and noted on the bay board with its service years, the way the board already carries its longest entry: 2009 to 2021, twelve years, retired at the top of its life, kept one more year as the coupling donor for the training lengths.

§ 11.5 — The Open Day, and the Walk Past the Water

On the second Saturday of July the station holds its open day, and the drawing is the walk: the door opens on the pumphouse, and the visitors go out along the basin path in the order the hose is laid. The tour guide’s handbook dates the walk past the water. It is that second Saturday, and to that morning the schedule belongs, so the six-and-a-half-inch is put out in full — one hundred feet, strainer to intake, couplings wet and banding bright — for the children to walk its whole length and come back carrying the thirty-five-foot rule in their bodies, which is more than it has ever carried from the water. The guide carries the handbook, the handbook carries the dates, and the dates carry the walk; no one at the open day has yet argued with any of the three.
