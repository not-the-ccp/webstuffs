Tuesday, 12 March 1991 — the review arrives

Eleven days of service, and this morning the Q dining pages came to the counter folded inside a bread bag. Graeme Kay sat at the second table, facing the water, and has given us two forks out of five for the kitchen — lacking the personality to qualify as anything more that an anonymous part of a not very memorable whole — while the flatbread, the anise, and the braised eel of the second visit stand trial separately and are acquitted. I have read it four times. Dot read it once, the way Eustace reads the grease-pencil sign above the register, and went back to the grill. The verdict of the room, so far, is the anise: the same, unexplained, and correct. For the menu as actually served — eleven courses on the back of a water bill, eleven pounds, the pigeon dish called “the second time” — the Q dining-page review of Swamp Terrorists, Grim – Stroke – Disease, is where the whole fortnight is already written down better than I can.

Wednesday, 13 March 1991 — what the Trust is

The flatbread man asked me again at the counter, after his third visit, what the Trust actually is. I told him industrial. Industrial is the name of a kind of music — a subgenre of experimental music that emerged in the mid-to late 1970s, primarily inspired by industrial society itself, while drawing on the avant-garde and the early electronic genres: musique concrète, tape music, noise, sound collage. The term was coined in 1976 by Monte Cazazza and adopted by Throbbing Gristle with the founding of Industrial Records. The early names I can verify from the crate in the store room are NON and Cabaret Voltaire; by the late 1970s the list had grown to Clock DVA, Nocturnal Emissions, Einstürzende Neubauten, SPK, Nurse with Wound, and Z’EV. During the 1980s, after the disbanding of Throbbing Gristle, the current spread, and what comes out of the room at 14 Marsh Road is a late descendant of it. The band of the same name that gave the house its name plays on Fridays. The menu is the set list. Grim, Stroke, Disease: the names were set in the same week the room opened, and I have not changed a syllable of them, and no one else will.

Thursday, 14 March 1991 — the slip under the till

Under the till, this morning, a slip of paper. Grease pencil. Not the hand of the sign above the register. One line only: “It is this that shoots through me, and influences me in the circular.” I asked Eustace. He shrugged and poured me something with anise in it and did not tell me what it was, which is the correct way to do it. But I understand the slip. It is how the loop works: the grinder on one tape, the broadcast on the other, the sound comes back into the room changed, and the room gives it back to the loop changed again; the circle keeps its turning and every pass is slightly off. That is what Dot does to the eel, and what the bar does to a man with two anises in him. I put the slip back under the till, where it will keep counsel with the sign and the anise.

The ledger keeps the counter’s own position to the minute, in the manner of Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 — latitude and longitude as angles, the datum re-checked before you commit. Our datum is the green lamp and the water, and both are re-checked every evening before the menu is nailed to the wall, and the standing order is printed in smaller type at the foot of every menu: Be back before the tide turns. The tapes for Friday come from the record shop on Halstrom, past the exchange shelf with the four short stories, at the far end of the Steakley Walk quarter; I go at six, I pay cash, and I do not ask which side is industrial. A placard reached the Trust by post today, framed in grey, from a museum that keeps a studio firework display from a New York television comedy; the note says it was staged to show up a rival who had bought a summer of his own — the museum has catalogued it under Fireworks, Case 47 — and I have pinned it beside the menu where it can be read over. Webstuffs home is at the gate, if you want the rest of the collection.

Friday, 15 March 1991 — the black parcel

The parcel from the record shop on Halstrom came by hand this evening, and it was not tapes. One cloth-bound book, black, with a title set in red: Liber AL vel Legis. No author’s name on the cover, only a number. I read the back, and the council read it with me in the cellar, and Eustace poured, and Dot did not come down and was counted present by the sound of the grill. The author is a man called Aleister Crowley, born Edward Alexander Crowley in Royal Leamington Spa in 1875 and dead on 1 December 1947: occultist, magician, mountaineer, poet, novelist, painter, and the prophet of his own new age. There is a tape in the parcel too, and the tape has a number, and the number is 777. The council has put the whole matter in writing — the minutes of session seven, held in the cellar last night — and the book is in the cellar now, spine in, where it will keep counsel with the slip under the till and the anise. The standing order has not changed, and is re-checked as ever: be back before the tide turns.
