Old Montréal · A Walking Tour in Eight Stops · 9:00 to 12:30, any dry day

Moses Judah Hays
1799 – 12 November 1861

The man who gave the city its water and then its watch — two public services, one pair of boots, and a circuit of two and a half kilometres that the wardens still walk on the first Sunday of October.

Businessman · engineer of record of the Montreal Waterworks · Chief of Police 1845–1861 · circuit length 2.5 km · 34 hydrants, 26 lamp-posts, one visitor’s sheet

Before Setting Out

Meet at the iron rail on Place d’Armes, below the tower of Saint-Jacques, at nine on the morning bell. The tour is made for a city that had not yet invented most of what it is now: Montréal in 1855, a population of 65,000 climbing the long slope of the mountain, with a main laid under Saint-Paul Street that Hays had argued for in open council in 1847 and laid stone for in the summer of 1849. He is the reason the tour is possible in the first place. He established and managed the first water-works in the city — the 1,500-foot main, the reservoir at the head of de la Gauchetière, the first thirty-four hydrants in the lower town — and in the spring of 1845 he was appointed the city’s chief of police, a chair he held until his death on 12 November 1861. Water and watchmen, the two things a city can see and the one thing it cannot. The wardens who keep this route wear the old grey overalls; they know the hydrants by number and the watch-houses by the scratches on their doors.

Bring a flat hat and a closed book. The sheet of the tour is read aloud, stop by stop, in the wardens’ own hand, and the final entry on it — a line no one has ever been able to explain — is read exactly as it stands. The tour does not delete entries.

Stop I

Place d’Armes, the Iron Rail

9:00 · depart · 0.0 km

The rail is 1838 iron, the same year the fire of the previous August convinced the council that the city could not depend on pails and the river alone. Hays was forty-nine in 1848 and already had the contract for the lower-town line; the warden points out, in the paving stones at the rail’s foot, the two bolt-holes where the surveyor’s chain was anchored the morning the main was set out — 1,500 feet, east and west, beneath the whole street of Saint-Paul.

Stop II

The Reservoir, 43 de la Gauchetière

9:25 · up the c&oeuml;te · 0.4 km

The first water-works stood where the warden’s hand rests on the stone: a masonry cistern, twenty-two feet square, holding 1.4 million gallons at the full of the winter draw. Hays ran the works himself for the first two years — the ledger, in his hand, records every inch of the valve stem, every leak found, every hydrant opened or shut. The council paid him his salary and, in the same breath, made him responsible for everything between the cistern and the last hydrant in the lower town. He signed the ledger M. J. Hays, engineer of record until 1853.

Stop III

The Main Under Saint-Paul Street

9:55 · down the slope · 1.1 km

You will not see the main, and the tour does not pretend to. What you see are the covers: eleven iron hatches, one every hundred and forty feet, painted the colour of the street so that a man could walk a hundred years over them without knowing. Through them the warden has walked the full length of the main, in the dry season, once a year since 1911. The iron is 4¼-inch cast pipe, riveted and leaded, and it has never burst. Hays chose the 4¼-inch size himself, in a 1848 memorandum the council still files in the vault: a main wide enough for a fire, narrow enough that no man’s house could drink the whole city dry.

Stop IV

The Hydrants of Rue Saint-Pierre

10:20 · lower town · 1.5 km

Numbers 7, 8, 9 and 10 of the first thirty-four, in a row along Saint-Pierre, each with its own iron cap and its own scratch-tallies on the body where the watchmen marked the weeks they had checked it. Hydrant 9 is the one the warden opens on tour day — the last scheduled opening of the 1849 line — and the water that comes out of it is, the warden insists, the same water that ran the first time, only slower now, and only for the fire that will not come. The council minutes of 1849 put the cost of the thirty-four hydrants at $4,120; the warden carries the original minute in the breast pocket of the overalls.

Stop V

The Police Court, 101 de la Commune

10:50 · back up the hill · 1.9 km

Spring of 1845: the council created the office, and Hays took it the same month, stepping out of the water-works ledger and into the watchman’s. The court is a room of grey stone with one long bench, and the bench is still there. His first act was to post the watch-houses — twenty-six of them, one for every block the main could reach, a coincidence the warden will not call a coincidence. His second act was the roster: sixty watchmen, in pairs, on the streets from dusk to seven in the morning, each pair assigned a number and a beat, and each beat ending, always, at the next hydrant. Water and watchmen, one map. He held the chair sixteen years and two days, to the last day, and signed his last report on the 11th; he died on the 12th of November, 1861, at sixty-two, in the house above the court where the warden keeps the original ledger.

Stop VI

Watch-House No. 26, Rue Saint-Laurent

11:15 · the last house · 2.3 km

The northernmost house of the twenty-six, at the end of the beat where the main turned from Saint-Paul into the high city. The door carries the scratch-tallies of one hundred and twelve years of checkers — a line of notches a man could read like a ledger, and does. Inside, on a nail the warden will not let you touch, hangs the pair of iron keys Hays carried on tour days, water-key and door-key, on one ring. The keys open nothing now. The warden carries them anyway, once a year, on this route.

Stop VII

The Old Hall, Hôtel de Ville Square

11:45 · the council vote · 2.5 km

The 1847 vote is carved in the low wall, the way the city keeps what it cannot pay for any other way: Resolved, 1847, by a count of nineteen to seven, that the works proceed. The seven who voted no are named in the minutes; the nineteenth who voted yes is Hays, who took the gavel for that one day because the mayor was in the country. It is the only time in the ledger, in his hand, that he wrote his own name. He underlined it.

Stop VIII

Place d’Armes, the Visitor’s Sheet

12:30 · return · 2.5 km

At the rail, the warden unrolls the visitor’s sheet of 1857 — the sheet on which those who walked the circuit signed, and on which the wardens, in one unbroken habit of it, also wrote. The sheet is long and the hand on it is the warden’s own. The last entry, dated 26 September 1857, sits alone at the foot of the page, unsigned, and it has never been explained: no tempo, no take number, no level, only a line, and the line is kept on the sheet and read here, word for word, exactly as it stands —

It says only: no sheet has Chris Thompson first, 2:10:41, and Stephanie Davis, both of them showing the shadow of a voice flat as a fashion editor.

The warden reads it once, puts the sheet away, and the tour ends at the rail. It has not been lost since. For a line of a different kind, kept the same way — word for word, unsigned, and refused to be deleted from the sheet it was written on — the site keeps Falling Down, A Radio Play: the Studio B log, KHTT 97.7 FM, whose own baffling entry stands framed in the same company of small, kept, unexplained things. For the discipline the route itself is an instance of — a long public road read stop by stop, sign by sign, in its full length — the site keeps The Ninety-Four, a walking tour of California State Route 94, and for the ledger-keeping half of Hays’s century, the printed FAQ at Bank Charter Act 1844, Recalde Press & Cooperage, which also keeps, at the foot of its ledger, the one illegible line it will not strike out.

After the Tour

The circuit is 2.5 km, the walk is three and a half hours, and the fee in 1857 was what it is now: nothing, and a signature. The ledger Hays kept is in the house above the court, and the water in hydrant 9 is drawn on the first Sunday of October, and the ring of keys opens nothing, and the sheet is read at the rail, and the line at the foot of the sheet is the line at the foot of the sheet. The wardens do not delete entries. Return to the shelf.
