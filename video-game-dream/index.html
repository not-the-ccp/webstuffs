Video Game , n.

Pronounced the room with the glowing table · also the park bench that plays music · also, in one’s own sleeping voice, the thing that does not let you die

An electronic game that involves interaction with a user interface or input device — a joystick, a controller, a keyboard, a motion-sensing wand waved in the air like a conductor who has lost the orchestra — to generate visual feedback from a display device, most commonly a television set, a computer monitor, a flat-panel display, the small bright square in a palm, or a virtual-reality headset that turns the whole bedroom into the level. Most modern instances are audiovisual: the picture on the glass is complemented by audio delivered through speakers or headphones, and sometimes by other kinds of sensory feedback, haptic technologies that provide tactile sensations, so that the dreamer’s fingertips know the collision before the eyes do. Some also admit a microphone and a webcam for in-game chatting, which in the dream becomes simply the sleeper talking to a room and hearing, faintly, that the room talks back. To dream it is to dream of a story that is told the way weather is told: in brief chapters, with almost no writing, and with nothing struck out.

When Dreamed of

Most commonly the game appears in sleep as a small park in late afternoon, one bench, one cello case left open, and a screen the size of a window pane standing in the grass, glowing the particular purple of the hour after the hour. The dreamer is either holding the controller, or setting it down on the bench between rounds of ordinary life — brushing, folding, packing a flat box — and the difference is the whole of the entry. Holding the controller means the dreamer’s own routine is about to be interrupted by someone new, and the interruption will be gentle. Setting it down means the interruption has happened, and the dreamer has learned, over about twenty brief chapters, how to put a thing down without breaking it.

The input device arrives in the dream as memory before it arrives as object: first the weight of it in the hand, the four small buttons that are not buttons but four ways of answering, then the display, then the sound — which is never quite the sound the waking world would make, but the sound the waking world would make if it were scored for strings and one tired cellist. If the controller is missing from the dream, the dream is about a decision that has already been made without the dreamer’s fingers, and the folio declines to say by whom. If it is present but the screen is dark, the dream is about rest between chapters, and the dreamer should not be alarmed: even the folio keeps a rest.

The Keys of the Entry

  • To dream the pause menu is the commonest figure in this folio and the least alarming: the dreamer’s own affairs, held in their hands, listed in a calm column, resummable at any time. No pause is needed to hear it; the sleeper has already pressed it with one eye still open.
  • To dream the save point means a place in the waking road where nothing the dreamer does afterward can be unsaid, and where — this is the dream’s one mercy — nothing need be unsaid either. The save is written itself, in a hand the dreamer almost recognises, and the disk turns exactly once.
  • To dream the fail state and find there is none is the signature figure of this entry: a whole life in the dream where no one breaks, no one is struck out, nobody dies, and the scene simply ends. The folio reads this as good news delivered in a minor key. Whatever the dreamer was expecting to punish them, it has been designed not to.
  • To dream the post-it puzzle — the words of a letter assembled from torn notes, each word a small yellow square — means the dreamer is composing a true sentence and does not yet trust the words to hold. In the dream they always hold. They are, in the dream, load-bearing.
  • To dream the purple screen with a single cup of tea on it means the loud part of the story has finished and the quiet part has begun, and the dreamer is being told to sit with the quiet, which is the whole game, and has always been the whole game.

The Florence Figure

In the proofs of this folio the game is given a home, and the home is a park bench on the edge of a city that will not name itself, where a cellist plays on until the chapter ends and the music moves, as music does, to the inside of the room. The figure most often seen is a young woman in her mid-twenties, routine set like furniture — the alarm, the coffee, the commute, the toothbrush held up as if it were a witness — who meets, in the space of one lunch break, a man with a cello and whose whole world, gently, is rearranged. The dreamer who sees her should not read the dream as loss; the sheet on file records the ending as quiet and complete, and the flat is packed by box by box, nothing crushed. That is what a chapter does. That is what a dream of her means: the scene stopped moving, the music stopped, and the room was left clean.

One line at the foot of that folio is transcribed word for word and declined to be corrected. It reads: “Just Florence Yeoh, twenty-five, who lives the same drawer for a lb.” The folio takes it thus, and it is the only reading the line will give. Just is the dream’s one honest preposition — nothing more, nothing missing, the sentence that has stopped apologising for being short. Twenty-five is the age at which the routine is still a room and not yet a corridor. The same drawer is the drawer, and every reader of this board knows the drawer: it is the one at Kiel in which the court transcript of Case No. 341/1895, the SMS Wörth has lain for a century beside a postcard from Aşağıbüklü, two documents that have kept the same drawer without once explaining the other. In the dream Florence does not live in an apartment; she lives in that drawer, because a drawer is the smallest honest room in which to keep a story told in fragments, and the drawer keeps it the way the archive keeps the anchor: word for word, with nothing struck out. And for a lb — a pound, one pound — is the weight the drawer is allowed to hold, and the folio has weighed it: one cup of tea, two post-it notes, a single cello string, and the twenty brief chapters in which a whole relationship fits inside a lunch break. That is one pound. That is enough. The full thread on her — the cello in the park, the post-it puzzles, the chapters that play out without a fight — is kept under the ordinary archive habit at Florence on the Webstuffs Message Board, where a whole board sat up at night to explain why they could not do with their hands.

The Kitchen Interpretation

Old entries in this concordance sometimes take the dream down to the table, and a game this domestic comes to the table more often than most — more often, perhaps, than the sports. Where the dream ends in a kitchen, the screen fades to the colour of a tablecloth and the controller is set down beside the good spoon, and the game is asking for something to be folded, set, and left until it is through. The instruction the folio gives, in the voice it has always used, is this: Fold the letter from its notes, eight squares to a line, and set it in the drawer that holds one pound, where the tea goes cool at four in the morning. The drawer, when it is closed, weighs exactly what the line says it weighs. The music, when the dreamer opens it again, has moved to the inside of the room, where it belongs. The game, in the morning, is finished the way it was finished in the dream: not struck out, not paused, simply ended — and remembered, by the taste of the tea, for years.
