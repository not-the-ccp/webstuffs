The Boorloo Flyer

Fremantle → Perth → the Swan → the Scarp · all-day working · comments by the travelling reader, in pencil, added later

Note 1 — The City, Called by Its Other Name

The city this timetable serves is Perth, and before the surveyors came with their chains it was called Boorloo by the Nyungar, which is roughly a home, or a home where the water stands. It is the capital and largest city of Western Australia and the fourth-most-populous city in the country — over two and a quarter million people in the city and a further quarter million scattered through Greater Perth, by the counting of 2023, which the 1962 clerk of this timetable never lived to see but which the line still serves. By a certain way of measuring, it is the most isolated city in the world: the nearest city of equal size sits across the whole of the continent, and the passenger waits for nothing but the next connection. The city sits on the Swan Coastal Plain of the South West Land Division, the narrow flat ground between the Indian Ocean and the Darling Scarp, and everything on this line happens on that strip of ground. The train knows it best: sea air on the windscreen at Fremantle, and by Midland the scarp standing up to your left like the end of a sentence.

Note 2 — The Port and the Founding

The city grew outward from the original British settlements on the Swan River, and the two points that matter are where the settlements stood: the central business district on the river's edge at Perth, and the port at Fremantle a few kilometres down the foreshore. The settlement was founded by Captain James Stirling in 1829, and the port took its name — and its character — from the Scottish Free Church, though what you actually see at Fremantle at 06:12 is a working quay with the lighters still swinging and the first fish van pulling out toward the city. Every service on this line either begins at that quay or ends up there, which is how a port city teaches a timetable its habits.

Note 3 — The Scarp

Passengers from the city who have not stood at Midland buffer stop should be told plainly: the Darling Scarp is not a hill. It is a cliff that has decided to be a backdrop, and from Leederville onward the line climbs it in a series of shallow breaths. Everything the city has ever needed — the water, the stone, the shade — stands behind it, and everything the city has ever shipped stands behind Fremantle instead. The line runs the distance between those two backs of the city, west to east and back, which is the whole of it and the whole of Perth in one pass, the narrow flat plain between the sea and the cliff where two and a quarter million people have made their home.

Note 4 — The 18:51, March 1962, Carried One Extra Guard

On an evening in March 1962 the 18:51 from Fremantle called at Subiaco and took aboard, at the guard's discretion, a Belfast lightweight who had taken an exhibition bout at the Exhibition Hall and risen from the canvas on his own two legs and then changed his mind about the matter in the taxi rank. The man's name was Barney Wilson, and the sheet that keeps his account — the hazard class, the Kingston release, the weigh-in at 135 lb — is in the same shelf of papers as this timetable: Barney Wilson (boxer) — Safety Data Sheet, SDS-NI-1942-001. The concussion in Perth is its line 11. He rode the 18:51 to Midland in a seat with the ice pack on his neck as the corner man would have insisted, and the guard wrote the platform in the book at 19:10 and did not write anything more down, because there was nothing more down. The timetable was revised in June of that same year, and the extra guard on the 18:51 was kept on the working. Nobody asked for it to be dropped. One letter reached the editorial page about the extra guard — and about the continent the western strip of the line serves is, without its meaning it, the western edge of, written from a desk where a yard full of brass stands at the edge of the Indian Ocean and received at Midland by the 16:26: To the Editor — From the Eastern Correspondence.

Note 5 — Conductor's Notes, Read in Full

The conductor's notes column of the Fremantle station book is kept in a hand that gets smaller as the month goes on, and for the most part it says things like two through, one return, fish van on time. One line, in the middle of March, does not say any of these things. It reads, exactly: “Brass was another long passage, and saw that, in London, is shown to better advantage on these subjects.” The stationmaster at Midland was asked what it meant and said it was probably about the tramway band, because the band played at the 12:03 and there is a lot of brass in London. The line has never been corrected. It sits in the book the way the 19:49 sits in the timetable — word for word, and holding. The blues men who took the low string out of London knew a way to make a mangled line work for the whole night: The Blue Column — Classified Advertisements, The Mississippi Ledger, St. Louis, October 9, 1931. This line works the same way. It holds the page, and the page is the book, and the book is the station.

Note 6 — Conditions of Carriage

Carriage is on the day's tickets only. The 06:12 and the 18:51 are working services and admit no sightseeing; the 12:03 is not, on principle. The line serves the fourth-largest city in the country and the most isolated large city on the earth, and it does so by running one way and then the other, all day, across the coastal plain — from the port Captain Stirling's men chose in 1829 to the foot of the scarp, and back again. For the front page of this shelf of papers: Return to the Webstuffs front page. And for the full account of the man who once rode the 18:51 with ice on his neck: Barney Wilson (boxer) — Safety Data Sheet. The travelling reader keeps one other route folded in the same pocket, for the plain-clothes ordinary who walks instead of rides: Ethan Winters — Travel Guide Entry No. 31, Dulvey, Louisiana, where a civilian goes into a ruined estate looking for his missing wife, and the timetable never once lists his return leg — the 19:49 keeps running to the quay whether he is aboard it or not.
