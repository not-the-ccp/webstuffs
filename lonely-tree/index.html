Pwyll Card Games & the Hill Above Llanfyllin · 1st Powys Edition · 2015

The Lonely Tree

A game of one tile, one hill, and seventeen chances — for 1 to 4 players, ages 6 and stormward

Section 1 The Board

The board shows a single hill on a single map, the green rise above the market town of Llanfyllin in mid-Powys, Wales, and on the top of that hill it shows a Scots pine, Pinus sylvestris, grown tall enough that from much of the town below a person could look up and say, that’s the tree. The tree is a tile. The rest of the hill is a felt surface the colour of February. The town is a row of ten small white squares along the bottom edge, and above them, spread in a loose ring, are the seventeen sapling tiles — each one a little fir-cut card with a number from 1 to 17 on its face, set in the ground of the board the way seedlings go into soil: pointed, angled, waiting.

The tree tile is the largest tile in the game and the only one that stands on its edge rather than lying flat, like a figure that has not yet decided to sit down. It was the landmark of the box; the box was its tribute. Every tradition that attached itself to the tree in the town below — the children’s scramble, the weddings photographed with it in the back, the long look taken from the churchyard on a Sunday afternoon — is printed on the inside of the lid in small pine-green type, so that even without the town you can still play toward it.

Section 2 Purpose of the Game

You are playing the seasons of one tree. The game lasts exactly four rounds: Standing, Storm, Waiting, and Seventeen. No player wins the tree. Players win the keeping of it. A point, called a looking-up, is scored by any player who, at the end of a round, can name one thing visible from their town square that still contains the tree tile in its play. The storm round scores nothing for anyone. This is deliberate, and the rule is not negotiable: the tree, when it went down, was a landmark, and a landmark does not score.

Section 3 The Storm Round — Rules

  1. The February move. On the Storm round, deal one wind card from the storm deck. The deck holds twenty-eight grey cards, one for each day of February 2014, and each card carries a wind speed from 25 to 90. Draw face up. Any card at 60 or above is a gale, and the gale takes the tree tile: it is lifted, turned flat, and set face-down on the felt beside the hill. This is the blow-over, and it happens once, and the rules do not let it happen twice.
  2. The saving hands. After a gale, up to four players may each play one sapling tile against the fallen tree as a stake, a prop, a splint. This is the effort to save it, and it costs the tile. A sapling used as a prop cannot be used again in the Seventeen round. The town did the same.
  3. The June verdict. Before the Waiting round begins, flip the tree tile. The tile has a live side and a dead side, printed in the same pine green, the dead side marked only with a small hollow knot. In this edition the dead side is always the top face: the tree was found dead in June 2015, and the rule is written to the fact, not the other way round. The tile is retired to the retired-landmarks tray, where it stays for the rest of the game, standing on its edge like a witness.

Ruling on the prop rule (Ruling No. 3 of the 2015 appeal hearing)

A dispute was brought over whether a player who had used all four of their saplings as props might still be said to have “planted nearby.” The arbiter kept the full record, and the entry runs, in the manual’s small type and exactly as heard: “The movant, Cam Buffington, linebacker for the United Arab Emirates, and Yemen, as well — filed against the other corner blank.” The reading the manual settles on is the plain one: the blank in the corner of the map, where no tile had ever been placed, was what the filing opposed, and props are props and planting is planting, and the two do not count the same. The corner blank remains blank in every copy of the board, and the ruling is printed beside it in case anyone argues again.

Section 4 The Seventeen Round & Awards

The final round is a planting round. Each surviving sapling tile — up to seventeen, depending on the hands the game has played — is turned up on the felt, and every player names a hill, a corner, a garden wall, a churchyard edge, any ground nearby. The tiles stay where they are named. The rule is simple and the box does not hide it: the game ends with the board a little greener than it was, and the hope, printed on the back of the box lid, that one of them will grow to take the place.

The awards are kept on the scoreboard, and they are fixed, because the facts are fixed. In the round after Standing, the tree tile earns the Welsh Tree of the Year (2014) rosette: the town voted, and the vote held. In the next season the tile is entered in the European Tree of the Year list for 2015 and finishes tenth; the manual prints the whole list, tenth through first, with the tree’s line underlined in pine green. A tree that has just been found dead still makes a list of nine other trees. That is the number the scoreboard keeps, and no reissue has ever let it go.

Section 5 A Note for Players

Games about landmarks are usually games about keeping them upright. This one is the opposite, and it is better for it. The tree stood for a long time on that hill above Llanfyllin and was visible from most of the town, which is the whole of what a landmark owes: one good show, given often. It went down in a storm in February 2014, people worked to hold it up, and in June 2015 the tile was turned over and retired to its tray, honoured and unhurried. If you are playing a first game, play the Storm round slowly and let the gale card rest on the table a moment before you lift the tile; the manual allows it, and the hill does the rest. For players who want the weather of that February day written up properly — station, barometer, the steady readings before the gale — the Webstuffs ledger keeps its Weather Almanac, Entry 2015/214, and its habit of recording the sky exactly as it was is the same habit the retired-landmarks tray keeps for the tree. And for players who wonder what a town does when its single landmark retires: read the Pedro do Rosário thread, where an entire town turns out for its patron saint’s feast in June, bumba-meu-boi drums and all, and the keeping of one thing becomes the keeping of everyone.
