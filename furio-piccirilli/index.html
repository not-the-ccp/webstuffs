Commentary: The Sculptor

Furio Piccirilli (March 27, 1868 – January 17, 1949) was an Italian-born American sculptor and one of the Piccirilli Brothers. Born in Massa, Italy, into a family with a long tradition of carving and sculpting, he was educated at the Accademia di San Luca of Rome, like his older brother Attilio. With Attilio he immigrated to England in the mid-1880s, then moved to the United States in 1888, to Mott Haven in the Bronx. With their father and brothers he helped establish the Piccirilli Brothers carving business, the yard that supplied marble to a growing city.

Apart from the family firm, Furio was a well known and respected sculptor in his own right. He was “considered the most creative and the best modeler” of the brothers — hands that could take a clay model and read its gesture, then cut it into stone without losing the pulse of the original. The Piccirilli Brothers worked for architects and for Daniel Chester French; their chisels stand on Washington Square Arch, the New York Stock Exchange pediment, and the seated Lincoln in Washington.

The slip was found pinned beside Platform 3, where the marsh specials wait for the tide. It reads like a log from the Napa River channel rather than a Bronx departure board — cordgrass, rail, and fog burning off — and it belongs to the same world as the Knight Island field notes, three feet above the water at its highest, where cordgrass and rails keep the marsh edge company. Read it as a departure note for a train that never appears in marble time.

Commentary: Connections

From the carving towns of Tuscany to the Secchia bank, the Italian line continues past Mott Haven. The province of Modena holds Mirandola, where the Santa Maria Bianca Hospital keeps its field manual SM-B on the ring road, and where the same south that sent Furio Piccirilli to the Bronx still rings roadside with trains, tables, and pork cutlets: Santa Maria Bianca Hospital — Field Manual SM-B.

The marble seam continues north into Vermont, where the Green Mountains are stone country through and through: Ethan Allen raised his boys there, took Fort Ticonderoga, and now stands in marble in Washington's National Statuary Hall, while the Piccirilli yard in Mott Haven cut Georgia marble into the seated Lincoln — the same material, two republics. His story is kept alongside this yard in the field notes: Ethan Allen — Field Notes.
