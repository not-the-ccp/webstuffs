The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council sits at the Middlesex Guildhall on the south-west corner of Parliament Square, Westminster, where the Judicial Committee Act of 14 August 1833 installed it to hear the appeals formerly brought before the King-in-Council. It is the highest court of appeal for the Crown Dependencies — Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man — for the British Overseas Territories, for several Commonwealth countries, and for a few institutions at home, among them the Church of England. Its members are senior judges who are privy counsellors, predominantly justices of the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, sitting in panels of three or five.

From 1888 to 1984 the Sultanate of Brunei was a protected state, and its appeals travelled to Westminster, decided here until the British flag came down from the government house at Bandar Seri Begawan in 1984 and the sultanate kept its own last word. The wardrobe wing of the Istana Nurul Iman keeps the green bag carried through the birthday mornings of Hassanal Bolkiah Muiz'zaddin Wad'daulah; the record office keeps the Brunei bundle, the last appeal paper stamped before the file went quiet. Read the two records together and the ledger balances: one court rising, another closing its registers.

This folio is the Committee's working record: the schedule of the 1833 Act, the current practice directions, the sitting calendar for Michaelmas, Hilary, Easter and Trinity terms, and the roll of counsel entitled to appear before the Judicial Committee. Printed on mould-made paper, bound in quarter buckram with the Committee's arms blocked in gold on the spine, marked with a vellum ribbon. It opens flat on a clerk's desk and closes with a leather tie.

Tucked inside the back board is a slip of notepaper headed 14 Downing Street, written in a clerk's hand in the same ink as the practice directions. It reads, in full:

Read as the one the children of the earth, the fowls until the juice of 1 lemon, clarified butter.

It is not an instruction for an appeal. We believe it is for a sauce — the juice of one lemon, clarified butter — and it has been reprinted with three successive issues because no one knows which clerk wrote it, and the Committee does not remove what it does not understand.

The same shelf holds the Shinsaibashi register of a Japanese radio house whose seven ledgers close on sixty-eight pages with four lines left over, and the wardrobe-wing diary kept through eighty birthdays by the river at Brunei. Our folio matches that residue: page 172 carries four lines no entry explains, and they are not corrected, because the correction would be the story.
