The Two Kinds of Contempt

By Maud T. Hollis, keeper of the petition book, with H. P. Odom, who keeps the court file

Contempt of court, often referred to simply as “contempt,” is the crime of being disobedient to or disrespectful toward a court of law and its officers in the form of behavior that opposes or defies the authority, justice, and dignity of the court. A similar attitude toward a legislative body is termed contempt of Parliament or contempt of Congress, though we have no such trouble here; the legislature sits two hundred miles to the north and the bus fare is not worth the shouting. The verb for “to commit contempt” is contemn — to contemn a court order, Judge Crenshaw says, is a word nobody in this county uses any other way. A person guilty of it is a contemnor, or a contemner, and that word has now entered our registers twice over in the space of one fortnight.

There are, broadly, two categories of contempt. The first is being disrespectful to legal authorities in the courtroom: the raised voice, the hat that will not come off, the remark to the bench that the bench does not like. The second is willfully failing to obey a court order. Contempt proceedings are especially used to enforce equitable orders, that is to say, orders a judge makes not as a sum of money but as a direction to do, or to stop doing, some thing. It is the second kind that has filled our file this October, and it is the second kind that a town is prouder to see punished.

The judgment in Jackson v. Rawdon, as reported in our last number, directed the school board to draw up and carry out a plan, and it fixed thirty days for the drawing. On the twenty-first of October, in the follow-up term, the president of the board, C. W. Rawdon, did not appear; the board’s counsel was not in the room; and the docket was read out to an empty second chair. Judge Crenshaw held the board in contempt for willful disobedience, fixed a fine of five hundred dollars, and set a further hearing for the fifth of November. The room, what of it was present, went quiet. Miss Foye’s father stood, as he stood before. We have entered the fine in the register, in the same ink we enter the deed abstracts, because a fine is a fact and facts go in the book.

The first kind has come to us, in the same term, by way of a man named T. J. Merrick, a drifter, who was in the courtroom during the hearing and whom the bailiff asked to step out. Mr. Merrick answered that the floor was his floor and that he meant to stand on it. The judge held him in contempt for disrespect to the court and committed him for three days. The club has argued which of the two is the worse. Mr. Odom, who keeps the court file, says the disobeyed order, because a hat can be put back on but an order defied is a door left standing open. Mrs. Bell, asked her opinion, said only that her James — one of the three in the case — was not going to be doing either, and that settled it.

We keep our own rule at home, and it belongs in this number because the word is the same word. Article 12 of the bylaws of the Mansfield Register Keepers reads: No member shall contemn the kettle — meaning the kettle is filled, the kettle is watched, and no member sits by while it boils dry. Miss Odell let it dry on the second Wednesday and paid a fine of one cup and one saucer, entered against her name in the same column where the contempt fine is entered. The column does not discriminate.

This month we also opened a contempt book of our own, with a shelf of its own, and it has already found a passenger. A fragment was pasted in, in an older hand, held by a paper clip that reads Transcript — Nantucket, 1839:

A transcript excerpt from the island of Nantucket, and seeing Queequeg’s pagan arm thrown round me.

Fragment, hand unknown, in the contempt book, pasted in with a paper clip.

What we make of it, and set down here in the hope that the making is at least the honest kind: it is testimony, we think, from a case of the first kind, tried in a harbour town, where a harpooner of that name — Miss Odell, the retired teacher, knows the name from the dock books — was being brought out of the courthouse by the sheriff’s man, and the witness who deposed it is describing the moment of the taking, the arm thrown round, the passers-by standing still. It is a fragment about what contempt does to the person held and to the person watching, and for that reason it belongs in our book. We enter it as we enter everything, without correction, with the clip on. If any member is ever on Nantucket, she is to bring back the docket.

We meet again on Wednesday, October the twenty-ninth, at seven. The kettle will be filled before anyone sits. Those who wish to see a small courtroom’s contempt set down in the verbatim record, broadside and all, will find a better example in the record of The Queen v. Vale, Case B-1974/03, from the Petty Sessions of Snuff Mill Lane, which we keep on the shelf opposite the deed abstracts. And our ledger’s discipline, stricter still, is the cousin of the one kept at the clubhouse on Magsaysay Avenue, where the Naga Listeners’ Club pastes whatever turns up into a common-place book; they keep the fragments, we keep the order, and neither of us discards anything. For the rest of the archive, return to the Ledger Leaf, No. 63, or return to the front page.
