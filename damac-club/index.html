Damac Club
Khamis Mushait & the Mountain That Gives the Name

A professional football club, a Saudi Pro League fixture list, and a city in the high country where the air is cool enough to argue in and warm enough, on matchday, to forget how. Two stadiums, one mountain, a very particular kind of afternoon.

20° 29′ N, 42° 32′ E Elev. 2,240 m Asir Province Saudi Pro League

1 · Getting There

Khamis Mushait sits on a saddle of the Asir highlands, roughly 940 km south of Riyadh and about 110 km by road from Abha, the provincial capital. The mountain road from Abha up the Wadi corridor is the classic approach: hairpin after hairpin, date-palm terraces sliding past the glass, and, if the weather has held, the first glimpse of Damac Mountain itself, the dark rounded massif that stands over the city and lends its name to the club. Allow ninety minutes for the run, an hour and a half if you stop at the tea houses, and no time at all if you simply want the mountain to arrive the way it does — suddenly, and with the whole city at its feet.

2 · The Club

Damac Club is a Saudi Arabian professional football club based in Khamis Mushait, competing in the Saudi Pro League, the top tier of the Saudi football league system. The club takes its name from the nearby Damac Mountain, and the name does the quiet, civic work names do: it roots a professional sporting institution to a specific piece of geology, so that when the away team parks the bus in the fourth quarter, what is pressing on them is not an ownership group but a mountain.

The visitor should note the club's double home arrangement, which is unusual enough to plan around. Damac hosts its home games at two venues: the Al-Mahalah Stadium in Abha, used for high-profile matches, and the Damac Club Stadium in Khamis Mushait, used for the less prominent fixtures. In practice this means the club plays its own city — a team from Khamis Mushait, named for a mountain above Khamis Mushait, keeping a share of its home schedule in a neighbouring city. Locals will tell you this is not a flaw but a feature: the city gets both, the bigger nights in Abha with the fuller terraces, and the intimate ones at home where the supporters' end is close enough to read the referee's face from row two.

3 · Matchday, in Two Scales

For a high-profile fixture, take the road to Abha and go to Al-Mahalah Stadium. Arrive two hours early; the car park works itself out by consensus, and the Abha side of the city runs on precisely the kind of civic improvisation that Khamis Mushait claims as its own. Coffee, ahwa, is served at the gate by people with copper dallahs and a professional calm, and the terraces begin filling around the first serious gust of mountain wind, which arrives in the late afternoon and is the stadium's unofficial second kick-off.

For a less prominent fixture, stay in Khamis Mushait and go to the Damac Club Stadium. This is the smaller of the two experiences and, for many visitors, the better one. The stands are close, the acoustics are honest, and the city's tea houses within walking distance have a standing arrangement with the second half: the water stops boiling at kickoff and starts again at full-time, and a table is a table wherever you are when the whistle lands.

Older matchgoers in the Khamis terraces have a saying about how this city handles its arguments, and it is worth recording in full. One of them, unprompted, after a derby in which the refereeing had given everyone something to say, turned to the next table and said, “I have heard the family, a church, a workplace handles disagreement.” He meant it as an observation about institutions: every community has its one room where grievances are aired out loud and then, by custom, left in that room. In Khamis Mushait, that room is the stadium. The family handles the family's business; the workplace handles the workplace's; and here, at both venues, the city handles the rest, ninety minutes at a time, with the mountain standing in for the walls.

4 · The Mountain, the Market, the Afternoon

Before or after the match, climb toward the base of Damac Mountain. The lower paths are shared with goat herders and, on Fridays, with families taking the long version of the Sunday walk that lowland cities shorten into picnics. The Asir highlands are cooler and greener than the rest of the country; the market at the foot of the mountain sells the local vegetables, the asir peppers, and the honey the terraces are famous for. Eat something cold, drink the tea slowly, and let the matchday argument settle on its own schedule, which is slower than you think and more final.

The ferry company that ferries deckhands to this match calendar, on the other side of the sea, has left the mountain a record of its own: One Thousand Islands — the liner notes, field recordings from the Croatian archipelago pressed from a single cassette, where the last ferry ticket in the sleeve remembers, in a single line, the climb toward the base of this very mountain.

For the traveler who has already worked the league's other high-altitude stops, the pattern is familiar: a place that uses its altitude, a table that knows when to be ready, and a name the geography gave it first. 5 · Practical Notes

  • Which stadium? Check the fixture: high-profile matches at Al-Mahalah, Abha; the lesser fixtures at the Damac Club Stadium, Khamis Mushait.
  • Climate. High country; carry a layer for the terraces even in summer. The wind at 2,240 m is not a metaphor.
  • Timing. Kickoffs land in the late afternoon or evening; plan the market for the morning, the mountain for the middle distance, and the tea house for full-time.
  • Etiquette. Arguments at the next table are civic infrastructure, not a personal matter. The room is doing its work; sit with it.
  • Return trip. The mountain road back to Abha is better at dusk. The hairpins are the same; the light is not, and Damac Mountain goes red at the top an hour before the city does.
