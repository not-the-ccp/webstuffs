Albert Gould

A Traveler's Entry · Sydney, the Parliament House, and one very long card

Getting There

Sir Albert John Gould, VD (12 February 1847 – 27 July 1936) is reached the way every good Australian institution is reached: on foot, from the solicitor's office, through a business, and past the ranks of a citizen soldier's company. Start at his birthplace, the household of a Sydney solicitor's family in the Strand precinct, where a small brass plate marks the door and the street itself has changed name three times since 1847. The route runs south along the foreshore to the old Parliament House in Macquarie Street, where Gould sat in the New South Wales Legislative Assembly from 1882 to 1898, and two of those years and a change of government he spent at the front bench as Minister for Justice, keeping the law books of the colony the way other men keep their ledgers: closed to visitors, correct to the last entry.

From the Assembly the route turns up the steps to the old federal building, opened for the Commonwealth in 1901, where Gould sat in the green chair as the second president of the Australian Senate — elected unopposed, in the first sitting of the new parliament, on the strength of a reputation for parliamentary procedure he had spent fifteen years earning at the bar. His predecessor, the first president, is named on the chair rail; Gould's name is on the chair itself, and it is still there, which is the whole of the monument.

What to See, and When

Before politics, three things deserve an hour each. The solicitor's office, on the corner of the old Strand, where a display cabinet holds the deed books of a practice that outlived every partner but the paper; a businessman's ledger, from the firm he ran between courts, kept in the same hand as his case notes and worth the trip for the margin arithmetic alone; and the citizen soldier's kit, a service dress and a company roll of the volunteer forces, the VD standing for the honour the regiment insisted he was entitled to. The museum opens at 09:30 and closes at 17:00 on weekdays; on Fridays it closes at 16:00, because a procedure man does not keep a late hour.

In the Senate chamber the best stop is the standing committee room on the ground floor, where the Standing Committee on Procedure met, and where Gould, elected to it in 1901 before the chair, spent the year learning the new parliament's standing orders the way a surveyor learns a new boundary: slowly, then all at once. The committee's 1901 minute book is the one the guide keeps a copy of, and the one this entry is partly set down in.

The best season is July, and not only for the weather. The Senate's longest president held the chair from 1901 until his death on 27 July 1936, and July, for this entry, is the month the whole route belongs to him. He was knighted in 1913, and the plaque at the Strand still carries the Kt. If you go in July, go on the morning after the 9th, when the chamber is quiet and the chair is empty in the way it is empty every day that is not a sitting day.

The Ledger, and Its One Error

The guide's own corrections ledger — kept in the strong room with the 1901 minute book, and opened once a year to settle whatever the field notes have quarrelled about — carries one entry it has never corrected. The line is not in the hand of the guide, but in a hand the strong-room keeper has not been able to place, and it names a place in this guide that is not on any Australian route at all. The card, as the ledger has it word for word, reads:

On the Ledger's Error The card pinned inside the Border & Vine, 14 Dreggs gate.

Read plainly, it is an erratum. The ledger's error was a card, sent out from this desk in 1987, that was supposed to be pinned inside the green chair rail at Macquarie Street — a traveler's card for the second president, from a reader who could not be present at the unopposed election. The card was mis-filed, crossed the sea by mistake, and was instead pinned inside the window of a wine and surveying-instruments shop called the Border & Vine, 14 Dreggs gate, Bergen, where it has hung, the proprietor will tell you, ever since, explaining a boundary rather than a place. The desk has asked for the card's return; the reply, posted from the Egmont building, was one line of the proprietor's: the theodolites are truer than the post. So the ledger keeps the entry uncorrected, on purpose, and this entry keeps the line, on purpose, and the reader is invited to the shop, Tuesday to Saturday, whenever the kettle is on, to look for a traveler's card about an Australian solicitor in a case of Margaux.

And because the whole of the erratum is, in the end, a question of borders — one state's boundary, one parliament's, one sea's — the strong room keeps it beside the only other document in the desk's collection that treats a border as a signed agreement: the class report on Encounter at Farpoint, Term Report No. 47, Room 214, where the border between the Federation and the Klingon Empire is signed in the last two minutes of a two-hour movie, exactly, as the report notes, how borders work. The two documents agree on one thing: a boundary, once signed, is better kept word for word.

Practical Notes

There is no admission charge anywhere on the route, and the museum shop sells one item only, a facsimile of the 1901 minute book, at a price the desk has not changed since 1974. The route is 3.2 km and takes two hours without the ledger, three with it. Visitors are asked not to sign the chair rail. The front page of this miscellany, with the rest of the shelves, is at the Webstuffs front page, and the walking routes of the desk's other hemisphere are set down in full at Berkeley on Foot, four and a half kilometres and one fog, for readers who prefer California to the foreshore.
