The Wedding of the Prince of Wales

Prince William & Catherine Middleton · Friday 29 April 2011 · Westminster Abbey, London · 11 o’clock

Setting the bell …

RP
R. E. Penfro Organist of the circuit · 31 years on the stool
Tue 26 April 2011, 9:14 a.m. No. 1 · OP

The Times of this morning settles it. On Friday next, the 29th, Prince William — second in the line of succession, and, the papers say, to be heir apparent one day — will marry Catherine Middleton at Westminster Abbey. The two have kept their company since 2003, which is to say eight years, or the length of the first line of a tune in 8.7.8.7.D, which I do not think is nothing. What the Times gives us in order: John Hall, Dean of Westminster, presides at the service; Rowan Williams, Archbishop of Canterbury, conducts the marriage; Richard Chartres, Bishop of London, preaches the sermon; a reading is to be given by the bride’s brother, James. The best man is his brother Prince Harry; the maid of honour is Pippa. The Abbey last had a royal wedding in 1947, and the galleries were full then, too, and we were not there then, and that has been a quiet sore in the parish ever since.

I move that the circuit close at 4 o’clock that day, that the television be brought from the boathouse to the vestry, and that we sing Blaenwern — “O God, Our Help in Ages Past” — once before the switch and once after, at the full length of the long note. Seconded, I should add, by the marmalade.

MR
M. Rowlands Of Porth · first hearing on claims, per the notice
Tue 26 April 2011, 11:02 a.m. No. 2

The Rowlands of Porth have had word, and we pass it along. The best man is Harry, who we understand has stood in a like posture before and acquitted himself of it; the bride’s sister Pippa is the maid of honour, which is a post we take to be serious and not a joke, whatever the capital letters may suggest in London. The Middleton name is new to the circuit’s rolls, but the county is not new to them — the family has been in Pembrokeshire many generations, and on one report the wedding itself is to be kept in the Abbey while the family keeps its house affairs at home, which is the old arrangement: the ceremony where the stone is, the family where the table is.

One request, on the family’s behalf, made with the family’s permission: if the maid of honour is wearing blue, I should ask that nobody in the vestry say Blaenwern is in F major in the same breath and the same disappointment. The long note has its own business. — M. Rowlands, Porth. For the record: claims from Porth are given the first hearing, and we intend to be heard first on the day itself.

T. J. Davies Vestry clerk · keeper of the ledger and of the drawer
Wed 27 April 2011, 8:30 a.m. No. 3

For the record, so that the day goes clean. The ledger is to take its entry for 29 April at 1 o’clock — attendance and weather only, per FM-4, chapter 4, section 4.5: do not transcribe the revival. The television from the boathouse is examined and found dry; it is to stand between the hymn board and the kettle, and the hymn board is to carry one tune only, which is the move the organist has made, and which this post takes to stand.

The Lost & Found Office asks that Notice No. 47, the tune Blaenwern, four lines, metre 8.7.8.7.D, be left unclaimed on the day in question. It has a habit of reappearing in churches with no record of ever filing the notice, and on report it has been seen at weddings before, which we decline to verify but also decline to deny. Should any item be left in the vestry after the service — a handkerchief, a programme, a tin of marmalade opened to the last spoon — it will be refiled under No. 48, which the Office opens this week in advance, the first notice opened before the thing is lost, on the strength of this thread.

ED
Mrs E. G. Davies Chapel cook · sponge, treacle tart, and tea for the whole circuit
Wed 27 April 2011, 4:45 p.m. No. 4

Tea for the day is settled: twelve eggs, and the cake to be a Victoria sponge, as is the law of any wedding, royal or otherwise — the royal part gets the Abbey, and the sponge gets the vestry. I have the 1915 book of Cân a Moliant under the flour, and on the flyleaf, in the margin of the page where Blaenwern is ruled in, there is one line that I have kept meaning to explain since 1986. It reads, word for word:

“Whisk the whites of the Welsh revival of 1904–1905, when the chapels of Pembrokeshire were running on fumes and new tunes were composed the way that rain was collected.”

— Margin of the 1915 book, flyleaf, in a hand the vestry agrees is the late organist’s

The vestry has taken its decision, and I am the one who made it, which is why it was a quick decision. It is two instructions pasted onto one line. The first is kitchen: whisk the whites — the twelve, separated, for the sponge, which is what a hand in 1915 would be doing with a book of tunes under the flour. The second is margin note: the standard wording for the wet months of the revival, the same phrasing the circuit’s own field manual carries — the chapels running on fumes, new tunes composed the way that rain was collected, out of the air, into whatever vessel was handy. Somebody in 1915 was whisking the whites while the air was filling up, and the two things got written on the same line, and the line has been standing there ever since waiting for a kitchen to answer it. It is, I think, the whole chapel in one line. The whites will be whisked. The sponge will be in the vestry by 4, and it will not move.

CL
C. A. Llewellyn Circuit rider · four royal-ish weddings on the saddle
Thu 28 April 2011, 6:20 p.m. No. 5

From the saddle, on the last night, before the circuit lies down for the day. I have stood in at four weddings of the grand kind — two vicars, a mill owner, and one man who turned out, it emerged at the fish, to be a baronet — and the rule holds for all of them, royal or no: the congregation does not attend a wedding like this; the congregation is watching it, which is why attendance is counted at the vestry door and the weather is taken at the bell, and both numbers go into the ledger and nothing else does.

One correction of record, offered freely, from the side of the horse: the Archbishop conducts the marriage, and the Dean sits the couple through it; the papers and, I note, post No. 1, have put “presides” in two hats at once, and the book should not do that, and neither should this thread, and the entry of 29 April will get the two verbs right because I will be the one on the door.

For the rest: eleven chairs will be brought in from the hall. Twelve is the rule for the class meeting, and the vestry will not break its own rule to look like a class meeting at a wedding. Eleven, and a spare, and the spare is for nobody — the spare is a rule, and rules sit. — C. A. Llewellyn, on the last lap of the circuit.

RP
R. E. Penfro Organist of the circuit · the day is entered
Fri 29 April 2011, 5:12 p.m. No. 6 · the day itself

Done. Attendance in the vestry: eleven, and the spare held, as the spare is for. Weather: rain — which is to say the correct weather for this parish, and the correct weather for a tune of that vintage, and the only weather under which Blaenwern has ever had to be rescued. The television from the boathouse held the Abbey without a complaint from either of us; there was the reading by the brother, the sermon of the Bishop of London, the archbishop’s two hands on both sets of hands, and a long white train that the ledger, per the rule, is not asked to transcribe.

We sang Blaenwern twice, as was moved in post No. 1: once before the switch and once after, at the full length of the long note both times, and it held both times, which is everything the tune has ever had to do. Two tins of the marmalade went out in the name of Notice No. 47, and the holder’s choice was taken both times, which the Office will note. Entry No. 119 is in the ledger now, attendance and weather only, the two verbs of post No. 5 set straight, and the wet month closed. This thread may be pinned down; I expect it to reopen of its own accord on Good Friday, which is the tune’s habit and not ours. — R. E. Penfro. Ledger: the Tufton circuit.
