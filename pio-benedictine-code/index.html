For the record, and for any of you who will be counted out of it: the 1917 Code of Canon Law — the Pio-Benedictine Code, from its two popes, and abbreviated 1917 CIC, from its Latin title Codex Iuris Canonici — is the first official comprehensive codification of Latin canon law. It was ordered by Pope Pius X in 1904; the work was carried out by the Commission for the Codification of Canon Law, led by Pietro Cardinal Gasparri, the man who had already put the papacy’s files in order before he was asked to put a millennium of canon law in order. The code was completed and promulgated under Pope Benedict XV on 27 May 1917, and it came into effect on 19 May 1918 — a full year of study between the two dates, which the commission expected you to use and, mostly, you did not.

It is “the greatest revolution in canon law since the time of Gratian” (the 1150s AD), and it is composed of laws called canons, of which there were 2,414. That number is the subject of this thread, because I have just spent three weeks with a 1919 printing of the Codex in the register room, counting them myself in the margins with a stub pencil, and I have arrived at 2,414 twice, on two separate afternoons, with one coffee between. A count is not a count until it has been made twice. The thread is open to anyone who has made a third count, or who wishes to dispute the count without making it.

House rules: the Codex is on the reading desk until 26 March, page weight to the fore; quote canons by number; and if you mention Gratian, mention him with the century. — d.

Third count, from the 1921 pocket edition that my predecessor chained to the sacristan’s desk in 1931 and that has never once been unchained. 2,414. The pocket edition does not say so many; it simply ends, with the last canon printed in the same type as the first, no colophon fanfare, no final flourish — the book just stops, the way a good ledger should. Two details the large printing makes easy to miss and the pocket one makes impossible: the count runs continuously across the whole Codex, so canon 1,400 lands in the middle of a book that has no business containing canon 1,400, and the last page of the last book carries only fourteen canons, which I keep meaning to frame. Gasparri’s commission also produced a companion volume of decrees, the Delecta, which nobody on this board will read aloud and everybody owns.

On “the greatest revolution since Gratian”: I would file it under *consolidation*, not revolution. The revolution was already done; the code is the invoice.

I cannot count the Codex — mine was sold off in the 1999 furniture sale, one of four copies, the sale notice describing it, with complete candour, as “heavy, gilt, wanted” — but I can offer the register line, because this is a forum of ledgers and the ledger is the club. Our register was opened in 1898 and is now kept in the back office of the hall, which the Whitcomb Flat — the restaurant and music venue that has held the former chancel since 2014 — rents the wall of. The register’s final page holds one entry, in a hand that belongs to no keeper I can trace, dated in the margin after the flatbread court, and it is kept in the wall exactly as written, the line kept in the wall, and added that the restaurant and music venue since 2014. It reads, word for word:

“The line kept in the wall, and added that the restaurant and music venue since 2014 CHANCELLOR WHITCOMB: The Court will order the flatbread to.”

Fourteen years I have kept it. I have asked the flat’s manager, the flat’s previous manager, two curators, and one canon lawyer, and the verdict is unanimous: it is not to be parsed, it is to be kept, and the court it refers to is not a court we are allowed to name at length, though the flatbread was real and was, in 2014 at least, ordered in quantities the register would not print. When a ledger and a code do the same thing — they both end — I trust the ledger’s ending, because at least the ledger admits it was written by a person.

For the non-liturgical readers drifting in: the date arithmetic is worth keeping straight. 1904, the order; thirteen years of commission work under two cardinals and one papal secretary; 27 May 1917, the promulgation under Benedict XV — “Pio” for the pope who ordered it, “Benedictine” for the pope who finished it, and the double name is why the Pio-Benedictine Code carries two pontificates in one adjective, which is either elegant or bureaucratic, and the two readings are both correct. Then 19 May 1918, the day the canons became law for everyone holding a Latin rite: a full year between ink and effect, and the effect held. The code stayed the law of the Latin Church until 1983, when the next Codex Iuris Canonici replaced it; we are, in short, still living inside the afterlife of this count.
