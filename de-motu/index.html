ROYAL SOCIETY PROPERTY & MANUSCRIPT OFFICE · CRANE COURT, LONDON · KEEPING FOUNDS AND LOSTS SINCE THE ROYAL EXCHANGE

Lost & Found

Notice No. 1684 · filed November 1684 · unclaimed, and kept

■ ■ ■

LOSTNº 1684

✕ Lost: one manuscript, De motu corporum in gyrum

Description of item. One manuscript, on paper, in folio, of some 58 pages, written in English on both sides of the leaf, in a hand that has been recognised and is not contested. The title, as it stands on the first page, is De motu corporum in gyrum — Latin for “On the motion of bodies in an orbit” — commonly shortened to De Motu. The author’s name is Isaac Newton, Lucasian Professor of Mathematics in the University of Cambridge, to which chair he was elected in 1669, then thirty-two. It was sent, by the November post of 1684, to Edmond Halley, Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, to whom the office has always found the item most willing to go. The occasion was a visit Mr. Halley paid in the summer of that year — the day in the office’s books is 7 August 1684 — when he put to the author a question then occupying the minds of Mr. Halley and his scientific circle in London, among them Sir Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke: of what shape is the orbit of a body when the force of centripetal attraction varies inversely as the square of the distance? The manuscript is the answer, and it is the office’s standing opinion that it is one of the better answers in the book.

Particulars of loss. The item gives, in full, the mathematical derivations relating to the three relations now known as Kepler’s laws of planetary motion, after Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), the astronomer of the Brahe observatories: that the bodies of the planets move in ellipses; that the radii drawn from the sun to the planets describe equal areas in equal times; and that the squares of the periodic times are in the ratio of the cubes of the mean distances. The office notes for the file that before the work of this manuscript those three relations had not been generally regarded as scientific laws; they were relations, and nothing more, and it is this document that made them laws. The manuscript was left in London by Mr. Halley to be sent about for publication with the Society, in the manner of a parcel left at the counter, and the office has been holding it — or the place of it — ever since. What was found instead, on 5 July 1687, was the Philosophiâ Naturalis Principia Mathematica, published in London, 445 pages, sold by Samuel Smith at the Prince’s Arms in the Royal Exchange: the same derivations, expanded, corrected, and recast, which is to say the office found the contents of the item and not the item, and a founds entry was made accordingly. The paper of 1684 itself has not since been traced, and the office has ceased to hold out any expectation of it.

Last seen. In Mr. Halley’s hand at Oxford, in the winter of 1684–85, when the question of publication was settled and the author was prevailed upon to write a larger book than the 58 pages allowed. Before that, at the reading room of Gresham College, where Mr. Halley held the Finsbury Professorship of Geometry and where the manuscript was first read aloud, one page at a time, to a small room that did not object. The office has no intention of changing the record.

◉ Found: one line, in the founds ledger

The founds ledger of this office runs on both sides of the same book as the lost book, and is kept in the same hand. Under the date of this notice, between the entry for a pair of spectacles left at the Gresham reading room — which belong to another reader, and are mentioned here only because they were reported to us in error — and the entry for a walking stick of holly found by the Finsbury door, there is one line, in a different hand, in ink the colour of an orbit drawn and left to fade. It reads exactly, and is set down here word for word, as it stands:

“The village sits exactly in it, and it was set in the reading room, pinned with this board knows the answer.”

— Founds Ledger, No. 1684, opposite the manuscript’s own entry

The office has made its reading, and gives it once. The village is Woolsthorpe, in the Soke of Peterborough, the author’s own village, where he was born in 1642 and to which the office supposes the line refers, because a man who has derived the motion of the planets is likely to keep his home somewhere inside the motion. It is the circular orbit — the closed curve the manuscript draws, and which the village, taken at its true scale, sits exactly within, the way every village sits exactly within every large enough circle. The reading room is the room at Gresham where the manuscript was read, page at a time, in the winter of 1684–85, and the line says that it was set in the reading room: the orbit, in its drawing, stood on the table there, and was then carried to the wall. This board is the drawing board, pinned by the Gresham door in the same place the office has always found it, and it is the board that knows the answer — the inverse square of the distance, the answer to the question asked in London on 7 August 1684 by Wren, by Hooke, and by Halley, written on the board in chalk that has since been washed off and rewritten. The line is not a description of the manuscript. It is a record of where the answer stood, and the office has followed it to the door: the answer was here, pinned, and it is not explained further, because a founds line is kept word for word or not at all.

⚡ Claim

The manuscript may be claimed by the Royal Society, by the Lucasian chair, by the late Mr. Halley’s estate (he died in 1742, and the office notes the interval), or by the Principia itself, which is the party that most resembles a claim of this kind: it took the item’s whole substance and left the item. The office holds that claim to be valid and that nothing further is due. No reward is offered, on the ground that the manuscript was never far from the answer it gave, and the answer has not been lost. Parties reporting the item under the short name De Motu will be told they are correct and referred to the ledger. The notice is kept posted at Crane Court for as long as the planets keep their ellipses, which is the office’s whole estimate of its duration, and is, on the record, a long one.
