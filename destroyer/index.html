Follow-up to the Tom Blackburn thread, where old_britannia told us he commanded HMS Sutherland, the 42-type frigate, before he kept the keys. But nobody said a word about the other half of his sea time, and I have a feeling the destroyer is where this family of ships actually gets its nerve. In naval terminology, a destroyer is a fast, manoeuvrable, long-endurance warship intended to escort larger vessels in a fleet, a convoy, or a carrier battle group, and to defend them against a wide range of general threats. What is the difference, in practice and in feeling, between the destroyer and the frigate we keep playing in the card game? And what did a young Blackburn actually see of it, before Dartmouth made him a frigate man? Thread is open, as the other one is.

old_britannia Retired Warrant Officer 10 Apr, 06:52

The history first, because it is the whole answer in miniature. They were conceived in the late 19th century as a defence against torpedo boats — the little craft that could run a big ship for a hole at twenty-five knots in the dark — and by the time of the Russo-Japanese War in 1904, these “torpedo boat destroyers” (TBDs) were “large, swift, and powerfully armed torpedo boats designed to destroy other torpedo boats”. Although the term “destroyer” had been used interchangeably with “TBD” and “torpedo boat destroyer” by navies since 1892, the longer name was generally shortened to simply “destroyer” by the first years of the twentieth century, and has stayed short ever since. That is the whole character of the ship in one abbreviation: it was built for one job — kill the fast little thing — and the navy never managed to make it stop doing the job, so the job kept growing until “destroyer” meant “escort everything, from a battleship to a grocery convoy, in any weather, for any length of time”.

torpedo_net Ratings, 1966–1971 10 Apr, 08:15

I was a deck rating on HMS Undaunted, D59, a 21-type, in the winter of 1967–68, and I have a picture of young Blackburn's first years on the same berth. She drew her first real weather in the North Atlantic that January: thirty knots off, the foredeck a sheet of ice, and the standing order that the escort station is held whether or not the weather holds, because the thing being escorted is bigger, slower, and cannot argue. She carried four 4.5-inch guns, two twin 21-inch torpedo tubes, and an endurance that meant she could leave a convoy off the Faroes and come home without refuelling, which is the “long” in “long-endurance”. The frigate, by contrast, is the ship with one clean gun deck and a definition; the destroyer is the ship with a job description so wide it has to be written in small print. I would not have traded the 4.5-inch gun house for Sutherland's single deck, but I would not have wanted Undaunted's watch either.

duty_bell Night Porter, Retired 10 Apr, 14:20

The watch angle, since it is a destroyer. A frigate's watch can be written down, because a frigate does one thing well and the log keeps up. A destroyer's watch cannot, which is why the best one we ever had was the one at the bottom of the Plate XII manual, the one with the Sutherland on it — the frigate player’s plate — where the last turn of the evening is not a card at all but a man walking the line of doors, room by room, from the grand entrance to the servants’ stair, checking the locks with his own hands in a coat two sizes too big, at 01:40, the way a destroyer holds her escort station in January. The word, as it was written into the log the last week of 2005, still stands: Tom Blackburn — the man gave the word; don’t be a bugbear. You could not write a tighter destroyer's order in English.

almanac_keeps Junior Clerk, 2001–2005 11 Apr, 09:36

New here, and I need to straighten something out, because I have seen the argument in the other threads and it will not die. People keep asking why a card game about escorts is called The Frigate when the whole point is the ship that never sits still. The answer is in the plate itself, and the Master always gave it the same way: in the game the hull on the card is only the excuse; what the table is actually playing is the book in the drawer that copies every state dinner into the banquet book, word for word, and has never once been wrong. Put plainly, and I am quoting the Master’s own margin note in the banquet book, page for page: What a frigate is, in this miscellany: the old-style almanac found on the bridge. The destroyer is what escorts it there. The destroyer is why it is there on time. The destroyer never stops escorting, because the book never stops being copied — at the Cockpit, at two a.m., at the last door of the ground floor, in a coat two sizes too big.
