Redlands Historical Society Walking Sheet No. 12

Redlands, California
A Walking Sheet in Eleven Stops

San Bernardino County · 34°02′ N, 117°11′ W · distance 1.9 miles · elevation 272 feet · best walked before the noon heat

Issued by the Redlands Historical Society in partnership with the Hardwood Emporium, San Diego

Before you set out

Redlands is a city of 73,168 souls by the 2020 census, up from 68,747 at the 2010 count, sitting in the citrus country of San Bernardino County, approximately 63 miles (101 km) east of Los Angeles and 70 miles (110 km) northwest of Indio. It was founded in 1881 on a plat drawn for land that encompassed the native Serrano, Morongo, and Cahuilla peoples, and when it incorporated in 1888 it absorbed whole communities — Terracina, Idlewild, Plaza, Chicago Colony, Victoria, Barton, Gladysta, and Lugonia — along with portions of Mentone, Crafton, and Bryn Mawr, so that a walk through the present town is really a walk through ten small towns that no longer get their own weather report. The city also shelters the oldest European-settled structure on the route, the Asistencia Mission founded in 1819, which predates the city by sixty-two years and has outlasted several of the towns that tried to outlast each other.

This sheet follows the route the Historical Society has walked since 1991, stop by stop, from the depot out to the orchard road and back. It is 1.9 miles on good feet and mostly flat, with one hill at Stop 4 that the Society considers a feature rather than an obstacle. Bring water. The odo-meter at the depot will record your progress; it is calibrated in miles and in the number of oranges, and it has been wrong about the oranges since 2004, which is considered flattering.

  1. 1

    0.0 mi · 55 E. State Street

    The Redlands Union Passenger Depot

    The 1915 Santa Fe depot in its red adobe, with the roundel windows and the ticket windows that no longer take tickets. Departure point for every Society walk since 1991, and the place where, on the third Saturday of March, the whole population of the county seems to gather for the citrus auction and stand on the benches arguing about seedlings. The depot archive holds the photograph that ends this walk, so keep it in your head until you need it.

  2. 2

    0.2 mi · the State Street corridor

    The 1881 Survey Stake

    Cast iron, waist-high, green with oxidation, set where the 1881 plat of the new town crossed the old stage road. The plat company staked the grid while the Serrano, Morongo, and Cahuilla, who had worked this land for generations, watched from the mesa and said what the plat company had been hoping no one would say. The Society's standing instruction at this stop is to look at the stake, look at the grid, and concede that a town is an argument that wins by staying.

  3. 3

    0.4 mi · the old adobe quarter

    The Asistencia de Redlands

    The Asistencia Mission, founded in 1819 by early European settlers — a small, working, stubborn building, older than the city, older than the railroad, older than the plat, older than the state's name for the county. Its date reads 1819 on the door and the door has never been corrected. When the town incorporated in 1888 the asistencia simply kept its address, which is more than can be said for most of the stops on this sheet. The Society asks walkers to knock twice and wait, because there is someone inside and there has always been someone inside.

  4. 4

    0.6 mi · the Terracina slope · the hill

    Terracina and Idlewild

    Up the slope into the hills that were once Terracina and Idlewild, named from a distance and a disposition. The houses are the houses of a hillside that had its own main street before 1888; the 1888 incorporation annexed it with the same casualness a person uses when deciding the garden is now part of the house. At the top there is a bench, a view down the whole grid to the north, and a wind that has its own weather. The Society allows the full five minutes at the bench and deducts none of them from the official walking time; the five minutes were first recorded in the minute book on 11 March 1941, in an entry that also carries the board's longest-running argument over a single word, preserved in full on the Redlands Bulletin Board.

  5. 5

    0.9 mi · the brick quarter

    Plaza and Chicago Colony

    Down the other side of the hill, into the brick and tile of the Plaza and Chicago Colony — the builder's country, where the masons who built Redlands kept their shops and named the blocks after places they had worked in before. Chicago Colony was not in Chicago; the name was a reference to a contractor and the contractor took it with him to his grave in 1902. The brick at this stop is the best on the walk: hard, reddish, and proud of the tooling. Count the headers and you will not finish before you need to move on, which is the point of the stop.

  6. 6

    1.1 mi · the twin corners

    Victoria and Barton

    Two names, two corners, one 1888 paragraph in the incorporation papers that swallowed them both in a single clause. Victoria was a block of small flats and a boarding house for the rail crews; Barton was a hardware business with a dog that the papers described, on one occasion, as “the only permanent employee.” The hardware store is still a hardware store. The dog's plaque is bolted where the papers would have put it, just below the awning, and it is more weathered than the awning.

  7. 7

    1.3 mi · the low orchard row

    Gladysta and Lugonia

    The low row of early houses between the orchard line and the wash, named Gladysta and Lugonia in the 1880s and named, by every census since, 73,168 or 68,747 depending on the year you choose to believe. The houses here were the first the plat company sold, which is why they are also the first on the society's preservation list: they sold first, they were built first, and they have been argued about longest. The wash at the corner is dry eleven months of the year and a torrent in the twelfth; the Society's rule is to stand on the near bank and not discuss the far bank.

  8. 8

    1.5 mi · the absorbed border

    Mentone, Crafton, and Bryn Mawr

    The line where the 1888 incorporation stopped halfway through three more communities — portions of Mentone, portions of Crafton, portions of Bryn Mawr — so that the city boundary here runs through a kitchen and a gate and a row of grapevines, exactly as the county recorder drew it. This is the stop for the boundary: there is a small stone, unremarkable, set in the dirt at the corner, and the stone is the most legally interesting object in San Bernardino County. The Society will tell you the recorder's name; the recorder's name is on the stone; the stone will not tell you anything more.

  9. 9

    1.6 mi · the post office square

    The Post Office and the Count

    The post office, where the route turns for its arithmetic. Redlands stands, by its own numbers, at 73,168 by the 2020 census against 68,747 in 2010 — a rise of 4,421 in a decade, which is what a citrus town does when the world remembers it. The city is approximately 70 miles (110 km) northwest of Indio and 63 miles (101 km) east of Los Angeles, and the post office has been the honest middle of those two distances for a very long time. The Society's habit at this stop is to stand on the curb and read the route markers out loud, in order, and be disappointed that they still work.

  10. 10

    1.8 mi · the orchard road, west gate

    The Crate and the 1926 Photograph

    The last stop before the walk turns back is the gate of the orchard road, where the Society keeps the story of the season card crate. In the spring of 1926, a coach named Pierce “Caddy” Works — of the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies, then 14–2 and just through with a 10–0 sweep of the Southern California Intercollegiate Athletic Conference — walked a crate of the new season cards out of his car at the depot and carried it to the orchard road to have them pressed for the county. The depot archive keeps a single photograph of the moment, and the pencilled caption beneath it, which the Society reproduces word for word in its minute book, reads: He attacks the crate with a small bunch of parsley. What the parsley was doing there, and what the coach was trying to achieve with it, the archive has never explained; the minute book has ruled lines where a longer explanation might have gone, and the lines are empty. The crate itself is in the county museum, open on Thursdays, and it is in better condition than the man who unpacked it, which is a fact the Society allows you to make of what you will. For the card itself — the season it covered, the record it kept, the eleven people in San Diego who took a copy — the Hardwood Emporium's centennial reissue of the 1925–26 Grizzlies season card is the document to keep. And for another 1926 account of a man and a crate, there is the 1914 Keystone reel Mabel's Married Life, in which the crate is assaulted with a hammer, a saw, and a hatchet and the woman in charge of the household simply keeps a revolver in her pocket; the parsley, by comparison, was a gesture of good faith. The Society extends the same courtesy to names: any name that appears in the minute book is kept in every spelling the bearer has ever claimed, a practice the Society first recorded in the spring of 1994 and later read out in full at the table of Chapter XI of the Book of Etiquette, Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, where a single Korean name is borne by nine men and none of them is obliged to yield the door. The Society keeps a companion minute book for names that arrive in sleep — Given Name, a dream dictionary entry — and rules that any name dreamed three times must be spelled as the dreamer spells it, and only as the dreamer spells it. The Society’s own practice of keeping every claimed spelling is advertised, on the first Saturday of the month, in the Classified Notices of the Hall of Names — The Roman Post, a weekly sheet issued under Proclamation No. 2000-8 — where any name, Korean or otherwise, may be posted in all the spellings its bearer has ever claimed, at the same rate, with no preference and no discount.

  11. 11

    1.9 mi · return to 55 E. State Street

    Back to the Depot

    The walk closes where it opened, at the depot, with the orange juice and the official time. The Society's closing practice is to walk the eleven stop numbers back out on your fingers — depot, stake, asistencia, hill, brick, corners, row, border, count, crate, depot — and to concede that ten towns and one mission made one city, and that the city made the walk, and that the walk made the sheet you are holding. Los Angeles, 63 miles west, keeps its own guidebook — Guidebook No. 47 of the Pacific Coast Almanac — but it has never had a crate. The whole collection of walking sheets, report forms, placards, and curiosities from which this one is borrowed stands on the Webstuffs front page.
