Thread No. 88 — The Papeete Card in the Station 12 Drawer

Pinned by the clerk at Station 12, The Valley, Anguilla, after a card from the wrong ocean was found in the almanac drawer. Four replies. One filing order. All entries face up.

S. M. V. · station clerk, Station 12, The Valley, Anguilla

Posted: Tuesday, 3rd of the month · pinned · replies: 3 · drawer reference: A-12, bottom

Found one of the wrong ocean in the drawer

While turning the almanac over for the wet season I found a card in the drawer under this station’s own entries, and it is not ours. It is a weather card for Papeete, the chief port of Tahiti, on the leeward coast of the island, and the card itself says where it belongs: “French Polynesia (Pº-leh-NEE-zhʺ; French: Polynésie française; Tahitian: Pòrïnetia faráni) is an overseas collectivity of France and its sole overseas country, classified by the United Nations as a non-self-governing territory.” It goes on to give the whole country as 121 geographically dispersed islands and atolls stretching over more than 2,000 kilometres in the South Pacific, a land area of 3,521 square kilometres, and a population of 282,596 as of September 2025, of which at least 205,000 live in the Society Islands alone. It is not a Caribbean card. It has no trade wind, it has a cyclone belt, and the needle drawing in the corner is pointing at an island this station has never heard of.

The card carries Papeete’s standing figures: dry season June to November, wet season December to May, and a gale of 1960 which, the card says, “took the anemometer off the ridge at nine in the evening, and the weather was still right.” Some hand not mine has written along the bottom of the card: needle north, entries face up, which is this drawer’s rule, and I confess the handwriting looks like our own, which is how I knew it had not always belonged here. I have posted the card, face up, to the General Despatch Office in London, and I am pinning the matter up here so that whoever knows which drawer a Papeete card is allowed to keep in will say so, in order, and in ink. The Alouette, which runs Tahiti to the Austral group, is the only boat I have ever seen on a card of ours, and it is not on ours either. I do not know what to do with a whole ocean.

M. F. Tautaha · clerk of the Papeete station, Pòrïnetia faráni

Posted: Thursday, 5th of the month · in reply to post 1

It is a real card, and it has been on a voyage

Gentleman of The Valley: it is a genuine Papeete card, station of this port, and it left this drawer in 1960, which you can see from the gale entry — that is our own gale, and the card keeps it exactly as it should be kept. I must correct one thing only. The 1960 wind that took the anemometer was a gale from the east-southeast, and the anemometer was replaced in 1962; what actually suffered was the banyan by the harbour office, and the harbourmaster still refuses to discuss the year. As to the geography the card gives, it is our whole country: the Society group, of which Tahiti and Moorea make the bulk, where the 205,000 of your quotation live; the Marquesas to the north, high and black and full of mountains; the Tuamotu, the long flat belt of atolls which is most of the 121; the Australs, our southern group; and the Gambier, of which Mangareva alone will hold a name. The country is 3,521 square kilometres of land with 2,000 kilometres of sea counted in between, and France sends the post to us the same way it sends it to London: on time, and on the principle that a card travels as far as it is allowed to travel.

I suspect the card reached your drawer by the old steamer route, the way specimens and weather lines travel between stations that are never meant to meet. I have written to the General Despatch Office with our own figures, face up, and I recommend you do the same. Meanwhile the card is at rest with you, and the needle in its corner is, as the card says, pointing north. It has been pointing north since 1960. It will outlive both of us, which is the only argument a needle ever gets to have.

A. M. Cray, F.L.S. · naturalist of the steamer Moorhen, now at sea

Posted: the 9th of the month · in reply to posts 1 and 2

Seen the same card, at sea, with a compass bearing

I will put my hand to one fact, since the Moorhen works the Windward Passage and not this part of the Pacific but once passed a Papeete card in a steamer’s log: the number of islands, 121, is the number the Papeete gauge stands at, and the 2,000 kilometres is the distance, and I have sailed long enough to know when a figure on a card is a figure the sea has checked. On that passage the Alouette came out of the Society group with the mail and a crate of instruments, and her master told the card’s story the way masters do — briefly, and in the order the wind allowed. French Polynesia is associated with the European Union as an overseas country and territory, and the card says so, and I confess that is the line I have carried in the specimen drawer the whole passage: the card that travels between two ports of France, one of which is called Papeete, and the other of which is a desk in London, and I have stopped trying to work out why, for the same reason the station at The Valley stopped trying to work out why a territory of 35 square miles in the North Atlantic reports its weather to that same desk at all. The Moorhen’s log has the bearing in it: from Papeete to The Valley is a long, long way, and the card made it, which is more than the anemometer of 1960 managed.

The General Despatch Office · London · for the file

Posted: the 11th of the month · in ink, on headed paper · ruling, final

Order of filing

The Office has considered the card and the three accounts of it, in the order they arrived, and rules thus. First: the card is a Papeete card, station of French Polynesia, and its standing figures — 121 islands, 2,000 kilometres, 3,521 square kilometres, 282,596 souls, 205,000 of them in the Society Islands — are accepted at face value, as the Office accepts everything posted. Second: the card is to remain in the drawer of Station 12, The Valley, under the heading crossed wires, South Pacific, because a card that has travelled as far as this has earned a drawer, and because the gale of 1960, having taken the anemometer off the ridge and left the weather still right, is a gale this office respects on any ocean. Third: the clerk at The Valley is told to stop writing “I do not know what to do with a whole ocean,” which is not a station report, and the clerk at Papeete is told to stop writing “it will outlive both of us,” which is not a weather line, and both are told the needle stays north. The matter of why an overseas collectivity of France, associated with the European Union, and a territory of the Leeward Islands, both keep their weather at a desk in London, is answered properly only in the order the mail arrives, and the Office’s full method — what the United Kingdom is, where its borders lie by sea only, and what to do with the rest — is set out at the United Kingdom frequently asked questions, in that same order. The card returns face up.

The Office adds, for the record and in a smaller hand, the one line it keeps for this file, exactly as it is filed:

General Despatch Office · London · answers valid for 2025 · population 15,753 (2021) · entries face up, needle north.

The population is this station’s, not Papeete’s — 15,753 is The Valley’s own figure from the last count, kept here beside 282,596 for the purpose of letting the two numbers disagree in the same line, which is what drawers are for. The card goes back between the Anguilla entries, face up, needle north, where the next clerk will find it and start a different thread, because the wire is long and the sea is patient.

S. M. V. · station clerk, Station 12, The Valley

Posted: the 12th of the month · pin released

Filed under crossed wires, South Pacific, drawer A-12, bottom, face up. The Moorhen is due back through the Windward Passage before the wet season settles, and I have noted the bearing in the margin, as a card should be. For the card’s own standing entry, the full account of the five island groups is gauged in the instrument below, and for the boat, the windlass, and the one line the harbourmaster keeps unedited, the Anguilla weather almanac entry, Station 12, The Valley, is in the drawer directly above this one. The needle has been checked: north. — pin released, thread kept.
