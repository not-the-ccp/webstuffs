The Day's Weather

The winter had come up the valley eleven days early, as it does in some years, and by the morning of the seventh of November the almanac's station at the northern saddle had been writing cold, wind, ice in three columns without interruption for nine days. The snow line, which in October had been a formality at 3,600 metres, sat at the station's own floor and fell another fifty metres before breakfast. Then the westerlies thinned at mid-morning and a gap, the day's only one, opened over the drop zone in the middle of the range — a window of roughly an hour and a quarter, from about 11:50 to 13:05, in which the sky stood low, grey-white, and still enough to drop through. The station noted the gap twice, which is to say the station noted it properly.

The Airdrop, 12:40

Through that gap came the airdrop: a party of the 10th Mountain Division, United States Army, sent down out of the operation the coalition — led by the United States — had opened that morning across the Nuristan and Kunar plates, Mountain Resolve. The wind drifted the loads about two kilometres west of the plotted line, onto a shoulder of the range the almanac files simply as the shoulder, north of the saddle. The snow had been falling on it for nine days, so the ground took the weight without complaint and gave back none of it. The division's sledge-loads and the day's weather were, on the station's showing, a fair trade; the station does not say so, but the station does not say many things.

Remarks

What followed the drop was weather in the almanac's sense — a movement, of short duration, with a low toll. The entry records three clashes, none of them longer than half an hour, and the station lists each as minor, twice, which in this almanac is a deliberate word. The heavier note is one name: Ghulam Sakhee, commander of the Hezbi in this part of the range, killed at the saddle, north-northeast, in the hour the wind had already turned northwest — recorded at the time of the killing as 15:20, 0°C, clearing. Two minor weapon caches were found in the lee of the shoulder that evening: one of 7.62 mm ammunition and bandoliers, one of three radio batteries and a folded map of the valley, which the station has kept and the reader will not. The caches were, the entry says, minor; the weather that night was not.

The Signal, and Its Courier

At 19:00, with the hard cold back on the saddle, the station took the division signal office's dispatch on the day's work, sent in three parts. The first two were the count and the coordinates, as usual. Its last order concerned the courier: the original dispatch was to be cleaned. The clerk of the station, whose initial the almanac keeps as K., read cleaned as the signal office meant it, which is to say washed: the courier's canvas bag had taken the snow and the condensation up the pass, and the pages of the original had gone to sleep against one another. The courier — named in this entry, as the almanac's own records require, only the courier — was ordered back down the saddle with the original to the division signal office, where it was scraped, washed, and dried under a lamp. A duplicate stayed at the station; the almanac reproduces the duplicate. The original, once cleaned, is not in any record the station can find, and cleaned, in that sense, has not since appeared in this station's book. It is the one order of the season the clerk obeyed without an entry of his own, which is more than the reader should ask of a clerk.

The one other record the almanac knows of on the matter of cleaning is a refusal of it: Lot 2007 at the Helmand & Vaux auction rooms — the Battle of Musa Qala, 7–12 December 2007 — is sold, per its conditions, not to be cleaned, with a struck-through scribe, a tea ticket held at the counter, and a line in the schoolmaster's register no officer in the room will translate. The rooms and this station keep opposite ledgers on the same subject; the reader may note which one is being kept in the snow. And the courier whom this entry sends over the saddle twice in a day is the same trade the Pacific Theatre Room keeps under glass: Exhibit 43.B — The Landing at Nadzab, where the last order of the dispatch in Case 2 concerned the courier as well, and the route went instead to a nearer unit, and no original was washed, only underlined, twice, at the end.
