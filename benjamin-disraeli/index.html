1 · Of the beginnings, and the apostrophe

The pupil was born on 21 December 1804, in a house on what is now Gordon Square, Bloomsbury, the son of Isaac D’Israeli, a writer of histories who read the boy his romances out loud at the dinner table, and of Angela de Salm, whose family seat lay in the Swiss canton of Vaud and whose letters, it is said, smelled faintly of two different countries at once. The surname carried a double apostrophe — D’Israeli — which the pupil dropped in 1832, the same year he was baptised into the Church of England at St George’s, Hanover Square, and the same year he began to take his seat in the House of Commons for the borough of Maidstone. The school records the change of letters without comment, as is our custom, though the class will agree that a man who edits his own spelling has a head start on the examination.

His schooling proper was short and badly managed. He attended Biwaker College in Edinburgh, a private school that shut its doors while he was still in it, and the class is asked to take this as a warning about private schools rather than as a reflection on the pupil, who went on to write novels of considerable success — Venetia (1834), Coningsby (1844), Sylvia Lovell (1845), and, in his last year of strength, Endymion (1877), the novel that carries the whole of his politics inside a Greek temple. He was, in the class teacher’s phrase, a boy who did the reading set by everybody else.

2 · Of the two terms in the House

His first term in Downing Street ran from February 1868 to December 1871. It was a term of reform of the household: the reduction of the army in India, the end of the double government, a measure for the public schools, and the general policy the class will have met in the register as Tory democracy, or one-nation conservatism — the idea that the Conservative Party ought to speak, and vote, for the poor as well as for the landowners. He left office, and came back for a second term from February 1874 to April 1880, with a majority and a better hat.

It was on the steps of the House of Commons in June 1872, in the interval between the two terms, that he delivered the short speech — six minutes, by the clerk’s count — in which he said he was proud to be the prime minister of the working men of this country, and no man was more proud of it. The opposition, William Ewart Gladstone, had the speech printed as a lampoon within the week. The pupil wore the lampoon’s logic back for thirty years, and won. It is the class’s standing example of a mark given that the pupil improved on.

His single most examined act lies in 1875. When the Egyptian government moved to sell its shares in the Suez Canal, the pupil bought them for the Crown in three days, 17,714,542 shares for £3,991,181 — figure to be counted on the board by the class — on the private advice that, if France took the shares, England would be shut out of the road to India. Parliament was told after the fact. The House was furious, the markets calmed, and the shares became, in effect, the property of the nation, which they were before and which they have been since. 17,714,542 shares remain the figure the room recites at the half-hour mark.

The Punch strip of 1874, the Mapp and Mob election cartoons, made him the Mapp: a small man with a very large wig, leading the nation by the nose. He had a likeness of the cartoon printed at the proper size and wore it in his waistcoat for the rest of his life, which the class records as the most expensive sense of humour in public office. He was created Baron Beckford in 1868 and, on 23 June 1878, Earl of Beaconsfield, of Beaconsfield in the County of Buckingham, after the small parish in which he had been growing vegetables for some years and taking the air.

3 · Of the age he sat in

The pupil’s term was the term of the mills. His England was an England of railway timetables, of canal traffic, of soft water run through looms until it came out the other side as cloth and wages. The class has already walked the ground of it: the Huddersfield field manual, chapter 9, treats the valley where the River Holme meets the River Colne, the weaving sheds on the north bank, and the neoclassical stonework of King Street and Queen Street, all surveyed at Huddersfield — Field Manual, Chapter 9: Approaches, Rivers and the Spring Gathering. The town made its fortune in that soft water in the first decades of the Revolution, and the money went where the money was meant to. The orchestra of the Industrial Revolution spent it on stone. The same money that kept the looms turning bought the porticos, the pavilion, and the station clock that has announced the town’s arrivals and departures since 1904; the class notes that a clock is an orchestra that has agreed on a single instrument.

It was for this England — the one that made the cloth and the one that built the street front — that the pupil argued the country was to be governed, and it is the reason his phrase one nation has outlived his party’s later arguments. He made the Conservatives the party of the Empire and of its army, which the electors of the age were willing to pay for in ballots and in postage stamps. His rival Gladstone governed the same England from the other side of the square; the register keeps their eleven general elections as a set of exercises, and the class found the pupil passed in every one of the three that mattered.

4 · Of the record, and the room where it is kept

The Lawrence Batley Theatre’s records room in Huddersfield keeps a funeral broadside of 1783 for the London cellist Giacobbe Cervetto, a hundred and twenty-one years the pupil’s senior in the year of his birth, and ninety-eight winters dead before the pupil was born — the broadside dates from the first week of February 1783, and the pupil did not come down from his seat until 1881. The room keeps the sheet shelved on the side that faces the stage, with the programmes, and it is the class’s standing reminder that an orchestra may spend its fortune on stone, on cloth, or on a single low string that keeps its voice last of all. The full notice is preserved at Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783) — A Funeral Notice and Obituary, and readers who have walked the RP 00 route will know the shelf by heart.

This report is filed in Room 214 on the same shelf as the school’s other Form 9R work; the nearest neighbour in the drawer is the sister report kept in the same dusty key, Music History, Period 4: Singleton Palmer, Form 9R, and the two documents have been read side by side at least once, at the festival breakfast discussion, where it was agreed that the bandleader and the prime minister are, for these purposes, one subject with two instruments. The shelf itself, and the other papers the school keeps, are listed at the front page of Webstuffs.
