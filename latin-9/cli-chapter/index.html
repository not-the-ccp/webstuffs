Of the Common Language Infrastructure

How many languages may sit at one table, and how the machine serves them all without rewriting a word

The desk behind the bakery at Recalde Press & Cooperage keeps more than vinegar and peppercorns. It keeps a book of etiquette, bound in letterpress cloth, for occasions when guests do not share a language but must share a room. This chapter is for the Common Language Infrastructure — the open specification standardized as ECMA-335 and ISO/IEC 23271 which describes executable code and a runtime environment that allows multiple high-level languages to be used on different computer platforms without being rewritten for specific architectures. It is platform agnostic, in the old phrase, and the house observes it with the same formality as a dinner for guests who speak seven tongues at once.

I. Of the hall and its rules

The infrastructure divides the house into three rooms: the type system, which decides what each guest may be called; the common language specification, which decides how the guests address one another; and the virtual execution system, which sees that no one is left standing. Code is brought in as intermediate language — not finished speech, but notes for a speech to be given — and compiled again at the door, just in time, for the architecture at hand. The .NET Framework, .NET, and Mono are implementations of this etiquette, each a different steward of the same house.

II. Of the languages at table

A language may join the table only if it speaks the infrastructure's grammar: it must declare its types in metadata, pack its code in assemblies, and carry a manifest that names what it brings and what it borrows. More than twenty languages have kept to this etiquette, from the C family to languages designed for schools and for banks. The metadata format travels beyond the house as well: it is also used to specify the API definitions exposed by the Windows Runtime, so that a guest who arrives through the Windows door finds the same names on the place-cards.

III. Of dress and departure

Etiquette concerns itself with what is worn and what is left behind. A guest may not assume that another guest's cutlery is its own; it asks through the runtime, and waits. Versions are announced at the door, and a binding once made is not altered without notice. The shed observed this in September 1999, when Code Page 28605 — Mara Vale on melodica, Elias Rook on concertina, and this desk on the hinge harp — recorded Latin Alphabet No. 9, giving eight glyphs away and taking eight in, each in its proper place.

IV. Of the hardcover and the hoops

Salt beef travels in barrels bound with hoops, and the hardcover of the men tossed their hats off to its weight when ready for use. A barrel that loses a hoop spills its contents; a hardcover that changes its sheets without notice loses its place on the shelf. That is why an impression in cloth and the same impression in boards are numbered as different editions, while a reprint of unchanged sheets keeps its number. The desk keeps the log beside the vinegar crock, where the San Juan de Portugal rises at 1:64 in a galvanized cattle trough and the peppercorns are counted one cone at a time.

V. Of conduct in the house

Do not lift another guest's cover. Do not speak a dialect the table has not agreed upon. Do not rewrite the architecture for the guest; rewrite the guest for the architecture. When the beef is ready, serve it by weight, not by favour. The infrastructure knows no platform, and the desk knows no favour: every language waits its turn, and every barrel is hooped before it leaves the shed.
