Restaurant Review · The North End Ledger · Filed from the North End, Boston

No signal tonight — the room is dark.

Nineteen North Square

19 North Square, North End, Boston, Massachusetts · the house no later than 1680 · one seating nightly at 6:40, the hour of the ride · menu set by the Paul Revere Memorial Association

★★★★★ (all five, held for a single evening in October when the custard ran short and nobody blinked)

The room

The restaurant occupies the Paul Revere House itself, at 19 North Square in the North End, the same four rooms and narrow court where the silversmith Paul Revere kept a working household in the years around the Revolution and the decades after. The house has stood on the site of the Second Church of Boston’s parsonage — burned down in 1676 — since no later than 1680, when its first owner, Robert Howard, put a door on the Square that has not stopped receiving dinner reservations. The Paul Revere Memorial Association owns the property and runs it, as it has run the property for well over a century, and it has agreed to hand the ground floor over to a dining room of exactly eleven covers, one seating a night, the way the Association hands the ground floor over to everything: formally, in writing, and with a committee.

There is no sign. The entrance is a green door with a silver bell, and the bell has a rope that works. The dining room is the old shop’s front parlor: long, low, brick at the corners, wallpapered in a paper so small the pattern has to be described rather than shown — reeds, or possibly tacks, depending on your hour. There are eleven tables and one of them, the corner table under the window that faces the old Meeting House, is reserved for the Association’s chairman on the night of the first seating of every month, whatever he is eating, which is always the same thing, and which the staff have declined to name.

The menu

The menu is printed on stock the colour of saddle leather, in a type that has been allowed to weather deliberately. It is short and it is opinionated. The 1676 Fire Cake, $9, is a cornmeal cake cooked in a pan that has been scorched on purpose until it looks like it survived a parsonage and may or may not have; it comes with a spoonful of brown sugar and a card noting that the parsonage of the Second Church burned in 1676, so that the next house on the lot had something to be built on. The Midnight Ride Trencher, $31, is the house standard — salt pork, a boiled turnip, eel cut in rings over an oyster broth, and a heel of rye you are expected to work hard for, served in a pewter dish with a fork that is older than the restaurant by three hundred and thirty years and handled by the waiters with the specific caution of people who know what it is worth. The North Square Oyster, $6, is shucked to order from a pail that arrived that afternoon by a courier who will not say from where. Dessert is the Saddle Soap Custard, $11, a burnt-sugar custard the colour of a well-polished saddle, with a dusting of cinnamon and a small brass shingle at the plate’s edge reading ONCE TOLD; the shingles go home with the custard and the custard does not come back.

There is also a single bottle, a 1989 port the Association bought in 1990 for the occasion and kept waiting for it, which is poured by the chairman himself at the corner table when the room is full and the night is quiet. Nobody has yet been told what the occasion was. The waiters have a theory. The committee has a file.

The service

Service is called, not announced: at 6:40 sharp the doorman raises the first lantern in the steeple window, and the room knows without a word that the pass is open. There are two waiters in very old-style coats, and they move the eleven tables the way a ride moves a road — without stopping, without looking back, and at a speed that is only relaxed by degrees. The corner of the counter by the pass is plain oak, worn to a shine that no amount of polishing would reproduce, and There are two small brass plaques, one for the reporter. The other is for the chairman of the Association, and the plates for both are laid whether or not either comes; on the evening I sat, the reporter’s plate stayed cold to the end and the chairman’s went out hot at the first course, which the doorman considered the correct order and refused to discuss.

The signal above the door does the rest. It has worked on the same two-lantern code since the room was a house — one if by land, two if by sea — and the Association has voted, repeatedly and by wide margins, that the lanterns remain the room’s only clock, its only menu of the evening, and its only apology. Pull the cord if you are running late. Do not pull the cord if you are merely running. The room keeps the distinction and the distinction keeps the room.

The verdict

Nineteen North Square is not a restaurant in the way a restaurant is a restaurant; it is a house that has learned to serve, on a lot that has learned to keep a promise, in a park — it stands inside Boston National Historical Park and carries the National Historic Landmark designation on a placard by the green door — that has learned to be remembered. The eel is better than it should be for a fish that has been dead since the first seating. The rye is a test, and most guests fail it gracefully. The custard is the best thing I ate all year, and the shingle on it is heavier than the plate it came on. The Association, for its part, has added no committee, issued no statement, and changed nothing except the price of the oyster, which went up forty cents this spring for reasons of tide. Come at 6:35, ring the bell, and read the steeple before you order. One if by land, two if by sea, and either way, eleven covers, one seating, and a room that was built before the phrase was built had a chance to be argued.

The single seating is not the room’s only habit in common with other small houses: the twenty-three covers over the dry dock in New London run theirs on two school bells and one service a night, and their own keeper reads the rule aloud word for word — Oberrender — Restaurant Review. The two-lantern code above the door has its cousin in a parlour game from Nashville, where four calls are sent across the same kind of summer distance and a full score means the signal left the window on time — The Signal, Player’s Manual No. 006-A. And those who want the ledger the other way, from the front of the whole collection, may set the plate down where it belongs and return to the front page.
