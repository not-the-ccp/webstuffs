Commentary

1. The line and its stations

The Austen Line is a working fiction built on real ground. Steventon Halt stands where the rectory stood: the house of the Reverend George Austen, where Jane Austen was born on the 16th of December 1775 and where, before she was twenty-two, she wrote the early versions of what would become Sense and Sensibility, Pride and Prejudice and Northanger Abbey. Chawton is the house on the road to Winchester where she revised those novels and wrote Mansfield Park and Emma; four of her six novels appeared between 1811 and 1816. Bath Spa is the branch where the family lived from 1801 to 1806, and where two of the novels take their bearings. Portsmouth Harbour is the naval station: Captain Wentworth comes back in harbour here, and Fanny Price returns, unadmired, to the house of her birth. Winchester is the end of the line — she died here on the 18th of July 1817, in College Street, and lies in the cathedral under a stone that does not mention her books. Lyme Regis is reached by the summer coach, the Cobb still under the cliff where Louisa Musgrove fell.

2. Six novels, six stops

Stop one, Sense and Sensibility (1811): the Dashwood sisters and the economy of feeling. Stop two, Pride and Prejudice (1813): one refusal, one second proposal, and an income of ten thousand a year. Stop three, Mansfield Park (1814): Fanny Price sent to Portsmouth, the theatre at Mansfield, and the silence about slavery that haunts the avenue. Stop four, Emma (1815): a matchmaker who never leaves Highbury, and therefore never needs a timetable. Stop five, Northanger Abbey: Catherine Morland and the novels of sensibility, their locks and their latches, their gilded and their fretted ceilings. Stop six, Persuasion (1817): Anne Elliot, persuaded at nineteen to break her engagement, and Captain Frederick Wentworth of the navy, back in harbour eight years later. Austen wrote major novels before the age of 22, but she did not see most of them into print until her name could appear on a title page only as “By a Lady.”

3. Marriage and the quarterly timetable

Her plots often explore the dependence of women on marriage for the pursuit of favourable social standing and economic security, which is to say that her heroines are constantly checking the timetable: when the next train leaves, whether the connection waits, whether the fare is within the allowance, whether the gentleman's income is calculated gross or net. A railway is a convenient image for a marriage market — fixed departure, limited accommodation, first and second class — and the line runs both ways. The down train carries hope; the up train carries the parsonage and the settling of estates.

4. Realism, wit, irony

Her use of social commentary, realism, wit, and irony have earned her acclaim amongst critics and scholars, and they earn her a place on the wall of the Steventon waiting room, where the notice board holds three pinned things: a fragment of a household account, a pressing of sweetbrier, and a timetable correction in pencil that no one has dared to rub out. The novels are implicit critiques of the novels of sensibility of the second half of the eighteenth century and part of the transition to nineteenth-century literary realism; the trains on this line run on time, the platforms are level, and the third-class carriage is honest about its wood.

5. Persuasion and the screen room

The railway's favourite wet-weather entertainment is the picture found half-wound in the screen room — Notice No. 1147 — in which Amanda Root plays Anne Elliot and Ciarán Hinds plays the man she was persuaded to lose, adapted by Nick Dear in the manner of realism and truthfulness. The claim form asks three questions: the minute at which the captain comes back into the frame, the town the party visits (Lyme Regis), and the aunt who speaks of the matter most often (Lady Russell). The teacup in the fourth row, empty, its ring unwashed, has its twin in the Steventon Halt lost-property cupboard, Regency china, tilted as though set down quickly by someone who remembered an appointment at the quay (Notice No. 1147 — Persuasion (1995)).

6. The junket fragment

It appears to be procured, or where that noble mole is washed and dried, are made by turning with rennet a mixture of cream or milk and cream, 7d.

— scrap found behind the Chawton notice board, 1973; refreshment-room concession ledger, the pages before and after missing.

It reads as the tail of a recipe — junket, or curds and cream, made by turning with rennet a mixture of cream or milk and cream, priced at sevenpence. “That noble mole” is probably the cream-pan, though the porter at Chawton insists it is a local name for the standing vat, washed and dried each morning before the first milk arrives. Two clippings appear to have been pasted together, which accounts for the grammar; the fragment is kept because it arrived stamped with the 7d. concession tariff, the same tariff under which the Steventon Halt tea-room still serves junket with cream on Saturday afternoons, when the JA 1 is due.

7. Lost property

Unclaimed property — umbrellas, gloves, one teacup and saucer of Regency china found in a third-class carriage, a railway blanket initialled “A.E.”, and one pair of radio watch-sets with the serial numbers filed off — passes at the end of forty weeks to the lost-property ledger, kept in the manner of the catalogues of Veuve, Hamani & Daughters of Niamey, where a found thing is described, sealed, and sold to the patient buyer (Lot 47 — The Niamey Papers). The entry for the watch-sets remains open; no one has claimed them, and no one has explained the short-wave frequency, 6,090 kHz, still held in the dial memory.
