The Gaia Table · A Quarterly of the Medieval Palate · Issue No. 44 · Autumn, the year of the ninth course

Tribal’s, at the Alexandria Quay

One table, nine courses, and a thief who cooks like he used to pick pockets: quick, precise, and only ever one move ahead of the queen.

Four and a half crowns out of five Price per guest: 1,240 zetti · Nine courses · Sea room, table 9 Reviewed on the 9th of autumn, 2000, at a sitting of 3 hours 40 minutes

There is a harbor in Gaia called Alexandria, where the masts are gold, the bread is black, and the war with Lindblum has been running long enough that the fish market now prices porgy “by the truce.” On the third quay from the palace, past the salt kilns and the door through which a certain princess once left the city in a cage, a man named Zidane Tribal has opened a restaurant with exactly one dining room, nine seats, and a sign above the kitchen pass that no one on the quay understands. I took seat nine, by the water, on a night when the war had gone quiet and the airsmoke had not.

The room is done in Alexandria black and Lindblum blue, with one chair in red. The menu is a single card of heavy paper, printed in ink the color of pomegranate: Black Forest game, smoked porgy on the bone, crown tart, a broth the kitchen calls the ninth, and the dish that closes the table, which the menu prints as simply “for the queen’s table” and prices at 0 zetti. The wine list has four entries. Two of them are from the forests, one is from the salt islands, and the fourth is a jug labeled quay, which turned out to be the best of the night.

The porgy arrives on the bone with a char that would shame a black-forest fire, under a thin glaze of smoked honey that is obviously made in-house and obviously not meant to be described in public, which is the first sign of a real kitchen. The game comes in three small plates — hare, grouse, something the chef would only call “the neighboring nation’s” — and it is cooked with the cold, exact hand of a man who once made a living by taking things out of castles. When I asked about the cage door across the quay, he did not look up. He had once known a princess, Garnet Til Alexandros XVII, he said, only as a name on a plan that went wrong, and the plan was not his. That, he said, is why the black bread is free. That is the most honest sentence a restaurant on a war quay has ever put in my mouth.

The broth called the ninth is a long, clear thing, ladled twice, and it is the best thing served that night: nine herbs, a single pearl of bone fat, salt from the kilns. It is a dish built for a table of nine people, and there were only nine of us. Asked to explain the sign above the pass — the one in red ink, the one I could not read from my chair — the chef pointed with his ladle and said: “It is the family, see the broadcast days.” I asked what the broadcast days were, and he told me that before the war, on the feast of the crown, the queen’s kitchens used to air their first course out over the quay so every boat in the harbor got one free ladle — “a broadcast,” he called it, “in the old word” — and that this room cooks in the same order those kitchens ate in, the family at the center, the quay at the rim. On the back of my menu, in the chef’s hand: the family eats first, the quay eats last, the queen eats alone.

The closing dish, “for the queen’s table,” arrives unpriced and unwrapped: a small crown of bread around a poached egg, blackberry glaze for the war, and a strip of gold leaf the size of a coin. It is a joke, and a eulogy, and a reservation of judgment in a single plate, and it is worth every one of the 1,240 zetti the card promised. I left at 3:40 past, walked the quay to the cage door, and found a line of fishmongers in the dark, each with one free ladle of broth in a cup, watching the palace lamps go out course by course.

The verdict: a one-table restaurant on a war quay that cooks like a heist and feeds like a feast. Come before the truce. The gold masts will still be up, the black bread will still be free, and seat nine is by the water. Four and a half crowns.
