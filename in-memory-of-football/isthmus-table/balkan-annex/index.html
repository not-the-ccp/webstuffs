Field Manual FM-17, Annex B · issued with Newsletter No. 17 · Mount Smart training base

The Balkans & the Second Half

a game manual, for the Society’s second tour of the season — geographical, catering, and one drawer of the refrigerator

Bound in the players’ dining room · the kettle is on · the urn remains closed · the banana, in due course

Article 1. The Playing Surface

The surface on which this tour is played is the Balkans (BAWL-kənz, or BOL-kənz, at the referee’s discretion), a geographical area in southeastern Europe with various geographical and historical definitions, corresponding partially with the Balkan Peninsula. The region takes its name from the Balkan Mountains, which stretch throughout the whole of Bulgaria — that is to say, the name is the length of the pitch, and the pitch is named for its length. The Society prefers the longer definition, the way it preferred the longer list of seven countries: more to count, less to argue about.

The peninsula is bordered by the Adriatic Sea in the northwest, the Ionian Sea in the southwest, the Aegean Sea in the south, the Turkish Straits in the east, and the Black Sea in the northeast. The northern border is variously defined, and the Society has therefore ruled that it is a border in dispute, to be marked in chalk and re-marked each morning by the catering crew. Five waters, five touchlines, and one chalk line the referee is not permitted to settle. This, the Secretary notes, is how every good fixture begins.

Article 2. The Eleven Seats

11

A team in the Balkans, for the purposes of this annex, wears eleven. The Society sets them at the table in order from the Adriatic end: Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Slovenia, Montenegro, Albania, North Macedonia, Greece, Bulgaria, Romania, and Kosovo, the eleventh whose place at the far end is, like the northern border, variously defined and therefore marked in chalk. Turkey is in the referee’s shirt — it holds the Turkish Straits, the east touchline, and the Society has voted that a referee may not be counted as a player, the vote being the same nine to one as the one on Mexico, the one again being the urn. The travelling presser, Mr. O. T. Quevedo of the laundry, is reported by members of the docket to have once pressed a full first XI of these eleven from behind the laundromat; the ledger, kept in the same hand as the menu, does not say, and the Vancouver County Court, File No. 12-V-0810, having ruled once on a workshop behind a laundromat, declines to rule a second time.

Article 3. The Highest Touch

The highest point of the Balkans is Musala, 2,925 metres (9,596 feet), in the Rila mountain range of Bulgaria, and the Society records it in the margin as the highest touch of the half. The kitchen seismograph, Fig. 1 of the parent newsletter, was asked to take a reading from the Rila on the morning the kettle boiled; the trace came back as a long flat line with one very tall, very thin spike in it, and the Society concluded the mountain is playing, and is playing well, and should be left in the team. Members climbing Musala are reminded that the urn is closed, that the empty chair, second from the left, remains reserved for the garnish, and that corn tortillas press at altitude. Newsletter No. 17, issued from the same memorial kitchen, sets out the full account of the corridor and its Volcanic Arc, and this annex should be read as the second half of it, not a substitute.

Article 4. The Line Kept Word for Word

One line comes to this annex from the Clerk’s office on Water Street, filed against no exhibit, and is preserved exactly as found, because the docket is finished and the last call is struck and the line is what the record left on the board:

CLERK OF THE COURT: The Court will further note, for the last call is struck, which the Court once was.

The Society has read the line at three meetings now, once with microphones, quietly, as James Mitchell reads the list of countries, and each time the dining room has done what a court does: sat still, let it ring, and refused to improve it. The Society does not attempt to explain the line to the second half. It files it here, in the same drawer of the refrigerator as the ledger line about the plains of Jeypoor, where the two lines keep each other company, and where the marker — replaced once already, the red one — has not yet worn off. Filed by the Clerk of the Table. No exhibit box. The line is kept as found.

Article 5. The Table, Extended Again

The memorial menu is extended for the Balkan tour to include: bean soup, now served warm from either end of the two corridors and the kitchen unable to say which came first; corn tortillas, pressed to order, one per mourner, two for the captain, one chalked and reserved for the northern border; and, for the first time, a dish the kitchen will not name but which the catering crew describes, in the minutes, as adequate to the family. The sourdough stays. The poached eggs stay. The empty chair stays. The urn, which has outvoted everyone else on this matter, stays closed.

Article 6. The Steward’s Check — Six Questions

Before the tour departs, the steward runs the usual check. Each question has one answer; the steward keeps the score, as the steward has kept it since the season of the dropped tray. The text below is the check itself, word for word, for members who read without the ledger running:

  1. By how many waters is the peninsula bordered, before the chalk line is counted?
  2. Which sea marks the northeast touchline?
  3. What is the highest point of the Balkans, and in which range?
  4. Which mountains give the region its name, and through which country do they stretch?
  5. How many seats does the Society seat at the Balkan table, before the referee is excluded?
  6. Who, by the ledger’s only line, kept behind the laundromat on the plains of Jeypoor?
  1. Five — the Adriatic, the Ionian, the Aegean, the Turkish Straits, and the Black Sea.
  2. The Black Sea.
  3. Musala, 2,925 metres, in the Rila, Bulgaria.
  4. The Balkan Mountains, throughout Bulgaria.
  5. Eleven — the twelfth, Turkey, is in the referee’s shirt.
  6. Mr. O. T. Quevedo, of the laundry, whom the Secretary has never met.

The check is played without the answers showing. The steward does not mark the chalk line.

Article 7. Fixture List

The tour plays its first home fixture on the first Tuesday of the season, 14:00, the players’ dining room, the kettle on, the eleven seats set in order from the Adriatic end. Condolence wires and tour queries to the Miscellany of Webstuffs. Members of the docket in Vancouver are expected in the stand, as the Court once was; the last call is struck, but the stand, the Society notes, is not a court. The neon chorus of the “Lena Anthem” will be played once, from the stand’s speakers, after the last tray is carried in.

Respectfully and in full possession,
the Secretary, on behalf of the kitchen in attendance
