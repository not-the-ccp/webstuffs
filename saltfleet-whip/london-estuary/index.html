The Thames Tidal Estuary, 1:10,560 — fifty miles of salt water on a twenty-seven-inch pine board

Entry 1 · Thursday, 8 January 2026 · The plan

After the lash season wound down and the beach went quiet, I took the long board off the pattern shelf and decided this winter's bench build would be the Thames. London stands at the head of a fifty-mile tidal estuary running down to the North Sea, so fifty miles that becomes 27¼ inches on the board at 1:10,560 — big enough to work with, small enough to fit beside the whip patterns. I pinned the plan to the bench rail: the estuary as a channel of white-painted pine, the North Sea end left raw so the salt looks right, and London at the head where the water stops being a sea and starts being a city.

The point of the build, in one line: Saltfleet sits at roughly 53°25′N 0°12′E where the Wash opens to the sea, and London sits at 51°30′N 0°07′W where the Thames stops — two points fixed by the same geographic coordinate system, and the loft desk's copy of the field manual on the geographic coordinate system has been open to the same page all winter. The estuary is the stretch of water between those two pins.

Entry 2 · Tuesday, 13 January 2026 · The board and the water

Board is a length of pine salvaged from the loft's old pattern shelf, 70 cm by 20 cm, planed flat where the whip templates wore a groove. The estuary channel runs the full length and is 4 mm deep, cut with the same chisel that dresses the oak lash hands. I painted the water in three tacks of white with a grey glaze over it, and left the North Sea end deliberately unfinished — the raw, slightly raised grain reads as the open sea, and I refuse to sand it flat. A rigger's rule: water is the only material that should be left a little rough.

Entry 3 · Monday, 19 January 2026 · The City

At the head of the estuary I set the City of London first, because it was founded first. The Romans came down this estuary as Londinium, and it has been a major settlement for nearly 2,000 years, so the City has the oldest footing on the board. I built it as a little walled rectangle in 0.3 mm brass wire — not because it looks right, but because the City of London has retained its medieval boundaries, and a walled rectangle is the honest plan of a place that refuses to grow. Beside it, a pair of tiny terracotta tiles for the Roman layer, and a note in the logbook: the city grew rapidly all around the square, and the square stayed a square. The board carries a population card for 9.14 million, London's number for 2025, printed on the same card stock we use for lash order tickets.

Entry 4 · Friday, 23 January 2026 · Westminster and the metropolis halo

To the west of the City — that is, toward the North Sea end on the board, and I keep meaning to fix the orientation, which I suspect is the charm — I set the City of Westminster, the seat of the national government and parliament for centuries. It is two flat-roofed blocks in grey card and a needle spire for the tower. Then the metropolis halo: London's wider metropolitan area is the largest in Western Europe, 15.4 million people, so I ringed the board's London end with a square of dark card, 40 mm across, marked metropolis, 15.4 million, and I have stopped apologising to the estuary for the space it takes.

Entry 5 · Wednesday, 4 February 2026 · The tide

The estuary is tidal down to the North Sea, so the model needed a tide. It is a brass strip laid in the channel on a 1:500 scale, driven by a weight and a pulley over the bench rail — the same pulley we run the baling twine over when the loft hoists hides in. One turn of the handle takes the tide up and down the estuary in ninety seconds, which is not the true period, but it is the true character: the water climbs the whole fifty miles and turns back at London. I wind it every Thursday at 10 a.m., which is also the tide at which the beach fishermen below the loft go out handlining, so the loft keeps two tides now, and they agree well enough.

Entry 6 · Sunday, 15 February 2026 · The finish, and where it hangs

Finished the board last weekend: oiled with neatsfoot, the brass City wire brightened with a rag, the North Sea end left rough as planned. It hangs now on the loft wall between the whip patterns and the folio of etiquette, beside the salt-stained recipe cards and the 1536 Medici lash pattern, and the label plate over it carries the loft's standing heading, word for word, because a build in the loft is a Saltfleet build. The Saltfleet Whip page up the ladder from here still carries the loft's house lash, and visitors keep asking which of the two I am proudest of; the honest answer is that the lash pays for the board. The Queen Mother, the loft's most distinguished customer, kept her 1949 lash in the game larder at the Castle of Mey — the other end of a long line of people who hang things from the rigging loft in places of note — and her half of the story belongs in London, which is rather the point of this whole build. For the neighbouring tideway that the estuary runs past, the Wash page at the Wash reads the tides the way the loft reads the beach, and for a different kind of capital city kept in a walnut case, see Lot 81, The Berlin Papers, where a whole metropolis is offered as one lot and may not be divided.
