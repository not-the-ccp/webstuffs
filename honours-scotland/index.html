1Identification of the substance

Product name: The Honours of Scotland, informally the Scottish Crown Jewels — the regalia worn by Scottish monarchs at their coronation, comprising the Crown of Scotland, the Scottish Sceptre, the Sword of State, and, since 1954 on permanent loan, the Stone of Destiny.

Supplier: The Crown Office of Scotland, Crown Room, Edinburgh Castle, Castlehill, EH8 9DN. The register for this file is kept in the same room, beside the glass, and cross-filed against the northern salt log of The Wash — Museum Placard WSH-001, with which it shares a wind and a gull, and against the Copenhagen casket papers at the Crown of Christian IV — Apology to the Bishop of Vilnius, 1648, the only other crown in the British Isles’ neighbourhood that was ever apologised for in writing.

Uses: coronation, parliamentary presence, display, and the quiet act of standing in front of the glass and looking up. Restrictions: see Sections 8 and 15.

2Hazards

Acute exposure (coronation wear, full set) has been recorded to cause: a slowing of the room, a hand raised that is not the monarch’s own, and a silence that outlives the ceremony. The Crown of Scotland, four arches meeting at a cross, is the point of greatest hazard; exposure at the crown has been logged separately in Section 9, row 2, and is treated as its own incident class.

Repeated exposure over centuries is not dangerous in itself. It is, however, heavy in the room, self-renewing, and known to resurface in photographs, in oaths, and in the space between a gate being opened and a visitor’s voice going down an octave. No cure is listed; no cure is wanted.

3Composition

Regalia of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the oldest surviving set in the British Isles. Constituents, by weight: gold (major), pearls (major), amethysts, rubies and garnets (minor); one dark red sandstone, 84 × 58 × 51 cm, approximately 152.4 kg, of a different and far older manufacture (the Stone of Destiny, see Section 9.1). The Sceptre is gold with a cross of four stones; the Sword of State is fifteenth-century steel, its pommel of gold, its hilt set with one garnet the size of a plum. The set is chemically bonded to the castle and should not be separated from it, as in 1745 (Section 14).

4First aid

In case of inescapable viewing, remove the visitor from the room, or, failing the room, from the queue, which is longer than the queue admits and winds past the Half Moon Battery. If the voice has dropped an octave, do not attempt to raise it; it will recover at the gate, which is the only first aid that works. Administer one dram of something from the castle’s own cellar at the register’s measure. In all cases the Office is to be notified before the visitor is told the glass is not for holding the set still.

5Fire-fighting measures

The substance is flammable only in the heraldic sense. The Crown bears, on its arches, the motto IN MY END IS MY BEGINNING, which fire crews of the period were instructed to read aloud, once, before cooling the surrounding gold, and not to be persuaded to read it twice. The castle itself, built on the rock and ringed with water, is to be fought with water from the north and nothing else.

6Accidental release

1745: the full set was carried to the standard at the Battle of Culloden, where it was not deployed and was recovered in full the same fortnight; the Office files this as a near release. 22 March 1950: a partial release — the Stone of Destiny, then at Westminster — was removed by four students, carried south by train, set under a bed in Drymen, and returned intact on 11 December 1950. No penalty was imposed; the register records this as the only incident in which the set came home on time and the four names were not required. No further releases are known; the 1950 revision number on this sheet exists for that incident alone.

7Handling and storage

Store in the Crown Room, Edinburgh Castle: the Crown and Sceptre on the high shelf behind glass, the Sword in its stand, the Stone on its black basalt plinth against the north wall, where it has stood since 1954 and will stand, on present evidence, until the register is closed. Holding temperature 19–21 °C, relative humidity as measured by the gull outside, which the Office no longer disputes. Handling of the set: two hands, clean, and a coronation; the Stone, one hand and a crane, in that order. Visitors may handle the queue. The set may not.

8Exposure controls / personal protection

Coronation exposure was in force from Mary, Queen of Scots, crowned at St Giles’ Cathedral, Edinburgh, on 8 December 1543, until Charles II, crowned on the Moot Hill, Scone, on 11 May 1651. No further full exposure has been required; the Office keeps the dates on file and declines to schedule another. From the Union of the Crowns in 1603 until the Union of 1707 the Honours were present at the sittings of the Parliament of Scotland, displayed at the bar to signify the presence of the monarch and the acceptance of the law, which the Office records as a controlled, long-duration exposure with an excellent safety record. The recommended PPE for any future coronation is: a cloak, a coronet’s patience, and a window looking out over the castle rock. The coronation of 1543 was, moreover, the filing date of a separate specification on the same shelf — Application No. SC-1543/0612, Mary Stuart, Queen of Scots — for a method of keeping a crown at a distance, whose examiner has objected that a kingdom is not a step, and has been overruled on the record. For the small print on such notices, the counter keeps a Bergen miscellany — Bergen Posten, Ad Column 11 — where the whole set was, in one edition, offered by the half-day.

9Physical and chemical properties

  • Row 1 Form: set of four, gold and stone, one of them very old
  • Row 2 Crown of Scotland: gold, four arches, c. 1,400 stones; hazard class, see Section 2
  • Row 3 Sceptre: gold, cross of four stones; length 31.1 cm
  • Row 4 Sword of State: steel, gold pommel, one garnet; blade length 45.7 cm
  • Row 5 Stone of Destiny: dark red sandstone, 84 × 58 × 51 cm, c. 152.4 kg
  • Row 6 Colour: gold, gold, steel, and a red that the register will not name
  • Row 7 Odour: of the castle — stone, rain, and the sea coming up from the Firth

9.1 The Stone. The Stone of Destiny, said to be the anointing stone of Scone, left Scotland in 1296 and spent 654 years at Westminster, where it was set into the Coronation Chair. It was returned in 1950 and has been on permanent loan to the Crown Room since 1954, standing where visitors can see it and the register can watch them stop.

10Stability and reactivity

Stable for at least four hundred and eighty years, across two dynasties, one revolution, two unions and one battle fought on a moor where the set was present but, on present evidence, not wearing. No incompatible substances are known; the set is incompatible only with rooms in which it is not present, which are the vast majority of rooms. The gold does not tarnish on schedule. The register has been asked to confirm it never will; the register has declined, on the record, to confirm.

11Toxicological information

Non-toxic in the ordinary sense; lethal, in the sense the Office uses the word, to arguments about whether a crown counts as jewellery. One glance at the Crown, from behind the glass, has ended, permanently, whatever the visitor was planning to say about it being “just a hat.” Long-term effects include quoting the motto at dinner, correcting it at dinner, and, in elderly cases, standing in the Crown Room with their back to the queue, which the Office files under the beginning.

12Ecological information

Safe for the rock, the castle, and the city that has kept it. The set degrades nothing; it is found in the Crown Room where it does no harm and is exactly wanted. No bioaccumulation. The gulls, which roost on the ramparts and are indifferent to the gold, are the habitat’s only recorded interaction with the set, and their verdict, as reported in the salt log of The Wash, is silence.

13Disposal considerations

There are no disposal instructions, because the substance cannot be disposed of. It is the oldest surviving set in the British Isles, and a set that old is not a candidate for any waste stream the Office recognises. Damaged pearls may be replaced; the record of the replacement is entered, and the new pearl stands in the old pearl’s exact place, where it will do the old pearl’s work and outlast the argument.

14Transport information

UN No. 1745 (provisional), Class 9, miscellaneous — regalia, for ceremonial movement. The set is transported, on the rare occasions it moves, in the cedar cases the Office keeps for it, lined in cloth, by foot if the road will take the carriage and by carriage if the road will take nothing. In 1745 the cases travelled to the north and back within the fortnight, in full, which the Office treats as a transit record of the first order. On the same shelf the Office keeps a single printed timetable — the Timetable of the Seven Brothers Line, Helsingfors–Heinola — retained for the standing record that a national writer may be conveyed, on his birthday, without a crane, in a cedar crate of no kind, and arrive to time at the end of the line.

Claim of the estate, 1912. An estate in the Highlands, holding what it described as the family’s share of the 1745 movements, lodged a query with the Office: whether the set had gone north in a cedar crate of hotel fixtures, and whether the same crate had come home with it, and was still in the estate’s cellar, in service. The Office examined the case and entered its answer in the minute book. The broad answer is a question, on present evidence, whether they still had the same cedar crate of hotel fixtures the estate took to working. The Office declined to rule on the crate, returned the query, and opened the gate, in that order.

15Regulatory information

Coronation exposure suspended under the Union of 1707, with the parliamentary-presence protocol in force 1603–1707 and since lapsed with the Parliament. The return of the Stone of Destiny was governed by the Scotland Act 1950, under which the 1950 removal was, in effect, regularised before it was discovered, which the Office records as unusual but not unknown. No further regulatory action is pending. This sheet is cross-filed against the coronation register (1543–1651), the parliamentary-presence register (1603–1707), and the 1912 crate query, which is open and, on present evidence, stays open.
