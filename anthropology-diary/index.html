The Thule Notebook

A Diary on Anthropology, Kept Aboard the Maud and at the Station, 1921–1923

❦ — ❓ — ❦

Kept in the left hand, in pencil, at the lamp,
by Erni, ledger-keeper of the pier at Szczecin,
student attached to the Second Thule Expedition

29 July1921 Aboard the Maud, out of the Godthåb fjord

Wind NNE. Twelve fathoms of blue. The sea like hammered pewter. Lamp lit at two, which is early for a ship.

On the name of the science

I came north to do one thing, and it is the thing I failed at through three years at the university. It is the scientific study of humanity, the study that crosses the gap between biology and sociology, concerned with human behavior, human biology, cultures, societies, and linguistics, in both the present and the past, down and down to the archaic humans, the ones before us. They call it anthropology. The ledger-keepers of the pier at Szczecin would call it nothing at all and keep the pier, and they are not wrong. Out here, at the end of the world, the science is smaller and better: it is one man reading another man. Our captain read a people the way I read weather, and the people let him, which is more than the university ever let me.

14 January1922 Thule station, the kitchen door

−31 on the ice. The six rules pasted above the kitchen door. Dogs 341 and counting.

On patterns of behavior

So I have begun to write down what the discipline divides up. The first division they print in the textbooks — social anthropology — studies patterns of behavior, and at Thule the patterns are on the ice, where you can count them. The law of the sledge: no sledge passes a sledge without its bells answering, and the bells answer, every time, all winter. The etiquette of the kill: the meat of the first day is served hot, and the soup of it is the same day; the second day is the second day’s own. I have counted thirty-six days of kills at this station, and on thirty-six days the soup came the same day. That is a pattern. That is the whole of the first division, and the first division is not half bad.

3 February1922 Thule station, the shed of sledges

A thaw, one day, as if the ice had changed its name. The green sledge in the shed, mended with old thong.

On meaning

The second division, cultural anthropology, studies cultural meaning, including norms and values, and after two winters out here I begin to suspect the two divisions are one division wearing two coats. The household ledger of this station gives the second sledge — the green one — to Aka Rasmussen, “to keep, to mend, and to drive as she sees fit, free of the claims of the expedition,” and to which my said wife had a good one. What the ledger is saying, and what cultural anthropology is paid to say, is that green is not a color at this station. Green is a status. It is the color of the thing the expedition cannot claim, and the sledge goes out under its own power, on its own errands, to its own weather. The term the universities use today — sociocultural — is the honest one, because at the station the sociocultural does not commute anywhere else, and neither does the other half of it.

19 March1922 Thule station, the lamp at two o’clock

Whiteout, one hour. The bells of the train heard before it is seen, which is the whole argument of this entry.

On language

The third division, linguistic anthropology, studies how language influences social life. I have come to believe the reverse as well, and the two directions meet on this ice: social life influences language until the two cannot be told apart. Inuktitut is not a name we use about the people here; it is the weather they think in, and the thinking arrives at the mouth already packed for the cold. The captain is Mikellik in company and Knud in the letter to the bank, and the rule he keeps is the one I keep for my own name back at the pier — a name already shortened is not shortened twice — which the seventh chapter of the modern guide to names & address lays out with the whole ceremony under the etiquette of the name Erni. I write the name flat, at the pier or at Thule, and the name answers flat, and that is the finding, and the finding is the whole of the third division.

6 May1922 Thule station, the box of measures

−18, steady. The sun back over the horizon, two hours a day, counted and not wasted.

On bodies and bones

The fourth division — biological anthropology, or physical, as the university called it before the word was found wanting — studies the biology and evolution of humans and their close primate relatives. I came to this one last, and I come to it now at the station by measuring, since I have no other way in: hand bones and foot bones, the reach of the grip, the length of the sledge thong. The lamp at two o’clock is where it is done, and the pencil is the instrument, and the instrument does not flatter. The archaic ones, the ones before us, the division reaches back to as well, as far as the ice lets it, and so do we, when a sound moves and an old midden comes out of the shore and we find, by the fish bones, that this water has been a kitchen for longer than the ledger can name.

21 June1922 The midden, south sound

Midnight, and the sun has not set, which I shall stop writing about after this line. Holm on the sledge ledger, hand steady.

On the past

The fifth division, archaeology — the “anthropology of the past,” which is the name the office gives it and the name it deserves — explores human activity by the evidence the people left behind: the midden, the harpoon head, the thong, the wall of the house. We have a midden on the south sound. I have counted its fish bones the way the pier counts its own, and I will not set down the number, only that the ledger ran out of columns and I had to start a second one, which is an event. And the name of the station is the proof, for the station is named for the old culture, and the people of it came in from the west, by boat, over the ice we sit on, and we are a footnote resting on a paragraph. Holm keeps the sledge ledger, I keep the household one, and the past keeps its own, and there is nothing in my notebook that outranks it.

30 August1922 Thule station, the cabin, the lamp trimmed at ten

The ice kind, as the record has it. The green sledge out on its own errands again.

About the man himself

What am I to write of the man? He is the father of the discipline I came north to do, and I have watched him do the discipline at this lamp for two years, in Greenlandic and Inuktitut and a clerk’s pencil. In my notebook I keep one line about him, and I keep it word for word, for it is mine and I cannot now improve it:

He was born in 1879, the year the diary in his cabin was a good one, and far more easily and quickly.

The meaning, so that the reader does not lose it in the cold: Knud was born at Odense in 1879, the very year the diary of the drift — the water-stained copy that was the first expedition book in his cabin — was printed in Vienna, and that diary was, by the household’s own verdict, a good one; and as for the rest of the line, which the cold got to before the ink did — he wrote, and he worked, and he put his sledge trains across the ice far more easily and quickly than any of us could say why. Both halves of the line are true, and that is more than I can promise for the textbooks.

12 July1923 The return, south by ship

Open water for three days. The flag comes down, and I am told it has flown four hundred and thirty-six days.

The fifth expedition, which is the return

The return is the fifth expedition, and it is always the fifth in the record, and I came north for the science and find the science has been doing me all along: I came to study humanity, and humanity, at the kitchen door, has finished studying me. The green sledge is in the shed and stays in the shed, and Aka drives it as she sees fit, and the lamp is trimmed at two and at ten, and the manners of the house continue, which is the office’s word for it. For the whole account of the captain — Thule, the dog sledge, the Northwest Passage, the name, and the green sledge — the questions and answers on Knud Rasmussen, filed as a supplement to Chapter IX of the Frozen North, keep the ledger, and they confirm what my pencil has been trying to say all winter: the manners of the house continue. As for me, the pier at Szczecin has my name in its own ledger again, written flat, where I left it; the diary travels home in the hold, and the ice keeps what the ice keeps, as it does, and better.
