The Siege Loaf

seven loaves, one sheet, no place repeated

BAKED 9 AUG TONE 5 HELD

This card was written on the back of a relay sheet. The kitchen on Srednii Prospekt — the house where the studio sat on the night of 9 August 1942, when the wire carried the Seventh to seven places at once — kept its cards in a drawer with a rosin tin and a court docket, and this is the one the family still bakes, on the ninth of every August and no other day. The oven is set at the hour the music began, and the loaves are named the way the sheet names its places: each one exactly once, no duplicate, no gap. The full relay log and remarks, with the starving players of the Leningrad Radio Orchestra and Karl Eliasberg on the stand, is in Thread No. 7 at the Relay Room; this card is what the kitchen kept of it.

Dmitri Dmitriyevich Shostakovich was born in Petrograd on 25 September 1906 — 12 September by the old style — the son of a senior official at the Nikolaevskaia station, and he grew up between the sound of the railway and the conservatory, a pianist first and a composer on top of it. He became internationally known after the premiere of his First Symphony in 1926, and from that night on was reckoned among the major composers of his time, a standing the family in this kitchen has never once disputed, not even in the lean years. The Seventh was the piece the city put its name on before he could: it left Leningrad by train in twelve boxes on 1 August 1941 with his note that it was the song of our city, and it was finished in Kuybyshev on 5 March 1942, and it came back to the wire on 9 August.

His relationship with the government was a complex one, and the card is written around it the way the loaf is written around the ration. The opera Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk of 1934 was an early success and was then condemned, and his career sat at risk for it; the denunciations of the late thirties shaped the music as surely as the ration card shaped the loaf. In 1948 his work was denounced under the Zhdanov Doctrine, and the professional consequences ran for several years, the way a cold wire runs until someone winds it warm again. Even after his censure was rescinded in 1956, performances of his music were still things that had to be arranged, guarded, and kept from going dead. He died on 9 August 1975 — the same day of the month the Seventh had crossed the wire thirty-three years before — and the kitchen keeps the coincidence in the card, and will not let it go.

So the recipe runs in sevens, which is the number of the sheet and of the thread and of nothing else. The players were living on soup and a ration of about a quarter kilo of bread a day, and two cellists could not sit for the full length, so the loaf is a quarter kilo apiece, no more, and it is built dark, the way the city went dark, and it rests through an interval, the way the performance rested through its shelling, and nobody stirs it in the middle of the interval. That is the whole method of the house.

INGREDIENTS

for seven loaves

  • 450 g dark rye flour, stone-milled if the mill is still running
  • 250 g soft wheat flour — the quarter-kilo ration, never more
  • 1 sachet dried yeast, kept in the rosin tin with the old rosin
  • 1 tbsp black treacle, for the dark crumb
  • 1½ tsp salt, the amount the studio kept for the tea
  • 450 ml water, at the temperature of a warm wire, about 37 °C
  • 7 rye seeds, dark, one per listening place
  • 1 line of the Smyrna bench, kept word for word — see the card line below

The flour is weighed the way the ledger was read: in front of the whole family, by name, and no one asks for a second portion. The bench that kept the chandler’s ledger of Of — Case No. 21, The Straits v. The Pontic Record — set the rule the kitchen works by: a number kept in a book outlives a number kept in a head.

METHOD

seven steps, one per place

  1. The studio. Warm the water and wake the yeast in it with a pinch of the rye; it should foam the way the test tone answers — a sound from the machine that means the line is warm. Leave it ten minutes.
  2. The Moscow relay. Work the rye flour in first, then the treacle, then the salt. Knead until the dough stops arguing, which the kitchen calls the hour the score was finished, wherever the finishing actually happened.
  3. The Stalingrad front net. Fold the soft wheat in in its whole quarter kilo — the ration, all of it, nothing held back for tomorrow. The dough will look weak. It is not. Cover it and let it rise while you do the stamping.
  4. The 34th battery, south shore. Divide the risen dough into seven equal pieces and stamp a rye seed into the crown of each, then press each crown with your thumb the way a battery answers its tone: once, held, and released.
  5. The Krestovsky hospital. Set the seven loaves on the sheet pan in the order the relay sheet names the places: the studio, Moscow, Stalingrad, the 34th battery, the Krestovsky hospital, the Narva bridgehead bunker, the listening post on the road to Shlisselburg. Every place named exactly once.
  6. The interval. Bake at 230 °C for 23 minutes, and through the middle of the bake there is an interval: the door stays shut, nobody opens it, nobody stirs the dough, and whatever comes in from outside the window is carried the way a hall carries applause — the difference being that nobody claps afterwards. Then out again, and the finale.
  7. The Narva bunker, and the listening post. Cool the loaves on the wire rack until they sound hollow when the back is tapped, and eat them in the order the sheet names them, the studio first, the listening post last, so that the sound arrives somewhere someone is still standing.

THE CARD LINE

The last line on the card is not a cooking instruction. The drawer kept the Smyrna bench’s docket beside the relay log, and the morning the docket came home, Grandmother read the first witness’s calling aloud over the rising dough and wrote it into the card exactly as the bench had kept it, because a line kept in a book outlives a line kept in a head:

“He said the record in suit; — Nikolaos V. Andreadis, 58, chandler and boat owner of Of, is called.”

Every 9 August the line is read once, word for word, before the first loaf goes into the oven, and then the oven is lit, and the line stays in the record and is not struck.

the line has not yet been read this August
