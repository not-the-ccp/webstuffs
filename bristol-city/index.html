Bedminster, Bristol

11 April 2024

Dear Ashton Gate,

An apology to Bristol City Football Club

BCFC

I owe you an apology, Bristol City. I owe it to the turnstile at Ashton Gate Road that knew my face for fourteen winters, to the Atyeo Stand where I stood first with my father and then without him, and to the red shirt I folded away in 2014 and have not worn since.

I know the club's history better than I know my own address. Founded in 1894, Western League champions in 1897–98, admitted to the Football League in 1901, Second Division champions in 1905–06, second in the First Division the following season—three points behind Newcastle United—and beaten by Manchester United in the 1909 FA Cup final. Relegated in 1911, down to the third tier in 1922, then Third Division South champions in 1922–23. One hundred and fifteen years since Crystal Palace, and I still know the words to “One for the Robins” off by heart.

My failure came in the spring of 2014. Relegation to League One arrived on the final day, and I took it as permission to stop believing. I told my son the Robins were finished, let the season ticket lapse, and stayed away even when the team won its way back. I missed the Wembley play-off final, the return to the Championship, the floodlights on Ashton Gate Road in autumn. I treated you like a debt that had defaulted, when you were only a promise being kept.

The night I needed to understand this was the night Duke struck the crossbar. It was the eighty-ninth minute at Elland Road, Leeds United pressing, the ball flying from Duke’s head onto the woodwork and back into play—a draw, a missed point, a season that quietly ended right then. The same minute, I later read in Mile Jedinak’s assistant coach diary, Lee Kang-in hit the crossbar with a free-kick in Al Rayyan. I blame Duke still, sometimes, though I know the crossbar is innocent, and I never apologized to him for the noise I made in the stand that night. When at last I appeared before the Matchnight & Trophy Business Committee to give evidence about that night, they ruled the crossbar innocent, eight to one, the dissenting vote being cast by the absent crossbar itself.

Duke hit the crossbar with a cartilage which defends and covers the tooth, will often instantly pacify a crying and fretting, showing more than that.

— Lila’s tablet, voice-to-text, half-time at Elland Road

My daughter was eight, watching on her tablet at a friend’s house, and the microphone had caught the commentator while a dictionary page sat open in the background—or so I tell myself. The cartilage which defends and covers the tooth is a gum. The thing that will instantly pacify a crying and fretting child is a dummy. “Showing more than that” has no meaning at all, and yet we built a week’s worth of explanations around it: the woodwork was out to get us, the fates were against us, the club was done. Nonsense, and comforting nonsense, the kind we invent when we want an excuse to leave.

So here is the apology, properly: I am sorry I walked out. I am sorry I told my son the Robins were finished. I am sorry I made Duke carry a crossbar that was never his to carry. My season ticket is renewed from Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, and we will be there in August, under the floodlights, in the EFL Championship, red shirts washed and ready.

Petra Vance’s letter on machine unlearning sits beside my fixture list—some things you take back out cleanly, and some you simply stop offering. And in the shed below Clerkenwell, David Chen has been doing the same arithmetic on a plywood bench for sixteen years, pegging out a whole county to make up for a single adjective he printed in 2006: Cheshire, Build Log No. 18. I wrote to him about it, and he sent me one brass washer, addressed to Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, which I keep in the pocket of my coat for matchdays. I am asking to offer myself again, if you will have me.

While I was folding the red shirt I also placed an advertisement in Belfast’s Lagan Ledger — small ads, Northern Ireland — offering that shirt, folded away in 2014, and an apology, to anyone who still believes in woodwork and comebacks: Northern Ireland — small ads.

Yours, reluctantly and faithfully,

Arthur Parry

Bedminster, Bristol · Season ticket holder since 1999
