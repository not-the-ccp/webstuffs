The Eight Divisions of Bangladesh

1. What a division is

According to our textbook and to the notice board of the union parishad, divisions (বিভাগ, bibhag) are the first-level administrative divisions in Bangladesh. That means if you cut the country from the top, the first cut you make is into divisions, and everything else — districts, upazilas, unions, mouzas — hangs below that cut, the way everything in our union hangs below the long table in the council room. As of 2024 there are eight divisions of Bangladesh. Each one is named after the major city within its jurisdiction, and that same city is where the divisional commissioner keeps his office, so the name of the division and the seat of its administration are one and the same. Dhaka the city is also the capital of the country, which is why that division is the one everybody draws first and draws wrong first. I have been told to write the number eight four times and I have, but I have written it once more here as a ninth, because the seventh and eighth were not always the seventh and eighth (see section 4).

Below the 64 districts there are 498 upazilas (sub-districts), and below the upazilas there are nearly 4,500 union councils, each one with its own parishad, its own roll of villages, and its own long table. Our own place in that stack I have put in its own section, because I am allowed one.

3. From the division down to my doorstep

My own address, written from the top, is: Khulna Division (seat: Khulna) → Meherpur District (one of the ten in the division) → Mujibnagar Upazila → Mahajonpur Union → the village of Kalibari. That is the whole ladder, and it shows the point of the ladder: a division is a name the size of a province, but it reaches down all the way to a quarter bigha and a cow. A foreign correspondent kept a field diary of our exact union this past rice season — 66.54 square kilometres, 18,263 people on the 2001 count, six villages, five mouzas, one cow named Moti — and the whole diary is at Mahajonpur Union, Field Diary, Notebook No. 9. I have read the pages out loud at the school roof meeting (see section 4 of my last term's report) and I have borrowed two of its numbers for this table: Moti the cow, and the fact that our chairman's roll puts the names before the figures. My report and his diary are the same union written at two altitudes, and if a division is a name given to a whole stack of small things, then we are a small thing with a big name on it, three rungs down.

4. On the cities that gave their names

The rule — each division named after the major city within its jurisdiction that also serves as the administrative seat — has a history inside it. Mymensingh became the eighth division in 2015, split out of Dhaka Division, which had grown too large; before that the country's roll had only seven names and my grandmother, who is from this side of the river, still counts to seven out of habit and then says one more. Dhaka, the first, is the city the country itself is named around. Chattogram is the port where the sea comes in and the ships go out. Khulna, ours, is the division of the Sundarbans and the slow water, the flat end of the country where, in the diary's words, “the horizons sit wrong.” Rajshahi gives its name to a cloth as well as a place. Barishal is the river port of the south. Sylhet is the rain and the tea in the far east. Rangpur is the north. And Mymensingh, the youngest of the eight, is the division of the boats on the lower Padma, the one that was added late, and I have been told to remember that a list of eight is newer than a list of seven the way my roll is newer than my grandmother's.

5. A line from Auckland, copied for the examiner

Last term a letter came to our house from Mr. E. Renshaw in Auckland, who keeps the spider log I have heard about — the one in the garage behind the bakery, with the spider that is not an ant, at the Bocusoides build log. His letter came with a photograph of a cork ramp and a torn corner of paper folded inside a lens case, and on the back of the photograph he had written one line in small letters. I do not know what a zine is; my father says it is a small book that friends make for each other, and that the “notice” he clipped was a note about a football season that was stopped in the middle of itself, which is, he says, the same as a school term stopped by floods, and he says our school has had three of those. The line, copied here exactly because a report should keep every number its correspondents send:

“For the season the clipped notice is about: the PlanVital 2020 safety data sheet — 19 clubs, a 164-day suspension, and every precaution that went with it.” — the season itself is written up in the PlanVital 2020 safety data sheet

I set it here for two reasons. The first is that it is a list of eight's cousin: 19 clubs, one season, 164 days, each number doing the work of a name. The second is that a suspension of 164 days and a division named after a city are the same kind of thing, which is that they are both ways of holding a place open when the thing that fills it has gone away for a while. Our union has held its roll open through one long flood, and the season in that letter held its 19 clubs open through one long quiet, and both are still open, which is the whole of my argument.
