South Philadelphia Sports Complex · Letter No. 9 · Ink: red, half a bottle

An Apology to the
Philadelphia Phillies

From a desk on the tenth floor, the window that faces the park · 6th & Beresford

N.L. EAST · EST. 1883 · KEEP WORD

South Philadelphia, on a Tuesday when the sky went the color of the outfield wall

Dear Phillies,

I am writing to say I’m sorry, in the one place left where no one can argue with the record: on paper, in red ink, addressed to you in the plural the way the franchise has always been addressed — the Phillies, continuously, since the National League approved a new club for Philadelphia at its annual meeting in Providence on December 7, 1882, and the team took the field for the first time in 1883. You have not once changed the name to make an apology like this one easier. That is a lot of years to hold a single name, and I have not held a single anything nearly so long as I have held my grudges against you.

I am sorry about the counting. I kept my own ledger of your failures from the first season I could reach for the radio, and I was meticulous about it, which is the only crime I’m willing to plead. I watched you win two World Series — 1980, against the Kansas City Royals, in the city that used to lose them, and 2008, against the Tampa Bay Rays, on a night when the whole Delaware Valley forgot to go to bed — and I watched you take eight National League pennants, and I was in the wrong seats for several of them, which was my arrangement and my arithmetic. You are a member club of the National League East Division, and I let the division bully me into believing the pennants were borrowed. They were not. They were batted in, one at a time, in the way you do everything, and I should have written them in a different color than the color I keep my apologies in.

I am sorry about the window. Since 2004 your home has been Citizens Bank Park in the South Philadelphia Sports Complex, and my desk has faced it for all of that time, which is longer than most marriages and exactly as negotiable. From the tenth floor I can see the third-base line, the walk-up rows, and the wall out on sixth street side where the row houses come up close enough to argue with the outfield. The wall is the one thing in this letter that has never once apologized for anything, and I have decided I admire it. It runs, in the office's way of its own border. Not toward the office, not away from the office, but along, exactly, the whole length of the boundary, the way a good sentence keeps its line while the paragraph goes wherever it needs to. I have been the paragraph. You have been the line. I am asking to be filed where the line ends.

There are two further apologies, smaller, for the record. One: in 1980 I owned a Kansas City ticket-stub collection, and I displayed it, briefly, on the desk, which was a kind of vandalism I now classify as seasonal. Two: I once told a stranger at the walk-up rows that the pennant count of eight “only counted if you believed in it,” and the stranger was wearing a 2008 Rays jersey, so I had it in at least one witness. I have retired the stub collection to the box it was born in, and the box is under the desk, and the desk is still facing the park, because I have read that the only honest position from which to apologize is the position you were in when you did the thing.

So: I am sorry, in red ink, from the tenth floor, on a Tuesday, to a club that has been called the Phillies since 1883 and will be called the Phillies long after the ink dries. Play the next one. I will be in the window, which is the closest seat to the park the city has left me, and I will count the pennants in the right color this time.

Respectfully, from the wrong side of the glass,

The Tenth-Floor Desk
6th & Beresford · NL East · pen down, ledger closed

P.S.

I should tell you where the old ledger has been lent, so it stops sitting in the margin of this one. The Detroit crate — the river, the bridge, and the ledger leaf with the 1926 season card pinned to the bank — is catalogued at Lot 143 of the Southern Branch Auction, Catalogue B, and it is there that my counting has been kept these two seasons: beside the river that runs like a border, with its own ribbon marker. And if you want the wider index of the collection this letter is filed in, the front page keeps the drawer closed. One more loan, for the record: the season ledger of the London Symphony Orchestra — the one on which John Boyden kept his April 1975 appointment and his October dismissal in the same cold hand — is catalogued at the field notes, Specimen Log No. 88, filed with the sleeve from Richmond years in and the appointment letter on top.
