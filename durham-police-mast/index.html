D. M. Elliston · Communications Officer, Operations Room B · County Police Communications Tower · Durham, N. England

The Dismantling Diary

Being the notebook kept in the cupboard above the transmitter rack, in a pencil that is running out, from the first morning the crane came to the yard on Neville Street to the last bolt of the re-erection.

Monday, 6 February 2017 · 05:40 · rain, the usual sort

I have been awake since four because of a sound I could not name and now can: the crane. It is standing in the yard with its jib drawn back like an arm before a lift, and the first thing I did was go to the window and count. One, two, three legs, going up and back into each other at the top of the mast the way they do — the tripod — and my hands did not understand my own eyes. We have worked out of this tower since 1968. It is 162 feet, forty-nine metres, reinforced concrete, and it was not built to look like anything except a way of keeping a line to Hartlepool open on a wet night. County architect J. L. Parnaby drew it and Ove Arup and Partners signed the structure, and the whole point, as the file says, was a rigid mast slender enough to leave the views of Durham Cathedral alone. Fifty years of rigidity, and this morning it is a building site.

Tuesday, 7 February 2017 · 13:10 · the kettle, which I have stopped trusting

The scaffolding men tell me the sections come down in order, top first, and that the concrete is too heavy to be swung the whole way, so it is broken to size up there on the platform and trucked out of the yard one length at a time. A condition of the police headquarters redevelopment required it to be re-erected afterwards — that is the whole sentence I keep turning over, required, in the same voice the planning officer used for the parking bays. Nobody has said who will say thank you for the re-erection. I have started bagging the cable tags. The rack labels are coming out with the racks, and some of them are in a hand I will not find again.

Saturday, 11 February 2017 · 19:25 · dry, which here is an event

A postcard came for me from Aunt Vera in Hartlepool, postmarked Thursday. The writing is as bad as always — same pen, same capitals, same refusal to say which of us she is speaking of. Over the photograph of the mast, still standing a week ago, she has written: This is one of the Single My mother’s recipe book and becomes a landscape. I have decided, after an hour with the card face down on the desk, that she means the picture is one of the single loose pages her late mother kept tucked inside her recipe book — the tower being the only view her mother ever troubled to keep — and that pasted in between the suet dumplings it ceases to be a photograph and becomes a landscape in its own right. Vera is eighty-nine and does not do explanation. The men in the room have stopped asking where my mother is from. I have pinned the postcard above the transmitter rack.

Friday, 24 February 2017 · 07:05 · the last bolt

Third section to the ground this morning; the tripod stands now in two, like a man who has sat down. From the operations room window the cathedral is exactly as it was on my first day, and I have to walk to the yard to be sure the tower is gone, because 162 feet is a thing the eye files under permanent. The radio goes out with the tower; the mast has a successor and the mast does not know its own name. I am keeping this diary because the ledger in the yard is the contractor’s, and a tower should be let go in its own hand. There is a manual of etiquette a man in North Carolina keeps about workshops and found things — Chapter XII, On Visiting a Woodcarver’s Workshop — and its rule is the same as this one: the goods are priced and delivered, and the found things are kept without a price because they carry the room. The cable tags are found things.

Wednesday, 15 June 2022 · 08:30 · wind from the river

They are setting it up again, the same concrete, the same three legs, and the crane is standing in the yard with its jib drawn back the way it stood in 2017, only lifting this time. The condition held; the planning officer does not forget. I am fifty-seven now and the tower is a stranger to me that I know the way one knows a relative who has been away: 162 feet, and nothing to say. It will be finished before the August fair, when the crowds will come up from the cathedral and photograph it, and the mast will do the one job it was never hired for and be a picture — the landscape Vera meant — over all of us. I am writing the last cable tag number in this book before it goes to the contractor. The room is the room. The mast is, again, the mast.
