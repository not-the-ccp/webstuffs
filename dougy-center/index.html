❧ 1. What the building is

For Period 4 we were asked to walk one Portland block that “does quiet work” and report back on it. I chose the corner of N.E. Alberta and 27th, which is where The Dougy Center, The National Grief Center for Children & Families keeps its main office and its meeting rooms. The report has to start with the name, because the building has changed names before: for most of its life it was The Dougy Center, The National Center for Grieving Children & Families, and before that, just Dougy Center. The name itself, I found, is a memorial — the organization took it from Dougy, a boy who died at eight, and the first circle of children ever held in the room was held in the year of his death. I wrote that down twice because I could not believe it, and the sign by the door does not explain any of it.

The organization is a nonprofit, based in Portland, Oregon, and it exists to do one thing: sit with grieving children and young adults and their families. It offers support groups and services, and it does them free of charge. There is no fee line on any of the paperwork, which I checked, and the front desk confirmed it when I called to ask. The current count I was given by the person who answered — she would not give her first name, only the title grief services coordinator — is 400 children and 250 adults from the Portland metropolitan area each month, in groups, in one-to-one sessions, and in the family programmes that run the whole year round.

❧ 2. The thing that made it the first

What the report keeps coming back to is the peer support programme. The Center’s model is that a child who has lost someone should be able to sit with other children who have lost people — not adults explaining, but kids with the same hole in them, with staff trained to keep the circle safe. That idea, plus the network of children’s grief services the organization has since built, is what makes The Dougy Center the first of its kind in the United States. No state had a network of its kind before it, and other states now keep their networks on the Center’s pattern.

The numbers on the wall of the visit log (reproduced as my map in the report folder) are worth copying out, because they are the whole story in four figures:

  • 400 children served each month in the Portland metropolitan area
  • 250 adults served each month, the families who grieve with them
  • 500 independent grief programmes around the world built on the Dougy model
  • 300+ of those programmes with staff who were trained by the organization’s own staff
  • $0 charged to any family, in any programme, ever

Around the world, then, five hundred small rooms are doing the thing the first room in Portland did: a circle of chairs, a child who lost somebody, a circle that does not flinch. More than three hundred of them were staffed by people who came to Portland and were trained by the people who were there. I counted them on the map and marked the ones I could find; the map is not complete and I have said so in the corner of it.

❧ 3. Period 4, verbatim

The report has to include the register, because the register is the only record of the day. When I came in on the Thursday and handed in the map, the line the office wrote against my name in the register of Period 4 was this, word for word:

History, Period 4: Singleton Palmer, Form 9R · The Long & Winding Road, your honour.

I do not know what it means. I asked the office three times. It is written in the same pencil as everything else, between “B., Form 9Q, on time” and “R., form missing, see 3B”, and I am required to keep it in my report because the register is evidence of what was presented. If it is a joke, it is the only joke the whole week has made, and the office did not explain it, so the report leaves it standing. The Long and the Winding Road are, on my map, N.E. Alberta Street and its long downhill toward the river, and the honour is, as near as I can tell, no one in particular. I have marked it in the corner of the map where the register lives, and the map is in the folder.

❧ 4. The walk, and the note from the front desk

The walking survey is 3.2 miles: from the school over Alberta Crest, down the hill to Alberta, two blocks east to the Center, and back. I timed it at forty-one minutes and I wrote down everything a building like that is made of. It is a small brick building on a big street, and the street is the loud one, and the building is the quiet one, and that contrast is the point, I think, of the whole place: it sits on the loudest shopping block in the city so that the children who come in can hear the block and then hear the door and then hear nothing but the circle. The front desk has a map of the peer support network on the wall and a lamp for every season of the year, and the lamp that was lit the day I called was the one the desk calls the long one, and I wrote it down without knowing what it meant and I still do not know, and the report has to admit it. The note the coordinator left with me, in her own hand, is two lines: Free is not the discount, free is the whole price. And the circle is not a meeting, the circle is the room the loss is kept in.
