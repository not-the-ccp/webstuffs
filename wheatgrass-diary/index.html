The pocket diary of Hazel M. Rutter, laboratory assistant, Cereal Grass Bench, Station Farm

December, 1925

— being the winter in which the eggs began to keep their own accounts, and the doctor began to be called, by the men at the fence, the father of wheatgrass —

CEREAL GRASS
· TRIAL 12 · S. C.

Tuesday, 8 December. Fourteen degrees at the barn rail, the lake over at Bloomers' neck gone to black ice, and a light of one kind only all day, the grey that puts a person inside before the hour.

We weighed the eggs at half past four this morning, both of us, because the count was not something to do alone. Two hundred and twenty hens in the winter house. A hundred and ten on the old ration, a hundred and ten with the doctor's addition — four ounces of the dehydrated cereal grass mixed into the feed each day, the same small quantity every morning, weighed on the little brass balance so that nobody, not even Dr. Charles Franklin Schnabel himself, could be accused of a generous hand. The plain-ration side laid 71 eggs. The grass side laid 219.

Three times. Nearly three, in a December, in a house with no windows that the sun ever reaches. I wrote it in the bench ledger in my own hand and then, when my hand had stopped being a little unsteady, I wrote it again more carefully, and the doctor read it over my shoulder and said only, again tomorrow, and note it, as if the number were a guest he intended to let stay the night. It has stayed.

He is a quiet man about the work, the doctor, and a loud man about nothing else that I can tell. Born in 1895, he tells the new hands, though it is a fact I looked up rather than a fact I was told, and he went on from these hens to cattle and to pigs and, later, to everything with a manger, and what he found in each was the same small fortune for a small addition: larger litters in the sows, richer milk and more of it in the cows, less of the infant mortality that has cost this county a count I will not put in a diary, better fur in the rabbits, and a general health that shows in the eyes before it shows in the scale. A small amount of dried grass. That is the whole of it, and that is the size of it.

Today's errands took me to the telegraph window, where the clerk — new boy, cannot yet tell a wire from a wire — was packing his lunch early because his wires run three coasts: the Norfolk line for the Atlantic station, the Galveston line for the Gulf, and the Sitka line for the Pacific, all three answering to the same clock here at the bench. He said it first to me with his mouth full of bread, and then, chewing and more careful, he gave it its proper form and I wrote it down in this diary as I was given it. The office closes at three coasts at once. It is true in the way of these small farms that the important thing happens quietly: at three o'clock the last message of the day goes out on all three wires and then nothing, all three coastlines going dark together while the snow does its business in the yard and the grass in its sacks by the door waits to be what it is going to be.

He sends the same message to all three stations, a half-inch of paper, and it reads: grass trial running, hens up, send the March order of the dehydrated crop, and keep the March order out of the summer heat. Norfolk. Galveston. Sitka. One message, three coasts, one bench in the middle of it. I think the doctor knows this is how it will end up. I think he has been saying so since 1924, that cereal grass is not a farm curiosity but a substance, and that a substance travels on the wires as well as in sacks. The almanac men are already at the fences asking him about the seasons. Somewhere a weather book will keep a winter entry by his name the way it keeps the others — the weather almanac, entry 2004/363, kept over Bethesda in the season book of a biochemist named Axelrod, shows what a name does for a season after the man is gone: one entry, one steady instrument, one December that kept its numbers. I do not envy him the company; I think he would like it.

Cold supper of coffee and bread, the stove doing its one good deed of the day. The hens do not care about any of it. The hens have been laid their eggs and gone to their roost, and the ledger is dry, and the three wires are dark, and the grass is in its sacks, and tomorrow I weigh again. That is the entry. That is the whole winter, I am beginning to think: weigh it, write it, send it to the coasts, sleep.

P.S. — The old pocket almanac from the drawer, the one reckoned in the old style and a fortnight adrift of the newspaper, is in the stationmaster's hands now; there is a lost-and-found notice for it in the Pittock Block, fourth floor, Portland, and I should have known from the start that a reckoning gone thirteen days would not stay in a drawer but would walk out to wherever reckoning gets lost. The eggs, I suspect, will be the next thing to wander. They have begun keeping their own accounts.

— from the pocket diary, kept by the assistant at the bench, and returned to the family with the March order of 1926 —
