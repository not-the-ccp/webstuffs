7 February 2009 — The office cleared

The Clerkenwell office went out in six crates this morning, and with it the last hope of an issue eighteen in which to correct myself. So I have decided to make issue eighteen out of something the printer’s account could not eat. The subject is the county I insulted: in the letter to Manchester I stood up for the bluff, the bank, and the year, but I left the plain standing. I called the Cheshire Plain “uninteresting” in the same spring-2006 piece that put Mamucium on the wrong bank, and I never retracted the adjective. Scale is fixed at 1:100,000. It holds in one place I like: 2,344 square kilometres at full size is exactly 2,344 square centimetres on the bench, so the area survives the scale with nothing lost.

16 February 2009 — The board and the borders

Two sheets of marine plywood, 129 by 88 centimetres, lapped and screwed to the bench, and the boundary pegs driven in the order the county is bordered, so I keep saying the neighbours’ names out loud while I hammer: Merseyside to the north-west, Greater Manchester to the north-east, Derbyshire to the east, Staffordshire to the south-east, Shropshire to the south. The west side takes two pegs, Flintshire and Wrexham, the Welsh counties, and I have driven them a shade prouder of the others, because the line on the map is a border and the sound of it is two words. Along the north-west corner the county reaches water: a short coastline on the Dee Estuary, 31.2 centimetres of curved brass, twenty miles at scale, the place where the county stops being bordered and starts being washed.

2 March 2009 — The river and its towns

Mersey first, because everything dense about this county hangs on it: a 96-centimetre inlay of blue river glass, 3 millimetres deep, running the length of the north from the Dee westward. On the glass, in the order the water meets them, Runcorn, Widnes, and Ellesmere Port, dowels of 5, 5, and 4 millimetres. Warrington takes the 9-millimetre walnut, set a little south of the waterline; it is the largest settlement in the county and it sits where the flats open out, which the model makes obvious once the dowel goes in, because the whole bench suddenly has a centre. The city of Chester gets 6-millimetre oak with a ring turned into the end—the only city, the ring being the walls, which the town has been famous for since before any of us had opinions about it.

11 March 2009 — Density, and the first of the spills

The areas around the River Mersey in the north are the most densely populated part of the county, so the density must be a material and not a sentence. Three tins of brass filings: raked fine and nearly invisible across the south, where the plain is, and thickened, almost standing up, along the river in the north. For the people I have used salt—one grain per hundred, 11,400 grains for the 1,139,884 the county counted in 2024, the log having outlived its scale and so spending current figures; the jar is set into the board’s southern edge, sealed under glass. Mabel Ellison came up from the Rochdale Canal to watch the pour. She brought the dockside blend. She spilled it on entry one. In the letter I kept her other line for eleven years because I was sure of nothing else; this spill I can keep whole, tea and all.

3 May 2014 — The sister failure

Read the letter to Ashton Gate from Bedminster. A season-ticket holder blames a striker and a crossbar for a decade of not believing, and the committee rules the crossbar innocent, eight to one, the dissenting vote cast by the absent crossbar itself. I like that the woodwork got a vote. My version of his failure is smaller and uglier: I called a plain uninteresting, printed it at 56 Clerkenwell Close, and then spent six years not writing about anything I could get wrong again. The filings are still tacky with varnish. I keep going anyway, one peg a week, which is the whole discipline of this bench.

19 July 2025 — The line the radio left behind

One evening in July I was voicing notes to the phone while the radio played an abridged Moby-Dick and the dictionary lay open on the bench at “cat,” which Mabel had put there as a joke about how she climbs the loft ladder. The voice memo caught all three at once, and the transcription has read in my file ever since, word for word:

I treated you like a man as a cat, Tashtego mounts aloft; and once more, there was on him from his mouth.

— voice memo to phone, 19 July 2025, 21:40

It is three voices braided into one sentence, and it is the most accurate description of this bench I have. “I treated you like” is me to the model, mid-apology: I had treated the county like a sentence in a piece about a fort, like a plain to be dismissed in a clause. “A man as a cat, Tashtego mounts aloft” is the harpooner from the radio, going up the shrouds the way Mabel goes up that ladder, all weight and no noise. And “once more, there was on him from his mouth” is the dictionary, the entry on a cat’s mouth—whiskers, teeth, the works—caught by the microphone as I turned the page. None of the three sources is wrong; the sentence is only broken the way the dockside line was broken, and I am keeping it in the file because it is the truest thing any machine in this shed ever said to me.

2 August 2025 — Hung in the hall

The board is done and hung in the hall of the old office, which is a café now; they let me, because the man who runs it went to the poetry night in the redundant dockside hall, reader number nine of eleven, and he did not need the model explained to him. The salt jar rattles when the door slams. The Dee Estuary brass catches the afternoon. The entry goes into the register beside TM-2008 in the magazine’s error file: CH-0081, the plain called uninteresting, corrected—the plain was never uninteresting, it was unmeasured. That is the whole of it, in a line I can set in type at last. To Manchester, the sister letter; to Bristol, the woodwork and its vote; to Cheshire, 2,344 square centimetres of a bench where the scale held and the tea, as ever, was a witness.
