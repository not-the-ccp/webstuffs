The Museum of the Ledgered Realm · Case VII, the Stuart Case · Placard No. 1068

Charles I, 1600–1649

The King Who Was Counted at the Lamp Post · King of England, Scotland and Ireland from 27 March 1625 until his execution, 30 January 1649

The Object

The object in this case is a king, exhibited the way the case exhibits everything else: held, counted, and not finished. Charles was of the House of Stuart, born 19 November 1600 at Dunfermline Palace, second son of King James VI of Scotland and younger brother of Henry Frederick, Prince of Wales. In 1603 his father took the English throne as James I, and Charles moved south, where he spent much of the rest of his life. He became heir apparent to the kingdoms of England, Scotland and Ireland on 6 February 1612, upon the death of his elder brother. An unsuccessful and unpopular attempt to marry him to the Infanta Maria Anna of Spain ended in the eight-month visit to Spain of 1623, which demonstrated the futility of the marriage and the fitness of the ledger. When James I died on 27 March 1625, Charles succeeded him at once, was crowned at Westminster on 2 July 1626, ruled a Parliament he kept dissolving, and on 30 January 1649 was put to death at the Banqueting House, Old Palace Yard, convicted of high treason against his own realm. The twelfth bell of that day is counted in the register below and has not finished falling.

The Note, Kept Word for Word

Pinned inside the chest in which the last state dinner was kept, the case holds one note in a steward's hand. The museum reads it as a direction to the kitchen of the Banqueting House for the last ordinary dinner, and it is preserved, unamended and pending, exactly as the sister sheet across the hall preserves the sentence of the Aigues-Mortes quay, and exactly as the field diary beside the shelf preserves its library stamp. The hand is not named. The case notes compare it, by practice and not proof, to the hand of a certain Hugh Bardulf, who in an earlier reign served three kings for the price of one bell-stroke and whose vacancy is still open on the classifieds sheet filed with this case, The Lionheart Ledger, No. 340, Westminster, anno 1198.

Keep stirring with a sudden seemed somehow, in some exceptional case, be tolerably brisk.

It has never been translated, and the case does not require it to be. A note kept word for word is a note held, deliberately, without being finished, and the Stuart Case is one of that practice as well as of the king.

The Reign Ledger — Twelve Entries, 1600–1649

Tap an entry in the counter to read it against the case; the whole register stands as printed, with or without the counter working.

  1. 1600 · The second son — a prince who is not expected to become king, filed at the bottom of the column.
  2. 1603 · The double crown — the father crosses the water; the son crosses after him and does not cross back.
  3. 1612 · The heir apparent — the elder brother is collected, and the column, which had been pending, becomes the king's by inheritance.
  4. 1623 · The Spanish visit — the marriage is advertised, inspected, and ruled pending; the prince returns without the Infanta.
  5. 1625 · The succession — king of England, Scotland and Ireland; all writs run as before, and the absence is, for now, the father's.
  6. 1626 · The coronation — the crown of the corridor is passed once more, and the bell is wound.
  7. 1629 · The dissolved house — the king rules without Parliament for eleven years; the column is left open, the way columns are.
  8. 1637 · The book in the kirk — Scotland objects to the form of the service; the objection is, in the case's phrase, a sentence that will not translate.
  9. 1642 · The standard — the war begins; the two houses of the realm take up positions, and the king takes up one of them.
  10. 1648 · The surrender — the last castle goes; the king, the prisoner, is delivered into the ledger's custody.
  11. 1649 · The lamp post — the king is put to death; the twelfth bell is rung and left, in this case, deliberately unfinished.
  12. 1660 · The restoration — the line is counted back in; the ledger opens again, and whatever was corrosive has already been ruled in solution.

References in the Case

The sheet filed beside this placard is the classifieds sheet of an earlier reign, The Lionheart Ledger, No. 340, Westminster, anno 1198, where a certain steward is wanted for one bell-stroke and every account remains open on purpose; the bell of 1649 is the same bell, rung again. The field diary filed across the hall, the Lodge at the Gate's field diary, Entry No. 14, keeps the trail register under the number fifty-three, and notes that a column held, deliberately, without being finished, is a practice the house has kept since at least 1198. Both references are in the case. Both are pending.
