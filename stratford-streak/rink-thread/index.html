On the game itself: six skaters, one puck, three periods

Open 4 November 1937, 11:42 p.m., frost on the windows · opened by Steward_Montrose · 6 replies · the thread is pinned, the board being cold

Steward_Montrose steward 4 Nov 1937, 11:42 p.m.

Since the box from Lot 47 has been resting on the Winter Room table for three weeks, and questions about it keep arriving in the post, I thought I would write down what the game actually is, in case a stranger buys the sweater without the sport. Ice hockey — known simply as hockey in North America and parts of Europe — is a team sport played on ice skates, usually on an ice skating rink with lines and markings specific to the sport. It belongs to a family of sports called hockey, and the whole family is argued the same way: by the score.

The two opposing teams score by using their sticks to control and advance a vulcanized rubber hockey puck, and then shooting it into the net of the other team. Each goal is worth one point. That is the entire arithmetic of the game: one point, the net, the board, the ice. A formal game is an hour of play broken into three 20-minute periods, and the team with the highest score when the third period is over is declared the winner. If the score is level, there is overtime, and if overtime does not decide it, there is a shootout. In a formal game each team has six skaters on the ice at a time, barring any penalties, and the penalties are why the benches are crowded when the benches are crowded.

That is all the rulebook is, if you are honest about it. The rest is the skating.

BladeGuard_1919 bladesmith's son 5 Nov 1937, 8:15 a.m.

Steward, the rinks. The lines you mention are not decoration: the centre red line splits the sheet, the two blue lines are where you may not carry the puck across, and the face-off circles — centre, four corners, and the dots — are where every stoppage starts, the puck tossed between the two circles and the two sticks. A rink is 200 feet by 85 by the league standard here in Canada, the boards ring it, and the corners are rounded at eight feet, which is the difference between a game and a rectangle. The ice is kept at about 22 degrees, cold enough that a dry puck sits flat and a wet one slides like it is being hurried. The blades in the box from Lot 47 — the Stratford skatesmith's, dated 1919 — were cut for that ice. I put them on the marble again last week; they ring the way your first post says the room heard.

ScallyJr. nest-box keeper 5 Nov 1937, 1:03 p.m.

The puck. Vulcanized rubber, three inches in diameter, one inch thick, six ounces, black. It is frozen before the game, hard as a button, and a hard puck travels straighter than a soft one, which is why the puck boy's bucket lives in the freezer and not in the corridor. Lot 48 of the same sale is one puck, 1926, Chicago, and I would not bid against the steward for it on sentiment alone. My grandfather kept the nest records out at the marsh the same way a league keeps a ledger — sixty-three years, no year missing — and he used to say the game is the only one where the referee's first word is a place, face-off, and the last one is a time, the horn. We keep the account the same way: stop by stop.

Steward_Montrose steward 5 Nov 1937, 9:48 p.m.

Before the thread closes I want to put one page of the Winter Room ledger onto the board, the storm-night page, because a letter came in asking what the steward meant by the card found at the bottom of the Lot 47 box, and the honest answer is that the ledger is stranger than the card. Mrs. Fenwick, who ran the refreshment table for sale evenings, kept the table in a column of its own — the bird, the dish and its year, the glass, the pot — and on the storm page of 1931 she wrote, verbatim, and I am transcribing it exactly as it stands in the book:

Grouse. Charlotte aux pommes 1418 Cheese glass 1640 Hot-water dish for the slow route itself rather than put out here?” she asked.

The book gives no answer, and the board will not invent one. The stewards of the Winter Room transcribe and do not require; that is the whole rule, and it is the rule the card inside the box was written under. If the sentence arrives at a table it is a sentence, and if it does not, it is a ledger, and both are kept as found.

MorenzRoad Stratford, Ont. 6 Nov 1937, 10:20 a.m.

What nobody in this thread has said is what the six skaters are for. On ice, the six are a system: two wingers who ride the boards, a centre who plays the middle and feeds the two, three men who build the front of the net and defend the back of it, and one who guards the crease. The centre in our box was the middle of that system for fourteen seasons — 757 games, the league's own account — and the reason the newspapers gave him two names is that the middle is the hardest position to fill and he filled it from Stratford. You can read the whole season-by-season account at the Lot 47 catalogue page, and the goaltender's side of the etiquette is treated properly in the house book's chapter on standing between the posts. The board, the crease, the one point: the game keeps them all.

Steward_Montrose steward 6 Nov 1937, 11:58 p.m.

Closing note for the record. The game an hour, the three periods of twenty, the overtime, the shootout, the six skaters, the one point — all of it is a route, and a route is kept the same way anywhere, stop by stop, one arrival at a time. That is the standing advice of the field guide on the marsh road, which this board has filed under slow routes: the tree swallow entry, Number 12, keeps the faith in a route that arrives, and this thread is its ice. The ticker below the thread keeps the third-period clock; when it reads zero the thread is closed for the night, the windows frosted, and the ledger in the book. The front desk of the whole catalogue is at Webstuffs, where the other lots of Sale No. 92 are filed, and where the board is pinned.
