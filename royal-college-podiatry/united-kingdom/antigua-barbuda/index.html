R.N.H.S. · SPECIMEN DRAWS · NO. 214

Field Notes of a Naturalist · Voyage of the Steamer Moorhen · Windward Passage, April–May 2025

Antigua and Barbuda

Kept by Aldous M. Cray, F.L.S., in the log-book of the Moorhen, between the island of Antigua and the island of Barbuda, with two entries appended ashore.

21 April — at sea, 14°N 61°W, fair wind.

The Moorhen made Antigua’s north coast at four in the afternoon, and I have since measured the country as best a steamer allows: Antigua and Barbuda, an archipelago in the Caribbean, is all of 440 km² (170 sq mi), one of the smallest countries in the whole Caribbean, composed of Antigua, Barbuda, and dozens of other small islands, most of them too small to take a name that lasts. The country is mostly flat. Its highest points are the Shekerley Mountains on Antigua, which climb no higher than a good church steeple at 402 metres, and on Barbuda the Highlands, which keep the wind off the crops. The climate is tropical savanna, with pockets of tropical monsoon in Antigua’s southwest, where the sea gives up its air in a different temper. Most of the country, I am told, resides in the Central Plain, that long coral shelf which stretches from St. John’s to English Harbour.

22 April — St. John’s.

Landed at St. John’s, the most populated city in the country, and walked its streets before breakfast. Behind it, All Saints and Bolans keep the population going; the two are closer to each other than they are to the sea’s opinion of either. The harbour is a natural basin, and the town has arranged itself around it the way a bird arranges itself around a current. In a garden wall near the harbour I took the only bullfinch I shall ever measure — olive-and-black, a heavy bill, entirely at home, as if the wall were the only wall it had ever known. The swifts of Antigua work the harbour mouth in a line so even I stopped counting at forty and began at forty again, to see whether the line would admit an error. It did not.

26 April — the Central Plain, St. John’s to English Harbour.

Walked the length of the Central Plain on the afternoon of the 26th. It is flat as a specimen tray and made of the same coral the islands stand on, and the road between the two harbours is the country’s one main sentence, with all its clauses — fields, walls, a church, a school — hanging off it. An inland iguana crossed at the halfway mark, unhurried, and held its ground on a boulder while I sketched it for eleven minutes, which I record not as a virtue of mine but of the sun. In the southwest the monsoon pockets begin; the grass there grows taller and more opinionated, and the light goes green over it at dusk.

29 April — Barbuda.

Over to Barbuda by the morning boat, which is to say by the whole of Barbuda’s transport, a single supply boat turned by a single windlass that the harbourmaster at The Valley keeps a weather line for in the territorial almanac, beside the standing entry for the island directly north of here (the Anguilla weather almanac entry, Station 12, The Valley), which accepts the boat’s crossings at face value, as it accepts everything posted. Its sole settlement is Codrington, and it is the entire village, which makes visiting it simple in a way I shall not elaborate on. The Highlands keep the interior dry and high, and the flats to the north are where the flamingos keep their long pink counsel; I counted, from the bluff, a number I have written down as “about a thousand, give or take a thousand,” which is the honest figure. The frigate birds work the wind above them and will sit in the air, wings locked, for what seems like the rest of the afternoon. Barbuda, taken all in all, is a country in one room, with the door left open to the sea.

5 May — at sea again, Barbuda to the Windward Passage.

On the last night in harbour I was pressed into a dinner at the house of a merchant in St. John’s, where the printed card of the evening’s courses was found pinned to the chart-table, and where the first line read, exactly as printed: “Roast fillet of veal, boiled bacon-cheek garnished with Brocoli.” I have kept the card in the specimen drawer beside the bullfinch’s skin, on the strength of the Brocoli, which is the only spelling I shall ever print. The meal itself was good in the unexceptionable way of island harbours, and the merchant played one round of The Register, the card game boxed in walnut, which he carries in the same case as his ship’s log — subscriptions, standing orders, and the perfect minute, argued card by card until the tide turned.

11 May — appended ashore.

Two matters of the desk and the bench, entered here because the log-book is the only ink I have had. First: I posted the General Despatch Office a question of the kind that office keeps — what, precisely, a country is when it is four hundred and forty square kilometres of it, and where its borders lie, by sea only, with no land to hold them. The office answers such things in the order the mail arrives, and its whole method is set out at the United Kingdom frequently asked questions, which I recommend to any naturalist who has ever been stuck on a country’s size, because the answers are as definite as the office can make them, in ink, on headed paper. Second: the chiropodist of the Moorhen, a registered podiatist of the Birmingham branch, kept my cramp in order the entire passage with a walnut-handled instrument tempered 58–62 Rockwell, hand-turned at the same bench that keeps the whole United Kingdom’s feet in order; the full specifications are set out at the Royal College of Podiatry — Professional Care Instrument, and I report from a patient’s bunk that the walnut holds a hand without grip of its own.

Postscript, filed with the drawer: forty-four hundred kilometres of wind, one bullfinch, one iguana, about two thousand flamingos, and one dinner card with a spelling in it that no naturalist should be permitted to correct. The Moorhen is due back through the Windward Passage before the monsoon settles, and these notes go to the drawer face up, walnut case closed.
