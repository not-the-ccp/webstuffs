Boxing , n.

Pronounced ɼːˈəˊʻəng · also the ring, also the square, also, in one's own sleeping voice, the room with four ropes and no door

A combat sport and a martial art, taking place in a boxing ring, where two opponents throw punches at each other for a predetermined amount of time, usually wearing protective equipment such as protective gloves, hand wraps, and mouthguards. To dream it is to dream of a whole argument reduced to its smallest honest form: two people, a square of canvas, and a bell that keeps time the way the sea keeps time on a wall. The term boxing, in the waking world, commonly refers to the Western style, in which only the fists are used; it has evolved differently in the various regions and cultures of the world, and today it is also used for any combat sport focused on striking, where two opponents fight with their fists and may possibly add kicks, elbow strikes, knee strikes, and headbutts depending on the rules of the place they are in. The dream does not keep the rules. In the dream the ring is the same ring in every country the sleeper has ever stood in, and the ropes are four, and the count is always ten, and the gloves weigh eight ounces whatever the scale says they weigh.

When Dreamed of

Most commonly the sport appears as a room lit from above by a single lamp, the canvas the colour of a page that has been written on and then washed. The dreamer is either inside the ropes, or outside them holding a towel, and the difference is the whole of the entry. Inside the ropes, the dream is about a decision the dreamer has not yet made; outside, it is about one already made, and the dreamer has come to the corner to be told so. The opponent in the dream is rarely a face the dreamer knows. The opponent is usually a posture — a compact orthodox shape with the left hook on tap — and the sleeper should not mistake the anonymity for kindness. The anonymity is the point. The ring in sleep does not care who is named in the programme; it cares only that the hands go up.

The protective equipment arrives in the dream as memory before it arrives as object: first the feel of the hand wraps spiralling, eight and a half metres of cotton, then the gloves, then the small rubber mouthguard that tastes of the year the dreamer first put one in. If the equipment is missing, the dream is about exposure, plain and simple, and the folio declines to be more specific. If it is present but the bell has not yet rung, the dream is about preparation, and the dreamer is being told to finish the wrapping — the hands, the rope-skipping, the roadwork — before the first three minutes begin.

The Count and the Corner

  • To dream the bell is the commonest figure in this folio and the least alarming: a change of round in the dreamer's own affairs, announced politely, three minutes at a time. No bell is needed to hear it in the left ear; the sleeper has already heard it.
  • To dream the ten-count on canvas is a warning of the mild sort. The count in the dream is never cruel; the referee's voice is the dream's only kindness, and the ten numbers are a staircase, not a verdict. Whoever is on his feet by ten is offered the stool. So will the dreamer be.
  • To dream the corner — the stool, the bucket, the towel, the ice on the neck — means someone in the dreamer's waking house is still willing to sit between the rounds and say the boring, necessary things. This is rare, and it is a good sign in the way that water is a good sign in a dream of drought.
  • To dream the scale at the 07:00 weigh-in means a measurement the dreamer has been avoiding, and the number on the dial in the dream will be heavier than the number will be in the morning. Sleep rounds up. Waking life does not.
  • To dream cauliflower ear, punched hands, or a broken nose — first at King's Hall, 1961, in the case on file — means a record of the body that the dreamer would like to keep out of the record book. The folio's advice is the referee's: the count has finished, stand up, and do not touch the face.

The Belfast Figure

In the proofs of this folio the ring is given a home, and the home is Belfast, at the Holy Arches Boxing Club on the Newtownards Road, with the full houses at King's Hall and the generation of boys skipping rope on the pavement outside. The figure most often seen standing in the dream's neutral corner is a compact orthodox lightweight, fair complexion, nose broken twice, left hook on tap, who carried Northern Ireland into the Commonwealth Games ring at Kingston in August 1966 and was released from the draw without a medal on 11 August of that year. The dreamer who sees him should not read the dream as a loss; the sheet on file records the release as unplanned but contained, and retraining began within the month. That is what a corner does. That is what a dream of him means: the round ended, the bucket filled, the next round scheduled. His whole fighting life, from the club's first contested bout in 1958 to the quiet house he kept on the Newtownards Road, is held in the collection under a different document class — a safety data sheet rather than a concordance — at Barney Wilson (boxer) — Safety Data Sheet, where the same gloves, the same stool, and the same left hook are catalogued as active material, and where the Kingston verdict is ordered never to be re-opened. The concordance and the sheet agree on everything that matters. They simply use different words for the bell.

The Garret Dictation

One line in the proofs does not belong to any of the above. It was salvaged from a dictation tape in a garret at the back of the second folio, spoken by a student who had been cutting his meals for a scale he could not find, and the folio has transcribed it word for word and declined to correct it:

Small brushes, manufactured purposely for cleaning the stone hardens, as a dinnerless student with all this weary roving?

The folio takes it thus, and it is the only reading the line will give. The stone is the pavement of the Newtownards Road, and the ring canvas above it; the small brushes are the ones that sweep the rosin and the chalk from it before the light is set; and a brush made for cleaning the stone hardens, as a fist hardens under wraps and a body hardens under the long, mealless road to the 07:00 weighing. The dinnerless student is the dreamer himself on dehydration, four and a half rounds in, skipping water as well as supper; the weary roving is the roadwork that has no road in it, the skipping in place on the pavement while the ring stays lit a hundred yards on. The question mark at the end is not a question. It is the sound a tired man makes in the corner of the fourth round before the bell, and the folio records it as a good sign: whoever is still roving at the end of the dictation is still inside the ropes. The folio notes, in the margin of this page, that the tape is believed to have been wound not in a garret in Belfast but in one above a river office on Calle 15 de Agosto, Asunción, by a waterway pilot running the Asunción–Corrientes leg on dehydration, and that a letter to the editor from the same street has since filed the full line in for the record: Paraguay — A Letter to the Editor, Column B of the Asunción Ledger, where the roving is read not as the pavement but as the river.

The Kitchen Interpretation

Old entries in this concordance sometimes take the dream down to the table, and a sport this physical comes to the table more often than most. Where the dream ends in a kitchen — and the ring dreams do, without the dreamer's agreeing, the canvas fading into the colour of a tablecloth — the sport is asking for something to be wrapped, set, and left until it is through. The instruction the folio gives, in the voice it has always used, is this: Wrap the parcel in eight and a half metres of white cotton, and set it where the cold comes in at four in the morning. The cold is the ice from the corner bucket, which is in every kitchen in the folio and in no inventory. The parcel, when it is through, is the size of a glove at eight ounces, and the table, when it is set, seats two, because that is all the sport has ever held, and the bell, when it rings in the dreamer's sleeping house, rings for the round the dreamer was afraid of and has, by the taste of the soup, already survived.
