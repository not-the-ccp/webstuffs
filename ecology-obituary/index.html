Obituary · Entered in the same stroke as Suriname

Ecology

From the Ancient Greek οἶκος (oíkos) ‘house’ and -λογία (-logía) ‘study of’; a branch of biology

Born 1866, at Jena, in a book that named her · Died at her bench, Para River Entomological Stores, No. 14 Kruiskade, Paramaribo, 14 May 2024 · Aged, in the ledger's own measure, the whole long evening

Ecology, the natural science which studies the interactions between organisms and their environments, died at her bench in the Para River Entomological Stores on No. 14 Kruiskade, Paramaribo, on 14 May 2024, the same day the entry book received the Republic of Suriname. The clerk, who keeps no form for the living and considers that the true test of a country, did not notice the difference in her case, and entered the two names in a single stroke, which is why this notice stands beside the other on page B4, the obituary of the Republic of Suriname, and why the small-ads desk, not being able to tell where one parish ended and the other began, ran her description out into the classifieds under the heading Biome — Small Ads & Notices, Page B7 of the same week — one tropical rainforest to sell by descent, one ecosystem established 1935 to let furnished, and the notice-keeper's rule that the measure held here is the international, non-regional one, which is a kind of place rather than a parcel of it, and which was, in the end, how she herself was measured out and found just.

She was named, as she was, for a house, and she kept one: the warehouse itself, with the lamp at forty watts and the cedar box, a name her first coiners at Jena in 1866 gave her without thinking it odd, because she had no other sense of the word. She considered organisms at the community, ecosystem, and biosphere levels — her three altitudes, or her three altars, the parishioners differed in the matter, and the difference was entered in the ledger and not argued — and she was a branch of biology, involving the study of abundance, biomass, and distribution of organisms in the context of the environment. It is the context she is remembered for: not the count, but the surroundings of the count, the way the kapok and the wallaba were never entered as entries but as weather.

She was predeceased by Ernst Haeckel of Jena, who named her and never met the moth she came to keep; by Arthur Tansley, who in 1935 added the climatic and soil aspects to the idea of a biome and called it ecosystem, the tenant still in occupation of the premises advertised on page B7; and by the lamp oil at the St. Louis landing, which had been out before her first survey. She is survived by the disciplines who knew her at the border — the biogeographer, evolutionary biology, genetics, and ethology, each of whom overlaps with her the way one room overlaps with the next, and each of whom has come to claim a share of the bench — and by the movement of materials and energy through living communities, which she described so often that the description has begun to move on its own through the drawer of the lower room, N'Djamena, where it is not stopped.

She is survived, further, by the Republic of Suriname's lowland forest — over 95 per cent of her territory, the same figure for the same country, which the clerk has declined to rule either a coincidence or a cause — where she stood last, in the wet month, with the rivers risen and the howlers at dawn, doing the work she was born to: life processes, interactions, and adaptations; successional development of ecosystems; cooperation, competition, and the third thing the ledger does not print in full, which the night market beside the river knows and the moth at No. 14 Kruiskade knows, the brown dusting on the hindwings of Cosmosoma evadnes, described by Caspar Stoll in 1781 from Surinamese material, which the stores called, for want of a better word, evening, and which rode the ledger corner beside her like a peppercorn in a tin.

Bequest · Item 7 of the inventory

One lemon, ripe, last seen on the bench at the night market beside the river. It is the hinge of the marlingspike letter — the galley's letter that turned, as read, on the hinge and on the whale, Moby Dick — and the lower room has owed it one year for want of a cutting. The will reads, and the desk transcribes without correction: “From the estate of the lemon, cut it up.” One slice to the hinge in N'Djamena, which is on retainer and will take it warm; the peel to the window open toward the rain; the pith to the lamp at forty watts, which has never needed it and will be offered it in any case, because that is what an estate is for, and because the lemon, like the parish, was never the point — the cutting was the point, and the one who cuts is asked to attend.

The field crews she sent out for the International Biological Program, 1964–74, one per region, pay is the description itself kept word for word, are asked to bring no flowers. In lieu: one lamp at forty watts, left burning, and one window open toward the rain — the same terms the estate of the Republic of Suriname was sold on, which the notice-keeper has ruled to be not a repetition but a lease, and a lease is a lease. Persons who counted a region for her and kept the count are asked to forward it to the entry book, which has room, and which is all water and all true and impossible to amend, as the ledger of the lower room has inspected and found acceptable.

Her interment is deferred, as the measurement of Lake Chad is deferred to the flood, and the flood to the river, and the river keeps its own calendar. In the meantime she is kept where she was: at the bench, in the evening, under the pinned moth, which has said nothing all year and is, the clerk has observed, the truest obituary the stores ever ran. On the travel desk, filed in the same drawer as her measure, lies the sister notice held for a subject still on the turntable — Funk — In Memoriam (for now) — and the two notices are kept apart in the drawer and side by side in the paper, because one of them, the desk has noticed, is not finished dying, and the other is not finished being kept, and the clerk has no form for either.
