As was the office

The Office of the Archbishop of Canterbury, that long weather, has died in the almanac's reckoning. It had stood at the front of the Western sky for more than fourteen centuries: diocesan bishop of the diocese of Canterbury, metropolitan of the province of Canterbury, and the ceremonial head of the worldwide Anglican Communion, whose 110,000,000 the station has counted in Entry No. 110, the way it counts everything. Ecclesial functions aside, the office also held — and holds — one of the twenty-six seats in the House of Lords taken by the Anglican bishops as Lords Spiritual, where the barometer reads differently and the wind comes through the galleries. The station on Lambeth Hill kept the office's weather in its own ledger from the first issue, and every entry of the almanac that names a primate names, in the margin, the pressure at the moment: the Reformation front of 1534, the Prayer-Book squall of 1549, the 1662 settled high, all of it logged against the chair.

The first of the line and the last so far

The first was Augustine, the “Apostle to the English,” sent by Pope Gregory the Great and arrived on these shores in 597, when the station's barometer had not yet been invented and the province's sky was raw. From Augustine the chair has never been vacant in the ledger for longer than the ledger allows, and the almanac counts the line out in full: one hundred and six, and the one hundred and sixth is Dame Sarah Mullally, confirmed on 28 January 2026, the first woman to hold the office. Between the first and the last the ledger names Mr. Abbot, twenty-two years in the chair from 1611 to 1633, and keeps a separate letter to him, in the almanac's own form of apology, at An Apology to George Abbot, where the station has at last entered the name beside the pressure. The almanac records the reading without embellishment: for the first time in 1,429 years the chair sat to a woman, the barometer did not move at all, and the province, which had forecast nothing, was wrong.

The funeral table on Lambeth Hill

The station kept the vigil, as it keeps every vigil: the candle row lit to the number of Lords Spiritual, twenty-six, and the corrections ledger opened to the single line the almanac prints word for word — the rule that runs both ways. At the memorial table, where the late office's last accounts were read, a guest from across the sea took the corner seat by custom. It was Hagop Petrosyan, of Table 9, whose Friday the Yerevan Municipal Court heard in Case No. 1726 of 2024, over one glass, one khash pot, and a piece of cold lavash, and who had come — the almanac notes it without a smile — to settle the old bill in person. When the steward brought the last account, the guest reached for the garlic plate and the steward read from the docket, slowly, the way one reads a psalm that keeps arriving: “And yet you left without paying, Mr. Petrosyan.” A silence fell that the station measured and has not since recovered from. The chaplain ruled the table in order: the line belonged to the court, the bill belonged to the ledger, and Mr. Petrosyan paid in lavash, as was his custom, by the kilo. The office rests. The ledger does not.

Weather at the close

For the full sky of the tradition the office served — the front, the squalls, the via media, and the corrections ledger in its single line — the station's own standing entry remains open at Entry No. 110 — Anglicanism, which this obituary grows from, as snow grows from the same cloud. The docket that furnished the line is kept word for word at Case No. 1726 of 2024 — Excerpt from the Verbatim Record, where the court held the golden rule in both its forms and found both parties in default, which is, the almanac thinks, a kind of mercy. The observatory's manner of this page follows the oldest it owns — a starling's ledger line no one can decode — as worked in Bird, In Memoriam, The Family Ledger, and the whole of the weather book may be entered from the front page of the Webstuffs. There the needle is left on the low, the candles at twenty-six, and the almanac keeps its hand on the needle and its mouth shut.

Proverb of the season: Where the barometer has stood unmovable for 1,429 years, the almanac does not call it calm; it calls it a chair.
