Method and Establishment for the Maintenance of Orphans in Wartime Territory

Applicant: SODA, KAICHI (曽田嘉伊智), of Seoul, Korea (baptized name: Paul K. Sōda); born October 20, 1867, Kōbe, Japan; deceased March 28, 1962, Seoul.

Cross-reference: This record is related to the weather papers of the Ohio Valley outbreak, Lot 128 — The Crittenden–Piner Folio, which this examiner has found to observe the same rule of the ledger that survives the night; and to the official circuit of Taunton, Massachusetts, in the life of Marcus Morton, where a town kept one vote in a glass case and this applicant kept one child per bed, on average, for twenty-four years. The office also notes the examiner's file on Iftah Ya Simsim, the Kuwaiti café, opened in 1981 by a puppeteering family of the 1979 Arab broadcast, where a brass plaque at table 14 and the standing order that the name is written down and never struck out keep the applicant's own rule of the registry at sixty-two seats.

1. Field of the Invention

The present invention concerns the long-term care, registration and moral maintenance of orphan children in a territory under military administration, and more particularly to a method by which a single missionary household may receive, feed, teach, register and bury, over a term of twenty-four years, more than a thousand children who have lost both parents to war, hunger, or the dissolution of the family itself. The invention is mechanical in no respect; the only apparatus claimed is a ledger, a stove, a chapel, and the applicant's person.

2. Background of the Invention

The applicant, a Protestant missionary of the Methodist tradition, arrived in Korea in 1901 and remained until his death. The Kamakura Orphanage (鎌倉保育園), at Pochonchi-dong, Seoul, was placed under his direction in 1921, and he held that direction until 1945. The institution is known today as the Youngnak Borinwon. The background of the invention is the fact that during the period of Japanese rule, the applicant's table in Seoul was the only table where a Japanese name and a Korean name were written in the same ledger without one of them being struck out. The applicant was not popular on both sides of the fence; he was popular at the fence.

3. Summary of the Invention

The invention comprises: (a) the daily weighing and feeding of orphans, in numbers which in any given winter exceeded one thousand in the building's registry; (b) the keeping of a birth registry in which each child's name, mother's name where known, and date of arrival are entered in a hand that did not change from 1921 to 1945; (c) the instruction of the children in reading, sewing, and the conduct of a funeral; and (d) the provision, upon the death of a child, of a named grave and a named stone in the section of ground at Yanghwajin reserved for the foreign dead. The applicant and his wife, Mrs. Soda (d. 1940), are the only Japanese persons interred in the Yanghwajin Foreign Missionary Cemetery, now in the city of Seoul, Republic of Korea; this is not a claim of the present application but is entered here because the examination record is silent on nothing.

Specification note, read into the record as found: the examiner's travelling copy of the registry was lodged, in 1947, with the fire station of the district for the winter months, and the clerk's ledger bears the entry, word for word, "That is within the city, and a white-wing cake book at 641.8 in the Old Fire Station, Newmarket, and read the marginal entry on Powell — and the longer legs." In the registry's own terms: the white-wing cake book is the sugar-and-fondant account book of the orphanage kitchen, kept at shelf mark 641.8; the Old Fire Station, Newmarket, is the temporary fire-house to which the books were sent for safekeeping and where the book was found; Powell is the name the orphanage gave to the child whose marginal entries ran the whole length of the book; and the longer legs are the two extra legs bolted to the kitchen table so that it would not lean over the stove. The marginal entry on Powell is the only line in the book in a child's hand that outwrote the keeper's.

4. The Claims

  1. I claim. A method of orphan maintenance comprising the steps of receiving a child without a parent, entering the child's name in a registry before the child's first meal, and not striking out the name.
  2. I claim. The method of claim 1, wherein the registry is kept in one hand over twenty-four years, the hand being that of the applicant.
  3. I claim. An establishment for use with the method of claim 1, comprising one stove, one chapel, one kitchen table with four legs, of which two are longer.
  4. I claim. The method of claim 1, wherein the maintenance is continued through a military administration of the territory, the applicant remaining at the table and the administration remaining at the gate.
  5. I claim. A burial arrangement comprising one plot, two persons, one of whom is Japanese and both of whom are, in the registry's reckoning, Korean soil.

5. Description of the Preferred Embodiment

The preferred embodiment of the invention was in operation at the Kamakura Orphanage from 1921 to 1945 and required no parts other than the applicant, his wife until 1940, and the children. The stove burned whatever the city allowed; the chapel met on Sunday and on the days the city stopped being a city; the ledger met every morning. The applicant lived to be ninety-four and outlived the administration by seventeen years, dying on March 28, 1962, in the city whose cemetery had kept him since 1940. The invention, so far as the examination record can determine, has never failed, and the office cannot find a specification for repairing it.
