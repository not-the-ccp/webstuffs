An Apology to the Dual Monarchy

To the late Austro-Hungarian Monarchy,
officially the Österreichisch-Ungarische Monarchie,
Vienna and Budapest, 1867–1918
addressed to both thrones; one of them is empty, and this letter is aware of it

Your Imperial and Apostolic Majesties, or whatever remains of your archive: this desk writes, on behalf of the bench, in black ink, in the same hand that copied the courier order at Nadzab and underlined its last three letters twice — so you may take it that this apology, too, was underlined twice, because the desk underlines everything, and the desk is sorry.

What we are sorry for is this. The bench built your war on a table in the order it happened — 41 metres at 1:35, in 1 September 1939's grey putty to 2 September 1945 — and it spent six years of its own war on that table, and then, in the last entry, it wrote the whole of your ending in a single clause: “Germany, Austria, Japan, and Korea occupied by the victors.” One clause for fifty-one years. You were a dual state — the Lands of the Reichsrat and the Lands of the Holy Crown of Saint Stephen, bound at the Compromise of 1867 after Sadowa, with one man who was emperor at Vienna and king at Budapest, Franz Joseph, who sat the Viennese seat for sixty-eight years, and his last successor, Karl, who had it for eleven weeks — and the bench gave you a comma. Thirty-three nationalities, two capitals, one Danube run through the middle, and a comma.

We are also sorry about the flag. The tray at the east end holds four sets of occupation flags, and the set filed under your name is black-yellow-white, which was the house flag of the dynasty, not the flag of the state, and by the autumn of 1918 there was no state, and what the bench had glued to the Hofburg was the colour of a household, held up where a constitution had been. The correct flag is in a packet on the barge, which ties up twice weekly at the landing stage, and which has been late once this season, and the dock has already been blamed — but the flag will arrive, because everything on that bench arrives by water, and the rate of arrival is a thing the war itself never managed.

And we must apologize for the folio. One slip of the Table No. 6 ledger came back to this desk torn along the date line, and where the wash went over the paper twice the headings ran together, so that the entry for the end of the war now reads, word for word, and has been pasted into this letter exactly as it arrived:

Entry 05 August–September 1945 — the beach The Normandy end.

— the date of the close of the war over-written by the heading of the beach entry, “the beach” and “The Normandy end” smudged across a line that was supposed to say the end. The desk misfiled it for a season as a note about Austria, which is how the wrong flag got its slot in the first place; a beach in Normandy, 1:35, with its tide line in dark wash, is not the Hofburg, and a line that means the end of everything should not be allowed to mean the sand. That is our one error of geography in five years of correspondence, and we own it in full, in both capitals.

The bench will do better. A corner is being marked on the tabletop for a double gate — the Viennese gate and the Budapesti gate, one Danube between them, forty-one chairs at 1:35 for the Reichsrat and the Országházus, and no comma, only a dash, which is the correct punctuation for a state that ended on a Tuesday in November with two governments and neither of them in a hurry. The sawdust in the drawer is the sawdust of the glass cut for Exhibit 43.B, the airfield in the Markham Valley, so the corner you will get is built on Pacific wood and Central European putty, which is to say it is the whole table in miniature, which is to say it is the whole war, which is what the table has to carry. The full record of the error and of the bench is kept where it belongs, in the Second World War — Build Log, Table No. 6, six years in wartime order, every number written down so it cannot be rounded away.

The desk keeps every parts slip, every courier order, and now every apology, and stamps them all in the same hand. We ask nothing of your archive except that the letter be filed, both copies, one in each capital, and that the wrong flag be kept in the packet until the barge brings the right one. It will bring it. It is twice-weekly.

Respectfully, in ink, and twice underlined,
The Message Desk, Gallery 4,
with the keeper of the bench of Table No. 6
