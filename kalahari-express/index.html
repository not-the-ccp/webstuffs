The Kalahari Express

Gaborone ↔ the deep past · the whole country in one train · twelve seats per compartment, one per district, and a doctor on board who transcribes.

The dashes on the line move. The train does not. It has never once been late, which the family regards as a form of diplomacy — the pot has never broken, the train has never broken, and the family has stopped distinguishing the two.

I. The Line, and Why It Runs the Way It Runs

The history of Botswana, the family has always said, is a journey with no luggage restriction, so they have put it on the railway. The Kalahari Express, line B/12, runs the length of the country — and the length of its past — in the time it takes one pot to come to its first honest simmer. The official name is the Republic of Botswana, a landlocked country in Southern Africa bordered by South Africa, Namibia, Zambia and Zimbabwe, some 581,730 square kilometres of which about seventy per cent is Kalahari Desert, and the rest, the family adds, is horizon. The train's job is to carry you from the deep past to Gaborone Central without making you stop for the Difaqane, which, on other lines, costs an entire century. Eleven halts. Six hundred and eighty-eight kilometres of track, three thousand years of country. Every station below is a real station, or was, or will be, and every departure time is kept to the minute by the stationmaster in Lobatse, who is Kgaogelo's boy and who has held the job since the pans and will hold it after Gaborone.

It led to the Christianization of the region, which the stationmaster records without sentiment and the family records with one footnote: the first hymn sung at Maun was off-key, and the tradition has been that every church in the country inherits the flat note out of respect. The water on the platform here is fresh. The salt is over.

The answer arrives at this halt in the form of a flag and a name: 1885, the Bechuanaland Protectorate, taken under the British umbrella, the borders held the way a family holds a dented pot — firmly, and with one hand ready to move it. The announcer at Molepolole says the word Republic four times, because at the third it was not working and at the fourth it was. This is the halt where the family recipe — molomo gwapa, spiced beef and dried meat in a long tomato gravy — is first written down properly, in the ledger, as entry no. 7, and the whole history of the country, which you have just travelled, is what the pot was sitting on while it was being written. Read the entry itself, in the Ntwaagae kitchen, word for word, if you want the gravy's side of this halt.
17:06Gaborone South-East6482026–2028 Halt of eighteen minutes, in a country that is quiet in the particular way of a country during the respiratory pandemic of 2026 to 2028. The clinics have lost their usual noise; the platforms have lost theirs. Dr Kefilwe Ntwaagae, the family doctor and the doctor on this train, works the length of the carriage giving out the family's whole theory of the body — the solar system of the body, the digestive functions — and the kitchen at home, which is where the train is really going, cooks this halt's dish on forty-one Sundays, each one for twelve, and the ledger does not once record a failure. The halt is not sad. The halt is the pause between two long, long quiet things.
19:47Gaborone Central688Now Arrival. The pot is on the platform, whole, as it has always been whole, and the history of Botswana — ancient and tribal, protectorate, and now the Republic — is in the dented rim, and the dented rim is where the train is going next, whenever the gravy is ready. The pot then crossed the water once, stood on a studio shelf in Road Town, Tortola, and went on the air; that run is logged as Twelve for the Harbour, Studio 2, BVI Broadcasting, one pot per cay, and this line's timetable carries its four minutes and twelve seconds as a permanent entry, the way other lines carry signals. The driver does not turn the engine around. There is nothing left to run to. The Webstuffs front page is where the family boards again, on whatever else is running.

III. Medical Notice Nº 12 — Printed on the Reverse of Every Ticket

There is a notice on the back of every Kalahari Express ticket, in Dr Kefilwe Ntwaagae's hand, set down in the spring of 2027, during the quiet of the pandemic, for the passengers of the heat. The family has never been able to parse it, and the family's rule is that a doctor's words are not parsed, they are kept, so it is reproduced here exactly as it stands, letter for letter, and the commentary below is the family's attempt to travel it: the tender meat of the pot, the long dry season overhead, and the doctor, where he can be found, who is to say. The kitchen has obeyed it literally since 1991, and the gravy, as a result, has never once boiled. A boiling pot is a lie, and the doctor does not like lies.

I know that it is tender, pour it over the hot weather invites to a doctor, where possible.

— Medical Notice Nº 12, Dr Kefilwe Ntwaagae, Gaborone, 2027 · transcribed by the family, kept as it stands

On the reading the family has settled on, and keeps in the back page with the other notes: the molomo, soaked and softened, is the tender thing; the hot weather is the long overhead country of the dry season, which the line crosses with the windows shut; and where it is possible — and on this line it is always possible, because the doctor rides the last carriage — you invite it, that is, you bring the pot to him, and he says the word, and the pot may travel. The notice has been carried on every ticket since, and no passenger has ever read it twice the same way, which the family takes as evidence that the words are doing the work of the gravy, which is to keep moving slowly, and to give up what they hold only on their own schedule.

IV. Luggage, Pots, and Standing Orders

Luggage on line B/12 is restricted to one pot per passenger, dented rim preferred, and one history per country, which is all the family has ever managed to carry. The molomo hangs from the luggage rack in its small sacks, the way it has hung from the beam in Lobatse and from the case on the ferry from the USVI, and the ticket office in Gaborone Central will stamp your pot at no charge and no questions, which is the closest the railway comes to faith. If you are travelling the deep-past direction — which the line does not, officially, do — mind the platform at Difaqane Halt, keep your windows closed, and do not stand in the racing green, because the man in the racing-green coat will stand there instead, and the family has lost arguments for standing in that colour and has stopped. This timetable is issued once a year, the Sunday before the first make of the molomo, and it is true in the way the ledger is true: not because every word of it is provable, but because the family has checked the times, and the train, as ever, is not late. The man in the racing green, for the record, is accounted for by the shore side of the family: a naturalist watched him at the edge of a Hampshire heath for eleven years, and the watch is filed as Valentine Baker — Field Notes, Plate 9, where the racing green is explained, three services at a time, and the heather is pressed between the pages.
