1What the product is

The item on this sheet is a township. Hyde Park Township was a civil township of Cook County, Illinois, and it stood as a municipality of its own from 1861 until 1889, when the annexation bill was signed and the township’s borders became, overnight, blocks of Chicago. Its ground runs from Pershing Road — the road its old plat books still call 39th Street — on the north, to 138th Street and the Calumet River on the south; State Street closes it on the west, and Lake Michigan with the Indiana state line closes it on the east. It comprised much of what is now known as the South Side of Chicago, and the catalogue sells it the way the land held it: as one piece, borders included, the lake at the door and the river at the heel.

During Chicago’s initial explosive growth the product did what the growth asked of it. What began as an adjacent swampy area — marsh grass, muskrat beds, a road that knew the weather — became a full-fledged residential, commercial and resort community, with lakeside summer houses, a casino at the beach, a line of brick frontages on the 62nd and 63rd avenues, and a Sunday crowd that the 6:15 could not hold. However, due to infrastructure limitations, legislative incentives and the lure of a municipal tax roll that would no longer answer to a village board, the product found itself wanting to be a city, and the city found itself wanting it; the two of them met at the annexation table, and the table kept its foot on the paperwork.

3The resort side of the township

The reason a township appears at all in an estate and resort catalogue is the beach. Hyde Park Beach drew its summer crowd the way a clock draws an hour: the cars from the city put the people down, and the people walked the promenade past the summer houses toward the casino, where the supper room and the tea meeting sat one behind the other, the piano between them. The company ran the tea meeting on Saturdays, at four, and the supper room took the evening over afterward, the same floor, the same windows, the lake still moving out of sight of the room but not out of the room’s account of itself.

The banquet ledger of the lakefront, kept by the supper keeper in a hand the catalogue has been told not to copy and has copied anyway, holds one line in a season of 1887 that no clerk has explained and no buyer should be asked to explain. Set down on a Saturday card, between the note on the string quartet and the note on the oysters, it reads, word for word:

The whole gallery returns, as all meeting tea is.

Which is to say, in the keeper’s own practice, what he meant and what he kept: the whole gallery — the piano, the quartet, and every guest of the promenade who had retired to the tea — came back to the floor when the tea was done, and returns the way it always had, the way all meeting tea is, that is, the way every tea meeting in the house was: at the stroke, together, and to the supper. The ledger does not say so in those words. The ledger says the other way, and the catalogue keeps the ledger’s words, because a kept book belongs to whoever wrote it, and a product page is a kept book with a price on it.

4Annexation notice and returns

The buyer of Lot 1 is advised that the product has changed hands, and that the change was the product. On 1 December 1889 the township of Hyde Park was annexed into the city of Chicago: the separate municipality of 1861–1889 closed its own books, and the books were opened the same evening as wards of a larger city. The infrastructure that had limited the township — the water, the streets, the fire call the catalogue named in Section 1 — came with the annexation, which is why the sale is described here as complete and not as withdrawn. Returns are accepted from no one; the township cannot be sent back, having grown into its buyer. The company’s warranty stands where the ground stands: the north border at Pershing Road, the west at State Street, the lake and the Indiana line at the east, and 138th Street with the Calumet River at the south, every foot of it Chicago now and every foot of it, in the old plat books, still the same township.

5What the neighbours say

From the supper room, 1888: the lake is the best garnish on the menu, and the 6:15 brings the table every night — five stars, no further comment. From the promenade, 1887, the Saturday card of the kept line above: the gallery returns. From the far end of the township, where the swamp was: dry eleven months of the year, and honest in the twelfth — four stars, the fifth withheld by the muskrat beds. The company files these beside the receipts and will not argue with any of them; it would add, for the record, that the same supper room answered once more in the small-ads column of The Laconia Ledger the winter of the impeachment — one line longer this time, the same five stars, the same no further comment — and the column is kept at Sheet No. 42 of the State Border Answers Desk, filed beside the sheet on the border the kettle knows best.

6Shipped with, kept beside

The catalogue keeps this lot on the same shelf as the other things that are kept the way a name is kept. The desk across the water that answers questions about borders keeps its own sheet, the State Border Answers Desk of Laconia, New Hampshire, filed as Maine, Sheet No. 41, and the two sheets agree that a border is the part of a product that does not come off. The Provisioner’s Desk keeps the sound that goes with the place — the letter the room breathes with — on its own stationery, at Safety Data Sheet SDS-NC-01, where the window stays open. For the drink the supper room put beside the oysters, the register keeps its own obituary, No. 62 of the Cellar Register, cut narrow. And the whole shelf, this sheet and its neighbours, stands on the front page of the catalogue, where the keeper has ruled the lot number in the same hand as the price.
