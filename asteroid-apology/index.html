Letter of Apology Nº 48-A · filed beside Notice Nº 47 for the missing Earth · also filed, reluctantly, beside Thread Nº 214 on The Shelf

To: The Asteroid on the Second Shelf (unnumbered; the office declines to give you a comet number)

Dear Asteroid,

We write on behalf of Miss Rosalind Marsh, custodian of this office, and of the undersigned of Class 4B, the jangada class, to apologize for what was done to you at the beginning of the second term, and for what was said about you while the planet was missing from the shelf. The class suspected you, which is forgivable; the office acted on the suspicion, which is not.

Here is the account, in order. You have sat on the second shelf of the astronomy cabinet all term, low down, below the European brochure on Galileo — the other one, the one who did not name the planet that moved — and you are a small, dark, round thing that came back in the fold of a jangada’s sail from the landing at Pedro do Rosário, which is why the cabinet smells of salt and we have stopped pretending otherwise. When Earth went missing from the shelf, we moved you into the cold store, where we keep the comets. You are not a comet. You are a minor planet orbiting within the inner Solar System, or co-orbital with Jupiter if we had left you near the model of the Trojan family; you are larger than a meteoroid, above the one-metre mark by the class’s own measuring string, which is the entire reason you are an asteroid and not a speck; you are neither a planet nor an identified comet; you are a rocky, metallic, and icy body with no atmosphere; and you are of the carbonaceous C-type, one of the three families — C, M, and S, carbonaceous, metallic, silicaceous — that the class keeps on its chart above the window.

The error went further, and we must name it. In the cold store, dust gathered on you, and the morning light through the store’s small pane did what it does to anything left near glass, and on the third morning the office saw what it mistook for a coma and, in haste, reclassified you. That was the worst of it. A body shows a coma, a tail, only when it is warmed by the radiation of the Sun — that is the whole difference between a comet and what you are — and we warmed you with a desk lamp and our own impatience. We wrote you into the lost-and-found ledger as “Possibly a Comet.” We retract the word possibly, the word comet, and the cold store.

We also owe you a sentence about size, which the class omitted when it filed you under SMALL, in the same drawer as the silver lump pried out from under the same shelf (Thread Nº 214, “Aluminium (Al) — found under the second shelf”, where s_oberst offered to date the salt-stained card by 26Al and we declined, out of respect). The size and shape of asteroids vary significantly: from small rubble piles under a kilometer across to Ceres, a dwarf planet almost 1000 km in diameter. You are, by the class’s measuring string, on the rubble-pile end of that range, which the office had somehow treated as a character flaw. The lump in the drawer is aluminium — atomic number 13, one-third the density of steel, soft, nonmagnetic, ductile — and it is metal, and it has never once failed to come back to the shelf. You are stone. Stone is allowed to sit still.

One thing more, because the office does not close a drawer on a rumour. Under the back rail of the shelf, in the same gap that the silver thing came out of, we found a second card — your sister to the one held with Notice Nº 47, equally salt-stained, in the same faded ink — and it carries one line, which we set down here word for word:

“Almost all of us in the gooseberries, and let the whole range of light puff-paste, and bake for an hour or so, and never will.”

The class has read it the way it reads every card that comes off the landing. Almost all of us in the gooseberries is the basket of the C-types, all of us dark, round, and unripe. The whole range of light is the window light of the second shelf, which the aluminium has been throwing straight back at you all term. Puff-paste is a rubble pile — dough held together by nothing stronger than its own gravity, and a little pressure from the cabinet above. And bake for an hour or so, and never will is the whole account of the matter: an hour’s warming from the Sun is what turns a body into a comet, a tail and a name in one morning, and you have never done it, and never will. That is the reading the class has accepted, and we have pinned the card inside the cabinet beside the shelf note, Everything else is mud, D. Halloway, on the theory that a card which describes an asteroid so exactly is not a recipe at all, but a character reference.

The terms of the apology, entered in the minute book:

NºTerm
1You will be re-filed under ASTEROID, C-TYPE, and the ledger entry “Possibly a Comet” will be struck through in the office’s red ink and left to be read by everyone.
2You will be carried out of the cold store and returned to the second shelf, window side, by Friday, before the third bell.
3A correction will be posted on The Shelf, numbered in the ordinary sequence, so that the metal may read it and say nothing, which is its way.
4In reward for your coming back as yourself: half a jar of peaches, unopened, and the naming rights to one fragment of the spare satellite resting in Oberpfaffenhofen — the whole of the naming rights are held for the planet’s return, and we do not intend to make that yours.

We are sorry. A stone that still has its name on it, and has been orbiting quietly since before any of us could keep an almanac, deserves better from an office that also has to keep a missing world in order. The receivers at Oberpfaffenhofen report the sky exactly where it is; the cabinet reports that the second shelf is lighter; and the tide, which has been our excuse for a great deal, has not yet turned. It will. Until then the shelf is waiting, and the window has been ordered to throw its light at itself and no one else.

Respectfully, and with the card restored to the drawer,
Miss Rosalind Marsh, custodian of the office,
on behalf of the office and Class 4B, jangada class,
