Shed Ledger · Entry 57 · The Dales Shelf

The Darrowby Van

Build log — one veterinary practice, one card file, one long road, at 1:48

Entry 1 — The Photograph in the Skip Lot

Wednesday, 8 January · 19:30 · kettle on, bench clear

It came in a parcel of trade ephemera from a bookbinder's skip in Skipton: a cabinet card, unbacked, of a van. Not a delivery van, not a lorry — a 1949 Daimler body with a hand-painted white panel reading DARROWBY VETERINARY SURGERY · SKELDALE & DISTRICT, standing in a yard behind a surgery whose window glowed. On the back, in a pen that pressed hard: "J.A.W., D.V.M., the year the card file went into the drawer. 1949."

J.A.W. is the whole pitch, the way eight pinnacles were the whole pitch of the last build. James Alfred Wight — 3 October 1916, Sunderland, to 23 February 1995 — better known to anyone who has ever read a sentence about a horse than to anyone who has ever met him, as James Herriot. He left Sunderland for Glasgow Veterinary College, graduated 1939 into a war, and came back north to become a veterinary surgeon in Yorkshire, where he practised for almost fifty years. Fifty years. The van in the photograph was his trade for the middle of those years: the card file in the drawer, the drawer in the surgery, the surgery on the road.

I am building the van at 1:48, from scratch, in basswood and card, because the photograph is 64 mm wide and it will be 312 mm long on the bench, and because a card file of 1949 is not a model part in any catalogue in the world.

Entry 2 — The Body in Forty-Faceted Plank

Monday, 20 January · 20:05 · the 19:40 programme on low

The body is 312 mm long, 62 mm high over the chassis line, 54 mm wide. The Daimler shape is all in the shoulder — the cab wall steps up four millimetres over the bonnet, and if that step is soft the van reads as a biscuit tin. I cut the shoulder from 1.5 mm basswood, sanded the step true on the block, and dry-fitted the cab for three evenings before a drop of glue. The bonnet gets two grilles and a single badge, 4 mm, turned from a scrap of brass. The nearside door is the loading door; the paint panel sits over it and over the window line, dead centre, the way the lettering sat dead centre on the real thing.

Parts list so far: 1 body, 2 cab doors, 1 loading door, 1 paint panel, 2 grilles, 4 wheels (28 mm tyres from an old model kit, sanded and re-treaded with a burnt cotton thread), 1 card file — which is not a part, really, and which I will get to. The wheels turn. They should. It is the one part of the build that was ever on the move, and I will not put it on the shelf with them locked.

Entry 3 — The Card File

Saturday, 1 February · 10:15 · frost on the skylight

The card file is 41 mm long, 19 mm high, 12 mm deep, sprung on a wire that came off a broken book hinge. It holds ninety-one cards, each 24 mm by 13 mm, cut from 0.3 mm card and edge-painted in the red the old Darrowby file carried. I printed the entries at 1.1 pt, which reads under the loupe as handwriting at arm's length: "Hollins, Mrs. — terrier, Bess. Worms. Done."  "Mr. F. Oates — two Shorthorns, the mottled one. Calf fever. Watched."  "The pony at Thornfield. Not dead, not alive. Went back at four."

The last card in the file is the one from the photograph's drawer, and it is not an animal at all. It is a ledger slip, the kind of thing a practice kept for the neighbours who paid the way the Dales paid, in kind and over time: "It was cold and the file as a matter of credit." I have checked the sentence against the slip three times; I am not the kind of person who adds words to a ledger, and I did not add words to that one. The meaning, once I sat with it, is the meaning of a whole practice: it was a cold day, the road was a cold road, and the day's last account — the file itself, the whole drawer of who owed what — was closed not in cash but as a matter of credit. The patient went home; the ledger took it on trust; the trust held. That is why the file went into the drawer, and that is why the photograph was taken of a van standing still, because the trust held for a while and the van could stop.

Entry 4 — Weathering the Dales

Friday, 14 February · 15:40 · the shortest light of the year, almost

Two weeks of weathering for a van that spent a quarter-century on Dales roads. Rust wash in the wheel arches, umber under the loading door where the hay and the chaff worked up, a wipe-back of cold grey over the white panel so the paint reads as 1949 white and not new white. The card file gets the worst of it: every card edge darkened to a soft brown, the last card — the ledger slip — darkened to a deeper brown, because the last card in a drawer is always the one the drawer holds longest.

The base is a slab of pale slate, 420 mm square: a yard, a strip of lane, a hedge, and the field going off the edge unfinished, the way the field behind the Darrowby surgery went off the photograph and did not come back. On the base, standing in the lane at the yard gate, is a Dales pony, 33 mm tall, carved from a single offcut, the one part of the whole build I carved by eye and do not intend to correct. It is the pony at Thornfield. It is not dead and it is not alive. It is waiting to be gone back to at four.

Entry 5 — The Drawer and the Sixty Million

Sunday, 4 March · 09:00 · flat light, the good kind

The file sits open in the cab, on the seat, at an angle that the loupe forgives and the eye forgives at arm's length. The drawer, from the photograph, is modelled beside the van on the base — an open drawer with ninety-one cards fanned out of it — and the whole bench is what the photograph was: a van standing still while the trust held.

Wight published If Only They Could Talk in 1970, at fifty-three, and wrote seven sequels about those same years of Dales practice — the animals, the owners, the weather, the card file — and the books have since sold some sixty million copies, and the films and the television series carried the name around the world so that now you can say James Herriot in eleven countries and be understood. Sixty million copies of a card file. The file I made is ninety-one cards; I have counted them eleven times, and I have counted them nine times right the second time around, which is a Dales kind of thing. The build is complete pending the frost going off the lane.

LEDGER ENTRY 57 — FILED & CREDITED

From the same shelf

The last build that got me into files and drawers was the St Adwen's Church build log — eight pinnacles, one gate ledger of 218 pages, one warden who lets you photograph things — and this van owes it the habit of copying a ledger line and leaving it exactly. The church's gate log held the field for 113 years; the Darrowby card file held the yard for the middle of a life, and both of them kept their rules the same way: write it down, and do not erase it.

For the ground the van actually ran on — the rivers, the water, the town that sits where the Holme meets the Calder — the shelf keeps the Huddersfield field manual, Chapter 9, which is West Yorkshire rather than the Dales proper but reads, chapter by chapter, like the same country with the hills turned up. The lane on the base is cut to that chapter's proportions, and the hedge is its hedge.
