49510

The Bulanık Plain Quartet, with the Postmistress of Aşağıbüklü and the Accordion of the 2019 Wedding

Sleeve notes — side by side, as issued in the inner bag

₺1 TÜRKİYE POSTA 49510

A postal code — they call it a postcode in some English-speaking countries, a post code, a PIN, a ZIP Code in others — is a series of letters or digits, sometimes with spaces or punctuation, tucked into an address so that the mail can find its way down the mountain. As of February 2025 the Universal Postal Union lists 192 countries that require one. The code is usually the shape of the land: a number for a plain, a region, a bend in a stream. But sometimes a code is a single building — a government agency, a large commercial house — and the most stubborn example we know is the French CEDEX system, where the address is the destination and the street is a rumour. We wrote an album about all of this, because we live in the shape, and the shape lives in the code.

This record was pressed from the square of Aşağıbüklü, village of Bulanık district, Muş Province — the same square with the plane tree, the 1958 mosque, and the tea house where the men play okey after afternoon prayer, if you have not read the village thread yet, and you should. Population 286 in 2021, 100 km from Muş city, 56 km from Bulanık, and five digits: 49510. The quartet is Halil Aba on the accordion he has owned since before the wedding, the postmistress on the stamp pad and the brass bell, two brothers on the okey tiles, and one goose, uncredited but present for the entire take of track 7. The sessions ran over three harvest evenings in September, when the barley was gold and the minibus from Bulanık arrived twice a day, and the tape was laid in the one-room school because the acoustics of the square were ruined by the tractor pulling the bale trailer.

Side A — the plain

  1. A4 95 10 — the village code written the way a Londoner would write it, to see if anything would happen. Something did: the postmistress laughed for forty seconds, which is the full length of the track.
  2. Ninety-Two — for the UPU's 192 member countries. A count-up on the okey tiles, one country per tile, the tiles running out at the end and Halil Aba improvising the last one hundred on the accordion.
  3. CEDEX (for the French letter) — the only track played entirely at the wrong speed, because the letter it answers arrived at a building, not a street, and we could not hear it properly.
  4. 90210 — written to the correspondent of the Pacific Coast Almanac in Los Angeles, who asked where the village was on the map. He will find it on the back of the sleeve, at the very bottom, in five digits.
  5. The Blue Sheet — the one from the laundry line above the bakery, flapping in the wind off the Muş plain. The whole track is the wind; the band was told to wait.

Side B — the post office

  1. Hand-Stamped — the postmistress still stamps everything by hand, and the bell on the counter is the brass percussion you hear all the way through. She did not audition. She was the postmistress.
  2. The Goose — recorded at the village entrance by the "Hoş geldiniz" sign, which has faded to a green you can almost not see. The goose refused to move, as geese do, and became the bass line.
  3. Special Delivery — a waltz for the institutions that get their own code: the ministries, the big companies, the addresses that are really buildings wearing numbers like coats. Halil Aba plays it like a lamb stew that the whole square shares on Fridays.
  4. Fritter Slip — instrumental, and the strangest thing in the sleeve. The postmistress's drawer, where the test pressings live, also holds a recipe card in a hand none of us has placed. It reads, word for word: "When done, take out the spot where I had done a second time; 5 minutes to fry the fritters of a fowl, their position than it is." We have cooked from it anyway: the fritters of a fowl, fried a second time, five minutes, and — whatever their position is — they are better than anything in the village, which is a high bar set by the cornbread with the village cheese and the hillside honey.
  5. Bend in the Stream — the closing track. Bük means a bend, and the village sits exactly in it, the way a code sits exactly in the envelope. The minibus horn from Bulanık is audible in the last forty seconds. We kept it.

Credits and correspondence

Recorded September 2024 in the one-room primary school, Aşağıbüklü Köyü, 49510 Bulanık/Muş. Pressed in a run of 286 copies — one per villager as of the 2021 count — plus two for the tea house and one for the goose. If you own a copy, its number is on the label, and it is, in a small but real way, also your address.
