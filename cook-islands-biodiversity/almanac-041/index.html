First observation — the combined column

Station: Avarua desk, Kairara Field Press store, western rim, Rarotonga · Date: 14 February 2011 · Hour: 06:10 local, the low of the low tides · Sky: thin cloud over the eastern mountain, lagoon flat, good for a long record · Temperature: 29.4°C · Wind: SSW, 9 kt, steady · Barometer: 1012 hPa, holding · Sea: 0.5 m · Visibility: 18 nmi to the Aroa reef

The Avarua desk keeps one combined column for weather and life, on the theory that they are the same instrument read twice — the water tells the barometer what it is doing, and the water tells the field record what it is holding. On this morning, against the figures above, the season book asks its standing question: how much life is there, today, where it can be counted? The record that answers is biodiversity itself — the variability of life on Earth — measured, in the book's four ledgers, as genetic variability, species diversity, ecosystem diversity, and phylogenetic diversity. The database now stands at 23,418 records across 4,812 species and varieties, lagoon, reef, mangrove and upland, each with the field name of the collector, the date, the islet or the reef, and, where the original notebook allowed, the collector's own wording — struck through only by time, never by editors, under the no-eraser clause the data stewards at the Bishop Museum in Honolulu enforce and the weavers at the Avarua cooperative have made into the rule of the mat.

Passage I — the gradient runs through us

Ledger: the latitudinal gradient · Position of station: 21°S, out of the tropics by the book's own reckoning · Trend: diversity thickening toward the equator

The reading, as every year, is out of place with the book, and the almanac says so in the same line as the pressure. Diversity is not distributed evenly on Earth; it thickens toward the equator, where the warm climate and the high primary productivity of the region near the equator do their slow work, and there are latitudinal gradients in species diversity for both marine and terrestrial taxa. Tropical forest ecosystems cover less than one-fifth of Earth's terrestrial area and contain about 50% of the world's species. Rarotonga sits twenty-one degrees south of all that and is out-counted by the tropics in every ledger the book keeps. The gradient does not bend for the Cook Islands; it runs through them — from the marine shelves of the north group, three days' sea in the Tongareva Runner, down to the upland gardens of Rarotonga and the inland scarp of Atiu — and the almanac records the decline rather than apologises for it. What the station holds is what the shelf holds: the Cocos nucifera groves of Aitutaki, the frigate birds over the Aroa reef, the black-scorpion-tailed skink of Atiu's inland scarp, the 312 recorded land snails of the mangrove flats, and the complete non-native inventory — every introduced fish, every garden plant, and the one notorious rat lineage — catalogued side by side with the native flora, so that the record shows the whole, not just the catalogue of loss.

Passage II — the five great drops

Ledger: the sudden drops · Count since the record opened: five major events, several minor · Shape in the column: gaps

Since life began on Earth, five major mass extinctions and several minor events have led to large and sudden drops in the count the season book keeps. Each of them leaves exactly the kind of gap a no-eraser clause can point to — an interval in the column where the words stop, and the almanac will not write a word over them. The drops are priced where they come down, the way the keeper of the desk prices everything: at the landing, not at the peak. The record since the last landing is this morning's — 23,418 lines, none of them struck, and in the same spirit the column keeps, uncorrected, the entry everyone asks about: Felix Nori's notes claim he crossed Arkansas in the thirty-fourth minute, and the record stands exactly as written, because a database of this kind is also a memory, and memories are not edited in public.

This entry is the checksum day of the 2011 rebuild: the Natural Heritage Trust's rebuild, the Bishop Museum's hosting, the case sealed, the weaver's signature on the sleeve undisturbed, the difference between the box and the live site always zero records. The steward's advice for the reader stands, and the almanac prices it at low tide: query the lagoon records at low tide, when the data and the water agree about what is there.

Closure and cross-reference

The almanac closes this entry with the sky over the eastern mountain thinned to a seam, the barometer holding at a thousand and twelve, the frigate birds over the Aroa reef working circle by circle at first light — the same morning the Lagoon Line visitor's brochure sets out as its Day 2, the reef by boat, lunch at Muri where the sand is pale and the shade is rented by the chair. The box this entry is the checksum of ships from the Kairara Field Press store beside this desk, in its Raranga Edition No. 1, wrapped in a second raranga panel and a sheet of banana leaf, and the routes, fares and low-tide query windows for anyone carrying it on the water are the brochure's own, Avarua to Aitutaki at 08:10, the reef treated as a departure point rather than a destination. The season book's whole shelf, and the front of it, is filed at the front page of the press. Weather is only ever as good as its record; this one was read at the low of the low tides, priced at the landing, and is written down.
