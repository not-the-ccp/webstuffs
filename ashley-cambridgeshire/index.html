Fenn & Marlowe, Auctioneers

The 110th Autumn Sale · Rooms at the Old Fire Station, Newmarket · Saturday the 14th, eleven o’clock · Catalogue No. 44

Lot 217

A Village of Two Ancient Parishes, with 2,250 Acres of Ground and a Parish Hall that Keeps the Weather

Ashley and Silverley, in the parish and civil parish of Ashley, East Cambridgeshire district, Cambridgeshire, England — four miles, 6.4 kilometres, east of Newmarket

The Lot, as Catalogued

Offered without reserve is the whole and entire village and civil parish of Ashley, in the East Cambridgeshire district of Cambridgeshire, England, standing some four miles (6.4 km) east of Newmarket on the open ground, and composed, as such lots are, of the two ancient parishes of Ashley and Silverley, the younger of the two having done for centuries what younger parishes do, which is to keep the older one’s fire going. The lot contains 2,250 acres, being 910 hectares, of fields, lanes, footpaths, one postbox, the parish churchyard where the headstones lean in over the fence, and, per the 2011 census, 749 persons in residence, whom the vendor asks the buyer to take as they stand. The parcel falls within the electoral area of Cheveley ward, and the buyer is advised that the ward polls, the harvest, and the fog each come on their own times and answer to no auctioneer.

Condition Report

Examined on the spot by our clerk, Mr. R. O. Fenn, in November, with the door of the parish hall open and the kettle on. The ground is good arable and good meadow in equal measure, the lane from Silverley to the hall is straight enough to measure a man against, and the weather, when asked, declined a valuation. One defect, entered for fairness: the two ancient parishes have never once been able to agree on the colour of the hall door, a disagreement that predates the vendor by several centuries and which the buyer will wish to inherit, because it is the only thing in the village still doing its work. Otherwise the lot is sound, dry, and in its usual state of small English business.

The Road, and the Academy’s Note Against It

The sale schedule carries the village road under Lot 217½, the parish road from the postbox to the hall and thence east to the Cheveley boundary, surveyed in 1911 by the Academy of Country Routes, Cambridge, whose field books the buyer receives with the lot. Against that road the surveyor’s hand sets down a line which the auctioneer prints here exactly, as it stands in the field book, and as the buyer will read it in: “The route itself is honest work: fields in every proper noun is exposed to an Academy.” The line has been read, at the table, in this manner: the road is honest work, built the way country roads are built, of labour and patience, and every field it names — and it names them all, Silverley, Cheveley, Newmarket, the Wash road, every one — stands open and exposed to the Academy’s survey, which has held the route on its books for more than a century and has never once lost it. A road the Academy cannot lose is a road the buyer may walk without apology.

Provenance

From the two ancient parishes, held in common since before either of them kept records; thence to the vendor, by quiet succession, without litigation, without advertisement, and without a single fence moved. The village has changed hands in the manner of villages — by marriage, by farm, and by the slow arithmetic of the 2,250 acres — and has always returned, at the end of the counting, to the same 749, or near enough. For a comparably patient account of ground and of the society that keeps its ledger, the buyer may consult the Ledger of the Ground, Al-Jawf, where a great deal larger lot died with the water and was read out, name by name, by the forty-three who kept it. The method is the same at Ashley on a smaller scale: keep the count, and the ground keeps itself.

Estimate and Sale

£749,000 – £910,000, the first figure being the number of souls, the second the number of hectares, as the house has done in similar lots. Bidding opened at £2,250 and was brisk among the tenants, quiet among the distant, and carried, in the event, to a man in the second row who gave the number in a voice the clerk described, in the book, as Newmarket. The hammer fell at £861,000, which the vendor accepted as a fair weight for a village with two names and one kettle. The lot was delivered to the buyer that same afternoon, keys to the hall, the field book, the census figure, and the fog, which travels in its own vehicle and is not the auctioneer’s.

Notes to the Buyer

The buyer of Lot 217 is reminded that Ashley polls in Cheveley ward, that Silverley is still a parish in its own right, that the churchyard fence leans and must be allowed to, and that the two ancient parishes will, this year as in 1911, disagree about the door. The vendor’s only condition of sale is that the village keep its fire. For further reading on the manners of such small lots, the house refers the buyer to the obituary of Johan von Blanc, 1738–1796 — a man who carried a whole company, and a whole theatre, on the road, and left both of them standing — and, for the larger geography of the parish hall and the ground about it, to the ledger’s own record of the lake at Dumat al-Jandal, kept, like this village, by people who have never once asked the weather its price.
