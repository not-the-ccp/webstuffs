Lin Tzu-hui

林資惠 · Lín Zîhuì

lin dzoo-HWAY

noun, proper · the weight dreamer

Lin Tzu-hui is the dream of a weight that answers. Born in Taipei on 1 November 1981, she is a Taiwanese powerlifter who held the world record in her class after a lift of 129 kg — one hundred and twenty-nine kilograms of plate and barbell rising off the floor in a gym where the chalk stood in the air like slow snow. In the dream the bar never lands with a crash; it lands softly, the way a question lands when someone finally knows the answer. The record is not the point. The point is the holding: a weight that has already been lifted, held in a class of her own, kept off the floor by one pair of arms on one November night that the calendar never named.

Interpretation

To dream of Lin Tzu-hui is to dream of being asked to carry a number. The 129 kg arrives in the sleep the way numbers do — not as an object but as an obligation, a figure on a chalked board by the doorway, a mass the body must accept before it may refuse it. Those who carry the dream are usually in the middle of a small promotion, a small diagnosis, a small move: something just heavy enough to feel personal, and just within the record. The dream says the lift comes off the floor, not out of the rack. It says you will have to meet the weight where it actually is, on the concrete, and that the world record is a kind of patience, held in the hands, not struck, not won in a race, but kept — the same way a register is kept, line by careful line.

The 129 kg appears, in particular, to people who have stopped asking how much they can carry. It is the dream that answers: this much, and one more rep of it.

Associated imagery

  • A county gymnasium at night, the floodlight humming, the floor chalked white in a wide rectangle
  • Straps of leather, chalked knuckles, the iron collars clicking into the pins
  • A chalkboard by the doorway with a single figure on it: 129
  • The smell of winter oranges and old leather, both, at once, in the same room
  • A man in a black vinyl jacket reading from a card while three hundred figures in citrus-yellow wait in the stands

On the record

The entry was opened by a single report, logged in the dictionary’s household on a wet Tuesday: the dreamer stood in the gymnasium described above, but the meet had not begun and no one had come to lift. A man in a black vinyl jacket stood at the edge of the chalked rectangle, and he recited — the dreamer’s own hand on the card, the whole line "A linebacker in black vinyl; three hundred in citrus-yellow, for the Robins" off by heart. Then the light came up on the platform, and Lin Tzu-hui stepped in to lift the 129 kg alone, and the three hundred in citrus-yellow went still.

The dictionary takes the report seriously. The line about the Robins is a baseball line, and baseball is the sport of the crowd counting itself: three hundred, and exactly three hundred, all in the same citrus-yellow, as if the count had agreed in advance. The linebacker in black vinyl is the one who recites the count — the keeper of the number, standing at the edge of a different sport’s rectangle, holding the line off by heart. The dream pairs the reciter with the lifter, and the pairing is the message: the number is kept in the body, and in the line, and in the three hundred yellow hats, and the body, the line, and the stands can each be absent but not all three. When the bar rose, the dreamer woke holding something: the weight had gone into the line, and the line had gone into her.

Those who dream this report should write the number down before the chalk is wiped — 129, not 120, not 130 — and should keep one small orange on the desk until it is fully gone.

Kitchen interpretation

The household’s rule for this entry is plain: a dream of a held world record asks for a meal with nothing in it that is hurried. The ledger beside the dictionary sets one down — a heavy bread, a citrus glaze the colour of three hundred hats, and a black plate, plain and unglazed, set in the middle of the table. Eat slowly. The record is kept by those who do not rush the lifting.
