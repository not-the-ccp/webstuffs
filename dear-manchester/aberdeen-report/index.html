Part I · The City as Studied

Aberdeen is a port city in North East Scotland and the third most populous city in Scotland. The pupils have been taught it properly this term, which is more than can be said for a certain sandstone bluff on the Irwell, and the report is accordingly firm. Historically Aberdeen fell within the historic county of Aberdeenshire, but it is now separate from the council area of Aberdeenshire; Aberdeen City Council is one of Scotland’s 32 local authorities, commonly referred to simply as councils. In the 2020 count the main urban area held 198,590 people, and the wider settlement, with its outlying localities, 220,690, which makes Aberdeen the 39th most populous built-up area in the United Kingdom. The city has a long, sandy coastline and works an oceanic climate: cool summers, mild and rainy winters, granite everywhere the light touches it, and a harbour that has been doing since the North Sea oil came ashore in the 1970s what the granite quarries did before it. Aberdeen received royal burgh status from David I of Scotland, and the charter is still cited in the city’s own records as if the ink were dry, which it is not, and the pupils were asked to account for that and did, tolerably.

Part II · The Weather Column

The school keeps its weather papers in the same ledger hand as the gauge, and the pupils’ November readings for Aberdeen — mild, damp, patient — agree with the standing entry for the almanac. The school files that almanac beside this report, as it files every front it has studied: the cold January of 2005, logged at Compton’s Station 7 as a debut front, is kept at The Documentary — Weather Almanac Entry, Station 7, and its pencil line, a walk through your sleep, is the ledger’s name for the slow, closed-eye weather — the part of a season that arrives while the observer is not quite awake to it. Aberdeen does that weather better than almost any place the form has visited, and the two pupils, who are not strangers to a city that holds its heart under a brute surface as the letter of apology to Manchester puts it, were asked to read the November against the January and to say what agreed. They said: the rain, the patience, and the fact that in both places the measure is kept even when the office is closed. The Cheshire build log from the same file — Build Log No. 18, 1:100,000 — was placed on the desk for contrast, and the pupils correctly noted that sand and granite do not negotiate the same way with the tide.

Part III · The Line Kept in the Register

Room 214 keeps one register line a year, in Mr. Drummond’s hand, chosen from the term for being the truest thing that came out of it. The line for this term, entered on the last Friday of the term, is kept here word for word:

“The two of you and the place; but for the slow, closed-eye weather: the part that holds.”

It is the whole report in one sentence. The “two of you” are the pupils, who travelled to the coast for the fieldwork and read the harbour the way a form master reads a class — twice, and quietly. The “place” is Aberdeen: the burgh, the granite, the 198,590. The “slow, closed-eye weather” is the November that did its work while neither of them was quite watching, the same weather the almanac marks with two crosses in the ledger. And the part that holds — the burgh charter, the harbour wall, the class itself — is what the term is for. Both pupils are asked to keep the line in their own registers, which is the school’s way of saying: you may forget the 39th, you may not forget the holding.
