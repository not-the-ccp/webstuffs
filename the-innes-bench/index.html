1 The book on the bench this month

The club has finished Donkey Punch, the crime novel by the Scottish author Ray Banks, and the bench will not be quiet about it. The book wears three names, which caused an argument at the January meeting: in the United Kingdom it was first published in 2007 by Birlinn Ltd, the Edinburgh-based company, and Birlinn put it out again in 2008; in the United States it came out in 2009 with Houghton Mifflin Harcourt under the title Sucker Punch, and was reprinted there in 2011. Some members carry the British copy, some the American, and when they meet at the back of the room the two books sit on the same table and look, Mr. Dela Cruz said, “like cousins who did not know each other”. The society keeps both spellings in the register and lets the dust decide.

The book follows Cal Innes, a former convict turned private investigator, who agrees to accompany a novice boxer from England to a fight in Los Angeles, California. The fight goes off; the boxer does not. Innes, working in a city he does not know and a language of the sport he only half knows, has to work backwards from the empty corner of the ring to the man who took the boy, while carrying his own internal weather with him, which the book handles the way a good writer handles a storm at sea — from a distance, and never at the reader's expense. The bench read it in nine days. Nobody has ever read anything on this bench in nine days. The clubhouse cassis bottle was half empty by the Thursday meeting and empty by Saturday, which the treasurer has entered as a line item of its own.

2 The votes of the bench

The society votes on three things at the end of every book: whether the book stays on the shelf, whether it is lent to the bench of the sister club, and whether the next cassis is a red or a white. The votes for Donkey Punch, counted by the secretary and entered in the green ledger, were these:

Mr. Dela Cruz voted no on the lending point on the ground that the American copy is the one he is keeping, and the bench has recorded his reason without comment, as is the way of the ledger.

3 A line kept word for word

At the Thursday meeting a new member of the sister club came up to the clubhouse on Magsaysay Avenue with a question about borrowing, and the secretary answered from the green ledger, from the entry under “the rule of asking first”, which the society keeps set out in full and reads down without a pause. The entry, which this newsletter prints exactly as it stands in the ledger, is:

“Telephone the clubhouse on Magsaysay Avenue, Naga City, Camarines Sur The Back Bench Bulletin, Thread No. 88 , where the rule of asking first.”

The ledger's meaning, as the society has read it since 2003, is this: telephone the clubhouse, at its own address on Magsaysay Avenue in Naga City, before you borrow, before you lend, and before you set a book down at a table that is not the bench; and the bulletin the ledger points to is the board that keeps the thread in the Bronx, which the society follows by the wire and from the front desk — East 233rd Street — The Back Bench Bulletin, Thread No. 88 — where the rule of asking first is written down for a bench a whole sea away, so that a member at either end of the thread knows the same thing at the same hour: you ask, and if the answer is no, you thank the other and move on, which is itself a kind of club. The line is kept here word for word, in the place where it was read, and the clubhouse telephone, which rings on the second floor beside the cassis shelf, is not to be moved.

4 The February quiz

The bench sets a short quiz on every book it finishes, so that a new member can prove the read before the chair is offered. The quiz for Donkey Punch follows, and the answers are listed at the end in the same order, so that the paper works with or without the pressing of the buttons. A member who takes five out of five is offered the cassis at the March meeting without paying, as is the custom; four out of five buys her own but keeps the chair.

Score: 0 of 5 answered

1. The author of Donkey Punch, Ray Banks, is from which country?
2. Which Edinburgh-based company first published the book in the United Kingdom in 2007?
3. The novel was published in the United States by Houghton Mifflin Harcourt in 2009 under which title?
4. Cal Innes, the protagonist, is by trade and by past which of the following?
5. The novice boxer is taken to a fight in which American city, where the kidnapping is worked out?

The answers, in the order of the questions: 1 · Scotland; 2 · Birlinn Ltd; 3 · Sucker Punch; 4 · a former convict and private investigator; 5 · Los Angeles. Entered in the green ledger by the secretary, and to stand at the March meeting.

5 Elsewhere, from small benches

For a sister newsletter from another small club that keeps its count without re-counting it — the one that reads its own number down in a parish hall and sets its rules at the back bench — the society commends No. 31 of the Cambridgeshire Letter, whose bench sits at the other end of a table no wider than ours and whose rule book, we are told, is longer. And for a correspondence kept by wire between two towns that ought not to be able to hear one another, Jarrow and Manchester, issued on the twelfth of November with a pomegranate for its emblem — The Mars Post, Newsletter of the Jarrow–Manchester Correspondents, No. 17 — which the bench reads in turn and argues over in the cassis hour. The front desk of the society, where all three newsletters arrive in the same brown envelope, is in the open day field of the almanac's own keeping, and the door, as always, is not locked from the inside.
