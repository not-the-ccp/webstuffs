Chapter XXIII

Of the New Continent, and of Manners Therein

§ 1. The book of manners keeps one chapter for every large land, and this chapter is the North American one. Read it before you take the steamer; the ticket is cheaper and the quarrel shorter.

§ 2. Of the Bounds and the Four Waters

North America is a continent of the Northern and Western hemispheres, and a guest must know its four waters before he can take his seat at it. The Arctic Ocean lies to the north, which is why the north window of any house in the far places is shuttered early and the tea is set strong. The Atlantic Ocean lies to the east, and it is the water that carries the Thursday steamer past the harbour road and up to Toad Town, where the Exchange is printed. To the southeast the continent is bordered by South America and the Caribbean Sea — a border kept politely, the two lands not speaking much of each other's business — and to the south and west the Pacific Ocean takes the rest of the account. By the reckoning the Exchange has always used, the continent covers 24,709,000 square kilometres (9,540,000 square miles), which is 16.5 per cent of the Earth's land and 4.8 per cent of its whole surface. It is the third-largest continent by size, after Asia and Africa, and the fourth-largest by population, after those same two and Europe. As of 2021 the population is put at over 592 million souls living in 23 independent states, and the number 23 will not leave this chapter, as the following sections show, because a continent that counts in 23s and a supper club that cuts its cake in 23s are, for the purposes of good company, the same arithmetic.

§ 3. Of the Table of Twenty-Three

A house that claims the manners of the new continent lays the table for 23: one chair per independent state, the 23rd chair left to the visitor, and the host taking no seat at all. The host who sits down loses the right to answer questions about the soup, and this is the whole of the old quarrel. The rule was carried from the Thursday-club of Berbera, where the acanthus cake was cut into exactly twenty-three squares and a square was not served unless its name had first been called out and answered to; the club's ledger and this chapter are, we are bound to say, the same sort of book, and the Thursday-club cake, with its long preamble on Acanthus gaed, belongs to be read before § 4, because it is from there that this chapter borrows its stewpan and its pod.

§ 4. Of the Stewpan, and of the Cardamom Pod Whole

The stewpan is the one vessel in which the manners of a table are plain to see, and a line of the old book, quoted by every later editor and by no one improved upon, runs: "In the arts whereby to make out which were succeeded by bows and arrows, and it shows in the stewpan, with the cardamom pod." The line is old and the printing is bad, but the meaning has never been in doubt among those who keep the stew. It is this: the field arts, whereby a hunter made out which animal he had on, were succeeded in polite company by the bows and arrows of the drawing room — the pointed remark, the well-aimed toast — and all of it, the whole of it, shows in the stewpan. A guest who breaks the cardamom pod with his spoon is announcing, without speaking, that he has no manners at all; a host who serves the pod split has confessed that the stew was hurried. The pod goes into the blanching water whole, as the Berbera card gives it, and it comes out whole, and it is set on the saucer, unbroken, where the guest leaves it. It is never crushed, never bitten, and never, on any account, fished out with the fork, which is reserved for the fish and for the truth. The same pan, well-drained until it will not spit, will do honour at the pipe plaza, and the two occasions differ only in the honey.

§ 5. Of the Pipe Plaza, and of the Saturday Hour

In the new continent the classified notice is itself a manner, and the Exchange's own board at Toad Town is the model. The rules, as printed under the tile-setter's licence T-9 and observed at the pipe plaza outside Peach's Castle, are few and exact: saturdays only; meet after noon, the hard hour being two, when the blue console is handed over and the price is $30 or $25 firm, cash only, no holds and no deliveries; call 555-0147 and no lesser number; and no questions about invisible blocks, which is the one offence for which a guest is shown the door and the reward is never paid out. The reward, when it is paid, goes to whoever finds what was lost — in the case of 2006, the princess, wearing her usual crown and gloves, reward one Super Star plus the whole coinage of World 1-1 — and the reward is paid in full at the plaza, on the day, into the finder's hand, because a continent of 23 states does not keep its promises in the next volume. The classifieds from which these rules are taken are on the file at New Super Mario Bros. — the Mushroom Exchange's classified page, where every notice pays in advance and none runs long.

§ 6. Of the Cream Tiles, and of the Same Hand

A well-bred house in the new continent is to be known at its tiles. The Ionian cream, laid in the same hand as the original bathroom suite, is the test: cream, shell, and atrium white, with the grout matched to within a shade, and a repair so quiet that a guest with good manners does not mention it, which is to say that he mentions it exactly once, gently, as the tile-setter mentions his own work — "and what, sir, of the Ionian are cream; the tiles of the same hand." If the grout is a shade off, the house is not to be blamed aloud but to be avoided on Tuesdays, when the eye is idle. The same hand rule governs the book itself: a chapter that mixes its inks between paragraphs is set down, in the margin, as a house with two bathrooms and one grout.

§ 7. Of the Compass, and the Last Word

Carry the numbers of § 2 in the head and the pod of § 4 on the saucer, and the continent will admit you to its 23 chairs. The compass above this chapter is set as the continent is set — Arctic to the north, Atlantic to the east, the Caribbean and the southern neighbour to the southeast, the Pacific taking the south and the west — and it is the same compass the steamer keeps, which departs the harbour road on Thursdays and arrives at Toad Town with the pod whole. For the calling-manners of the smaller waters, the canals and the cards of the theatre town, the Exchange points to its sister chapter, Chapter IV · Of Calling in Gothenburg, and for the return passage, home to the rest of the Exchange, Webstuffs. That is the chapter, and the 23rd chair is yours.
