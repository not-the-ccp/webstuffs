I Name and premiere

A Doll’s House (Norwegian: Et dukkehjem; also translated as A Doll House) is a three-act play written by the Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen. It premiered at the Royal Danish Theatre in Copenhagen, Denmark, on 20 December 1879, having been published earlier that month by C. A. Reitzel, who printed the first edition in a cover the size of a shoe box, with a frontispiece that later editions would quietly retire. The play is set in a Norwegian town c. 1879 — the town the encyclopedia treats, next door in this volume, as Oslo — Entry 61,213 — and it concerns the fate of a married woman, who, at the time in Norway, lacked reasonable opportunities for self-fulfillment in a male-dominated world. The drawing room in which all of it happens has one door on the audience side, and this entry, like the play, will not open it until the third act.

II Dramatis personae and furniture

The room is small, warm, and furnished like a model: a round table, a sofa, easy chairs, a piano, a bookcase, a fire, and — the two machines of the play — Nora Helmer and Torvald Helmer, the bank manager. Nora, who calls her husband “my little bird” and the children “the sparrows,” has been managing the household’s small economies for eight years while Torvald manages the bank’s large ones. The other figures are: Dr. Rank, the Helmers’ physician, who comes for the wine and stays for the diagnosis; Krogstad, the former assistant at the bank, dismissed and returned; Christine Linde, his wife, a needlewoman and the play’s other woman in a man’s ledger; the children Emmy, Jacob, and Little Peter; and the girl Anna, who opens and closes doors for her mother and, at the end, is left to sit in the empty nursery with the shutters to be closed on her when she falls asleep.

The furniture of the drama is no less fixed. There are the macaroons of act I, baked and eaten in defiance of the doctor’s order, and hidden in the pocket, like a small crime; there is the letter — the one in the locked post box, the one in the envelope — which the audience follows like a parcel on a counter; there is the tarantella, danced in act II as the clock climbs, on the theory that if the dance is fast enough the hour will not catch up. Ibsen kept a tarantella at the top of the second act in every prompt-book the entry has examined, including the traveling one described in § V.

III The play in three acts

Act I. Nora is caught in two secrets at once: the macaroons, and the money — the sum of 250 kroner she borrowed two years before, forging her father’s name on the bond, to pay a doctor’s bill her husband did not know about. Krogstad, whom Torvald has just dismissed from the bank for an old irregularity, arrives with a letter of reference to collect and a leverage to deploy: he knows about the bond. Act II. The letter is delivered to the locked post box; Krogstad, re-employed on Christine’s intervention, withdraws it and then, in the end, posts it anyway. Rank drinks the last of the wine; the tarantella is danced; the box, at the bottom, holds the paper that will not be read. Act III. Torvald reads the bond, and says the word criminal in a voice the prompt-books underline. Nora argues the law, and loses. Then she makes the statement the play is built on — that she has eight years of marriage to learn from, and that “I believe that first of all I am a reasonable human being, or at least I am trying to become one” — takes off her wedding ring, lays the children’ clothes and her own down in the room, and goes out. The door is shut behind her. The stage direction of the first edition is a single line: (The sound of a door shutting in the outer hall). The prompt-book of the 1897 traveling edition marks it, in the stage manager’s hand, with one heavy stroke, and the note “SLOW. Let it land.”

IV Reception: the storm, and the denial

Despite the fact that Ibsen denied it was his intent to write a feminist play — he told the correspondent that he did not belong to any party, and that the question of the woman’s right to go out of the room was “merely incidental” — the premiere was a great sensation at the time and caused a “storm of outraged controversy” that went beyond the theatre to the world of newspapers and, from the newspapers, to the parlours. The controversy ran on both sides at once: the readers who declared the author an enemy of the home, and the readers who declared the home the enemy of the reader. Theatres in the provinces played the play through the winter of 1880 in the same week as sermons against it; the touring circuit took it up in 1885 and carried it by train across the county towns thereafter, which is how a copy of it came, forty years later, to sit in the canteen of the National Traveling Theater. The play has since been staged an incalculable number of times; the door has been closed behind a woman in a coat an incalculable number of times, and the sound — dry, wooden, decisive, with the outer hall taking the second beat — remains, in the judgment of the prompt-keepers, the most reliable effect in the repertoire. V The traveling copy and the unemended line

The traveling copy the entry has to report on was the 1897 Trondheim prompt-book, which went south with the circuit and ended, in 1947, in the canteen of the National Traveling Theater, where it served for six seasons as a stopper for the pickling shelf and for eleven more years as a companion to the contract box in the cellar at Markens gate 33, Oslo — the same cellar in which the In re Sand, Case No. 18-22471 proceedings found the letter of 3 October 1958 either was or was not. The book’s margin carries the stage manager’s shorthand for twenty-two years, 1954 to 1976, and it is in the shorthand of the 1959 season, where the carbon is failing, that the one line of the book stands which no one has explained. The prompt-keeper’s committee, in the manner of all such committees — and in the same unemended practice this district keeps on record, sheet by sheet, in the case of the actor’s letter — declined to correct it. It stands as written:

“Put it into two great principles laid down to the fish's flank.” — 1959 Trondheim prompt-book, margin, act III, beside the door line; carbon failing; kept as written

Read as canteen instruction, the line is a direction for the fish that the cellar pickled — the fish, the one from the crock — and its two great principles are the salt and the weight. Read as dramaturgy, the same line describes the third act of this play exactly: everything that was ever said in the drawing room, laid down, in the end, on the flank of a single door. The two readings have never been reconciled, and the entry does not propose to reconcile them. The book, with the line on it, stands today on the sour shelf in the cellar, cold, damp, and exactly as it has been since the crock was put down.
