Folio 2364 · Chapter LIX

Of the Storming of a Gate on New Year’s Eve

Being the observances observed, or left unobserved, at Quebec, the city of the cliff, on the 31st day of December in the year 1775

I.

Of the Approach, and of Dividing One Army into Two

It is first laid down that a general who has taken a city by negotiation, as Richard Montgomery took Montreal on the 13th of November, 1775, is not thereby released from the observances of winter; and a general who divides his army across a frozen river in December, half of it to walk the south shore and half to take a boat at Sorel, has divided it for purposes of convenience and must thereafter answer for the convenience of both halves. The St. Lawrence was ice at the time and the ice was not to be trusted, which is the book's standing instruction for all ice, in all matters, civil or military, and is the reason the chapter on the goaltender's post, Chapter XI of this same Second Series, opens with the same sentence in smaller type.

Benedict Arnold, who had taken the northern road through the wilderness, arrived at Quebec on the 11th of December with a company of men who had suffered more than any column in the war: three hundred and twenty-three fit at the start of the march, fewer than two hundred at the end, the rest sick, frozen, or left in the snow. He was to wait for Montgomery. Montgomery did not come. The convention of the book is that a party which is kept waiting past the hour agreed upon is entitled to act, and that this entitlement, once exercised, cannot be entered against the actor, though it may be entered against the result.

II.

Of the Scaling of the Cliff, at the Point St. Roche

The city of Quebec sits above its river by a cliff, and the wall above the cliff was broken by a gap of some two hundred feet that the garrison had filled with a fence of poles. Montgomery's plan, formed at the last hour, was that a party of his own men should climb the cliff in the dark, and that Arnold's men should follow them over the gap in the wall and take the town before the alarm could be made. The plan required the dark, the ice, the snow, and the negligence of the picket; it had, by the hour of execution, the first three and not the fourth.

A picket, of a company of militia under Captain Haldimand, watched the water and saw the boat land, and the alarm was struck before the boats had finished landing. What follows is the book's favourite passage in etiquette, because it is conduct with no answer. The party of Montgomery's men went up the cliff through the snow; when they reached the gap they found the wall closed, and some forty of them, under Captain Brown, went up it by the fence with their bayonets fixed, and were met by the fire of the wall. They broke the fence, they passed over the wall, and the whole of them were killed or taken. Captain Brown's men are the chapter the book sets beside the small climbers of Chapter XI, for the same reason: a party of men going up a wall in the dark is a party of men doing the one thing that cannot be done politely, and it is to be met with the utmost respect, which the garrison met it with fire.

III.

Of the Losses, Entering Them in Order of Rank

The losses of the day are entered in the book in order of rank, because the book keeps to the order of rank at all hours. General Richard Montgomery, killed by a bayonet in the belly, is entered first, with the note that he had taken Montreal, had come up the river through ice, and was shot through the head a fortnight earlier at the same action and had lived to be bayoneted anyway. Benedict Arnold is entered second, wounded in the hip by the first ball of the action, which took him from the field and left him to learn of the day by report. Colonel Daniel Morgan is entered third, taken prisoner with the body of his brigade, the prisoners of the day running to more than four hundred men. The whole American loss stood at a little under a hundred killed and the four hundred and odd taken. The garrison of the city — a motley assortment of regulars, militia, Canadians and Indians of the province, under its provincial governor, General Guy Carleton — lost, by the book's count, fewer than a score. This is entered without comment, and the comment the book withholds is that it is the figure that made the day famous in Europe.

A reader of this house who keeps a ledger of her own will find the companion volume for the northern approach of the same winter at the field notes on Ethan Allen, who took Ticonderoga in the spring of the same year with a farmer's company and a farmer's manners, and who is entered in this chapter only because he had been promised his men would be employed here and were not, which the book regards as a breach on the side of the plan, not of the man.

IV.

Of the Register Line, and its Offering, As Found

At the bottom of folio 2364, in a hand that is not the book's and in an ink that the binder has never matched, stands one line, unruled and unattributed, entered against the margin of this chapter by no visible hand of the house. The book's rule, followed in this series without exception, is that a line found in the register is offered as found, and is then made to answer for its own presence. The line reads, word for word:

Recording ran from the apprenticeship that followed, one thing of record 2364 Offered as found.

The book's reading of it, entered above the line and not crossed out, is this: the recording is the register itself — the act of entering the day, the names, the four hundred and the score, which ran on from the apprenticeship of every clerk who took the ledger in turn and set the numbers down as a trade before he had any opinion of them; the one thing of record is the folio number, 2364, which is to say this page, which is to say the day, which is the one thing the winter left that could be offered as found. The number is the page. The page is the offer. The book leaves the line standing, uncorrected, because a line found in the register and offered as found is already in the right place, which is the only place a line of that kind is ever in.

V.

The Rules, Set Down for the Season

  1. A party that is kept waiting past the agreed hour is entitled to act, and the answer for the act is the result, not the actor.
  2. Ice is to be crossed only on days when it is still ice, and the crossing is to be entered in the ledger the same night.
  3. A wall met in the dark is to be met with the utmost respect; the respect of the defender is fire, and this is entered as conduct.
  4. Losses are entered in order of rank, and the small number of the victor is entered without comment.
  5. A line found in the register is offered as found, and a line offered as found is in the right place.

The city held. The river froze in the morning and the river froze in the afternoon, and the army went down to the winter quarters at Sorel, and the book closed the chapter the way it closes all its chapters of this series: with the numbers entered, the line offered, and the table left for whoever comes in off the ice next. The shelf keeps its far entries in the same order it keeps its near ones: the long board to the south, the field sheet on the Union of Myanmar, FM 14-2, which leans on the Andaman Sea the way this chapter leans on the St. Lawrence, and is priced, at the same counter, at one can of peaches — because a gate taken on the ice in the north and a kingdom set to the table in the south are, in the book's arithmetic, the same course.
