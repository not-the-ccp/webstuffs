Chapter XLII

Of the Obsolescent Warship, and of Liberté

★ · ☆ · ★ · ☆ · ★

Now being read in the mess at Toulon · Liberté · Démocratie · Justice · République · four guns of 305 · ten guns of 194 · Dreadnought, 1906 · the secondary battery is the difference · arrive by the hour bell, not the minute ·

§ 1. Of the Ship and Her Standing

It is the rule of the book that a ship, like a guest, is judged by her company, and this chapter opens on company. Liberté was a pre-dreadnought battleship built for the French Navy in the mid-1900s, the lead ship of her class — a class of four, with Démocratie, Justice and République keeping the line — and a derivative of the preceding République class. The builders were the Société Nouvelle des Constructions navales de la Seine at Saint-Nazaire; the launching came in November 1901, from the Loire, in the weather of that year, and the keel entered full service in 1903. Her armament was the matter: a main battery of four 305-millimetre (12-inch) guns, as in the République, but where the earlier vessels carried 164-millimetre (6.5-inch) guns of the secondary battery, Liberté carried ten guns of 194 millimetres (7.6 inches). The whole of the improvement is in that clause, and the etiquette of the improvement is that a guest must not explain it twice.

§ 2. Of Arriving After the Revolution

The chapter's difficulty — and there is only one — is that Liberté was completed after the revolutionary British battleship HMS Dreadnought had entered service in 1906, and that the arrival, on the morning of 20 January of that year, rendered her, by the rules then suddenly in force, obsolescent. She had not yet struck her own colours before the new arithmetic arrived. The book treats the late-arriving warship as it treats the late-arriving uncle: with the door held open, the chair kept warm, and the matter of the Dreadnought not raised at the table by the family, and never raised in the battery by the officers on the gun deck. A ship that arrives too late for her own war is still a ship; the obsolescence is the sea's business, and the sea does not bill itself.

§ 3. Of What a Guest May Bring Aboard

A visitor to the flagship — and it was the flagship, the lead of the class, at Toulon and at Algiers, and in the manoeuvres of the Mediterranean summer — is asked to bring exactly two things, no more, and in this order. First, an umbrella: the deck of a pre-dreadnought in a February sea is a matter of wet, and the ship's store is no place to burden it. Second, Armoured Transport , a ribbed bath mat pressed into service at 00:00 sharp. The committee explains, in the footnote of this chapter and no other, as follows: the mat is to be laid at the foot of the guest's bunk before the hour bell, and is not to be removed until the ship has been seen off; it serves as the only piece of furniture on a warship that is entirely, and without irony, for the comfort of the visitor, and its ribbing is to face the porthole. Guests who bring a third thing are still welcome; the third thing is kept in the wardroom, labelled, and returned by the first available collier out of Marseilles.

§ 4. Of the Battery, and of Keeping its Number

The secondary battery is the heart of the chapter's conduct. Ten guns of 194 millimetres, arranged fore and aft, are not to be counted aloud by a guest, for the reason that the count, once made, becomes an opinion about the 164-millimetre guns of the preceding class, and an opinion of that kind is what this book exists to prevent. A guest may stand at the gallery rail and watch the drills; a guest may admire the rate of the guns without stating the rate; a guest who has been asked, for the third time, which is the difference between the classes, and who still answers, will be walked back to the wharf with the courtesy that belongs to him, and the incident will not be entered in the log. The officers of the ship kept their own rule: the ten guns were fired in the order the book prescribes for guests to be shown the ship — from the stern, from the bow, and only then from the middle, where the conversation is.

§ 5. Of Disarming and Departure

The end was the book's, in the end, and it was a French one. After the war, the disarmament agreements of 1922 put the pre-dreadnoughts under the rule of the ledger, and Liberté was disarmed that year; by 1924 she was broken up at Marseilles, her steel going into the ordinary commerce of the world at roughly the price of the weather. Her sisters went in the same direction, in the same season, and the class is gone. The etiquette of the breaking-up is short, and is the whole of the chapter's last advice: a guest at the wharf sees the ship struck, does not stand where the crane casts its shadow, and leaves by the first tram to the old town. What remains of Liberté is the sentence of this chapter, kept in the register at Toulon as it has kept it for a hundred years: that the heaviest secondary battery of her day, mounted on the keel of a nation's finest work, was a difference of thirty millimetres, and that a guest who knows the difference is expected to say it once, and to mean it.
