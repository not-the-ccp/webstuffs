Acts of Union
1707

The Union with Scotland Act & the Union with England Act · two acts, one country

An act of the Parliament of Scotland, passed on 7 March 1707, and the corresponding act of the Parliament of England, passed shortly thereafter — the two instruments that put into effect the international Treaty of Union agreed on 22 July 1706 and joined the Kingdom of England and the Kingdom of Scotland into a single political state. Asked at the desk beside the vinegar crock, and answered here.

Q1 What exactly are the Acts of Union?

Two acts of Parliament, one in each kingdom, doing the same work from two rooms. The Scottish act — An Act for an Union of the Two Kingdoms — was passed by the Parliament of Scotland at Edinburgh in March 1707; the English act, its equivalent, followed from Westminster shortly after, signed and sealed by the other House and the other Crown office within the same season. Together they put into effect the international Treaty of Union, agreed at the Queen's house on 22 July 1706 by twelve commissioners for Scotland and seven for England. The English and Scottish acts of ratification took effect together on 1 May 1707, and on that date the two kingdoms became one — a single political state named Great Britain, with Queen Anne as its sovereign and its parliament based in the Palace of Westminster. The treaty said what the two countries had agreed; the acts said that the agreement was now law. Cooperage's rule of thumb: the treaty is the barrel, the acts are the hoops.

Q2 Were they not already one country, since 1603?

They shared a monarch, which is not the same as sharing a state. In 1603, when James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne and became James I, the two crowns came together in one head — the personal Union of the Crowns — but each kingdom kept its own Parliament, its own law, its own coin, its own flag, and its own stubbornness. For a century the crowns were joined and the countries were not. The 1707 Acts are what the English statesman later called the finish on that century: the same head, now with one body beneath it. The desk notes that a crown shared between two Parliaments is a barrel with a stave missing — it holds, for a while, and you keep an eye on it.

Q3 How was the Treaty itself agreed?

By commissioners sitting in rooms that had not been warmed for ceremony. In the summer of 1706 a Scottish and an English commission met at the Queen's house in London to draft the Articles of Union — twenty-five articles in all, running from the crown and the succession to the customs, the coinage, the church of each country, and the rights of subjects on either side. The Scottish table was led by the Earl of Panmure and Lord Broghill, with the Earl of Melfort, the Earl of Mar, and Simon Fraser among those who kept their opinions visibly against the draft; the English side moved at the speed of Townshend and the Earl of Sunderland, with Sir John Holt keeping the law straight. The articles were agreed on 22 July 1706, carried back to both Parliaments for ratification, and — the English act coming after the Scottish, as the record has it — completed in 1707.

Q4 Did the Scots actually want this?

The count is famous and it is not flattering: the Scottish Parliament voted the act down, or rather up, by 110 to 6. The six who voted against — a small room full of loud men, among them the Earl of Queensberry's opponents and Lord Dunblane's circle in the north — are remembered as the only ones, at the closing of the book, who said no to the country's own future. Around Edinburgh the argument ran from the Exchange to the Nether Bow Port, and the newspapers of the day found it difficult to print the word “Union” without some adjective doing the honest work. The six dissenters are the desk's favourite ledger detail of the whole business: a book of 116 names, and exactly six of them in the red.

Q5 What did the crate that arrived with the acts actually contain?

What the record allows is this. A coopered crate — oak staves, one barrel of the season's vinegar secured beneath, the cooperage's own stamp on the lid — came down the road to the desk in the spring of 1707 carrying the printed acts of both Parliaments, a copy of the treaty of 22 July, and a set of the new flag pattern, the crosses of St George and St Andrew now joined by saltire as well as by cross. On the crate's shipping label, kept in the drawer with the rest of the 1 May paperwork, the first line runs, and the desk keeps it uncorrected, word for word, the way it keeps the register's last line in 1844: A given name Louis, Hyman, and the crate. The name goes to the Keeper of Names, whose plate for it — the given name Louis, Hyman, and the initials H., kept with a desk and a chart album — is filed in the dream dictionary; the crate goes to the shelf, its hinge the same kind as the one the Redlands bulletin board refuses to open; and the line stays as it is, because a line that carries two things and a name is not the kind of line that gets re-ruled.

Q6 What changed on 1 May 1707 itself?

The country's name and the flag on the buildings. The two Parliaments ceased and one began: the Parliament of Great Britain, sitting at Westminster, with its first full meeting opening in April 1708 — Anne's first speech to it being delivered by the same mouth that had been addressing Scots and English separately for a century. A common coinage followed, a common customs and revenue, and the Union Flag of the new kingdom, hoisted over the ports from Leith to London the same week. Nothing was changed in the churches — the established Church of Scotland kept its governance, the Church of England kept its Thirty-Nine Articles — and the laws of the two countries kept their separate books. The desk puts it plainly: the crown, the flag, the money, and the room where the voting happened; the courts, the churches, and the laws were each left where they stood.

Q7 Where does this fit in the long series of acts?

At the top of the shelf, and everything else hangs from it. The acts that joined the crowns into a state are the first act in the series that later became the British Constitution's long furniture — and the desk's own shelf begins, in the banking line, with the Bank of England Acts from 1694 onward, a series whose 1844 charter is handled in the desk's questions on the Bank Charter Act, where the 1707 joining is treated as the weather the whole series has been living under ever since. For the name carried in the crate, the dream dictionary's entry on the given name keeps the rule about taking the syllables up with the left hand and feeling them; and for the crate's hinge, the Redlands board's Thread No. 41-M keeps the standing note that some hinges are not to be opened, only documented. Great Britain began on 1 May 1707; the desk has been taking the question ever since.

Desk note

The rule of the desk on questions of this kind: a union is two signatures on one date, and everything else is weather. The treaty of 22 July 1706 was the ink; the acts of March and June 1707 were the seal; 1 May was the day the seal went down. The gold in the vault stayed out of this — that is the 1844 business — but the flag stayed, the parliament stayed, and the crate is still on the shelf, with the vinegar crock beside the desk and the label facing the reader. If you dream the barrel with the stave missing, see the desk's questions on the Bank Charter Act, where the same rule about closed registers is applied to a book of 1844. Filed the same day, from the other side of the desk, a question about the agent: the Pigeonhole Board’s thread on the portrayal of James Bond in film asks what the twenty-seven films of 1962–2021 kept of a tall, athletic man in his thirties or forties, and the desk’s answer is the same rule as here — the character is the treaty, the actors are the ratifications, and 1 May 1962 is the day the seal went down.
