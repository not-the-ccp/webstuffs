The Allele: Six Seats, One Locus, and a Jar Older than the Critic

By the paper's food correspondent · dined alone, seat 3 · the 8:10 service · score filed at the ledger, unappealable

✳✳✳✳✳ four of five · the fifth held at the reserve, not met, as at every sale since the thirteenth

There is a back room in the boatmen's canteen at the ferry, behind the ledger and below the window where the cress is washed, and in it stands a counter with six seats and, at its far end, a shelf. On the shelf, between the flour and the silence, is the jar labelled 12·10·62. That shelf is the whole house. The Allele — the name is Marta's, the cook's daughter, and she will tell you it came to her in a dream about a counter and a sequence and was not discussed — serves one service a night, at 8:10, because the brine was dictated at 8:10, and the canteen's rule, inherited and not appealable, is that a house which began at 8:10 does not open at 8:20 on the grounds that the critic is late.

The paper went to dinner on a Tuesday in October, which means it went to dinner during Sale No. CXLII, and the canteen has a view of the canteen, which the family considers a quality and the canteen's own leaflet describes at length: Lot 62, The Secchia Sale, the jar offered unsold by design, its estimate an em-dash, its reserve one second jar the family does not have and does not intend to have. The jar is not on the menu. The jar is the locus. Everything on the counter that night was a variant of it, in the technical sense the house keeps a dictionary for: a version of the sequence of a particular region, differing from the other versions, at the same address, in the same molecule. The sequence, here, is the brine. The address is the shelf. The rest of this review is the dinner.

The Sequence, in Six Courses

Course I · the amuse

The Locus

One small round pickling onion, drawn from the 1962 jar at 8:12 — that is, at the moment the last of the twelve jars was filled, sixty years and a few seconds late — on a square of the walk's linen cloth, with nothing. The sourness is not a flavour the way the canteen's other sours are a flavour; it is an industry, run by a colony the jar has been keeping since October 1962, and it reads on the tongue the way a ledger reads to the clerk's nephew: everything that ever happened in this back room, in order, and nothing twice. The plate returned to the counter cleaner than it came. The house does this to every amuse, and the house means it.

Course II · the pairing

The SNP

Two bowls of the same charlock soup, from the stand above the canteen row, served side by side, and the ticket between them reads only: one position different. In one bowl a pinch of salt is replaced, at a single position, by pepper. The house's note, folded under the second bowl, is the house's whole philosophy of cuisine in three lines: “Alleles can differ at a single position. The diner may not be able to tell. This is not an error. This is most of the soup.” The paper found the pepper. The counter, on a Tuesday in October, is not the kind of room where it announces that. The correct response, which the paper eventually learned, is to finish both bowls and let the house keep the difference, the way it keeps the difference between the lamp and the brine, which it has held since the 1964 case and has not once, to this critic's knowledge, lost.

Course III · the gap

The Indel

The third course is a plate with a strip of it missing. The ticket reads: insertion, 812 bases, held at the chart office, second floor, same drawer as the tide chart. A whole capsicum, which on the paper's first visit had been on the plate, has been deleted from the plate and, the house says, inserted into the freezer above the canteen counter, where it has been doing nothing in particular since 1963 and is recorded by the kitchen as a quality. The canteen knows a thing about sequences and tables: they can carry insertions and deletions of up to several thousand units and still set, still ferment, still come through the night; and a plate can carry a missing strip of capsicum and still be a plate, which is the most it will say and the least it needs to. The gap is eaten around, and the around is enough.

Course IV · the doubling

The Diploid

Every organism that sits at this counter has, at some point in its life, two sets of the sequence — the paper was reminded of this by the room itself, twice, without being rude, in the form of two identical lamps that light the counter from two angles, one of them a copy the family made and has never explained. The course is the cullis, served twice: once as Marta's first grandmother kept it, sour and quiet; once as the second grandmother kept it, sour and not. The diner must eat both, and the phenotypes at the table are two opinions and one appetite, which the house bills as the human form of the same arrangement that lets a jar hold a culture and a shelf hold a jar and a valley hold both without choosing. The paper chose, in the end, to have a third helping of neither, which Marta recorded in the ledger in her own hand as a fifth opinion, and the ledger, which is the canteen's and not the paper's, let her.

Course V · the main

The Quorum

The nasturtium cullis — the nasturtiums from the bottom of the jar, which the condition report has long recorded as performing no function anyone has asked them to perform — is the main course, and it is served only when there are seven at the counter: the six seats and the seventh place, which belongs to the ledger. The house calls this, in its dictionary, the number at which the cells stop counting themselves and start acting together; the ledger's copy of the ledger calls it a meeting. The paper arrived as the seventh. The cullis came out of the back room at 8:41, with the nasturtiums on top, doing at last the one function anyone had asked of them, and the counter went quiet for the length of one serving, which at this house is a unit of time with legal standing, having been the unit in the 1964 case, and being the unit, still, in which the cake at Stop 7 is measured, twenty-two minutes, give or take the crackle.

Course VI · cake nights only

The White Wing

On cake nights — the family counts them, there have been nineteen — the last course is the cake from the tin that came from Aden by way of Suez, grey at the rim, the almonds 140 grams, the bake twenty-two minutes, eaten with black coffee and no plates, and the story is a true one and it ends with a moth at the lamp at ten minutes past eight, under the same condition the Court imposed on the lamp in 1964, which binds this back room as it binds the porch. On the night the paper dined, it was not a cake night, and the dessert was instead one onion, second from the bottom of the jar, which the paper ate standing at the counter, because the paper had used its sitting up, and because the onion was, in the technical sense the house keeps a dictionary for, the same as the first one, at the same address, in the same brine, and because that was not, in the end, a complaint. The family's walk carries this cake from this back room up the hill, eight stops, 4.6 kilometres: The Mustard Walk, to which the paper would direct any reader who has finished this review and wants the daylight half.

What Changes, and What Doesn't

The house is careful, and the ledger is careful, that the paper record this: most variants of the sequence change little or nothing observable in the organism that carries them, and a diner who orders the whole sequence of this counter will leave having been changed, in any observable way, very little at all, which the house offers as the point of the meal and the paper, after two visits, accepts. The kitchen keeps a card in the window for the exceptions, the ones that do change what can be seen: the resistance in the chilies, which have been resistant to the vinegar since 1962 and are, by the canteen's own entry, eating well; the mutations, listed but never served, the way the ledger lists the twelve jars the Court never needed — present, counted, not guessed. The card ends, as the ledger's cards end, with the house rule in full: “The code is counted, not guessed. A number that comes out wrong is scratched out and counted again.” The paper counted. The count, drawn from the 1962 sample and reconciled at the chart office, stands at 1,342 bases and one repeat, and the repeat is the quorum, which the house will not describe again and the paper has agreed not to describe a third time.

One paragraph of this review must go to the culture itself, because the back room would be a curiosity and not a house if it did not. The sourness of the counter is a living population, a colony running in the salt and the sugar between the flour and the silence, and the house's dictionary is precise on what the colony is and on what it is not. The culture is not, however, a closed one. The cells pass small loops of extra sequence from one to the other, and what one cell learns the whole jar learns, which the canteen has long described as gossip at the ferry, and the same is true of the cooks, who take their new lines from the ledger and give lines back to it, so that the brine and the back room are running the same demonstration at two scales and the family's standing view, printed in the leaflet for bidders and read, this time, by a critic, is that the lamp and the brine are the same story, and that one of them won the 1964 case, and that the case is closed, and that the counter opens at 8:10 on the night it is needed, which is every night, and which is the most a house can be asked.
