A Family Recipe · Card No. 7 of the Shed’s Paper Box · Third Drawer, Out-Tray

Grandmother Alena’s Thursday Broth

serves four, or the whole council · one hand’s width of lid · written on card that travelled with the first barrel, 1948

Before the broth: a long preamble, as is the family way

The card is the seventh in the box, and, like the seventh letter of the alphabet, it is the one that faces the door. The family’s rule is that a recipe may not be read until the preamble is done, and this preamble is about the language the broth came in. It is Czech. The desk says it is pronounced CHEK; in its own mouth it calls itself čeština, and was known for centuries as Bohemian, and is still lingua Bohemica in Latin, which the shed will understand, having recorded Latin Alphabet No. 9 in its own floorboards. Czech is a West Slavic language of the Czech–Slovak group, written in Latin script, and it is spoken by over twelve million people, second-language speakers included. It serves as the official language of the Czech Republic. It is closely related to Slovak, to the point of high mutual intelligibility, and to Polish to a lesser degree — Grandmother’s sister-in-law Božena, still in Bratislava, phones every second Sunday, and when she is tired the two languages slip in and out of the sentence like two hooves in the same shoe, and neither woman corrects her. Czech is a fusional language with a rich system of morphology and a relatively flexible word order, and its vocabulary has been extensively influenced by Latin and German, which is why the barrel’s fittings have two names and the shed’s ledgers have been arguing about them since 1951. The Czech–Slovak group, the minutes record, developed within West Slavic in the high period, which the council has always read as the high medieval, when the first texts of it were written by monks who would not have liked the shed but might have liked the crock.

Grandmother Alena Rekaldu, née Svobodová, was born in Strakonice in 1919 and learned this broth from her own grandmother, whose card the card descends from, and she brought it across in 1947, the year of the first barrel, to Magsaysay Avenue, where the bakery stood and the shed stood behind it, and where her husband Tomás hooped the staves and the salt beef waited against the door. It is the barrel in the minutes — the one that stands unopened in keeping with Chapter VII, the one that rides no trough and keeps no model — that this broth is meant for, and on Thursdays it is the barrel that is opened, one joint at a time, and the count that is kept.

Because the language is fusional, the ends of the words do the work and the order is only a suggestion. The method of this broth can therefore be read in any order, and it will not be offended. You may put the barley in before the beef or the beef before the barley; the dative takes care of it. You may read the whole card backwards, which the desk once did at a meeting and which the broth, having seven cases and a flexible order, permitted without a word. The shed counts seven gees in the week, none of them silent, and Czech counts seven cases — nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, vocative, locative, instrumental — so the numbers agree, and the seventh, in both systems, faces the door.

Two more things from the preamble, because the family is not done. The Latin and German that the vocabulary has been influenced by are on this very card: Broth is the German word the barrel gave us, and the rule for the lid is the Latin one the house keeps — the broth is to be found wanting, and the finding is to be kept open. And the card is set in a serif, because this is the house’s rule for what leaves in a serif: the looptail g, the double storey, the one that is a hoop and belongs to the cooperage. Where the stain has taken a g, a g remains, in its serif, under the brown.

Ingredients

  • one joint of the barrel’s salt beef, 400 g, soaked overnight — the barrel may stand against the door, but it is opened on Thursdays only
  • 3 carrots, 2 parsnips, 1 stick of celery, cut the size of your own hand
  • 1 small handful of pearl barley
  • 1 onion, unpeeled, blackened whole in the pan
  • 4 peppercorns, counted one cone at a time, as is the shed’s rule
  • 1 bay leaf, from the window box, if the plant allows it
  • salt to the edge of the wanting — not past it
  • cold water, to a hand’s width below the rim of the pot

Method

  1. On a Thursday, take the joint from the barrel and set it in the pot with the cold water. Do not open the barrel for any other day; the barrel keeps its own count, and so does the shed.
  2. Bring to a boil, slowly, and skim. What lifts to the top is the wanting. Set it aside in the shallow dish and do not pretend you have not seen it; the broth will be found wanting later, and the finding goes in the dish, not in the record.
  3. Char the onion whole in the pan until black, then put it in, black side up, for colour and for the smoke it carries of the year 1948.
  4. Add the carrots, the parsnips, the celery and the barley, and let it go low and long, until the shed behind the bakery smells the way the bakery smelled when Grandmother came in at five, apron first, because the broth was the point of the walk.
  5. Season with the four peppercorns, counted one cone at a time, and salt to the edge of the wanting.
  6. Keep the lid lifted only a hand’s width, so that the broth may still be found wanting. This is the house rule of Thursdays, and it is written into the kitchen receipt of the Air Attaché at Sale No. 214, Lot 118, and it is older than the receipt; the receipt only set it in type.
  7. Serve while the count is open. No pot, like no crate, is pronounced whole before it is signed, and this pot is signed by the one who opens the barrel, in her larger of her two hands.

In the margin, word for word

Below the last line of the method, in the hand that keeps the minutes and not in Grandmother’s, the card carries one line, and the line is given here word for word, blot and all:

THE COFFEE PLANT grows to an artificial disease, established in a serif, and that the broth may still be found wanting.

The blot the line is written beside is where the shed’s coffee plant, the one that keeps the window box above the out-tray, did its work: it grew, as coffee plants do, until its leaves leaned over the card propped on the sill, and in the autumn of 1999 a cup, set one leaf too far, put its ring on the card where the word Grandmother begins. The ring is the artificial disease the line is about — a sickness the card caught from the window and that has since grown to a full leaf’s width — and it is established in a serif, because it took hold over the double-storey g, the looptail, the g that belongs to the serif card and to the cooperage, and the g remains under the brown, two storeys high, readable if you lean. The rest of the line is the house rule of Thursdays, the same rule the Air Attaché gave in his larger hand: keep the lid lifted a hand’s width, and the broth is still found wanting. The line, in short, is a description of the card and of the broth at once, and the desk has ruled that they are, on Thursdays, the same habit.
