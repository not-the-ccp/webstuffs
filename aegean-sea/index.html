Chapter 7 — The Aegean Sea

Aegean Sea · Aegean Sea · elongated embayment of the Mediterranean · between the Balkans and Anatolia · approx. 36°–40° N, 23°–27° E

7.01 Purpose

This chapter gives the officer or naturalist at the helm the working description of the Aegean Sea, the elongated embayment of the Mediterranean lying between Europe and Asia, and the procedure for reading its coasts, its islands, and its soundings. The chapter grows out of the smaller water already in this manual's file, the gulf in its western wall: the field notes from Güllük Gulf, where the same method was tried on a basin of thirteen and a half miles between the Bodrum and Dilek peninsulas. What was a gulf there is a sea here — the same water at a larger measure.

7.02 Situation

The Aegean lies between the Balkans to the west and Anatolia to the east, running in a long finger from the Dardanelles in the north to Crete in the south. It is an embayment of the Mediterranean, not a separate sea; the Mediterranean pours in where the finger bends at the straits. Whoever is holding the chart: the north end is the narrow end. Everything else — the islands, the deep, the open water of the Sea of Crete — opens southward, like a hand opening.

7.03 Measures of the Water

Take the sea's area at some 215,000 km² (83,000 sq mi). That is the figure to carry into any estimate of wind fetch, island spacing, or the number of island groups one may reasonably refuse to count. For comparison of scale, the gulf I paced from the north mole to Iasos at the head of the inlet comes to a little over forty square kilometres — a rounding of this number, in fact, if you round hard enough.

Cross-reference: the method of fixing position against such a water is given in FM-9, Chapter 4 — The Geographic Coordinate System, written up off the Maranhão coast. Latitude and longitude work identically here; only the light changes, and the light here is the worst in your favour for drawing a chart and the best in your favour for living.

7.04 Passages at the North End

In the north the Aegean is connected to the Sea of Marmara, which in turn connects to the Black Sea, by the straits of the Dardanelles and the Bosphorus, respectively. Pass through both and you have left the Mediterranean entirely. The Dardanelles runs between the Gallipoli and Asian coasts, thin as a drawn wire; the Bosphorus runs between Europe and Asia at Istanbul, and ships pass it in the morning grey with both continents in sight at once.

The rule of the straits, noted in the margin of the older chart: current runs out of the Black Sea at the surface and in at the bottom, so that a vessel at anchor in the Dardanelles is, in effect, standing at the seam where two seas change places. Do not argue with the seam.

7.05 The Aegean Islands

The Aegean Islands are located within the sea, and some bound it on its southern periphery, including Crete and Rhodes. Crete closes the sea the way a thumb closes a fist; Rhodes stands at the east end of the southern line like a lighthouse that has decided to grow. The islands divide into several island groups — the North, East and South Sporades, the Cyclades, the Dodecanese, the islands of the east and north coasts — and every group will tell you it is the only one worth naming.

Practically: the groups are not chains so much as scatterings, and the spacing between them is the sea's true measure, not the area on the chart. A day of clean sailing in a light north wind may cover the length of two islands and the silence between them. I have seen the same constancy on the Napa River marsh, where cordgrass holds a mostly-submerged island three feet above the tide (Knight Island — Field Notes ), though there the water boils with small fish.

7.06 The Deep

The sea reaches a maximum depth of 2,639 m (8,658 ft) to the west of Karpathos. That is the sounding to be quoted, and it is taken not in the open central sea but on the western side of the largest Dodecanese island, where the floor drops away under a waterway that looks from the deck like ordinary mid-summer. A lead thrown in ordinary water in that line can return ordinary water for a mile or more and then, without warning, nothing.

For the record of comparisons: at Güllük the water is the colour of olive brine in the shallows and pewter at the entrance, and a lead finds bottom before a conversation can finish. Here, west of Karpathos, the lead finds only the argument that the sea is bigger than the chart.

7.07 Main Subdivisions

The Thracian Sea and the Sea of Crete are the main subdivisions of the Aegean. The Thracian takes the northern half of the finger, up to the Marmara and the straits; the Sea of Crete takes the open southern water, the part with the least land in view and the longest line of horizon. Everything between is named after the nearest island or coast, and the naming gets denser toward the middle, where a dozen small waters crowd between the Cyclades like a row of rooms in a harbour town.

The Thracian side is the working sea for the west-coast gulf already filed in this manual — Güllük Gulf opens straight off it — and the Sea of Crete side is the passage water for anyone running from the Dodecanese to the open Mediterranean. Keep the two subdivisions separate in your head; they behave differently in a blow.

7.08 Practical Notes

Yellow-legged gulls quarter the whole west coast the way they quartered Güllük; expect the same kites-over-a-river-bank display at every harbour from the Marmara to Rhodes. Terns dive over the shoals wherever the water boils with small fish, and the boil is your best reading of the water, better than any instrument you can carry below. At the head of an inlet, keep the ruins in mind: Iasos at the gulf's end, a marble theatre on its promontory, is the Aegean's habit of putting the old city exactly where the water closes in, and you will meet it again on every second island with good stone and good anchorage. The file also keeps a guest record of the sea's later occupant, the stranger from Stagira whose Peripatetic walk at the Lyceum is seated in the household's book of manners at Chapter XIV — Of the Stranger from Stagira, and that chapter holds the standing chart order for the run from the north mole to Iasos.

If you want to know the water's mood before you leave the harbour, do what the naturalist does in the estuary notes of The Wash, where the tide falls six metres and the sea gives up its whole floor to the birds: read the birds, read the boil, and only then read the chart. The chart in this sea is a record of islands; the sea itself is what happens between them.
