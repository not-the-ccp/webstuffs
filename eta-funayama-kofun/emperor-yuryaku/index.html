Yūryaku-tennō (雄略天皇)

21st Emperor of Japan · 417/18–479 · Kofun period

Who this label concerns

According to the traditional order of succession, Yūryaku-tennō was the 21st Emperor of Japan, and he is the first emperor of the line whom archaeology can verify from objects rather than from chronicle alone: inscribed iron, mirrors and horse trappings of the fifth century carry the names of his court, where the Kojiki places only the reign.

The Kojiki gives his reign as beginning on the thirteenth day of the eleventh month of 456 (Heishin) and ending with his death on the seventh day of the eighth month of 479 (Kibi) — twenty-three years across the middle of the Kofun period, when the great mounds were still being built and the court at the Kofun capital received bronze, glass and craftsmen from across the sea.

The court and the plain

Yūryaku's court sat at the Kofun capital, Nara, and the same decades are held in the earth of the Kikuchi Plain, far to the south in Kumamoto. There stands Eta Funayama Kofun (江田船山古墳), the inverted-boat mound at Nagomi, designated with its neighbours Tsukabōzu and Kokuzōtsuka Kofun as a National Historic Site in 1951. Its excavation produced a bronze short sword, arrowheads and kanabō-shaped bracelets cast in the style of the Korean kingdom of Baekje — workshop gifts of exactly the sort the fifth-century court is recorded as receiving — and those bronzes are now designated National Treasures, kept in a case in Tokyo at the National Museum, their catalogue numbers matched to the site's own record card so that the mound and the museum can be read side by side.

A Class 2-C report from Nagomi Junior High walked the mound's loop and filed its numbers — 1951, three names, one museum — and their teacher's margin note, “Careful about the second pass,” is kept in the museum's education file beside this placard. Class 2-C's full report on Eta Funayama Kofun.

The line on the back of the card

Accession card KP.214.456 reached the museum in the 1967 reprint of the handbook with one line printed across its reverse exactly as follows:

"During the period of which funnel nets are placed; along these the butter, and wring it almost seemed as two visible needles in two hours, and serve very hot."

It reads as three records pressed onto one galley: a river-watch note from the Kikuchi plain (funnel nets are placed along the irrigation channels at flood season), a steward's kitchen instruction (wring the curds and serve very hot), and a workroom tally kept in needles and hours. The card was scrap paper before it was an accession card, and the museum keeps the misprint because it is also a record of how the plain's work entered the museum's files — nets, kitchens, needles, and the emperor's reign on the same sheet.
