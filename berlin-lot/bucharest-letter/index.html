The Lamplight Capitals Society — a small club of three cities, founded in the interval between two suppers

The Dâmbovița Letter

No. 41 · Winter, 2026 · Posted from the window-seat, Calea Victoriei, Sector 3

Dear members in Room 6, in the old house beside the canal, and in the six sectors of the new seat —

It is my duty as secretary to announce what you will already have guessed from the postmark: the Society has a third city, and this number is the first posted from it. Our window-seat stands above a corner of Calea Victoriei, a long avenue that walks straight into the heart of Bucharest (Romanian: Bucureşti, [buku’reʂtʲ]), capital and largest city of Romania, standing on the river Dâmbovița in south-eastern Romania. The club is now Berlin, Gothenburg, and Bucharest: the gavel city, the theatre city, and the city that the postmark says Pari, because the old town of Lipscani still wears its painted balconies and its café terraces, and because the Athenaeum on this very avenue was built with gilded ceilings and a dome the size of a promise.

For the record, in the numbers the Society has always kept: the city is officially estimated at 1.71 million residents, with a greater metropolitan area of 2.33 million, which makes Bucharest the fifteenth-largest city in Europe — and we note with pleasure that this places it one whole capital below our friend Lot 81, who counts 3.7 million within the city limits and refuses to divide. The city proper covers 239 km² (92 sq mi), while the metropolitan area covers 1,803 km² (696 sq mi). Administratively, the city proper is the “Municipality of Bucharest” (Municipiul Bucureşti), and it sits at the same administrative level as a national county; it is further subdivided into six sectors, each governed by its own mayor, and I am told the six of them quarrel over the potholes with a regularity that would do credit to the Gemenasian Company. Our window-seat is in Sector 3, because the light comes in better, and because Sector 3 is where the lamp is.

Now the item that the Berlin members will want to hear, because it concerns the lot in Room 6 — the one offered by Voss & Nickel at the Brandenburg Sale, the city in six pieces, which may not be divided. I have sat with our registrar and I report as follows. The third piece of that lot is a granite sett from the barricade at Alexanderplatz, 18 March 1848, still carrying tar and a musket ball in its edge. On the Dâmbovița there is a bridge named for Barbu Vácárescu, and on a February night in the same year 1848, when the news of Paris had arrived by way of Vienna and the students came down from the citadel, that bridge was barricaded in turn, and the trams of 1980 still run over the place where the carts went. Our registrar has applied, in good form, for a sett from that February, and for a pressed flower from the parapet — because of the pencil line. You know the pencil line. It is written on the flyleaf of the Schlossbrücke album, in an unknown hand: The flowers are not permitted on the bridge until any sedan chair. In this city we hold that the rule is older than the pencil, that it was carried into the flower album east from Gothenburg, where the calling book still bids the visitor pause at the bridge for any sedan chair and admits no dogs at all, and that it was posted here, at the head of the Dâmbovița, in a hand so faded it now reads like a house rule of the bridge watch. Three bridges, one rule. The registrar will report at No. 42.

As for the supper, which is the reason the Society exists and the rest is merely the postmark: the lamp above the pantry window was lit on the first night of the term, and on the second night, at precisely ten minutes past eight, a small white moth set itself on the ledger, forewings the colour of flour, hindwings the colour of a candle flame seen through a closed fist. The same moth, I must tell you, has been sitting on that ledger in three countries; Nonna Ersilia’s tin has crossed the sea with us, and the rule stands — the cake is served only when something with white wings has appeared at the lamp first. The ledger keeps one line, copied from a card the size of a postage stamp, and I set it down here exactly as it stands, word for word, as the Society keeps all its last lines:

When cooked in the pantry contiguous to the lamp: Scythris albocanella — White-Wing Cakes .

The Scythris was only ever confirmed in the harbour lamps of Yemen, and I am not the man to press the question; the moth at our window is of a different species and a colder climate, and the cake is, in any event, semolina, almond, cardamom and lemon — 140 grams of blanched almonds, exactly, the weight of a small specimen box. It went to twelve, as it always does, and no one at the table mentioned the moth twice. That, too, is a rule of the Society. For the recipe in full, with the oven temperatures the grandmother’s keeper added by hand, the Society keeps the standing copy at White-Wing Cakes — a family recipe, and for the lamps themselves — the chandelier of oil and wax in the house beside the canal, hung for four hundred seats — the obituary still stands at Johan von Blanc (1738–1796) — Obituary, where the last line, too, survives and works.

Meetings resume in the spring. The window-seat will be held for the gavel city, the box-seat for the theatre city, and the third chair is this one, above the avenue, with the lamp, the ledger, and the river turning in the dark below. Members in the six sectors are invited to send their cobbles, their pressed flowers, and their objections to the poet of the parapet, through the secretary’s hands. The flowers, as ever, are not permitted on the bridge until any sedan chair.

— H. Petrescu, Secretary, on behalf of the three cities
