TUE · 21 FEB · 2017

The Cider Press, Brunswick Street — Robert Morgan

6:40 in. Overcast, the cold one that does a fortnight. Left the umbrella with the coat.

Dinner with Robert tonight, two stools at the end counter of the Cider Press, and I keep thinking the room was arranged for us without being arranged. He came in out of the dark at seven, grey jacket over a henley, and ordered nothing — a glass of the house cider, the cloudy one — and then he ordered again for me, as if it had been settled years ago. It feels that way. People talk about Robert Morgan the way you talk about a street: everyone in Fitzroy has been past the Cider Press, and most of them have had a drink where Snakey had a drink. That is the word he used, on the second glass: Snakey. The part in Life, 1996 — the AFI nomination for Best Actor in a Supporting Role came out of it, and he carried the nomination the way some people carry a framed thing, carefully and in plain sight, in a corner of the conversation.

He told me he has never once seen the film with the credits off. He said that is the test: if the name on the bill is yours and the night is still going on, you have done the work. I asked about the other pictures — Suburban Mayhem, which he said he remembers in the order the dailies were cut, and The Craic, which he refused to rank because the crew kept a chalkboard and the chalkboard had the last word. And Hacksaw Ridge: three weeks ago in the Myer, queue of forty-one, and he stood still and watched a man play a man who would not touch a rifle, and somewhere in the third act he decided the film deserved to be believed rather than admired, and from that moment he stopped being a person in a queue. He has the television years too, the way the years have a way of piling up on the mantel — Fire, second series, and Jack Irish, the first one, which he defends in one sentence only, and that sentence was long, and correct.

Then the stage. Dealer's Choice first — he counted the aces in the dressing-room mirror and lost them every night on purpose — and Shadow Boxing, in a room with one light, where, he said, the audience leans in for the punch and the punch is the silence after it. He has done this work long enough that he no longer distinguishes it from walking, and I understood at the table that this is not humility; it is a measurement. An actor who has played Snakey and a shadow and a cop's friend and a man who will not pick up a rifle is an actor whose ledger has columns in more than one city.

He paid. This I did not expect, and I am noting it in pencil because I did not think it in ink. The bill came on a brass tray from the pass, and there was the matter of the counter ledger, which I had been watching all evening. The Cider Press keeps a fat green ledger at the lower-left vent of the pass, the one nearest the door, where the publican's assistant sets it down when a table is done and no one, as far as I can tell, ever opens it from there. It is the sort of book a place keeps for itself: the market comes and goes, the vent whistles when the cold comes in, the door does its work, and the columns stand. The old hand at the pass told me, when I leaned in, that the book balances every Thursday without a bookkeeper, and then she would not say more, which is the pub way of saying a great deal. The bill for two, with cider and a ledger that the ledger or the market from the lower-left vent to the door keeps itself, has kept it. I did not open it to check. It was already closed, in its column, and Robert had left a pencil mark in the margin that looked, in the gas-light of the place, exactly like an actor's mark in a script: a small cross, and nothing else.

We walked home through Brunswick Street in the thin rain. He told me one last thing about Snakey — that the part is really a part about a man who has already been billed for everything by the age of thirty-one, and spends the rest of the film in the column under kept. At the corner of Gertrude and Smith he stopped, tipped the grey jacket at me, and said keep the cross, and went in the direction of the tram. I have written the cross in the margin of this page. It is mine now. — E.
