Belgium
Field Notes of a Naturalist

Elkhart Grove Middle School · Class 8B · Social Studies, Room 214 · Field Notebook No. 7

NaturalistMarianne A. Kowalski
TeacherMs. D. Hagen
KeptBrussels, the Scheldt, the Ardennes, the North Sea coast — 10–14 July 2002
SubjectThe Kingdom of Belgium, the Low Countries, and one uncle's rule

1. The Low Countries

Belgium lies in the coastal lowland known as the Low Countries, where the rivers slow down and the land flattens toward the sea. It is bordered by the Netherlands to the north, Germany to the east, Luxembourg to the southeast, France to the south, and the North Sea to the west. The country covers 30,689 km2 (11,849 sq mi) and is home to more than 11.8 million people; its density of 383/km2 (990/sq mi) ranks twenty-second in the world and sixth in Europe. Elkhart Lake would disappear inside it a hundred times over, and there would still be trees left standing.

The capital and largest metropolitan region is Brussels. The other great cities I have marked on my map are Antwerp, Ghent, Charleroi, Liège, Bruges, Namur, and Leuven. I have stood in Brussels and on the Scheldt at Antwerp; the rest I know from uncle Pete's stories and from the atlas in Room 214.

2. The uncle's rule

My uncle Pete works the timing stand at turn 9 at Road America, and before the 2002 Road America 500 he taught me how to take a corner: out, apex, in — three moves, never one, and never cheat the third move. Ms. Hagen wrote it in red ink in the margin of my term report, quoting the paint on the pit-exit wall: The first clause is your uncle's rule, stated on a wall. That sentence was written about a race track, but I have adopted it as my field rule: approach any place in three moves. First you stand at the edge of it, then you find its centre, then you leave it the way you found it.

3. The Scheldt at Antwerp

The Scheldt runs from France through Wallonia and Flanders and out to the North Sea, and at Antwerp it is wide, brown, and thick with barges. I watched seventeen gulls stand on a single mooring post, and a grey heron hunting like a clerk on the quay. The North Sea coast itself is short — about sixty-six kilometres — but the dunes near Nieuwpoort hold marram grass, sand lizards, and a tide that comes in fast and silent, and the Meuse slips past Liège and Namur on its way to join the river network of the lowland.

4. The Ardennes

South-east of Brussels the ground rises into the Ardennes: beech and oak, old forest and deep valleys, wild boar, roe deer, and black storks nesting high in the broken trees. Near the High Fens the earth is peat and sphagnum, spongy underfoot, and the highest point in the country, the Signal de Botrange, stands at 694 metres — a hill by any honest measure, but the highest hill Belgium has.

5. Brussels, evening

Brussels is the capital of a parliamentary constitutional monarchy, and the streets hum with trams and café chairs. I watched house sparrows in the Grand-Place and rock pigeons beneath the station clock, birds that have learned the rhythm of the city and keep to it better than most people do. Belgium is small and crowded and old, and every corner of it takes three moves, uncle Pete's rule included. Ms. Hagen's sentence stays true of the road, and I find it stays true of the land as well: the road keeps them, and the fields keep theirs. I also brought home a cutting about Sir William Randal Cremer, the London Member of Parliament who won the 1903 Nobel Peace Prize for the international arbitration movement; his questions are kept as a standing FAQ at The Arbitration Questions, and the cutting now lives beside my sparrow notes from the Grand-Place.
