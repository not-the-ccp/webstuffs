The Barra Ledger · Dining Correspondence · Filed the Fourth Month, Year Three of the Table

A Canhoneira

A Dinner in the Casemate · Forty-One Covers · One Window on the Water · Nº 3 on the Lintel

Reviewed by: M. T. Vaz, dining correspondent · Address: 7 Píer Onze, Barra do Rio · Cover charge: 48 cruzados, the casemate, no additions · Guns: four of five

The house, briefly

The name on the lintel is the name of a ship. Rio de Janeiro was an armored gunboat of the Brazilian Navy — Canhoneira Couraçada Nr. 3, the third of three — built in Brazil during the Paraguayan War in the mid-1860s, designed as a casemate ironclad, commissioned in April 1866. She did not enter combat until September, when she went up the river and bombarded the Paraguayan fortifications at Curuzu. On 2 September she hit two mines and went down so fast the river had time to do very little else, taking fifty-three of her crew with her. The restaurant keeps the name and the number — Nº 3 is cut into the lintel in the same height of letter as the street address — and it keeps the two dates the way a house keeps anniversaries: it is quiet on the first of April, and it does not open on the second of September. That is all the history the menu allows itself, and it is enough.

The room

The dining room is what its name says. A low iron partition — the casemate, painted river-dark, rivets and all, some of the rivets original, which the proprietor will point to and will not number — runs the length of the room at waist height, and the kitchen works behind it in plain view. The window wall, one side of the pier, holds eight of the forty-one tables, all of them single, all of them facing the water, and if a boat is coming in off the pier you will hear the horn before you will hear the floor doorknobs. The other tables are two-tops in iron grey with the linen the color of old chart paper. Service is unhurried in the way that only a room of forty-one covers can be unhurried: nobody is being kept waiting for anything to be made a place for. The lighting is low and brass. The air smells of the river and of one particular onion, which is the whole cuisine in a sentence.

The Minebitter chocolate and charred lemon with a crust of salt; the only course the kitchen apologizes for, once, and only to the window side7

The casemate itself is 48 cruzados and gives you all of it: the soup, two of the plates, and the Mine, which is a bargain I am not going to pretend it is not. I took it. The Armor Plate is the course; the lid comes off and the room goes quiet the way a room does when a thing is opened that is supposed to be whole. The Curuzu broth is darker and sharper than it should be for the middle of a meal, and I want it back at the end of the meal, which is the only correct way to review a course. The Mine is bitter enough to be an apology that means something.

The card at the soup station

Taped to the partition, at the soup station, at the exact height a seated guest can read it, is a card in the hand of the cook, older than the proprietor, older than the partition. It is the room's one fixed instruction, and it has never been rewritten, only read. It says:

“Spoon into forty-one cups, eight of them does well interrupted.”

The kitchen makes sense of it the way the room is built. Forty-one is the count of the covers, and the soup goes out one cup at a time in that order — you can watch the count climb along the window wall, cup by cup, from table one to table forty-one. The eight of them are the eight single window tables facing the water, and does-well-interrupted is the one rule the room does not enforce: at the eight, the spooning stops mid-cup when the horn comes off the pier, and the cup goes out as it stands, the level of the broth exactly where the interruption put it. The cook does not finish the cup. The cook never has finished the cup. A soup served to the water side is a soup that answers to the water, and the room, which keeps fifty-three names on a small black board by the door on the second of September, does not think that is sentiment. It thinks it is the order of the service. I am at table eleven, a window, and my cup went out level at what the horn said, and I am telling you the broth tasted of the pier that afternoon, which I would have written down anyway, because it is true.

Verdict

Four guns of five. A Canhoneira is a small room doing one thing to a count it will not round: forty-one covers, one window wall, one card at the station, one date it does not open. It is the kind of table that keeps its own record — the same habit, at a different scale, that the walking ledgers keep of their counts, as the Hardwood Emporium keeps its in full across the eight stops of the Middle Passage itinerary, and that the storm shelf keeps in foam and dry snow on the bench record of the blizzard of 2003, the PDII build log, where a suspended service is entered as a service. The front shelf of this collection holds the index of the whole house, and this table sits on it, Nº 3 on the lintel, window to the water. The soup goes out in order. Come at the horn.
