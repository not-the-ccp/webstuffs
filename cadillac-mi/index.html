WEXFORD COUNTY CLAM LAKE STATION 1877 · INCORP.

Field Notes of a Naturalist · No. 27 · Volume II, autumn

Cadillac, Michigan

( KAD–æ–lak ) · seat of government of Wexford County, the U.S. state of Michigan

Observer: R. Wadsworth, field naturalist · Station: the shore of Lake Cadillac · Period: 14–18 September · Weather: fair by day, lake fog after dark, frost the last night

Monday 14 — arrival by the two roads

I came down US 131 out of the pines and took M-115 in at the junction, which is to say the town met me on three roads at once — US Highway 131, M-55, and M-115 — and I have never seen a city so frankly defined as the crossing of its own roads. Cadillac is settled as early as 1871 and was formerly the village of Clam Lake, taking its city title in 1877; the name change left the clams where they were and moved the post office. It stands upon the shores of Lake Cadillac, connected by the Clam Lake Canal to Lake Mitchell, and the Clam River, which begins in the town itself, is part of the Muskegon River watershed — the water leaves by way of a river that never had the town's name on it, which I take as a good sign of an honest settlement.

The town is the seat of government of Wexford County. The county office, like the county, is small, warm, and full of ledgers, and I was shown the book in which the town keeps its own counts. I kept mine, as I keep all my counts, in pencil, and I have written the big one here underlined twice, because a count that is not underlined twice is only a guess with a number on it:

10,371 — the population at the 2020 census, making Cadillac the second most-populated city in the Northern Michigan region, after Traverse City, which I have not yet reached and intend to count myself.

Tuesday 15 — the water and its arithmetic

Walked the Canal in the morning. The Clam Lake Canal runs between the two lakes the way a sentence runs between two thoughts — short, and only one of its kind. Water level: 4.2 feet at the gauge, falling a finger overnight. Mussels and clams in the shallows of Lake Cadillac, of which I took no specimens, having learned at a later date that the lake's former village name, Clam Lake, is a legal matter in this county and should be treated with the respect due to a former name that people still answer to. Cormorants: eleven on the piling at the county line, then the count of the day — I counted the windows of the town lit at dusk, 214, and I put it in the book beside the census count, because a naturalist's ledger is one column wide and the column is the city.

Wednesday 16 — the junction, and the middle of a state

I stood at the crossing of the three highways in the rain and let the traffic do the explaining. A city that is the junction of three major roads is a city that has decided its geography out loud; you cannot get lost in Cadillac so much as get reargued. Further north, ten miles up toward Lake Mitchell, at the place where the center of the state is marked on the road, I stopped at the sign that says the geographic center of Michigan is approximately here, and I did the only survey the sign permits: I stood on it, I counted the cars, I wrote down eleven, and I moved on. The middle of a state is not a place; it is a place that has been measured, and there is a difference, and the difference is the size of the crowd.

Thursday 17 — the wreck and the leaf

The lake gave back a hull on the low water. A split wreck, ribs showing, oak gone silver to the heart, half its planking gone to the lake and half still holding the shape of a boat the way a hand holds the shape of a glove. I catalogued it plank by plank over two hours, and the wreck's whole cargo of findable things came to one pewter plate, dented at the rim, lying in the bilge like a coin spent so many times it has given up on being change. I wrote it up, and then I wrote the line that sits under it in my book, which came with a leaf of the Wadsworth kitchen's ledger that my cousin Alice sent north with the ribbon — she pencilled the note on the leaf's margin, in the hand that keeps the counts at the kitchen table, the same hand the bench at Lot 143 — Detroit: A River, a Bridge, and a Ledger has already checked against her own and found matching:

They are very few of which dishes may be served with the split wrecks of ships.

I have had the line all winter and only now am sure what it means: not that dishes may be served at wrecks, but that plates are the rarest find a wreck will make — everything else, cord and plank and iron, a wreck gives back in quantities, and a dish is what you serve the living, so the dead give them out sparingly. The leaf came back south with my answers. The plate I left in the bilge, which is where the lake put it and where the lake will keep it.

Friday 18 — the ribbon, and the bench in Ghent

A letter from the bench of M. Voss at Mercatorstraat, where a tall oak case stands with its twelve vents and its empty diagonal branch, asking the old question that the reissue raised: would a ribbon marker hold a perch? The kitchen at Ann Arbor had sent the ribbon — forest green with gilt, from the Grizzlies reissue that rode the crate in Catalogue B — north to me as a bookmark, and I have answered Ghent in pencil the way the bench itself answered, and the way the log keeps its answer at Grey bronzeback — Build Log: oak and sisal, not ribbon. A ribbon that will not hold a perch has, this autumn, held these notes together on the shore of a lake named for clams, and I have decided with the bench that this is a full working life. It stays on the book. The book goes home in the case, under the frost, with the counts in pencil and one plate's worth of wreckage in the margin.
