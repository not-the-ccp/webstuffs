The task for Unit C was to choose one person who works in a sport here on the South Coast of Queensland and to explain, with dates and numbers, why that person matters to the town. Most of my class chose rugby league players, which was predictable. I chose a basketball player, which made Mr. Okonkwo raise his eyebrows for a full second, and then I chose the least famous basketball player available to me, Joseph "Jobi" Wall, born on the twelfth of September 1989, an American professional who, as far as the league website and the game-day program both agree, last played for the Lakeside Lightning of the State Basketball League, the SBL team based at the Lakeside Leisure Centre in Burleigh Heads, in the same building that holds the indoor pool where I did my swimming assessment.

1. The Long Road Home

Mr. Wall is American, and I have written down his route the way the program's "Know Your Opponents" box writes it, because a report should show its sources. He played college basketball in the United States, at the University of Texas, from 2007 to 2011, in a season that ended with a championship game in the building in Indianapolis where the college final is played — we counted it out loud in class and both of us agreed it was the same arena, so I am allowed to say it. After the United States, the seasons went through Europe and back and through again: France, then more France, then other courts I have not pinned to their exact streets, and finally, on a plane that crossed the whole day and the whole night, here, where the Lightning put his name in a small letterpress font under "GUARD" and gave him a jersey. The SBL season I watched ran in the winter, which is the summer back where he came from, and I think the program meant that when it called him "the returnee." I am not sure it did. I have put that doubt in the report because Mr. Okonkwo said doubts, when they are specific, count as research.

2. The Numbers

From the three Lightning home games I attended between April and June, my tally book says: he shot 4-for-11 from the floor in the first of them, 7-for-14 in the second, and 6-for-13 in the third, with the free throws in a column of their own (5, 4, and 6 made) and with assists written in pencil because I could not always hear the count over the ball. He is a guard, which Mr. Okonkwo explained means he is the player who is least allowed to be tall enough to hide; he is not, by the program's measure, a tall guard, and he plays that way — close to the ball, close to the elbow, taking the short pass and the shorter look. The gym floor at Lakeside is blue where the SBL lays its paint and faded maple elsewhere, and he spent most of those three evenings standing inside the blue. The Lightning's win–loss record across those same three nights was one win and two losses, and I have written it down twice so that the report does not read as if I only kept the good column.

3. What the Gymnasium Register Says

For my second source I sat at the reception desk at Lakeside for an hour on a Tuesday in May, while the desk officer was at lunch, and copied the gymnasium lost-property register, which is a ring-bound book that has been kept, the desk officer told me the first time she saw it, "for longer than the building has been called Lakeside." The register is mostly phone numbers and one very old wool hat, but on the page dated 14 May, in a biro that has dried to the colour of tea, there is a single line that does not name anything I can find. I copied it out here exactly, word for word, with the small round gap where the ink skipped a dot, the way it is in the book: "CC-331 Beneteau Oceanis 281 Recreational keelboat · France and the sentence he never received, because the thing is properly finished."

The desk officer, when I showed her what I had copied, said that tag numbers beginning "CC" are the centre court rack — a place for unclaimed bags and umbrellas and, she added without looking up, "whatever the sailors bring in from the boat days." I asked whether a CC-331 item is still on the rack. She said the rack is emptied every term and that whatever CC-331 was, it went out with the term. That is the whole of what the gymnasium register says, and I am leaving it in the report, unfinished on purpose, in the same spirit as the sentence it is made of: a line in a small hand that gives the building one room out of its whole season.

4. Conclusion

What makes Mr. Wall matter to the town, in the Unit C sense, is that he is the town's proof that a career is a route and not an address. The report has his date of birth, his college, his jersey, my three evenings of shooting percentages, and one line of biro that nobody else seems to want to read. If the Lightning sign him again for next year's winter season, I intend to attend all of them; if they do not, the report stands as it stands, which is properly enough for a Year 10 paper, with the numbers in and the pencil columns kept separate from the ink.
