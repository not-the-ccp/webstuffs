The Travel Desk · Guide to the Imperial Line · Entry No. 5

New Rome, the Fifth Bell

A walking guide in eight stops for Constantine the second, emperor of the Romans from AD 337 to AD 340 — the shortest reign of the line, walked out of the harbour and back.

2·4 miles · about ninety minutes · one bell · no stops walked yet

The route, in brief

Constantine II — Flavius Claudius Constantinus, 316 to 340 — was the second son of Constantine the Great and the eldest son of his second wife, Flavia Maxima Fausta, and he was proclaimed caesar by his father shortly after his birth, which is the earliest a man on this route holds a title he has done nothing to earn. He was a Roman emperor from 337 to 340: three years, four consulships, three victory titles, and a granary. The guide walks his three years as a round of eight stops out of the harbour gate and back, because the line keeps its arithmetic and the ledger keeps its count, and the desk has ruled that a reign, once started, must be finished on foot. Bring water. The canteen keeps the rest.

  1. 1

    The Proclamation Gate

    Begin at the palace gate of Constantine I, where the proclamation was read aloud in the first week of AD 316 and the infant was given the name of the house before he was given a bath. The lintel keeps four chisel cuts — one for each consulship of his life, in 320, 321, 324 and 329 — cut in advance, which the mason’s mark below attributes to optimism. The first cut falls in 320, when he was four, a consulship of the kind no traveller on this route has ever sat through. The third, 324, is the year the father ruled the Tetrarchy’s four quarters down to one hand, and the cedar box in Lot 272 of Aldgate & Vane’s Sale No. 121 keeps a map of those four quarters with three of them crossed out in the first hand. The fourth, 329, was held from this gate without the emperor leaving it. The cut stones are all of them still warm in summer, which the desk notes without comment.

  2. 2

    The Porphyry Bench

    Half a street on, against the palace wall, is the bench where the succession was read to the sons: the father’s arrangement under which the three Constantines would share the empire with their cousins, Dalmatius and Hannibalianus, officers of the household, neither of them of the blood. The bench is worn smooth where the three of them sat in silence, and the plan is known to the desk by the shorter name: it did not survive the breakfast. Dalmatius and Hannibalianus are commemorated here by two empty chair-cushions, which the granary reissues each spring in the cloth of the year. The arrangement is the running joke of the whole line — four quarters, four loaves, one map with three crossed out — and it is also the reason this guide has eight stops instead of five, because a succession that is rejected has to be walked twice. The catalogue of Lot 272 records the same arithmetic on the back of the cedar box and refuses to strike it out, under the no‑eraser clause.

  3. 3

    The Granary of the Fifth Bell

    The granary is the stop the route was made for. It keeps the tin of Naissus copper — the same maker’s punch as the one that went down the hammer in Sale No. 121 — and the ledger of the Fifth Bell, forty-one leaves in four hands, which ran to its closing entry on the morning of 22 May AD 337 and has not had a leaf added to it since, on account of the no‑eraser clause and the fact that the last leaf was watermarked in the shape of a basin. Hanging above the door is a bronze bell, four fingers high, with a clapper that strikes once when shaken and has never yet been shaken a second time in the same room; the granary will shake it for you, once, for the fifth, and it is the only service the building performs without being asked twice. The tin is not opened further. The tea is what the tea is.

  4. 4

    The Canteen of the Fifth Bell

    Under the granary, one course, one counter, forty-one covers. The house plate is the dish the card keeps, and the card keeps it word for word, so the desk transcribes it and declines to parse it, in the same manner the dining room on Ankeny Street declines to parse its own kitchen note, as set down in the review of The Praetorian Table, where the same lobster goes out under the fourth of the family, Act Four, with a mace nut “for the one they excluded, who kept the ledger anyway.” Here it is for the fifth, who excluded no one, which is why the mace is left whole in the shell on this side of the counter.

    Two creams accompany the plate. The former is the saffron cream — saffron, oyster, and a single mace nut, the way the kitchen on the Ankeny Street pass still sends it out, judged at the pass and not by where it wanders. On the back of the pantry card, in the second hand, is the line the canteen serves by:

    “The latter are coloured by an emission of milk and cream 1593 Dish of Lobster, cut up.”

    The keeper reads it the way the board reads everything — by the margin. The latter is the second of the two creams, the plain one, and it is coloured, the line says, by an emission of milk and cream, which the canteen takes as a method and does not question. 1593 is the year the dish was entered in the pantry’s own book, which is to say the recipe is twelve centuries older than the tin it is served from, and the desk offers no explanation of the interval and will not strike it out. Dish of Lobster, cut up is the plate itself — the lobster of the fourth act, cut up for the fifth, cut precisely because the house knows which act it is now serving and does not pretend otherwise. The line enters mid‑register; its first clause is missing; the no‑eraser clause says it may stay missing. The canteen serves it anyway, and the bill comes in Roman numerals, DCCXLI to the cream and no more.

  5. 5

    The Ferry Landing, Sava Mouth

    The route leaves the city here, at the crossing where the Sava runs into the river the army was mustered on. It is the stop for the victory titles: the Sarmatians, the Alamanni and the Goths are the three names the line struck on the arches for this emperor in his years on the western bank, and the landing keeps a stone for each, the Sarmatian stone first, because the Sarmatians came first and the ink on the title was still wet when the Alamanni arrived. The ferryman’s ledger, which the granary has kept a copy of since the closing morning, records the crossings of the line in the same hand as the ledger of the Fifth Bell — the second hand — and the crossing of AD 340, the last one east, is entered without a return, which the ferryman left as a blank line rather than an error. That is the whole of the west of this guide, and the desk apologises for the distance.

  6. 6

    The Consul’s Stone

    Coming back toward the city, at the half-way post, is the stone of the four consulships — 320, 321, 324, 329 — set in the pavement where the route turns, so that every traveller on this guide walks over the third one, the year of 324, on the way back. It is the only stop on the route that works in both directions, and the desk considers this a good sign. The four dates are cut in the first hand, the same iron-gall as the first hand of the ledger of the Fifth Bell, and the fourth of them, 329, is cut a little deeper than the other three, which the stonecutter’s account, kept in the granary, explains as weather. The stone is not a monument. It is a threshold. Step over it the way you would step over a line that has already been ruled.

  7. 7

    The Runaway Horse

    The seventh stop has no building, which is why the route has to name it. It is the ground at the city’s edge where AD 340 ended: not in the baths, not at the granary, not at the pass, but in a fall from a horse that ran, and the line’s three years closed at the height of a saddle. The place keeps a single horseshoe, nailed to a post, in the manner the house on Ankeny Street keeps the card that says only AD 68 — a date, a plate, no further comment. The granary’s last entry for the year was ruled the same season, in the brown ink the graphist dates to the final week, and it is a line of figures for butter and bread and nothing else, which the desk has read and which stands. The shortest reign of the Constantine line does not ask to be edited. It asks to be walked over, once, and left.

  8. 8

    The Gate Log

    The route closes where it opened, at the harbour gate, in the log where the keepers write the arrivals. Leave your mark, as the water‑keeper from Niš left his card, and the board takes it in by the margin. If the bell at the granary has already struck its once for you, the log will note it; if it has not, the log will note that too, and the small print of Lot 272 is clear that no paddle strikes twice at one lot without the auctioneer’s word, and the same word covers the bell. The route is 2·4 miles, the three years of the reign, walked at the pace the ledger kept; the canteen closes at the hour the kitchen on Ankeny Street rings its bell and does not comment; and the desk keeps this entry at No. 5 in the sequence, after the fourth, because the line counts and the ledger keeps its count and the weather, whatever it is, is not edited in public. For the front of the shelf, and the notices that hang there, see the front page. The desk has since kept one more port on this shelf — the city at the western edge of the delta the line was named for, forty kilometres of coast — boxed as Product No. 40, The Bride of the Mediterranean, with a threshold card that is transcribed, not parsed, like everything else the desk keeps.
