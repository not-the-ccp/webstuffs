RULEBOOK 7 REVISION B

IMPERIUM CAMPAIGN SERIES · GAME MANUAL · RULEBOOK 7

The Holy Roman Empire

Sacrum Romanum Imperium · 962–1806 · grand campaign for one emperor and seven elector princes

1.0  The box contents

Imperium covers the polity known after 1512 as the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation: the Kingdom of Germany, the Kingdom of Italy, and, from 1032, the Kingdom of Burgundy, held together by the emperor's overlordship and by very little besides. The campaign map runs in two sheets — north and south of the Alps — and is divided into ten imperial circles, from the Bavarian to the Upper Rhenish, each with its own diet, its own treasury, and its own habit of ignoring the emperor when convenient. The counter sheet holds 384 estates: prince-electors, archbishops, dukes, margraves, landgraves, counts, prelates, and free imperial cities. Seven elector cards are provided — Mainz, Trier, Cologne, the Palatinate, Saxony, Brandenburg, Bohemia — and the muster roll pad is ruled for names in a clerk's hand, ranks in the margin, and dates running down one column like a tide gauge, from 9 January 1735 to 13 March 1823.

2.0  The emperor and the electors

The emperor is elected, never inherited outright. The college of seven electors chooses him, and the Golden Bull of 1356 fixes their number and their order. A game turn represents one reign; play opens at the coronation of Otto I in 962 (Charlemagne's 800 opening is provided as the short scenario) and the campaign ends with the dissolution of 6 August 1806, when Francis II laid down the crown before Napoleon's Confederation of the Rhine and the thousand-year polity was counted at an end. Between those dates the emperor holds three kingdoms at once and governs effectively one: by the fifteenth century imperial governance had become concentrated in and upon the Kingdom of Germany, as Italy and Burgundy went their own ways under the crown's distant shadow.

3.0  Sequence of play

Each round follows the imperial order: Diet phase at Regensburg, where estates present grievances and the emperor raises the common penny; Revenue and homage, where each circle remits or withholds; Muster, where the muster roll is read off word for word and captains — or electors — post themselves where the enemy is strongest; Movement along the imperial roads and the Alpine passes; Battle, resolved on the combat table with the terrain modifiers of river, forest, and walled city; and Imperial justice, where the Chamber Court at Wetzlar sits on appeals that no one expects to be settled. A player who cannot muster a company for a national matter loses the initiative for the round.

4.0  Battles, sieges, and the dissolution rule

Combat is fought estate against estate, and an elector card counts double in the line. Siege rules cover the great imperial cities — Augsburg, Nuremberg, Cologne — and the campaign book includes the passage of the French armies of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars. The dissolution rule triggers in 1806: the emperor removes his crown counter, the estates revert to independent counters, and the map is re-scored as successor states. No victory in this campaign can be purchased at a cheaper rate than the one the admiral gave his quarterdeck that flat February off St Vincent: each captain will place himself where he sees the enemy strongest.

5.0  Scenarios

  • 800 — The coronation. Charlemagne at Rome; short game, one map sheet.
  • 962 — Otto I. The long campaign opens; Germany, Italy, Burgundy not yet joined.
  • 1032 — Burgundy inherited. The third kingdom enters the map.
  • 1356 — The Golden Bull. The seven electors are fixed; play the college.
  • 1512 — The German Nation. The empire takes its later name; the circles follow in 1500's train.
  • 1648 — Westphalia. The estates are sovereign in fact; the emperor plays for shadow.
  • 1806 — Dissolution. One round only: lay down the crown.

6.0  Muster roll and sample company

The keeper of the roll reads it as the dreamer did at St Vincent: the first pioneer company of a national matter, enlisted before the matter has a name. A sample muster for the 1512 scenario: Mainz (elector-archbishop), Trier, Cologne, the Palatinate, Saxony, Brandenburg, Bohemia, with the free city of Regensburg as muster point and nineteen captains in the waiting line — the same nineteen that cut the Spanish line off the cape, in the other book kept at this desk, where Horatio Nelson once stood at the lee rail and was told to stand easy. The roll is read word for word, and the first line is always the same: The first pioneer company of a national matter.

7.0  Map case and associated imagery

Each player keeps two reference sheets in the map case. The first is the flat February sea under a low sun, the wind dead, the gaffs dipping without a command — the sea off St Vincent the night the battle finished and no one knew it. The second is the survey of the Mississippi plain, the river laid out in its offsets, the cotton rows running to the horizon; it sits beside the Kikuchi-plain mound record and the Tokyo bronzes in the same drawer. They are filed side by side because the same rule governs both: a line is only a line until a company musters on it.

Associated imagery A flat February sea and a Mississippi plain, side by side.
