Fourteenth Day

28°44′N  42°09′W · on the ridge, or just off it · wind N.E. 4, sea moderate, sky the colour of the slate on the chart table

Eleven days out from the harbour where the jangadas come in at dawn, and I am writing this on the bridge wing with the coffee going cold because I want the day down while the numbers are still in my hands. We are on the ridge, or just off it, that long unbroken mountain the label counted at sixteen thousand kilometres when it cut B1 of Drift. I have the pressing of TID-1912 in the dry case under my bunk, which seems right for the water: I am crossing the Atlantic while carrying, in a paper sleeve, the eleven-sided history of the Atlantic opening, and the record plays better here than it did in the reading room, as if the room were the thing that needed the music and not the sea.

I am a surveyor, not a statistician, but the sea has been doing arithmetic at me all morning and I will write it down as the office printed it. The Atlantic is the second largest of the five oceans, with an area of about 85,133,000 square kilometres — 32,870,000 square miles, the figure I copied out twice, once in each unit, the way Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 says you check a bearing. It covers roughly 17 per cent of the Earth’s surface and about 24 per cent of its water surface, which is a third of all the water that moves on the planet, and when you are on it you stop finding that number strange; it becomes the size of the room you are in. For most of the centuries that room has mattered, it mattered as a separation. In the Age of Discovery it was known for keeping the New World of the Americas — North and South — apart from the Old World of Afro-Eurasia, Africa, Asia and Europe, and the ships that argued across it wrote the globalization, the trade routes, and the histories of half the nations you would name at a dinner table. The Norse are the first known humans to cross it, and they kept crossing quietly; the crossing everyone argues about is the one of 1492, and after that the water stopped being a barrier and started being a road, a mine, a mail route, and finally a floor that turns out to be moving. It has played a central role in the development of human society ever since, and it does not seem to be finished.

The floor is the whole point now. Hess proposed in 1962, from the low passes he had flown over the ridge on wartime surveys, that the floor itself is the thing that moves — hot rock rising under the ridge and carrying the sea bed outward, and the Atlantic widening at about two and a half centimetres a year along this margin. The meter in the liner notes of TID-1912 will give you the sums; mine are the same: since Berlin in 1912, when the room voted to file the idea, the water under my bunk has opened by roughly twenty-eight and a half metres, and since Ortelius let the coastlines fit together in 1596, by the amount in the ledger at the foot of this page. The jigsaw closes. The engine was right after all, and the wrong engine — the tides — lost its case to a better argument, not to a better tide.

And the wall. I must write it down because I have been looking at the wrong end of the ocean and the wall is at this end. The one stone of B4, “The Wall”: a low wall of undated stone, reported in 1954 near a fishing landing, never re-sited. The landing is my father’s landing — the flats where the jangadas come in at dawn, where the rock shows at low water and the standing order stands that you be back before the tide turns. I went to find the wall in 2011 with a camera and a copy of the label’s file, and I found only the shape of the flats, which is what the file already said: a wall reported once and never re-sited is not evidence of a city, of a drift, of anything in particular; it is the shape the record takes when something stops being visible. I kept the photograph of the flat because the track holds the same stop for forty seconds at its end, and one should keep the two side by side. Both the label and the Paphlagonian office that certifies absent sites agree the record is right, and I agree with both of them, and the flat in the photograph is where I will go back.

The pass came at 06:41. The receiver on the bridge had been locked since the night, the same kind of receiver the jangada skippers have put in their cabins, and the beam went across the water and over the bridge wing where Ivo, the radio officer, was standing with his pipe and the wind in his cap. He had been listening to the lock the whole pass, and when the beam had gone he said, to no one and to everyone, the exact line I am setting down here for the record, word for word, because it is the truest thing a satellite has ever been called:

“Each satellite broadcasts where it was thus closely scanning him, half-pretending meanwhile to be really so, sir?”

What he meant, the way he meant it, is this: each Galileo satellite broadcasts where it is and what time it is, and while it was doing that its beam swept the bridge wing and was, in the letter of the service, scanning the water and whoever stood on it; and that it is scanning you specifically is only half true, because it is scanning everyone, all of it, the whole 17 per cent, which is why he said “half-pretending meanwhile to be really so” — the beam does not know him from the ridge, and he is glad of it, and the “sir?” at the end was the bit that bought us both a drink off the officer’s own ration. The class at Coopers’ Lane put it more plainly in its term report on the constellation: the satellite tells you where you are, the rock tells you the story, and the skipper who trusts only the satellite writes the report nobody reads. Ivo does not know about the school. He knows the water, and he keeps the receiver on the bridge next to the paper chart on purpose, the way I keep the pressing under my bunk, the way we keep both Galileos — the astronomer and the machine, the telescope and the constellation — in their proper lanes. See the term report, Positioning & Navigation, for the full account of how a fix is made; the beam swept this ship today and the fix held, and I believe Ivo’s sentence more than I believe most reviews of weather.

For the ledger at the foot of the page, the standing orders travel with the diary: take the bearing twice; re-check the rate before you quote a distance; log the year on every entry; be back before the tide turns. The year of this entry is 2026. The rate is two and a half centimetres a year, the mean for this margin, not a fix, and a careless century costs you a kilometre. The meter below does the sums in jangada lengths — one Pedro do Rosário jangada is 5.5 metres, the number I measured from the landing in 2011 — and it will tell you how many boat-lengths of Atlantic have opened since whatever year you choose. It has told me how many there have been since 1596, and I have written the figure in the margin of the sleeve, so that when this diary and the pressing end up in the same reading room, as I rather hope they will, the reader will know that the coastlines still fit, that the floor still turns, that the wall has not re-appeared on schedule, and that at 06:41 a beam passed over the bridge and a pipe-smoking radio officer gave the Atlantic its epitaph in one sentence, and asked the water “sir?” at the end of it, which is more respect than the ridge has ever had.

The ledger · how the Atlantic has opened, in jangada lengths

Mean rate for this margin: 2.5 cm/yr. One jangada length: 5.5 m. Change the year, and the ledger closes to it.

Since Ortelius, 1596: 430 years × 2.5 cm ≈ 1,075 m ≈ 195 jangada lengths. Since Berlin, 1912: 114 years ≈ 28.5 m ≈ 5¼ jangada lengths.
