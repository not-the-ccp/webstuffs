Uruguay

Travel Entry, No. 7 · one and a half centavos the word · the fare, as ever, is unchanged

FROM THE FAR SIDE OF THE MOUTH

Uruguay, officially the Oriental Republic of Uruguay, is the country the Ledger has kept reserving a line for, the way it reserves the 334,500th line of the classifieds for the memorial. It sits in southeastern South America, in the Southern Cone, and it is bordered by Argentina to the west and southwest and by Brazil to the north and northeast — which is to say, the delta's two neighbours in every direction that matters — while the Río de la Plata runs along its south and the Atlantic takes the southeast. The country covers approximately 176,215 square kilometres (68,037 sq mi) and holds almost 3.5 million people, of whom nearly 2 million live in the metropolitan area of its capital and largest city, Montevideo. The area that became Uruguay was first inhabited by groups of hunter-gatherers 13,000 years ago, and the first European explorer to take the river up from the sea came by the water, in 1516, Juan Díaz de Solís, which the Ledger notes is the only entry in this book of which a river is the subject and the travel is by water. The town's own thread — Pedro do Rosário — anyone been? — will confirm the fare; it does not change at the water.

Getting there by the mouth

The Ledger holds the mouth of the Itapecuru at low water; Uruguay holds the mouth of the Río de la Plata the way a town holds a river it has decided to be true. The two mouths are not the same mouth, and the military pages — the Delta Ledger's classifieds, taken this week by the Forças Armadas Brasileiras — have argued about whether they should be, by one shell from the flats and a letter marked for the mouth, not the rock. The travel desk's position, unchanged: a fare is printed once, in small type, and it reaches as far as the water reaches. From São Luís the fare is one shell, or a number, and at Montevideo, on the quay of the Río de la Plata, it is printed at the same rate, in the same small type, in Portuguese, which the port accepts without complaint and which the Atlantic accepts without reading.

The lost sandal — No. 1432–05 of the classifieds, one of a pair, air force, believed to have come down over the delta — was reported this season at the docks of Montevideo, walking, and the Ledger has opened this entry in its name. The other sandal remains on the shelf in the back room and will not speak of it, which the travel desk takes to be the honest position on any crossing.

Montevideo the 2,000,000

Montevideo holds nearly 2 million of the country's almost 3.5 million, which is the kind of arithmetic the Ledger does not verify but prints free of charge. The hill is Cerrito; the walk is the Rambla, and the Rambla runs along the Río de la Plata with the patience the drum tuner brings to a rope. The Mercado del Puerto sells the churrasco the way the back room sells the farinha barrel: one price, no second price, and the queue is not a queue but a schedule. Visitors arriving by the mouth are advised that the capital does not hurry, that it was scheduled for waiting, and that the waiting is the entry.

The year 1950 the final without a final

It is the year the Ledger cannot keep out of its travel entries and it will not. In 1950 the country of 176,215 square kilometres went north to the Maracanã in Rio de Janeiro and, on a Sunday afternoon, with the largest football crowd ever assembled to watch it, stopped the Republic in its own stadium. The Federative Line — then ten years old, born in 1940 — kept its shortest log entry of the century for the hour the whistle went: all platforms standing; nothing scheduled after the final without a final. The five regions kept company in the stands, in the order the Censo ran them, and the Sul platform, last to be notified, first to accept it, accepted it again that Sunday. Uruguay is the only country in this entry whose football score the Ledger prints free, at no centavos, because the family asked for nothing in lieu of flowers and the score is the flowers. Two world titles, 1930 at home and 1950 away; the Ledger holds both dates against the rock, which shows at low water.

The kitchen the puchero and its line

The table in Uruguay is built on the puchero, the slow pot: beef and bones and vegetables going into one pot before noon and arguing with it until evening, and the churrasco going on the grill in the same hour, which is how the country does its arithmetic, the way the Republic does its arithmetic on the timetable. The Ledger's typesetter received, this season, a page from the scrub ledger that belongs to no other page — the delta's misbound recipe line, kept word for word at 8d on the Paterson River side and kept here, word for word, uncorrected:

“Trim off the crusts and ragged edges, divide each bean into 4 or 5 onions, 5 turnips, 3/4 to 1 hour.”

No editor on either river has yet parsed it as a recipe, and the travel desk declines to parse it either. It reads as a schedule: a quantity of beef, a count of onions, a count of turnips, and a time of three-quarters of an hour to an hour, in that order, with the word divide doing the work of every word it is not. The kitchen clock below has been built from the line itself — four onions to five, five turnips to a pot, three-quarters of an hour to the full hour — and it keeps the misbound line as its only rule.

The Puchero Clock built from the misbound line

Set the number of eaters at the pot and the clock returns the pot: the meat, the onions, the turnips, and the hour. The line does not divide the bean; it divides the pot.

Where the line runs next in lieu of flowers

The Federative Line, on its official timetable and commentary, is scheduled to run 7,491 kilometres of coast and has not yet finished it; this entry is the line's only service that leaves the Republic at all, and it runs to the mouth, which is where the coast is. The scrub ledger, No. 312, on the banks of the Paterson River, keeps the misbound line at 8d in its own small type at The Scrub Ledger — Classifieds, Paterson River Edition, and the two rivers' editions agree on every word, which the Ledger takes to be proof of nothing and prints anyway. The family of this entry asks for nothing in lieu of flowers. In lieu of flowers, nothing. The pot keeps company in the fire until the hour closes, and the shell keeps company in the flats, and both are due, soon.
