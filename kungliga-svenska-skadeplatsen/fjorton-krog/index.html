Webstuffs · Dining Notes · Review No. 4 of the season

Fjorton Krog

Stora Nygatan, Stadsholmen, Stockholm · twenty-eight covers · two sittings, blue room

THE CROCK'S VERDICT: 4 out of 4 — four islands out of fourteen, and the fifth by dessert.

I. The room

Stora Nygatan climbs the slope of Stadsholmen, the island where Birger Jarl founded the city in 1252, and Fjorton Krog occupies the corner where the street turns toward the water. From the front window you look down Riddarfjärden toward the Baltic Sea: fourteen islands make the municipality, where Lake Mälaren flows into the Baltic and the archipelago begins to the east, and about a million people live in the municipality, with 1.6 million in the urban area and 2.5 million in the metropolitan area. The area has been settled since the Stone Age, in the 6th millennium BC, but this room is younger: it opened in the winter of 1754–55, when Maria Lindahl — once a dancer under Peter Lindahl at the Kungliga svenska skådeplatsen on Slottsbacken — was turned out of the Stora Bollhuset with the rest of the Swedish company, after Queen Louisa Ulrika engaged the French Du Londel Troupe and the house passed to the French. She took the corner instead, papered the walls with old prompt books, and hung a Saltfleet whip by the door — a fine thing on the Lincolnshire shingle, a foolish thing in a Stockholm box, but here it only keeps the coats. Twenty-eight covers, candlelight, and a clock that still keeps the house's old time.

The dining room seats fourteen at the long blue table, one cover for each island, and the rest at small tables against the wall. Service is in two sittings, and the kitchen does not hurry: the first sitting finishes when it finishes, and the second begins when it is ready. You come for the water view and stay for the food.

II. The menu

The menu is Swedish and the water is local. Gravlax of Baltic salmon, cured for thirty-six hours with dill, salt and a little sugar, served with sweet mustard sauce and knäckebröd baked the morning of. Räksmörgås, the open shrimp sandwich: a mound of North Sea prawns on rye, with egg, dill and a slice of Västerbotten cheese. Kóttbullar, twelve of them, with cream sauce, lingonberries and pickled cucumber sharp enough to wake the dead. Fried strömming from the sound, small and silver, with potatoes and dill. And for those who want the south as well as the north, äggakaka, the oven-baked egg cake, served with lingonberry jam and fried pork.

The house punsch is arrack, tea, lemon and sugar, warmed in a copper bowl and served in small glasses at just below boiling. Sip it once and it is medicine; return to the throat, and then simply irresistible. It was the drink of the old company between acts, and the house keeps the recipe in a ledger Maria Lindahl brought from the Bollhuset in 1754. Finish with prinsesstårta: green marzipan, whipped cream, sponge and a raspberry at the centre, — the colour of the archipelago in June, if June were made of sugar.

III. The blue mould

The chef dreamed of the blue club of Santiago before she dreamed of Stockholm — eighteen titles, the crest of 1943, the unbeaten run of 2011 — and the dream ended where dreams in this folio always end: at a set table. The kitchen instruction she follows is the one the dream gives: butter a mould, and pour over it before the sauce will be done through. The mould should be blue, or the dream will not close. So the dessert arrives in a cobalt mould, blueberry and buttermilk parfait with a sauce the colour of the 1943 crest, and the table, when it is set, is the stand of the Nacional full at last. Eat it slowly; the dream closes only when the plate is empty.

IV. The bill

Two sittings, fourteen covers, one whip in the lobby, and a clock that runs on the old house time. The check comes on a prompt book, scored in pencil, and the total is the sort of number you pay without arguing. Coffee afterwards, black, and the view unchanged: fourteen islands, the water going out to the Baltic, and the city still speaking Swedish on the street below. If you are coming from the playhouse, the street still feeds its actors — the house on Slottsbacken admits no one mid-performance, but Fjorton Krog admits everyone after the curtain falls.

One thing from this season's table, for the record: a copy of the 2046 Folio, the Kathmandu bindery's clothbound record of the 1990 People’s Movement in Nepal, came across the long blue table between sittings — a visitor's book, the kind of thing the punsch is made for. It stayed a fortnight at cover nine, and the waiters learned not to dust it while it lay there, which is how this house treats anything that has been kept by someone else.

One last thing from the visitor book, written in a Roman hand in January of the same year this house and its ledger agree on: two men had signed an agreement in a small council room off the corridor of a palazzo, on a reform of a constitution and the making of a prime minister, and the book's writer had come north to check whether the clock at Fjorton still keeps old time while governments are being rewritten elsewhere. It does. The minutes of that room, the Second Session of 18 January 2014, are kept on the shelf with the other records the kitchen does not dust.
