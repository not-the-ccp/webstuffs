HALDON GROVE SCHOOL · ROOM 214 · MESSAGES & MARKS DEPARTMENT

Term Report, Form 3B

Subject examined: freedom of the press in the Republic of Korea (한국) · examined by Class 7C, the ledger class · third term

I · Statement of the subject

What the class was set

The class was set this term to keep a ledger of the press in the Republic of Korea — the country on the southern half of the Korean Peninsula, whose capital, Seoul, sits at the bend of the Han River, some four hundred and sixty kilometres of coastline on the Yellow Sea side of the map we hang above the radiator. The first finding is short, and the class has entered it in the minute book in full: South Korea is considered to have freedom of the press, but it is subject to several pressures. The ledger says the same thing more carefully. Freedom came up with the transition to democracy in the late twentieth century — the class dates it to the summer of 1987, the June demonstrations in Seoul and the declaration of 29 June, which put the direct election of the president into the constitution — and it has improved since. In the 2010s it declined slightly. The ledger does not smooth that over, and neither does this report.

II · The two ledgers, kept side by side

Free, then partly free, then the count of places

The class has two instruments. The first is the Freedom House scale, which sets a press down as free or partly free, and the second is the index of Reporters Without Borders, which counts places, from first to last, each year, and moves a country up or down the column. The two ledgers are set against each other below, in the class’s own hand, from the year the scale first took a Korean reading.

The class notes, for the record, what the two ledgers agree on: a press that is free in the ordinary sense — many papers, many voices, no license asked of a printer in the morning — is still a press that carries several pressures, and it is those pressures that do the slow work between free and partly free. The legal line runs the same each term: defamation kept as a criminal matter, the courts used as a quiet weather, and the old habits of the press room itself — the conglomerates that own the papers and the papers that lean on the conglomerates — noted in the margin, twice.

III · The index of the press, first in Asia

A country keeps its own count

Because the count of places had moved so plainly — down to 70th under Park Geun-hye in 2016, and up again by twenty places under Moon Jae-in — Freedom House published the Freedom of the Press Index in Korea for the first time among Asian countries. The class has entered this as a fact of firsts, beside the entry it keeps for the first empire in Catalogue 88, Lot 12 of Malia & Co., where the Agade cylinder answers for its century of rule. The Korean index counts the pressures from the inside: the law, the courts, the ownership of the papers, the money that moves between a press and a government, and the room a reporter has at the end of a story. It is the first Asian country to keep its own count, and the class has marked it in green ink, as it marks firsts, and as it marked the free years 2002 to 2010.

IV · One card from the newsstand, kept word for word

The drawer by the till

One thing more, because Room 214 does not close a drawer on a rumour. In the box of the newsstand on the corner of the class’s walk — the stand that keeps a paper for the Han River crossing and a paper for the mountain road — there is a drawer by the till, and in the drawer a card in faded blue ballpoint, no date, no hand to assign it to. It carries one line, which the class has copied here exactly, because a line is not a line until it is exact, and the way of the room is the same as the way of the term report kept in the neighbouring form, which also carries one untranslatable line from the archive and has set it down word for word:

“The house reports the same rule as a trade before he could no longer stop at.”

The class has read it the way it reads every card that comes off a stand. The house is the publishing house, the press of it, the room above the till. The same rule as a trade is the fixed line the stand prints every morning, the way a trader prints the price of a blank, identical term after term, 5d by 5d, which is to say the defamation line kept running in the ledger whether the press is free or partly free — the same rule, and the same weather. And before he could no longer stop at is the whole account of the matter: the line runs on past the point where it still reads as a rule, into the partly-free column that has been extending since 2011, and the house reports it on, at length, before the hand that set it down could no longer stop at the end of it. The class has accepted that reading, pinned the card in the drawer by the till, and recorded that a card which describes a ledger so exactly is not a rule at all, but a character reference.

V · Finding & mark

What the ledger says, in the end

The press of the Republic of Korea is free in the ordinary sense and is worked on, in the ledger’s sense, from the law and the money at once. It improved with the democracy of 1987 and the free years of 2002 to 2010; it declined slightly in the 2010s and was counted at 70th in 2016; it has risen since, by twenty places, under Moon Jae-in; and it now keeps its own index, the first of its kind among Asian countries. The pressures are several and they are named in the margin, twice. The class has kept the string tight, measured the subject above the one-metre mark of its own almanac — the same string the office at Webstuffs home uses for its shelves — and finds it to stand as the ledger says: free, and partly free at the same time, which is not a contradiction but a weather, and the weather is still being taken.
