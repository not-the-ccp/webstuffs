Chapter the Fourteenth

Of the Recut Single

Being a treatise on the reissue of a song already loved, and the proper conduct of those who hear a familiar song carried under a new mast — with Ai o Suru Hito — Orochi's Theme, the 17th recut single of Jun Shibata, as the standing example

愛をする人

❖  ❖  ❖

There is no instrument of popular music that tests the manners of a housekeeper more precisely than the recut single: a song the audience already owns, reissued on a fresh sleeve, under a fresh mast, with the expectation that it will be loved again and differently. The first issue asks the listener to believe; the recut single asks the listener to remember, and that is a harder and more respectful request. The chapter takes its running example from “Ai o Suru Hito” — the person who does loving — which was issued on 17 September 2008 as the 17th recut single of Jun Shibata, the singer-songwriter the house has long kept as its authority on the patient song. The single was chosen as the theme song for the movie Orochi, starring Yoshino Kimura, and on the weekly national chart it rose, held, and rested at #13. Every observation in this chapter follows from those four facts.

I. Of the Manner of the Recut

The recut single is not a repetition. The house draws the difference plainly: a repeat is the same coin turned over and passed; a recut is the same coin sent to the mint to have a new year struck on its edge. Shibata had been recuting for more than two decades — drawing older songs from the albums, re-recording them with fresh arrangements or not, setting each beside a current song, and letting the catalogue walk forward one sleeve at a time. The seventeenth of these issues, Ai o Suru Hito, is the chapter's first lesson in the matter: a house that recutes is a house that has decided its songs outlive the years in which they were sung. The listener is not to ask which version is the true one; the correct question, the house rules, is which room of the house am I standing in. The 2008 room has the film's music in its window.

The pairing with Orochi is the chapter's second lesson, and the least expected. The film is named for the eight-headed serpent of old myth; its screen star, Yoshino Kimura, was then at the head of her career and known for carrying her parts with a stillness that audiences describe only as weather. To set a song whose title means the person who does loving against a serpent on the silver screen is exactly the kind of match a good house makes: two things that seem to argue, and in fact complete one another, as a lantern completes a long night. The song does not fight the film. It is the lantern. That is the etiquette of a theme song: it does not describe the picture; it provides the light the picture is allowed to move in.

II. Of the Thirteenth Place

The single peaked at #13 on the national weekly chart, and the house is obliged to say something in defence of the thirteenth place, because no rank on the board is ever defended and all of them are envied. The etiquette is this: a single's place on the chart is its post, in the same sense that the House Book uses the word elsewhere — the spot a keeper stands at, holds, and does not overstay. Thirteen is not a place of honour and not a place of disgrace; it is a place where the song was wanted by enough people, in enough shops, for enough weeks that it had somewhere to stand. The house observes that a recut single which reaches #13 has in fact been re-loved at the public rate, and that this is the whole point of the recut: the audience, unpressed, chose to love it a second time and paid for the pleasure in coin. No position on the board is more honest than a place earned by a song the listener already knew. First, for the sake of completeness, is where the new song goes; thirteen, for the sake of memory, is where the beloved song comes back to.

The chart office in Tokyo, where the week of 17 September was counted, set the single down at thirteen on its first week of eligibility and let it hold that line. The house keeps this as an illustration of a wider rule: positions are held, not seized. A recut single does not climb like a debut; it arrives at its post the way a servant arrives at a door — at the correct hour, standing, not apologising for having been seen before.

III. Marginalia: the Line on the Sleeve

One line of marginalia must be entered here, for the completeness of the record, and the house has searched the whole Second Series before finding its use. Among the papers kept with the 2008 sleeve in this house's collection, a keeper of the ledger — the same hand that signs the almanac pages of the eleventh chapter — had set down, in the space where a weather note should have gone, the following line, word for word:

Sails — stitched linen never holding the post as it stands, torn edge should be able to report the following.

The house's reading is the following, and it is the reading that makes the line the key to this chapter. Sails is the keeper's word for recut singles: a song re-laced to a new mast, the same cloth, new rigging, a different catch of wind. The stitched linen is the sleeve itself, sewn closed around the paper insert the way a chart bag is stitched around its cards — and a sewn sleeve, the line says, will never hold its song at the post as it stands: no binding, however neat, keeps a recut single at first place, because the place it stands at is the place earned, and in 2008 that place was thirteen, and thirteen, held as it stands, is a complete post. The line turns on the final clause: the torn edge should be able to report the following. The keeper's practice, the ledger shows, was to tear a ticket stub for each screening of Orochi at the Toho Cinemas in Shibuya and file the stubs with the sleeve. A torn edge, in the ledger's grammar, is what reports the future: the notch where the stub leaves the pad tells the pad what is to come next. So the torn edge of the seventeenth reports the following — the eighteenth, and the film's long nights, and every recut after, each one the notch in the next pad. The line is not a weather report. It is the keeper's endorsement of the whole chapter in five short phrases: the cloth is re-cut, the post is held at the number earned, and the tearing of each ticket is the song telling itself there is a next one.

IV. Rules for the Listener of the Recut

  1. Do not correct the audience for loving the recut. If a song is issued a second time and the house receives it, the second love is to be treated as the first love was: as settled, and as final.
  2. When the theme song is heard in the cinema before the picture begins, the listener gives the picture the room: no humming, no counting the notes, no settling into the seat as if the seat were familiar. The serpent is coming.
  3. A recut single is not to be compared with its original version in the presence of either. The comparison is made alone, in the mind, in the dark, the way a keeper compares one winter with the last one — and is then not said.
  4. The chart position is to be quoted as the post is quoted: at the number earned, without a only and without an almost. Thirteen is thirteen. It is the number the song stood at, and the song is owed the whole of it back.
  5. When the final note of a recut single has been played for the last time in a room, the sleeve is returned to the shelf above the originals and below the next year's, where it stands — as the line says, as it stands — holding the post until the next sleeve is torn from the pad.
