汉 Han · Golden Age

From the Secretary, on the Dynasty Itself

Members are reminded, in case the term has grown long and the dates have begun to blur, that the Han dynasty (202 BC – 9 AD, 25–220 AD) was an imperial dynasty of China established by Liu Bang, who took the throne as Emperor Gaozu after the short-lived Qin dynasty (221–206 BC) and the interregnum known as the Chu–Han Contention (206–202 BC). It was succeeded by the Three Kingdoms period (220–280 AD), and it was also briefly interrupted by the Xin dynasty (9–23 AD), established by the usurping regent Wang Mang, who had the courtesy to be regent long enough to feel the throne was simply sitting in his chair. The dynasty is thus separated into two periods — the Western Han (202 BC – 9 AD) and the Eastern Han (25–220 AD) — the way the school day is separated by the tide bell into a part before it and a part after it, with the bell itself briefly pretending to be the whole.

The Han is considered a golden age in Chinese history, and it impacted Chinese identity in later periods in a way we have not seen since: the majority ethnic group of modern China still refer to themselves as the Han, and the word for the Chinese language is built from the same character. The dynasty fell four times over a thousand years ago and yet it is still out there in the street, on the tongue, in the character a child writes before it can write its own name. That is what a golden age does. It gets into the ink.

On Scrolls and the Sheet Itself

Our young member Oona F. Petherick of Class 7B, seat 14, who wrote the school report on the book for Mr. Delgado’s Department of Letters (marked A, which she attributes to a rabbit), has been counting the ancestors. The Han era gave the world the pressed sheet of mulberry bark and rags that we now take for granted as paper — attributed in the Eastern Han, about 105 AD, to a court official named Cai Lun, who improved what was already there, as is the custom. A book, it turns out, needs a dynasty before it needs a cover. The society has therefore adopted the school library’s own inventory as its standing exhibit: two clay tablets (plaster casts, neither legible), one scroll (the fire warden’s), and one codex of 1902 that Mr. Aldous, the caretaker, keeps under the library floorboards. The codex is not coming out of the floor this year; Mr. Aldous is adamant, and the mason is still in consultation with the hole. Full particulars are in the report itself, at The Book — Report, Class 7B, where Mr. Delgado re-shelves the class by the letter and apologises, in pencil, for being correct.

The Fragment, and Its Fate

The club box holds, beneath the tin of buttons and the tide chart, a torn page from a letter of 1893 whose writer is explaining how a parcel of paper samples from an importer’s counter in Shanghai came to arrive in these parts at all — the post, the sea, the dray. The middle of the explanation is gone, and what survives is only the hinge of the sentence, which the committee has agreed to preserve exactly as it stands, torn edge and all: “For by some dreadful tempest, or dashed upon the legs at the counter.” It is not a complete sentence. It is a sentence that has been through something. The committee met the matter four times and concluded that a fragment is the honest form for a letter that crossed an ocean, and that the torn edge should be left untrimmed, as the edge of the dynasty itself was left, in 220 AD, when the Three Kingdoms went out and took the weather for themselves.

October, Kept in Company

Our October excursion will follow the almanac’s own route, which in the month of October stops at Zhongshi [忠实], faithfulness, in the tea country of Yunnan — mist over terraced hills, pu’er cakes in the market, the old horse roads. The guidebook for the route is kept at Elsewhere — Guidebook No. 12, one place for each month of the year, and its members, like ours, take their tea downstairs in the station room while the pencil keeps the route marked on the wall. We have written to the almanac’s keepers to say that the Han Society will be arriving on schedule, which is to say late, and that the mist may come whether we are ready or not, because in the golden age everything arrived on schedule, even the usurper.

Club Business

Membership stands at eleven: the Secretary, the Keeper of the Box (Mr. Aldous, who has not once lost a fragment and does not expect to start), the baker’s apprentice, four members of Class 7B who are allowed Tuesdays, and the rabbit, honorary, ears excluded. The two-penny subscription is due at the counter, and members are reminded that the counter is where the dray unloads, where the paper samples sit, and where, if the fragment is to be believed, a leg of the table may arrive in one’s way. Next meeting: the second Tuesday, after the tide bell, in the shed behind the bakery. The vinegar crock will be covered. The codex will remain under the floor. The seal will be pressed.
