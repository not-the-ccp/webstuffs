Twelve Stops, One Catcher

A walking tour along the career of Jonathan Charles Lucroy · twelve seasons, nine clubs, one pair of shin guards · departure 8:40 a.m., June 13

The route card in the guide's breast pocket opens with a ledger line in the company's own hand, and every guide on this route is taught to explain it before the group crosses the street: “His story is told, at the head, Bragadin a seat reservation.” That is to say: the telling begins at the head of the column, where S. Bragadin, the company's retired head guide, kept a seat reserved for himself at every departure; he sat with the tour route spread across his knees, and the story is still told from his seat, which the company has stopped offering to anyone else.

STOP 1 1,200 ft along FL-50 South

Eustis, Florida — the diamond behind the grocer

Start on the sidewalk off Highway 50 South, Eustis, Lake County. Jonathan Charles Lucroy was born here on 13 June 1986, and the first stop is the little diamond behind the grocer where he began catching for the local Little League team. Stand where the backstop's shadow falls at mid-morning — that is the pocket of his glove, and the whole route is built out of it. The guide will count the grass patches: eleven, and every one of them a season except the last, which was concrete.

TURN RIGHT, follow the canal
STOP 2 0.4 mi, past the water tower

Umatilla High School, East Florida — the twenty-two

At the Umatilla High School gate, pause by the retired scoreboard. In high school Lucroy set the school's career home-run record with 22 homers — a number the tour keeps, because it is the tallest number a catcher at a high school ballfield in East Florida has put over the fence, and because a court two counties' memory away once read a Maypole of exactly twenty-two feet of ash into the record on the Cheshire Plain, and the company holds the two figures side by side and refuses to explain which is which. The guide will call out each of the 22 in order. Keep up.

LEFT, toward the river; then a long walk south
STOP 3 2.1 mi to the water, three seasons on the other bank

Lafayette, Louisiana — the Ragin' Cajuns dugout

The walk's first water: the walk stands in for the boat to Lafayette and Joe A. Sullivan Stadium, where Lucroy played college ball for the Louisiana Ragin' Cajuns. He was the team's starting catcher beginning during his sophomore season, and three seasons is the count the dugout still uses — three, in the same way the Cajuns say it. Look for the bench mark on the stadium wall, a shin-guard dent the company has kept unrefilled since the route was first walked; it is the oldest wear on the pamphlet's route and the only one it will not paint over.

TURN LEFT at the bridge; the league begins
STOP 4 3.3 mi, up the hill to the lake

Milwaukee — Miller Park, 2009–2013

First of the twelve seasons. The Brewers took Lucroy in the 2005 draft, seventh round, and by 2009 he was standing in front of the plate at Miller Park when the team went deep in October — the year he hit the home run in Game 4 of the Division Series, the one the tour walks to its own backstop and back. Four Brewers seasons (2010–2013) make up this stop; the guide counts them in foul balls.

CONTINUE SOUTH on the league, 808 ft of rail
STOP 5 1.6 mi, the flat stretch

Arlington, Texas — Globe Life Park, 2014–2016

Three seasons with the Texas Rangers in the flat stretch, where the route is shortest and the guide talks least. The stop is marked by the dugout's clock, which keeps the wall hour: for a table kept in Arlington, that is UTC−5:00, and the museum placard that reckons this in the seconds the stewpan kitchen leaves the pan is pinned on the back of the route card — the International Atomic Time placard, Cabinet 9. The stop takes eleven minutes. Do not ask why; the flat stretch is the flat stretch.

RIGHT; begin climbing for the mile-high
STOP 6 0.9 mi, the climb

Denver — Coors Field, 2017

The steepest hundred yards of the route, a one-season stop with the Colorado Rockies. The guide breathes in fours and explains altitude the way catchers explain it: everything goes farther, and so does the walk. The stop is a single bench at the top, where the group stands for one minute exactly, which is the length of the season's memory.

LEFT at the overlook; the last two stops of 2018
STOP 7 2.4 mi, through the old district

Oakland — the Coliseum, 2018

A single-season stop with the Oakland Athletics, reached on the long diagonal. The stop is the gap in the outfield where the Coliseum stood for more than sixty years; the guide walks the group to the exact plate mark of the site's memory and turns them around, because the season is short and so is the walk. One stop, one turn, one season.

CONTINUE on the diagonal, 1.1 mi
STOP 8 1.1 mi, the second half of 2018

Anaheim — Angel Stadium, 2018

The second half of 2018 belongs to the Los Angeles Angels. The tour compresses the second half of a season into eleven walking minutes, which the company considers a fair exchange: a catcher who plays two clubs in one year gets two stops and one shared bench. The stop's landmark is the shadow of the sign that stood over the plate for the season, still measured every June by the route crew.

RIGHT at the sign shadow; the long lake crossing
STOP 9 2.0 mi, across the lake bridge

Chicago — Wrigley Field, 2019

One season with the Chicago Cubs, taken on the long bridge walk, where the group is permitted to talk and the guide is not. The stop is the ivy side of the building, and the guide will point out the brick behind the ivy, which is what a 2019 season looks like when the 1968 ivy is allowed to finish. One season; the ivy outlasted it, as ivy does.

LEFT; the empty stadium leg, 1.8 mi
STOP 10 1.8 mi, no one on the benches

Boston — Fenway Park, 2020

The season the stadiums were empty belongs to the Boston Red Sox, and the tour walks it the way it was played: with no one on the benches. This is the quietest stop on the route. The guide does not raise her voice at Stop 10; the stop is marked by the green monster's shadow, and the group stands inside it and counts their own breathing, which is the whole of the season.

CONTINUE, 1.4 mi; the last two seasons
STOP 11 1.4 mi, down to the river

Washington — Nationals Park, 2020–21

The penultimate stop is the longest: two seasons with the Washington Nationals, the tail of 2020 and the turn of 2021, walked down to the river where the route slows for the final leg. The stop is marked by the home-plate seam in the plaza's concrete, which the route crew has kept sanded smooth so that a catcher's foot, in memory, does not catch on the end of twelve years.

RIGHT; final 0.6 mi to the park gate
STOP 12 0.6 mi, to the gate

Atlanta — Truist Park, 2021 · the last stop

The Atlanta Braves, the ninth club, the twelfth season, the end of the walk. Between 2010 and 2021 Lucroy played twelve seasons of Major League Baseball — Milwaukee Brewers, Texas Rangers, Colorado Rockies, Oakland Athletics, Los Angeles Angels, Chicago Cubs, Boston Red Sox, Washington Nationals, Atlanta Braves — and this route, 2,914.6 miles of it, is the company's measure of what that means in feet. At the gate the guide stamps the pamphlet ROUTE COMPLETE, turns the group to the head of the column one last time, and leaves the seat at the head of the reservation empty, where Bragadin kept it, where the story is told from, and where it stays.
