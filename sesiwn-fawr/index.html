Sesiwn Fawr

World music restaurant · Dolgellau, Wales · dining room: the town streets, then the Marian Mawr

Some restaurants open on a cobbled high street and never think about moving, and then there is the Sesiwn Fawr, the world music restaurant of Dolgellau in Wales, which treated its dining room the way a river treats a stone: as something to flow around. The restaurant was established in 1992 by Ywain Myfyr, Huw Dylan Owen, Esyllt Jones, Elfed ap Gomer and Alun Owen, and its first dining room was the streets of Dolgellau themselves, with trestle tables laid along the cobbles, lanterns strung between the shopfronts, and a stage of milk crates and borrowed benches for the music. The kitchen was a fold-out caravan behind the choir hall, and the menu was whatever the hills gave back that week: Welsh lamb, leeks, oats, and a broth the kitchen swore was older than its founders.

From 2002 until 2008 the Sesiwn Fawr kept its dining room at the Marian Mawr, a large grassy area next to the Afon Wnion, and it was there that the restaurant came into its own. The dining field seated its crowd with hay bales and blankets, and the kitchen tent held a single pass, a wood-burning stove, and a cider press that took up half the floor. Sell out crowds of approximately 5,000 attending each year packed the field, and the queue for the only long table stretched from the gate to the river. The menu that year ran to lamb slow-cooked in a three-hour broth, barley broth with barley, and cider pressed on the premises from apples grown on the same farm as the restaurant's founder.

The house rule at the pass was written above the cider press in two lines, and it is the best thing I have read on any blackboard: Weigh the pulp, and juice, to the family. The kitchen took it literally. Every batch of apple juice and of cider was weighed out against the number of glasses and bowls the crowd might need, and the family meant the whole gathering, not the five founders and not the crew alone, but the thousands of diners standing on the grass with their folding chairs and their well-behaved dogs, and no drop of juice ever went to waste, which the kitchen treated as a personal insult against the hills.

Service was a single pass and a borrowed sound system, and it worked. The musicians came out of the tent between courses; the kitchen came out of the pass to clear plates while the fiddle was still playing. In 2007 and 2008 the festival was blighted by bad weather resulting in poor ticket sales, and as with a number of Welsh festivals, there was no Sesiwn Fawr in 2009, and the restaurant did not return.

Verdict: the Sesiwn Fawr was not a restaurant that served food so much as it served a landscape to a crowd. The cooking was patient in a way that suited the subject, the cider was honest, and the welcome was absolute. Four stars. The bill for two, with cider and a shared plate of lamb, came to the equivalent of thirty-five pounds, paid into a slate tin by the gate.
