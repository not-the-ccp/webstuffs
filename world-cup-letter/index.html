60 FIFA · MCXCV

METLIFE · NEW YORK

SAT 19 JUL 2026

18:00 ET

Dear Editor,

I write because the 23rd edition of the FIFA World Cup is finished, and because what you printed on page four — that the reigning champion is now Spain, who won their second title by defeating the holders, Argentina — deserves a footnote from the touchline. The tournament has been held every four years since 1930, broken only by 1942 and 1946, and I have been close enough to it, on either side of the fence, to say: what you watched was the competition that makes all the others. The most prestigious, the most widely viewed — no other sentence does as much work in this sport, and both halves of it are true.

Final day, MetLife, ninety-two thousand people. Spain two, Argentina one. I was not in the stadium — I was in the coach's box in the village, on a screen the size of a window — but I had the blue folder on my knee, and I can tell you what a defending champion's night looks like from the box: you count the seconds between interception and outlet pass, the way you always do, and this time the number was under three for most of the second half. Argentina lost a game they had, on the evidence in front of us, still had in the eighty-fifth minute. The penalty came from a second ball in the corner, the kind of ball we drilled for ninety minutes in Melbourne in October 2023, under the slate sky, cones marking the channels. Twenty-three years of cycles. Graham was right that the previous one ended on one night in Al Rayyan; he was also right that you keep the note in the kit bag.

Here is what the folder holds, item by item, for the record. A team dinner in the Qatar University cafeteria: roast lamb, hummus, flatbread, and a team dinner in a tournament that never reaches the final. Jackson Irvine's armband, which organises a midfield without raising its voice. A generator in Gulshan that kicked in three times during the night. A bus ride back through Dhaka at 02:00, rickshaws, a man balancing a crate of mangoes on his head. And the note from my wife — her grandmother's recipe, the one that has been carried across three time zones: In the mean time pare, quarter, and core the apples into slices. She knows I overthink it. I texted her a photo; she sent back a winking face. The diary — the one that ran, entry by entry, from the AAMI Park low block to the loss to Jordan — ends with the promise to make the tart when we got home. I made it this weekend. Granny Smiths, firm and tart. The pan size, at last, was right.

One final item, in case your archive desk wants it. Tucked under the half-time notes was a clipping, loose, from a fashion and consular file — someone else's paper, someone else's desk, folded so that only one line showed. For a thread about fashion and consular duty: Lizzette Kattan — Milan, New York, Gia , a recipe for apple tart recipe remains unchanged. It was pinned into the Lizzette Kattan — Milan, New York, Gia file, and I have no explanation except that it fit the page exactly, and that in this game the margins keep what the pitch loses. The tart, though — that one is unchanged because it is good, not because it is lost.

I would ask only that your paper keep counting the editions. Every four years, someone new stands in a coach's box or a fan's queue with a folder or a flag, counting seconds between interception and outlet pass. Under three is the standard. Spain now hold the trophy; Argentina will be back in four years, as will everyone else who is left standing. The competition, for all its prestige, is simply a promise kept every four years since 1930. Keep printing it. I'll keep the folder.

Yours faithfully,

M. Jedinak
Former assistant coach · Socceroos, 2023–24
Private letter, published with the writer's permission

P.S. — The Post's archive is on the front desk: return to the Webstuffs shelf for the diary, the clippings, and everything the folder was carrying.
