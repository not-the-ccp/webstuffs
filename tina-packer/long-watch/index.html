THE STANDING OVATION COLLECTION · FIELD ROUTES, BOOK TWO · ROUTE PLATE No. 1963-W

The Long Watch

A walking tour in seven stops, set out along the Doctor Who route of London — the series first broadcast by the BBC in 1963, its box, its fourteen chairs, and the record kept at the palate

The route: Wood Lane → 8 Shakespeare Street → the corner of the box → the chair row → the Palate Room → the companion’s bench → the ledger gate. Allow two hours. The guide carries the record book; the walker carries the bread.

Stop 1 Studio 1, BBC Television Centre, Wood Lane

Wood Lane, West London · the studio door, 11:59 a.m. on a Tuesday

The tour begins where the series began: Doctor Who is a British science fiction television series first broadcast by the BBC in 1963, and its first serial, An Unearthly Child, went out on 23 November 1963 from a studio on Wood Lane. The credits name the series as created by Sydney Newman, C. E. Webber and Donald Wilson. In the control gallery on that morning, the gallery lamps were the same colour as the box we will meet at Stop 3 — not the right blue, nothing so settled. The serial follows the adventures of the Doctor, an extraterrestrial being from a humanoid species known as Time Lords. The first Doctor was played by William Hartnell, and the first family of four — the Doctor, his granddaughter Susan, and the schoolteacher couple Ian and Barbara — stood in a quarry of red aliens with the studio lights hot above them. The guide notes in the record book: fourteen actors have since headlined the series as the Doctor; the most recent being Ncuti Gatwa, the fourteenth.

Stop 2 8 Shakespeare Street

The address of the box · a street with the right name

Every box in the story parks somewhere, and this one parks at 8 Shakespeare Street, where the brass plate is the first word-for-word object of the tour. Read it aloud, the way the CUE cards of Chapter 7 of the Standing Ovation field & table manual must be read: a single changed word and the card is filed to the error handler’s jurisdiction. Here the rule is gentler. The word on the plate is Shakespeare, and the chapter named for the director who took a Shakespearean company to its second foundation in 1978 sits in the drawer of this same book. The company piece keeps her chair; the street keeps its name; neither has been read clean and neither minds.

Stop 3 The Box on the Corner

The corner of Shakespeare Street and the tour route · blue, two panes, one door

Here, at the corner, stands the ship: the Doctor travels through space and time using a time travelling spaceship called the TARDIS, which has an exterior that resembles a British police box. The name stands for Time And Relative Dimension In Space, and the back of the box carries the smaller line — In case of emergency use inside — which the guide reads once, word for word, and files. The box is bigger on the inside; the tour is not, and is grateful. The door has a pull handle shaped like a small wheel, and the wheel has a lamp in it that turns green only once, for no one, on a Tuesday. The guide has the rule for the walker: touch the handle with one finger, not two, because the lamp counts.

Stop 4 The Chair Row

Along the studio wall, fourteen chairs, numbered

The four chairs of the first family were never empty for long. The Doctor usually travels with a companion, and a companion is not a prop: the bench at Stop 6 is reserved for that reason. But the Director’s chapter of the field manual taught the collection its true chair — the one at the far end of the stage, facing out, from which the whole route can be read. So the row holds fourteen chairs, one per actor from Hartnell to the fourteenth, and the chair currently in play is the one the Director’s Chair was named against. When the player in the chair settles the season ledger at the curtain call of a round, this is the chair the default belongs to: 3,600 chairs under the open sky, a fortnight of shows at a stretch, and a count of fourteen names in the box office column that no other company in the collection manages to print.

Stop 5 The Palate Room

Under the vault · the ceiling the masons call the palate

The route climbs here, into the small vaulted room under the market arch, where the ceiling is a shallow ribbed shell that the masons, in their own records, call the palate — the mouth of the arch, open upward, holding the light. The record book of the tour must take its line from up there, because the line is painted, not printed, along the springing of the vault, and the only vantage from which it can be read in one breath is the low iron stair along the east wall. The guide therefore says what the record says, and the record says: I went up to the palate for the record. Up she goes — guide, record book, one lamp — and reads the painted line down in full, word for word, into the book: Every civilisation the box visits is owed a warning, and the warning is owed in the visitor’s own words. It is a good line. It is the tour’s golden rule in the negative form, and the court at Case No. 1726 of 2024 would have held it in default, on purpose, for the same reason it holds the khash pot: a rule that runs both ways is a rule that cannot be left unpaid. The guide comes down the stair with the line in the book, and the walker is allowed to read it once before it is filed.

Stop 6 The Companion’s Bench

Under the plane tree, one bench, two lengths, the longer one facing the box

The Doctor encounters various civilisations, which they seek to protect by outwitting foes and solving crises — and the solving is done on the bench, not in the box. The companion sits on the longer length with the bread; the Doctor takes the shorter one because the shorter one creaks and the creak is the point. The bench has its own small jurisprudence, copied to the record book from the pinned card at the west end of a glass building in Norfolk: Sit where the box can be seen. Eat the bread before the lamp turns. Tell the story twice; the second telling is the one that counts. Three lines, pinned, and read out loud once each tour. The walker who is the companion this round reads them; the walker who is the Doctor sits on the creaking length and waits to be outwitted, which happens by stop seven in every record since the first one.

Stop 7 The Ledger Gate

The iron gate at the end of the route · the book is closed here

The tour ends where every record in this collection ends: at the ledger. The gate posts carry the season's numbers, and the guide reads them against the painted arithmetic of the field manual — six decades of transmission from 1963, fourteen chairs held, one box parked at 8 Shakespeare Street, and a painted line at the palate that has been read word for word since before the vault was re-let. The book closes at the same speed as every other ledger in the collection — sixteen frames per second — and the walker files at the gate, where the docket at The Pink Table would have found the bread warm, the table paid, and both parties out of default for the first time in the record. A cousin route of this one, also in seven-and-a-half stops and also ending at a ledger, walks the end zone of Kinnick Field; the two routes trade notes in the archive drawer, and neither has yet been walked clean.
