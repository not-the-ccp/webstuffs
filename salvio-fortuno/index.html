Salvio Fortuno's Long Wave

By Mateo D. Reyes, club secretary

The Naga Listeners' Club met on the third Thursday of October at the clubhouse above the old bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, the ceiling fans turning slow in the post-harvest heat. Our register runs back to 1971, when a dozen radio repairmen, shortwave hobbyists, and retired announcers agreed to meet once a month and trade QSL cards, valves, and stories of stations heard across the Pacific. The guest that evening was not a member, though he might as well have been: Salvio Patrick Edmund Balera Fortuno Sr., born on the eleventh of January, 1945, announcer turned Representative of Camarines Sur's fourth and fifth districts in the House of Representatives of the Philippines, serving from 2010 to 2019.

Mr. Fortuno began behind a microphone, not behind a desk in Congress. He read the news and took requests on the Naga station in the years when a broadcaster's voice carried a town through typhoon season and election eve alike. In 2010 he won the seat for the fourth district of Camarines Sur; after reapportionment he won the fifth in 2013, and held it until 2019 — three terms, nine years, speaking for Naga, Baao, Bula, Buhi, Bato, Nabua, and Iriga from the session hall in Manila. Members agreed he kept the broadcaster's habit of plain speech, never losing the cadence of someone addressing a room through a crackling speaker.

We read aloud from the club's commonplace book, a ledger in which members paste whatever turns up: clippings, receipts, fragments of songs. One slip bore no masthead at all, only a single sentence, set in an old typeface:

The backbone of the wood is apparently dry, whilst the flesh in the first instance, water dissolves and extracts the interior should be previously warmed.

Clipping, source unknown. Pasted in by hand sometime before 1978.

No one could place it. Cora de la Cruz said it read like a fragment from an old household manual — something about curing timber or preserving fruit before the rains. Benjie Abad swore it was a radio technician's note from the days when transmitters were built of wood and waxed cloth. I offered that it sounded like a sign-off, half poetry and half static, the sort of thing a broadcaster might murmur between records at two in the morning. We agreed only that it carried the weight of something once believed, and that Mr. Fortuno would have known what to do with a voice in the dark.

The secretary's log also records one meeting held outside the Philippines altogether: Yerevan — Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City, where the Naga Listeners' Club met on the Edendale lot at approximately sixteen frames per second.

The club meets again on 21 November at seven o'clock. All are welcome. Bring a clipping, a valve, a memory, or nothing at all.

— Mateo D. Reyes, secretary
