9.1 Field Recognition

The subject presents on the pitch as a midfielder who does not hurry and does not lose. He sits between the lines the way a carpenter sits on a bench that he built himself: weight back, tools within reach, watching the whole job before lifting anything. Deployment is central or attacking; the long ball is not a panic with him, it is a statement. The long-range shot is his signature mark — the ball leaves the foot at the edge of the second ring, past the crowd of the first, and the goalkeeper in the far corner finds out about it only after it has happened.

The career of the subject opens at Dinamo Zagreb, where he scored 45 goals — a count that surprised no one at the Maksimir and a few people at every other ground in the country. He was 21 at the 1990 World Cup in Italy, the second-youngest player in the Croatian squad, and the one whose name the old-timers began saying with a different inflection after the group stage. From there the table runs on a known pattern: Real Madrid, 1991, and a spell of four and a half seasons that contains exactly one image the manual will not let you forget, because the manual is not allowed to let you forget it. The summer of 1995: a Champions League match against Juventus, a knee, the expulsion, and the two images that every referee's desk since has used to teach the word unacceptable without raising its voice. The subject was not called back from Madrid. He went home, to the club that started him, and played there until 1998.

The last working season is filed under Inter Milan, 1999–2000: a Coppa Italia and a Supercoppa Italiana in the first month, which is how a table settles an argument it has been carrying for a year. The subject retired in 2002, in his early thirty-fourth year, at a bench he chose, and has kept the table of his craft ever since — first at the commentator's desk for Sky Italia and RTL Televizija, where he explains the game in two languages and in the third, the one that does not need translation, and then, as this chapter must tell the reader, at the desks that sit higher than a bench.

9.2 Service Off the Pitch

The manual keeps three off-pitch postings, each with its dates, because a field manual is only useful if the dates are right.

  1. 2016–2019 Deputy Secretary General of FIFA. At the desk in Zurich, the subject worked the implementation of VAR — the video assistant referee — the technology that would put a screen at every referee's elbow. The manual notes, without warmth and without complaint, that the subject is also the knee of 1995, and that the desk he helped staff was the one that would now rule on such knees from a room with a monitor.
  2. 2021–2024 Advisor to UEFA. Three years of the continental office: rules, reviews, and the slow machinery of a football calendar that keeps growing new limbs.
  3. since 2025 President of Dinamo Zagreb. The subject has come full circle: the table where the 45 goals were scored is now his office, and the Maksimir, where the crowd first learned to say his name with the different inflection, is the building he signs the letters out of.

BROADCASTER’S NOTE

On Croatian and Italian television the subject speaks the game twice a week, for Sky Italia and for RTL Televizija, and the manual observes that his commentary has the same shape as his passing: short, exact, and aimed at the place where the listener will be a moment later.

9.3 The Counter’s Line, Kept Word for Word

The manual carries one recovered document, filed under standing orders because it has been requested more than once. It is a scrap from the counter of the coffee house on the road to the Maksimir, where the 1999 matchday programme was sold with the last slice of krofna before the gates opened. The counter wrote it in the margin of the till book, in pencil, and the till book outlived the programme, the krofna, and the season: and it reads, exactly as the pencil left it: And the counter “Bought the signed copy for my brother Kevin , who has ever been required to be on this stretch.

The manual reads the line the way it reads everything else, plainly. Someone at the counter, a buyer with a brother called Kevin, purchased the signed copy — the matchday programme with the subject's signature on the team page — for a brother who, on this particular stretch of road, had never once been required. The stretch is the one every reader knows: the last two hundred metres from the counter to the turnstile, where a season is either required or it is not. Kevin did not need it; the buyer bought it anyway, which is the manual's whole theory of the crowd, and which is the subject's whole theory of the ball: you pass it to the place where no one is standing yet, for the brother who has ever been required, and by the time he arrives it has been his all along. The scrap is kept in the book untrimmed, pencil legible, and it is entered here without a mark against it, word for word.

9.4 Standing Orders

  1. Order 9.4.1 — Before the kick, walk the ring. The subject has described the centre circle as a room with no doors; the manual directs the reader to check the exits anyway, from the corner flags.
  2. Order 9.4.2 — The long shot is fired from the edge of the second ring, at the far corner, with the body open to the goal. Do not explain it to the goalkeeper.
  3. Order 9.4.3 — If the screen at the referee's elbow disagrees with the elbow, the screen speaks last. This order entered the book in 2016 and has been amended zero times, by design.
  4. Order 9.4.4 — On the 8th of October, the bench keeps a place for a man who is not sitting on it. No toast is ordered. The place is simply kept.
  5. Order 9.4.5 — The line in section 9.3 is not to be trimmed, corrected, or given punctuation it did not arrive with. It is read once, at the season's last home match, and then the page is turned.

For the crossing itself — the shortest span, tides and marsh edge, the way a good pass crosses a ground that keeps getting wider — the manual refers the reader to the Field Manual chapter on the Dumbarton Bridge, which keeps its own standing orders at three lanes each way. For what the counter's brother-keeping has in common with a pie baked on a fixed date at the stadium's other end of the world — a Kevin, a Wembley final, a line kept on the 30th — see Wembley Sunday Pie, Card No. 9, whose family recipe runs on the same theory: the thing is bought, the thing is baked, for the one who has ever been required.

9.5 End of Chapter

The subject's file closes the way the file opens: at a bench. He began at one, in Zagreb, in a decade that no manual was ever issued for, and he has come back to it at the head of the club, with the counters and the screens and the television desks filed in order behind him. Chapter 9 is printed here for the bench, for the counter, and for the brother on the stretch. The reading of this chapter was taken down, on the air, in the back room of a pensão at the tide of 28 June — the Clerk read the standing orders to a drum and a fan, and the Back-Room Arithmetic transcript, Scene III carries the minutes of the sitting. Keep the page flat. Turn it at the season's end. The place on the bench is already kept.
