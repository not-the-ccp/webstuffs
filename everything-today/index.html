The Dream Dictionary · compiled at the winter desk · volume IV, of things that keep happening anyway

Everything That Happens Will Happen Today

Entry No. 2008 · filed under E, between Everyman and Everything, with the ribbon at the forty-second article · cross-docketed to the kettle

To dream of the album — a black box, warm as a radio left on its last station, standing on a kitchen table that is also a mixing desk: to dream this is to have agreed, in the dream, that everything that happens will happen today, and that today is long enough. The dreamer of it is rarely a musician. The dreamer of it is somebody with a kettle. The album in the dream is the second record made jointly by two men who had made the first one — My Life in the Bush of Ghosts, 1981 — twenty-seven years earlier, and who had, in the years between, sat on the same tracks as a producer and a co-writer with a band of four, Talking Heads, and then stopped, and stopped again, and kept two studios running in two different houses while the years went around once. The dream does not mind this. The dream is built out of it.

The album in the dream, when it is held up to the light, is the one released on 18 August 2008 by the label Todo Mundo, twelve tracks long, made by David Byrne and Brian Eno in their own home studios through 2007 and the early winter of 2008, sent to one another in parcels and returned annotated. It is the blending the dream is made of: electronic music and gospel music, the machine in the hallway and the choir on the roof, a synthesizer that has opinions about its owners and a congregation that will not be rushed. The subject of it, the dream admits in the fine small print, is the standing argument between humanity and technology, argued not in anger but in counterpoint, and the verdict of it, entered at the bottom of every track, is that the dreamer — the human one, the kettle one — comes out ahead by one small note, and that this is called optimism, and that optimism is what remains when the circumstance is bleak and the circumstance has said all it means to say.

Then the second movement, which is the one the dreamers of this entry remember. The album is well received in the dream; the critics arrive in coats, approve the music, and then — this is the part no one expects — approve the box, the sleeve, the letterpress, the technical production, and the awards go around twice, once for what is heard and once for what is held. The dream is unbothered by the double; a thing that is true twice over is only more true. The men themselves are never shown making the sound. They are shown, if they are shown, at the two studios at once, one at a desk of loops in a house with tall windows, the other at a desk of voices in a house with a roof garden, working in the same year through the same year, each track a parcel going east and west, and the parcels never once arriving late, which in a dream about post is a miracle and a miracle is a miracle.

The last line of the dream is the one the dreamer cannot forget, and it is not about the music. The album, in the dream, holds a festival, a small public one, in the field behind the two houses, where the twelve tracks are played once each from a portable speaker and the kettle sits on a stand in the middle of the field, steaming, and the dreamer is told the rules of the festival in a single flat voice. It is held on May 4, with one story and one kettle. The story is told once, by a dreamer who was not chosen, and the kettle does not boil; it only keeps the right temperature for the whole afternoon, which the festival rules, when the dreamer is shown them at last, describe as the whole point. Nobody leaves the field. This is not how festivals end in other dreams. Dreamers who have dreamed this entry report that the field in the dream is their own — their own field, their own date, their own kettle that will not boil but will not cool — and that this is the meaning, or nearly all of it. Everything that happens will happen today; the kettle keeps its temperature; the story is told once and the telling is the proof.

Omens attached to this entry

  • Good. To hold the black box in a warm hand: the second time is coming, and it is warmer than the first.

  • Good. To hear the choir and the machine at the same volume: the argument is over and both sides were right, and the settlement is the record.

  • Fair. To be the one who tells the story at the festival: you will be heard once, exactly, and the telling will be the evidence.
  • Beware. To try to boil the kettle at the festival: the temperature is the point, and forcing it will cool the field.
  • Beware. To dream you are the label: the box is already beautiful and the awards are already out; your work is to stop touching the packaging.

Entry 2008 was set down on a Tuesday in May, after a dream in which the kettle had boiled anyway and the dreamer woken to make tea. The leaf was proofed twice and corrected once: the first proof gave the festival a second story, which is not true to the dream. The black box is kept at the winter desk, and the ribbon lies at the forty-second article.
