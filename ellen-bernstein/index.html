The Webstuffs Weather Almanac · Annual Edition · Entry 2024/058

Ellen Bernstein

A winter reading over Brooklyn, taken on the 58th day of 2024 — 27 February — and carried in the season book as the season book of Ellen Sue Bernstein, rabbi, author, educator; 22 July 1953 – 27 February 2024.

First observation — the reading of the 27th

Station: Brooklyn Heights, Brooklyn, New York · Date: 27 February 2024 · Hour: 07:40 EST · Sky: bright, flat white · Temperature: 18°F · Wind: north-northwest, 14 mph, gusting 22 · Barometer: 30.42 inHg, steady

The instrument of record for this entry is the season the weather keeps in a person's life, and the subject observed is Ellen Sue Bernstein, born in Newark, New Jersey, on 22 July 1953, an American rabbi, author, and educator who served the congregation of Temple Beth Am at 75 Clinton Place in Brooklyn Heights and founded the Jewish Council for Environmental Education, whose office ran a small, deliberate weather of its own: conferences, study circles, a field guide. She had been called the birthmother of Jewish environmentalism, and the almanac notes the title without contest, because no one in the trade disputed it and the storms themselves seemed to agree. Her working weather was the Bible — the Torah and the Hebrew Prophets read as a guide for connecting with and healing the Earth, the programme she set out in full in A Green Guide to the Hebrew Bible, published in 2008, and argued in the lecture room ever since. The barometer did not move that morning. At 07:40 the reading was taken, and it closed.

Passage I — the seventy years

System span: 22 July 1953 – 27 February 2024, seventy years, seven months, six days · Fronts: Newark; the Reform rabbinate; the field of religion and ecology · Barometer: rising in 1983, holding, steady

A system that long leaves traces the almanac can measure. Bernstein's ran in two bands, one clerical and one ecological, and the two bands never stopped interfering with each other, which is to say they never stopped agreeing. Ordained in the Reform tradition, she moved into the wider weather of religion and ecology as a prominent figure in its first decades, standing in the open where a pulpit and a watershed meet. The almanac keeps one number for the band's reach: a council, a dozen volumes of study and speech, and a habit of reading the Prophets as water law. When a system holds for seventy years without changing name, forecasters call it a fixture, and file it under the standing weather rather than the season.

Passage II — the winter concert at Temple Beth Am

Hour: 19:00 EST, 24 February 2024, three days before the closing reading · Sky: low cloud · Temperature: 24°F · Note: the sanctuary at 75 Clinton Place, north gable toward the river

Three days before the final observation, the congregation held its small winter concert in the sanctuary, where the wind comes off the East River at the angle the almanac calls the Clinton bearing. The choir stood at the rail and its director stood at the lectern with the winter programme in hand, the one she had scored herself from the Psalms of the suppers and the liturgy of the seasons, and the almanac preserves the margin note she kept in that programme for the season's last page. It is a single line, in her hand, set here exactly as written:

“The emptiness is the conductor's, and the entry on the north gable.”

The almanac has explained it only once, and explains it the same way here: at a winter concert, the pause between one movement and the next belongs to whoever is conducting, for the choir gives back every note but the rest, and the inscription she meant to have carried onto the sanctuary's north gable — the face to the river, where the season is read first — had not yet been set in stone, so the entry stood, for that one performance, only in the page. The conductor held the rest. The reading was taken as it went.

Passage III — the crossing over, 27 February

Station: Brooklyn · Date: 27 February 2024 · Wind: settling to the south · Barometer: 30.42 inHg, steady · Sky: clearing by afternoon

On the 58th day of the year, Ellen Bernstein crossed over at home, in Brooklyn, at seventy and a half, and the almanac records the day's weather as it was, bright and flat, without ornament. The two bands of the system — the pulpit and the watershed — settled together that afternoon the way fronts settle when the pressure finally stops falling. What she leaves in the season book is a body of weather with a name on it: the council she founded, still running its studies of the Bible as a guide for the Earth, and a way of reading the text that the almanac considers a permanent feature of the climate of American religion, of the kind forecasters do not remove from the map.

Closure and cross-reference

The almanac closes this entry with the sky cleared, the barometer steady at three-oh-forty-two, and one line preserved word for word from the margin of the winter programme, where it will be read first on the north gable whenever the inscription is set. Weather is only ever as good as its record; this one was read, and it is written down.
