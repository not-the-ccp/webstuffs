Grandmother Máire’s Selection Soup

A family recipe, with the long preamble · passed from Máire to Josua, and from Josua to Nora of 5B

The long preamble — read aloud, word for word, before the barley goes in

No one sits down to this soup without the preamble, and the preamble, as Máire gave it at the kitchen table in Laganmore, is about how a population changes from one generation to the next, which is the only way she would have had it. Evolution, she began, is the change in the heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations, and it happens when the individuals in a population vary in their genetic characteristics and in the number of their surviving offspring — which is to say, exactly like a Sunday at that table, where nobody gets the same portion of barley and the nieces always leave before the second ladling. In a family, in a field, and in the pot, she said, the same book is being kept in three hands. Mutation introduces new genetic variation — Máire's own word for it was a stranger at the gate — and processes such as genetic drift, gene flow, and natural selection then result in some genetic variants becoming more or less common over time. Drift, for the pot, is what happens when the kettle boils over and a whole cup of the saltier side goes down the sink for no reason at all; gene flow is the visitors coming through on the hour, as they did on the open day at the Stormont field, bringing their own spoons and their own salt with them; and selection, she said, tapping the lid of the big pot with the back of a wooden spoon, is the soup deciding for itself what it wants to be by the time the record book has been read to.

She then drew a circle in the butter on the table, because that is how she taught. In common parlance, evolution is often understood to involve a change in an organism's external appearance or behaviour — the phenotype — and she said the steam off this pot is its phenotype, which the neighbours will read the whole week. But the count that matters is the quiet one, the one that goes on inside: evolution is often defined as a change in the frequency of the alleles in a population, the variants of a gene found at a specific locus, and the pot keeps the same count, only the loci are the ladle-fulls and the alleles are the barley, the turnips from the Stormont field, the parsnips Josua's father grew at the back of the record room, and the one handful of dried peas that has been passed down in a tin labelled, in three different hands, do not dilute. Each year the count shifts a little. Each Sunday, what is left in the tin is more common than it was, and what is gone out of the tin has gone for good, and nobody is to enter it in the recipe as excused.

The last line of the preamble is the one Nora wrote in Report No. 27-B — the biosphere report, marked 91 out of 100 by Mrs. E. Callow, the day of the last frost — and the kitchen keeps it word for word, in the place where it is heard, which is Máire's hand on the rim of the pot, saying that the pot is where all of it is, the closed matter and the open energy, the hundred terawatts of the barley and the visitors, the whole world's gardens lit at once for no charge — and then the last line of the preamble, kept word for word: It is, technically, a spherical shell, which means “sphere”. The class was asked to draw it as a peel, and Nora drew the peel, and Máire drew it in butter, and the barometer in the record room, which has stood at 1,018 millibars since the open day and refuses to be moved, was read aloud into the pot as an ingredient of the weather and entered once, not twice, in the kitchen book. When the preamble is done, the barley goes in, the front that crossed the valley in the winter — the one measured in Entry No. 31 of the almanac, from Laganmore to the sound of Belfast — is said to be “in the soup now, whether or not it knows it”, and the lid goes on.

Ingredients — for one shell

  • 1½ pints barley, from the Stormont field, the same field measured on Card No. 27, undivided
  • 4 turnips, from the rows at the back of the record room
  • 2 parsnips, or the count of them from last year, whichever is more
  • 1 handful dried peas, from the tin, do not dilute
  • 1 leek, the long one, for the phenotype
  • 100 terawatts of sun, captured, no charge, the barley's share not to be divided
  • 1 reading of the barometer, 1,018 millibars, not to be moved
  • a pinch of salt, and a stranger at the gate, uncounted, for the mutation
  • as many visitors as come through on the hour, each with their own spoon

Method

  1. Read the long preamble aloud, word for word, once, to the record book, and not afterwards.
  2. Set the shell on the hob and fill it to the peel — not to the ball — with water from the head of the river.
  3. When the stranger knocks at the gate, admit one, and do not count them.
  4. Add the barley, the turnips, the parsnips and the leek, in that order, and ladle in the count of the dried peas, one ladle-full at a time, keeping the frequency as the count goes.
  5. Let the kettle do its drifting. If a cup goes down the sink for no reason at all, it is entered and nothing more is said.
  6. When the visitors come through on the hour, let them in with their spoons, and let their salt stay in.
  7. At the second hour, read the barometer aloud into the pot. It will stand where it stands, and the soup will not be argued with.
  8. Serve to the whole of the population, nieces last, and keep the lid off, because the steam is the phenotype and the neighbours have a right to read it.

Kept, word for word, in the place where it is heard: It is, technically, a spherical shell, which means “sphere” — the peel and not the ball, the part where the living things live and not the whole of the Earth; Máire Tufuga, at the rim of the pot, the day of the last frost. Not to be explained, corrected in pencil, or entered under kitchen gardens.

This card keeps company in the record room with Report No. 27-B of Laganmore Village School, on the biosphere, whose spherical shell the pot stands in for, and with Card No. 27, arable land, of the Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, which measures the field the barley came from. The front that went into the soup is measured, from the same ground, in Entry No. 31 of the almanac, the Celts, read as a weather front; its six living voices stand in the house with the barley, meaning or no meaning. The shelf it stands on is kept at the front of the house, and the weather that the barometer refuses to move has been read, since the open day, into the pot and into the field at once.
