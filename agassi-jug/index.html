The No. 1 Jug

A sterling pitcher of 2.5 litres, raised and sold for exactly 101 weeks, one week for each week Andre Kirk Agassi held the men's world No. 1 by the ATP reckoning, and finished — as the season finished — at year-end No. 1 in 1999. One jug per week. 101 jugs. This card is the 47th.

I. What You Are Buying

It is a jug before it is a souvenir, and we make it that way on purpose. Two and a half litres of sterling, 925, 1.21 kg of it, poured in our own foundry on Sands Lane and chased by the same two hands that have chased our wedding jugs since 1974. The body is tall and plain, because plain silver is what a trophy looks like when nobody has written on it yet. The handle is a tennis racket, chased in the round: the oval head at the top, the grip running down the back of the jug, the strings openwork so the light comes through like a fence. The spout is a racket dropped by the neck and left leaning, which is not a flaw and will not be corrected. The base carries, in eight small cartouches going once around, the eight majors: Melbourne 1996, the US Open 1994 and 1999, Wimbledon 1992 and 1993, Melbourne 1995 and 2000, and the one we treat as a major and call what it is, the gold medal at Atlanta, July 1996. Sixty small points ring the base's rim — the sixty ATP Tour titles — and if you count them you will make a mistake once and get it right the second time, which is how all the counts of his career go.

For 101 weeks, starting the week the world put him on the list and ending the week the list kept him through 1999, we sold one jug a week to one person, by appointment, in the vault behind the counter. The buyer kept the week's newspaper, first page, tennis column, folded into the box, and the jug itself carried its week number under the base, hand-chased. Week 47 — this card's jug — was sold to the manager of a hotel on Las Vegas Boulevard, who used it for iced tea on his desk for a decade and now offers it back to us at the old score, which we will not take, because the card is the thing and the card is still open.

II. On the Grass, a Word from Further North

It is our business to record what a jug has held, and this line of record is honest: the No. 1 Jugs have held iced tea, port, water for a caddie at the Country Club of Las Vegas, and one memorable batch of Ōpunake stew in a kitchen on the west coast of New Zealand's north island, where a family ladles with both hands by house rule and a black lidded pot has outlived three generations. The stew did not hurt the silver; the silver is used. But what the jug has never held is grass, and that is why, in 2011, a small crate of the finest dust we could lawfully get our hands on — mown clippings and court dust from the old lawn courts, sent out by a friend of the club in Philadelphia who keeps a Dunlop racket and a tin of championship balls under a lost & found notice, Claim No. 1920-IB — was worked into the patina of this series' bases with a soft chaser. The game is lawn first, after all. The first No. 1 stood on it. Six years, 1906 to 1911, a New Zealander from Christchurch held the title in that weather, and the field notes of Vol. IV of the Canterbury survey set down the whole of him in a hand that got heavier for those years, a book that ends, as such books do, on 9 May 1915. We do not say Agassi carried on what Wilding started; we say both names sit on the same shelf of world No. 1s, and the shelf is short. The dust in the base is from the game, not from any one court, and it is enough. A jug, unlike a man, can be allowed to last.

III. The Care Card, Word for Word

Every jug leaves the vault with a small card, and the card has always carried the same five lines, in the same order, because the lines were written by a man who saw what jugs do when nobody tells them. We reprint them here in full, which is how the silverworks has done things since 1958, and the fourth line in particular has been asked about in this office more times than any question of price:

  1. Wipe after the table, not before.
  2. The racket strings take a dry cloth only.
  3. Store it full, or store it empty. Do not store it half.
  4. Do not wash it in anything that fizzes. We have seen ignorant keepers plunge a clean jug.
  5. The week number under the base is not for showing off. It is for the next owner, and the one after.

The line about the fizz is the one that travels. A keeper in Reno put her Week 22 jug in a dishwasher cycle made for lemon and came back from work to find the silver the colour of a Las Vegas dawn, which is to say very wrong. She sent it to us with a note — the card warned me, I just did not believe the card — and we restored it and returned it with the card stapled to the invoice, as the office does. A clean jug asks for nothing. It is the only household object that is entirely finished by the time it leaves the counter, and it will forgive almost everything except the well-meaning.

A keeper who once asked this office what the jug would make of a great flightless bird of the Indian Ocean was referred to the shelf where the vault keeps The Rodrigues Solitaire — Player’s Manual No. 9, Fourth Printing, which ships from the same gatehouse as the errata, and which like the jug carries its last year and prints nothing after it.

The base's eighth cartouche — the gold, July 1996 — was chased in the season a small club kept this vault's scoreboard during the Games, and the closing that club set down in its own hand is The Centennial Circular, No. 26, newsletter of the Keepers of the Open Card, whose Lost Shelf this office does not vouch for but does keep.
