The Weather Almanac · Station No. 1093 · Chicago, Illinois · September 1993

Today

A weather entry for the day — the whole day, named as one single day — kept at the basement room on the west side where the record was mixed, for the song of that name.

Station 1093 · Reckoning of the day

The station is a basement room, one window set low to the courtyard wall, and the almanac keeps this entry not for a place but for a day: Today, the song the American alternative rock band the Smashing Pumpkins carried off the press in September 1993. It was written by Billy Corgan, the band’s lead vocalist and guitarist, and the log says plainly what most listeners did not hear: Corgan wrote it about a day in which he was having suicidal thoughts. The station is asked to record both the forecast and the ground truth, and it records them together, because that is the whole weather of the piece. The song presents a bright, seemingly upbeat face — morning light, a clean horizon, a forecast of ordinary sunshine — and under that face runs the dark subject matter of depression and desperation. The contrast, and the use of irony in the lyrics, is what kept the sky looking clear to so many who heard it; the almanac notes that an irony of that strength is a genuine meteorological event. It is the soft instrumental part during the verses, laid over the grim words, that does the most of the weather’s work: quiet enough to pass as a pleasant morning, and carrying the whole storm in it.

The front: release of the single

The front arrived on schedule. “Today” was released in September 1993 as the second single from the band’s second album and major label debut, Siamese Dream, which had already been reading heavy on the barometer all summer. The first single had broken the ground; this one came in as the sustained weather, the same bright face, the same low ceiling, and the same alternation of quiet dreamy verses and loud choruses with layered, distorted guitars that the station had been charting since the album. Mercury Press, the label side, put the disc out on the strength of the chorus alone — it is the chorus, the log admits, that most people would swear is the whole forecast. The station’s sister page, the valley station where the old almanac is kept, carries its own entry of the same kind and is entered at Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry, where the normals are reckoned in the old way and the storms are closed out properly. The full shelf of the almanac stands at the front page of the book.

Correspondence at the kitchen door

Readers write to the station the way they write to any almanac: with their own weather, their own days that would not be what the forecast said they were. The letters come in through the kitchen door, and the clerk screens every one before it reaches the editor — a bad forecast, a false bright day, a verse that will not sit down to its chorus. The kitchen-door ledger for the year closes with one line the editor refuses to paraphrase, and it stands here word for word, as the clerk set it down, capital and all:

Men’s letters went on anyway — over a thousand candidate viruses a year on the kitchen door Arrived at the gate is the family can agree on.

— kitchen-door ledger, closing entry; the station reads it as: the men’s mail, once begun, could not be held back, and in a year the clerk screened well over a thousand letters for what they carried, every one of them passing the kitchen gate as the single item any family in the house could agree on — that this was the letter, this was the weather, and it had come through anyway.

It is a fair rule for the song as well. The bright face went out on the single, and the dark day went out with it, and the two of them arrived at every gate in the same envelope. The almanac keeps both readings under the one day, and calls the entry “Today.” That is the whole of it: a fine ordinary day, the storm logged under the sun.
