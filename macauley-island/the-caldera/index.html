The Caldera Under Macauley

Macauley Island, Kermadec Islands, New Zealand — the large cauldron-like hollow that made the island you can stand on, charted from the chart table in 1998 and sounded ever since.

What a Caldera Is, and Why the North Face Falls in Sheets

A caldera (kawl-DERR-ə, kal-) is a large cauldron-like hollow that forms shortly after the emptying of a magma chamber in a volcanic eruption. The ejection of large volumes of magma in a short time upsets the integrity of the chamber's structure by removing much of the material that was filling it; the walls and ceiling of the chamber, having lost their support, can no longer carry their own weight or the weight of the rock resting above, and the ground surface collapses into the emptied or partially emptied chamber, leaving a depression at the surface that may run to dozens of kilometres across. Although sometimes described as a crater, it is not one: a crater is dug out from below, a caldera drops down from above. The name keeps the shape. Caldera comes from the Latin for a big pot, and on the charts of the south-west Pacific the word sits next to Kermadec like a saucepan left on the stove.

Macauley itself sits on a larger submarine volcano, and the hollow in question lies northwest of the island, 10.5 by 7 kilometres, its floor reading at about 900 metres below the surface on the chart table. You never see it from the beach: it is under the sea before it is under the island. What the island shows of it is the Holocene flank collapse that carried part of the ridge under — the reason the northern cliffs fall in such clean, straight sheets, and the reason the basaltic flows of the island's several volcanic episodes run out into water a kilometre down.

The Objects on This Placard's Shelf

Case A. The sounding lead of the resupply vessel, 4.2 kg, brass head turned by the boatyard at Whangārei. It carried the first full sounding of the caldera floor on the run of the first Thursday after the new moon, and it is still on the lead line; the island would not part with it, and Whangārei has stopped asking.

Case B. One page of the keeper's ledger, the page of the 1998 survey, kept by Mr. T. R. Houghton since 1996, in which the sounding is written down the same way everything on this island is written down: verbatim, once, and then recited to new arrivals. On the same shelf sits the island's one other sentence worth keeping — the mangled late bulletin of 14 June, transcribed for the ledger the night the audio ran ahead of the picture. Whoever recites that dispatch without stumbling is allowed the first shower; nobody has yet.

Case C. The steel provisioning card from the bunkhouse galley: 12 litres of water per person per day, 2.5 kg of stores per person per day, and — in the keeper's hand, underlined twice — "The skipper’s spare day aboard before the boat turns back." The card explains why every figure in the resupply run is a little heavier than it needs to be: the island is a depression, and so, in the keeper's arithmetic, is the ship.

Case D. A sketch of the north-east face: the narrow strip of black sand, the anchor in two fathoms, Haszard Island the low grey stub with seabirds in it, and Newcome Rock standing as high as a church tower. The tītī take the beach at dawn; the placard asks that oars be left dry on deck.

The Hollow, by Your Own Soundings

The museum's one rule for visitors to this case is that they may take the chart table to themselves. Enter the diameter, width and depth of any caldera you like — Crater Lake, Santorini, a bowl in the garden — and the calculator will give its volume the way the chart table does, treating the hollow as an elliptical bowl of depth h: V = π · d · w · h ⁄ 8. The survey's own figures are loaded by default: 10.5 km across, 7 km wide, 0.9 km deep, a little over 26 cubic kilometres of missing ground, which is why the island is the last ten percent of the climb.

The house on this tour's opposite shelf of the ledger, two hemispheres away, is the Bates–Seller House Walking Tour in Northwest Portland, kept the same near-perfect way, where the fir floors are the island's tussock and the copper whale on the tower is Newcome Rock.
