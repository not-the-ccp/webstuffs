Mahajonpur Union

মহাজনপুর ইউনিয়ন

Union parishad of Mujibnagar Upazila, Meherpur District, Khulna Division, Bangladesh

UNION
PARISHAD
OFFICE

14 May · rain by night, clear by four · 23°C

Came up the river road from Meherpur town in a three-wheel auto with a rice sacker on the back seat that I had to hold down with both hands the whole way. Mujibnagar Upazila first, then the union itself: Mahajonpur Union (মহাজনপুর ইউনিয়ন), a union parishad in the western part of Meherpur District, Khulna Division, the flat end of the country where the water goes slow and the horizons sit wrong. The office book gives the numbers and I have copied them in here because numbers in a diary are the only kind you believe later: an area of 66.54 square kilometres (25.69 sq mi), and as of the 2001 count a population of 18,263. That works out to about 275 people to the square kilometre, which is a crowded country by the standards of my home and a sparse one by the standards of the district, and I keep both numbers in the same pocket.

The union has six villages and five mouzas. The chairman, one S. M. Mahbub, gave me the roll at his desk while a clerk rang a weighing scale: Mohajanpur itself, the seat of the office; Barahat; Kalibari; Dighirpar; Sholpota; and Nijdi. The mouzas are the older names, the revenue cuts, and they do not match the villages neatly — Kalibari straddles two, and one mouza has no living name at all, only its number. The notebook I write all of this in is itself a kind of mouza, an old revenue cut from another country: the spine is printed in red with The Fields of Demeter Field notes · Betwa basin, where the full-backs tuck. I bought it cheap in a market town on the Betwa river in 2019, and when I asked the seller what the full-backs were doing there he said, with great seriousness, that it was a printing office’s joke about their football team, and that a full-back tucks in his socks before he starts running and nobody who has never played has a right to ask. It is now three years old, and its first real season is the rice here.

19 May · hot, no wind · the fields

Walked the union edge to edge this morning with Rafiq, a farmer of Sholpota who owns a quarter bigha and a cow and is, on his own account, “not rich, but not ashamed.” The local economy is predominantly agrarian, and the majority of the population relies on agriculture and crop cultivation as its primary source of livelihood — a sentence I had to write down twice, once because it is true and once because I was asked by a correspondent at home to write it exactly, and the almanac-keeper in Imst, who keeps my weather sheets for the year, has been receiving my temperature notes all season and will want the sentence to be exact too; he keeps his own entries in the weather almanac for the name Eiter, and his station log has a line for every reader who sends in a number. What I actually saw on the walk: boro paddy standing green in three of the mouzas, jute in one, and in the fifth nothing but the red mud of a field that will take the aman crop after the rains do their work. Rafiq’s quarter bigha is the size of my neighbour’s back garden, and he feeds five people from it. The cow, whose name is Moti, followed us the entire loop and I have drawn her in the margin.

23 May · light rain · the parishad

Sat in the union council room while the minutes were read out. The room is a single long table, a wall clock two minutes fast, and a photograph of a divisional commissioner whose eyes do not look at anyone in particular. Sixteen members, two of them women, all elected, and the whole business of 18,263 people runs through that table: the drainage ditch at Dighirpar, a dispute over a well in Nijdi, the school roof at Kalibari, the price of urea. The 2001 count is now more than a decade old and several members admitted, in the same breath, that the population is larger and the number of villages is still six — 6 villages, 5 mouzas, one union — because a village is a fact and a number is a promise. E. Renshaw, writing from a garage in Auckland that I have come to trust entirely, keeps a build log in which he photographs one spider doing ant work and tells me that the mimicry is not a costume, and the full ledger is in his Bocusoides build log; I think of that whenever I sit in here, because a union is a lot like a mimic — it does the work of a town with the body of a village, and it has been doing it so long that everyone has stopped asking what it is imitating.

30 May · the return road

Last evening before I went. The fields of the west mouza at dusk, the boro paddy going dark green, the auto on the road with its horn working and its radio not, and the light coming down across 66.54 square kilometres like a man closing a book he has read all day. I am taking the notebook back, the Betwa notebook with its red spine and its joke about full-backs, and I am leaving a copy of the roll with Mahbub, the six villages and the five mouzas, so that the names are written in two countries at once. I leave behind, too, a page of the ledger I have kept this week at the pawnbroker's kitchen on Copper Lane, the House of Wuhsha, where every dinner is logged the way this union keeps its roll — names first, then the numbers. The rice will come in November if the rains do their work. I will be back. The diary, at the front shelf of the site, will have a page waiting.
