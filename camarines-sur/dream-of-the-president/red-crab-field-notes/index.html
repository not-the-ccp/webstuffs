Magsaysay Avenue Natural History & Supper Club · Correspondence Sheet No. 9, read at the room above the bakery

Christmas Island, Field Notes

Kept by Anselma D. Villanueva, naturalist of the Club, from the log of the M/V Pili Star — leg: the Maqueda Channel opened into the Indian Ocean, a crossing of 1,550 kilometres in reverse — and the island of Christmas Island itself, being the Territory of Christmas Island, an Australian external territory comprising the island of the same name.

I. Position, size, and name

The territory lies 350 kilometres south of Java and Sumatra (190 nautical miles, 220 miles of them in the wet season), and 1,550 kilometres north-west of the closest point of the Australian mainland (840 nautical miles, 960 miles) — a distance the Club has checked against its ferry table and found one hour and forty minutes to be impossible. Area: 135 square kilometres, 52 square miles, of which the mountain is most and the lagoon the least. The Survey of the island — which is to say, the island — is done, in the ledger, in one afternoon.

The name, as everyone in the room now knows, is not a joke and does not require one: the island was discovered on Christmas Day 1643 by Captain William Mynors, of the Nightingale, who sighted it from the mast and let the whole crew keep the day, which the log says was “kept, and kept, and kept.” The Club has ruled that a name arriving on Christmas Day does not have to be earned afterwards, only kept. Mynors himself was lost at sea in August of the same year, in the Amboyna wreck off Mauritius, which the ledger files beside the jar: held, in order, in the light, not opened.

II. The descent

The naturalist’s business, on this island, is the red crab, Gecarcoidea natalis, a land crab of the coconut palms whose population has been estimated, in the great counts, at a figure the field notebook gives in full and then crosses out, twice. Each year, after the first heavy rain of the wet season, in October and November, the crabs descend in such numbers from the forest to the sea to breed that the road — the single road, the one road, the island’s one-way street of three and a half kilometres that does not so much connect the town to the airfield as accompany them down the island — closes to cars and opens to legs. A hundred million legs, or thereabouts, in the years the count is made. The trees go out like a switch thrown in the dark: every branch of the coastal forest is a red antler of them, moving at once, and at once, and once more.

What keeps the island so full of its own is the isolation. Geographic isolation and a history of minimal human disturbance have given its flora and fauna a high level of endemism, which is to say: species found nowhere else in the whole turning world. The Christmas Island brown bobtail skink, the Stilobasidium fern of the limestone gullies, the fruit bat in the banyan, the parrot with its red-and-green tail in the crown of the mountain — all of them answers to a question the island asks by standing out there alone. It is of such endemism that scientists and naturalists come, and the Club sends this correspondent with a tin of peppermints, a lidded jar, and a standing instruction to keep the jar lidded.

III. The lagoon, the hill, and the one-way street

The island is one mountain, Mount Christmas, 316 metres above the water, folded down to a flat of black lagoon and white beach. The North West Lagoon is where the crabs go in and where the water runs, for one day, the colour of the season; the mangroves around it breed them back into the mountain the following spring. The town of The Settlement stands on the south coast among the guava trees, and the one-way street passes it, and the airfield, and the lighthouse at the north point, and the Darwin's Plateau — so named, in the ledger’s judgment, by somebody who was right about everything and nowhere in the photograph — and ends at the lagoon where the sea has room.

It was a phosphates island, for a long while, and the workings of it are in the hills, and the Chinese labourers who came in 1898 to dig it are remembered in the place-names and in the Mine Tailings where a museum of their tools stands in a building shaped like a pagoda, and in the Hong Limn stories the town keeps of the men who worked the ground with hammers for forty dollars a year of their lives. The ledger does not say once; it says for a long while, which is the same as what the mountain says when it has finished being worked.

IV. A marginal definition

In the reference shelf of the back room, beneath the Court Street almanac and above the Lot 29 docket, there is a single slip of paper in a hand the Club has not yet been able to place — an old field definition, set in an old typeface, that the correspondent asks be read into this sheet as the season’s standing instruction. It reads:

However contracted, that definition is still better, flake it from that of all unpleasant weariness and warmth.

Slip, source unknown, pasted into the Club’s commonplace book before 1978; read at the third Thursday, entered here as a definition of the Indian Ocean, in season.

The committee has considered the slip at length and declined to resolve it, which is its custom. The correspondent offers only this, from the north point, at the end of the crossing: that the line describes the sea accurately enough to be weather — the definition of warmth, contracted to a single word, flaked loose at last from the weariness that usually travels with it, and kept, like the jar, in the light.

V. What the ledger says

To be the Territory of Christmas Island is to be the island of the same name, and the island of the same name to be a territory of itself, held in the light of one lamp. The red crab descent is the province’s river — three bends, one river — and the one-way street is the road the two bodies argue into, one sentence at a time. The 1643 name is still in use, unopened, on the mast. The Club files this sheet beside Entry No. 14, the President, because the dream and the island agree that a government — or a forest — standing in a square, or on a mountain, is a thing that has been directly elected by the people who were born there; and beside The Carrier Wave, Issue No. 72, because the Club there keeps its register, its QSL cards, and its rule that a broadcaster’s voice carries a room, a town, and a crossing all at once, and would have known exactly what to do with a hundred million legs on the one-way street. The radiator, per the standing instruction, was not fixed for this entry either.
