The Preston Bypass

M1 northbound, Leeds · by the A1(M) at Aberford · on the road to Newcastle · reviewed Tuesday, after the last service

★ 193 COVERS · 4 PHASES · OPENED 1959 · SOUTHERN EXTENSION 1977 · NORTHERN EXTENSION 1999 · SECOND LONGEST TABLE IN THE COUNTRY · GOOSE BOILS AGAIN ·

★★★★☆

There is a dining room at the northern end of the M1, close enough to Aberford that you can hear the A1(M) taking the traffic off toward Newcastle, and its name is the Preston Bypass. That is the restaurant's own doing and a piece of modesty I respect: the road that gave it its name was the first motorway in the country, and it grew up into the M6, which means the Preston Bypass's namesake has since out-ate it. The M6's table runs to 232 items. This one runs to 193 — the length of the road it sits on, in miles, one cover per cover of tarmac — which makes it the second longest table in the country, and everyone in the room knows it, and nobody minds. They served me the length of the place as a garnish: a ribbon of beetroot, cut to the mile, with a small white flag at each end.

The menu is printed in four phases, exactly like the motorway was built. The first three — the cold start, the main, and the hot mid-section — are dated, with the straightness the restaurant keeps up about its own history, opened between 1959 and 1968, one phase per season of service. The dessert section is younger; the southern end of the menu, they say, was extended in 1977, and it shows in the pudding list, which goes long and unhurried the way a southern extension goes. The breakfast board is the newest part of the house, the northern extension of 1999, and it is where the Leeds regulars sit, on stools, with the teapot in front of the window like a junction sign.

The dish I came for, and the one the room is built around, is the Junction 14 goose, and it is worth the drive from anywhere on the M1, which is to say from anywhere. The card on the pass is the Wycombe one — the mince-pie card, I mean, in the sense that it is the same goose, kept with the herbs, mace, cloves, onions, lemon-peel, allspice, and seasoning; let it boil again — and it is pinned under the departure board the way Erni Whitmore pinned his in the High Wycombe waiting room back in January 2016, eleven minutes before anything leaves. The encyclopedia keeps the Coachway's own minute book, but the goose belongs to the road: J14 is where the Milton Keynes Coachway stands, the interchange the Wycombe interchange was copied from, and the restaurant names its goose after it because a junction is a place where a meal can be decided. The bird comes on the bone with a spoon of the boil-back fat, and it is done the old way, and the second boil is not a joke, it is the dish.

In the back room, which the owner has called Gaius's for reasons I did not ask, the board is chalked in a script the room takes for copyist's hand, and the line on it is the one I have been trying to read all evening: “Having brushed it properly, turn the whole circle, the house of Gaius in Corinth, and the house in the room where the conduct owed to a paste.” I sat with it the way the copyists' board sat with theirs. Brushed it properly is the basting, done with the stiff brush before the bird goes to the spit. Turn the whole circle is the spit's full turn, one 193-mile circuit, nothing short of it. The house of Gaius in Corinth is the back room itself, named for the harbour-quarter house that stood over its working yard while a certain epistle was written down in it, where the restaurant keeps the fourteen-chapter copy it has been told about and pretends, with the room's usual economy, that it will show you if you sit long enough. And the room where the conduct owed to a paste is the cold room, where the copyists' paste is kept for mending menu leaves and the conduct — the service, the standing where you are told — is what the house owes to it, per the old folio kept at The Lord Protector at Table. A service instruction and a letter to everybody, chalked on the same board. The paste was not eaten. The circle turned.

Billing is per phase, not per course, which is the correct way to bill a motorway. I paid for three of the four and was waved off toward the A1(M) before I could argue for the fourth. The flag at the end of the beetroot ribbon had already been replaced by the next one. — M. Whitmore, travelling north on the long road
