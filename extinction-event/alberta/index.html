Alberta

Field notes of a naturalist · taken by H. A. Collins, in the summers of 1977–1981 · transcribed for the shelf by M. E. Collins · patron’s seal of Mr. Alistair Delgado, counter-signed

Subject
One province of Canada, Western, and one of the three prairie provinces
Method
Seven stations, north of the forty-ninth parallel, one ledger, one tin of matches
Weather
Continental, mostly; see Station 5 for the exception the wind makes
Companion shelf
The Extinction Event — Report, Class 8C, and beside it The Ediacaran — The Stratum, Vol. 7 No. 11

01

The threshold of the ledger

Station 1 · the crossing of the forty-ninth parallel, latitude 49° N, the road out of Montana

The notes begin at the border, because the province is a thing with borders and the borders are the only part of it you can see from the road. Alberta is a province in Western Canada and is one of the three prairie provinces. It is bordered by British Columbia to its west, Saskatchewan to its east, the Northwest Territories to its north, and the U.S. state of Montana to its south; the southern line is the forty-ninth parallel, the northern line the sixtieth, and the western line follows the spine of the mountains down into the plains, which is to say it follows a decision the rock made. The north of the province belongs, in the winter, to the same quiet as the shelves of the shed at home: nothing moving, everything kept. Alberta and Saskatchewan are the only two landlocked provinces in the country, and I have written that down twice, once in the ledger and once here, because two provinces with no sea in either of them is a fact the map does not hurry to volunteer. The eastern part of the province is occupied by the Great Plains, while the western part borders the Rocky Mountains, so that a traveller going east to west in a single day is crossing from one kind of world into the other the way you step from the kitchen above the bakery into the yard: the floor changes under you and you are expected not to remark on it.

For the measure of the subject: Alberta is Canada’s fourth-largest province by area, at 661,848 square kilometres — about 255,541 square miles, a number the class can carry the way it carries the five. The ledger keeps the province’s size on Card 12-A and its coal on Card 298, and the two cards sit together in the drawer because, as we shall see at Station 2, the province is where the family’s loaf gets its weather.

02

Medicine Hat and the coal

Station 2 · Medicine Hat, the butte at the edge of the foothills · coal measures, Mississippian

The coal of the western foothills is the oldest coal in the province and the first thing the province hands you. It is Mississippian in its weather, the early weather of the coal-bearing Carboniferous, laid down in swamps a little before 323 million years ago — the same wood, the same drowned forest, the same unrotted and patient trees that the family bakes at 325°F the August of every year since 1980, on Card 298, the beet-and-blackstrap Carboniferous Coal Loaf. I held a lump of Medicine Hat coal up to the kitchen light at home, against the lump on the bakery shelf, and could not find the difference, which is either a comfort or a problem and I have not decided which. The butte at the town’s edge is coal in a cliff face, and the coal is cut into its strata like the five great bands the report keeps against the wall of the shed: a wall that bears what it carries. At the mine office they showed me the ledger of seams and said the seams go back through the whole coal-bearing age, and I said the household keeps a rule about exactly that age, whatever is voided, bake, and the man at the desk wrote it down in the seam ledger under the heading for August, which is the only sensible place for it.

03

Crowsnest Pass, and the year 252

Station 3 · the Crowsnest Pass, the valley of the old workings, the Bearpaw shales of the western foothills

The coal country runs up into the pass, and in the same valleys, above and around the coal, the mud keeps the other weather: the Bearpaw shales of the early Permian, grey and marine and fine, laid down when the inland sea stood on the plains where the pass now is. A naturalist going up that valley in 1977 is walking in the neighbourhood of the Great Dying, the end-Permian, 252 million years ago, which the class report keeps as Number 3 of the five and the ledger keeps as the black band. The Permian sea that lost nearly everything at once had a western margin in this province, and the mud of the Bearpaw, set down in that spelling in both ledgers, is where the western side of that ending was kept, the way the Karoo keeps the southern side. At the pass the wind comes down through the gap of the Crowsnest and the temperature in the valley moves a whole degree in the time it takes to strike a match. I have noted the country’s climate here once, before the wind is named at Station 5, and noted it a second time now because the country keeps insisting: the province has a predominantly continental climate, but seasonal temperatures tend to swing rapidly due to its aridity — the air holds so little that a front can rewrite it in an hour, and the plains do not dispute the front.

04

The Red Deer River, the bone beds, and the tracks

Station 4 · Drumheller and the Red Deer River valley · the Horseshoe Canyon and Dinosaur Valley formations · Fosterville shale

The valley of the Red Deer River is the province’s long table, and the table is set. At Drumheller the river has cut through the badlands and left the bones of the Cretaceous standing in the bluffs, the way the almanac page stands pasted in the ledger at the kitchen above the bakery: a leaf of another season, kept where it can be read. The quarry at the river’s first camp, worked in the summers of 1905 and 1910 by the preparators of the museum in New York, took up the first great heads of the horned dinosaurs — Styracosaurus, with its brow spikes fanned out like the fringe of a card the ledger will not file straight, and the great Albertosaurus named for the province itself, a name the ledger approved without comment. The Fosterville shale of the river’s lower bends keeps the older weather still: the Protoceras of the Eocene, a little antelope-shaped thing with a skull that has argued the naturalists ever since, which I have filed beside Entry 314, Luolishania, because both of them are animals the record kept whole and the family has not stopped discussing. Higher up, in the Dinosaur Valley formation, the mud keeps tracks — the two-hundred-and-forty-foot trail of a herd that walked across a dry lake bed in the late Cretaceous and stopped, together, to eat, and was kept. And at the park on the river’s middle stretch, the Horseshoe Canyon formation, the same mud keeps the choking beds — the bone layer where a pack of small tyrannosaurs was found together in 2014, still close enough to count — and the tracks of Dakotaraptor, the little two-metre hunter whose trail the men of the first survey on the river read in the stone in 1884, and walked the same length of it, and filed under the province’s name. A herd that walks and is kept; a family that reads and keeps; the ledger’s rule holds at the river bend exactly as it holds in the shed, and the class report has already said the report and the loaf are the same instrument in different hands.

The margin note, in the older hand: the province keeps the track and the body and the coal, and wants nothing back. This is what a province is for.

05

The Chinook, or the wind that does the arithmetic

Station 5 · the plains below the divide, on the approach to the town in the valley floor

The temperature swings I have recorded at Station 3 are less pronounced in western Alberta because of its occasional Chinook winds, and Station 5 exists to keep the exception. On the morning of 14 April 1979 the thermometer at the station in the valley floor stood at −17°C at breakfast, and by eleven o’clock the wind had come down off the mountains dry and warm, snow on the fence posts turning to water on the fence wire, and the glass read 8°C. Twenty-five degrees in the morning, in one province, on one fence. The wind is the mountains’ way of settling a debt with the plains: it descends the other side of the divide dry and warmed by its own falling, and the aridity of the province, which does the swinging everywhere else, lets the wind have its say here. The farmers name it and count on it and are not sentimental about it, which is the correct feeling for a wind that is also a weather: it eats snow, which is why the western men call it the snow-eater, and it arrives in the deep of winter precisely when the cold has been counting its worst. The ledger keeps one line for it, in the hand the household uses for the dates it does not want to lose: a boundary the province keeps on its own terms, the same way the report treats the oven, at 325°, as a kind of boundary the family keeps. I have read that line at the clubhouse since, with Salvio’s carrier wave open between records, and no one in the room has asked what it measures.

8°C · 11:00, Chinook −17°C · breakfast

06

The plains, the mountains, and the country behind

Station 6 · the drive east along the forty-ninth, from the foothills to the Saskatchewan line

From the town in the valley floor the drive goes east over the plains until the line of the province comes up out of the grass at the horizon and stops being a decision the rock made and being, simply, a line. Behind it is Saskatchewan, the province’s eastern border and its fellow landlocked province, and the two of them together form the only pair in the country with no sea between them and the sea. The great plains keep no bones here and no coal they will give up; they keep wheat and weather and the long flat light of a province that has decided to be read at a distance. I stopped at the border and turned back, because the notes are about the province and the province ends at the line, and the way the record works — the way the report’s five work, the way the Ediacaran’s ninety-six million years work in the Stratum — is that a thing is known by its boundaries, and a province no less than a period. The Rockies keep the west as the Great Plains keep the east; the Northwest Territories keep the north at the sixtieth parallel, which is also the top of the country’s own map; and Montana keeps the south, a state that has no name for the Chinook, because the wind turns its face away from it at the divide. The province is what the four keep apart from each other, and what they bear in common: 661,848 square kilometres of the record, kept warm.

07

Back at the clubhouse, and the slip in the margin

Station 7 · the room above the bakery, Magsaysay Avenue · the shelf, the ledger, the kettle

The notes come home in the ledger’s own way: the cards are counted, the dates are kept twice, and one slip is filed in the margin of Folio 12-A, in the older hand, the one that keeps the registrar’s business at the household. The slip is short, and the room reads it the way the room reads everything — once for the meaning, once for the keeping. It says, and I set it down word for word, exactly as it stands on the slip, because the ledger’s rule is that the registrar’s hand is the hand and the hand is not amended:

We suspect the registrar somewhere looked at the end the religions prophesy and the Great Dying needs a sentence more than the others.

And I have kept my distance from the slip since, the way the class kept its distance from the glass at the museum in Copenhagen, and the way the household keeps its distance from the last day: the report’s council sat on the third Tuesday of Term 3 to keep an account of it — the end times, the end of the ordinary, the end the religions prophesy and the doomsday cults rush in for — and did what the ledger always does. The Great Dying, Number 3 of the five, is the year 252, and it sits in these notes at Station 3, in the Bearpaw of the pass, set down in the same spelling in both ledgers and left, like the coal, where it was found. What the slip amounts to, if a naturalist may amount it: the registrar looked at the end, the one the religions have a page for and the ledger has a card for, and found the largest of the five wanting one more sentence — the way the whole family is found, by someone who keeps a register, wanting whatever it is bearing. The kettle was on at the reading of the slip. The coal was in the oven at 325°, the August card turned over. The record, at the river bend and at the shelf and at the sixty-first shelf of the province, keeps what it bears.
