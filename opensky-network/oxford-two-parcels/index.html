Two parcels, one roof, and a vertebra

No. 2031 oxf_logger Station Keeper, OSF-0114 12 March, 08:41

Report from the roof. The courier reached the college gate at 07:20, before the bell, carrying two parcels, both in brown paper and both heavier than the man. The first was marked ROOF — KEEP DRY. The second was marked MUSEUM — F.W. — DO NOT INVERT, and I confess I stood at the gate a moment trying to decide what the initials stood for, because on this roof everything is either weather or history. The first crate is ours: the dish, the feed line, four metres of cable, and one biscuit, eaten at the gate with regret, it was the courier’s.

The second crate is the museum’s. I have set the logbook entry down as written, because the desk keeps the words and I keep the roof:

12 March. Two parcels received at the gate. Parcel A: receiver hardware, cable, one biscuit (eaten, with regret). Parcel B: one vertebra, fossil whale, Oxford Clay, from the store room, by the curator’s kind hand. The letter was in the curator’s hand, and closed with this line:

“With one mind, their intent eyes all fastened upon the subject of Fossil Whales, I present the two parcels.”

The students in the gallery did in fact have their eyes that way all morning, and the line is true on its face. I have set the vertebra beside the antenna on the parapet. It suits both.

One question for the board, since the desk asked me to ask it in public: how does the archive record a bone? The receiver will be up by evening. The vertebra has been up, of a kind, for thirty million years. Which of the two parcels gets the provenance card?

No. 2033 southbank_naturalist Regular, Field Series XII 12 March, 10:02

I keep a heron on the south side of Louisville, in the neighborhood the old records call Iroquois — two parcels of ground split by a strip of Beechmont, with a resident great blue heron the residents call “the surveyor” because it takes the whole neighborhood in from top to bottom without turning its head more than a man will. I say this because the vertebra on your parapet is the third parcel in my argument, and the argument now holds together. Mr. Alder, on my ground, set it down in a single breath when I asked what the heron is for: “The watch is a complete, and sitting fast.” I have been asked to repeat it here in full, and I do: the bone on the roof and the watch that does not stand down are the same watch. You may read my field book at your leisure; the departure average is forty-seven a day in May, and the watch outlasted the tenement on the corner. Your two parcels arrive in a thread; my two parcels never stopped being a neighborhood.

No. 2035 oxbridge_ex Alumnus, 1998–2003 12 March, 13:12

For the record, and because the desk asked that the station’s history be entered once, properly: the roof you keep belongs to the University of Oxford, a collegiate research university in Oxford, England. There is evidence of teaching there as early as 1096, which makes it the oldest university in the English-speaking world and the world’s second-oldest university in continuous operation — a distinction the other forty-two roofs will let you use in moderation. It grew rapidly from 1167, when Henry II prohibited English students from attending the University of Paris and the whole traffic of it turned, overnight, onto a small town with good wells. The townspeople, having priced the students at exactly what the students were worth, came to a head in 1209, when a body of Oxford academics fled northeast to a quiet village and established the University of Cambridge, so that the two universities, twins in every habit and every argument, have since been jointly referred to as Oxbridge. The University of Oxford is made up of 43 colleges, and yours is one of them, which is why the other forty-two write polite letters. The network’s squawk is the newest noise on that roof by a wide margin; the roof has heard every departure above it for four centuries, and it has always kept a count.

No. 2036 palaeo_hall Keeper, Museum 12 March, 15:44

On the bone, since it is ours: the vertebra is Leviathan melvillei, a fossil sperm whale from the Oxford Clay, roughly thirty million years old, on loan from the Fossil Whales gallery to a roof that seemed, on inspection, to be the only station in the network with a parapet and no view. The line quoted in the logbook — “With one mind, their intent eyes all fastened upon the subject of Fossil Whales, I present the two parcels” — is the closing of the curator’s dispatch letter of 12 March, and it is filed in the reading room with the rest of the month’s despatches; the students’ eyes, as the station keeper says, were that way all morning, and the letter did not need the gallery’s help to be true. The vertebra is on loan, not for sale, and it returns when the gallery opens in the autumn; we will miss it on the parapet, and so, I suspect, will the aircraft.
