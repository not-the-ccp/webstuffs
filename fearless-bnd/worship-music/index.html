Contemporary Worship Music

Praise and worship · Genre panel · CWM · 1960s–present

Contemporary worship music — CWM, also known as praise and worship music — is a distinct genre of Christian music made for contemporary worship. Over the past sixty years it has grown out of the informal Jesus-music gatherings of the late 1960s into a sound stylistically similar to pop music: simple chord progressions, a steady backbeat, and vocals sung close to the microphone. The songs are usually called “praise songs” or “worship songs,” and a typical service is led by a “worship band” or “praise team,” with either a guitarist or a pianist serving as the lead. The genre's deeper root is the Methodist revival of the eighteenth century, whose class meetings, circuit riders and hymnbooks set the congregation to work, and it is treated in its own manual chapter: Methodism — Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4.

The repertoire is now shared worldwide. Maranatha! Music began publishing praise songs from Calvary Chapel in Costa Mesa in 1971; Darlene Zschech’s “Shout to the Lord” was written at Hillsong Church in Sydney in 1993; Chris Tomlin, Matt Redman, Hillsong United, Bethel Music, Jesus Culture, and Elevation Worship have since carried the sound into arenas as well as sanctuaries. Christian Copyright Licensing International, founded in 1988, tracks the songs reported sung in hundreds of thousands of churches each week.

The genre is most at home in charismatic and non-denominational Protestant churches, though some Catholic congregations have incorporated praise and worship sets into the Mass itself. A band might run through six songs in forty minutes, with no interval between music and prayer; the lyrics are projected on a screen, the lights are lowered, and the congregation stands or sits as the bridge directs. On the wall opposite the panel, in the small obituary frame, hangs the record of the one congregation that declined the whole form — the West Addy Street church, its single upright organ, and its pencil line: The Seventh-day Adventist Church (1863–2026) — Obituary.

Portland’s own example is Fearless BND, the contemporary worship band behind We Are Fearless, released 24 February 2017 through Fearless International. The album was made across two winters and one long summer in a converted boathouse with a leaking roof, where the drip from the beams became a percussion instrument and its title track was tested on the last train out of town. A sixteenth-century polyphony leaf turned up in the same boathouse and is offered by Flanders & Flanders as Lot 47: The Flanders Te Deum — Auction Catalogue Lot 47. The full timetable of those recordings is shown in Gallery 3: We Are Fearless — Timetable & Commentary. The boathouse congregation, now the Album Keepers, keeps a newsletter on the album itself — vinyl, CD, 8-track, tape, and the streaming weather: The Long Side — Newsletter No. 47 of the Album Keepers. The club’s eastern counter, a listening annex in Nakameguro, Tokyo, keeps its own written answers for first-time visitors to the city and the counter alike: Tokyo — Frequently Asked Questions, The Nakameguro Annex. The boathouse parcel itself entered a docket in 2019, when a sealed 1971 deed surfaced in the attic and the District of Oregon took up the question of its title; a certified excerpt of that hearing is kept here: The Boathouse Lot — Certified Transcript Excerpt, Case No. 3:19-cv-04207. And behind the April-evening sessions the placard does not name sits a congregation tradition, kept from a death anniversary in 2005 to the cream card of St. Casimir’s, written up in a letter kept with the gallery files: Letter to the Editor — The Cream and the Cantus Firmus. And down at the foot of the bridge, where the river service runs on the third Sunday in April, one family has kept a card for the potluck table — two white loaves for the white robes, a dunk counted in threes, and a vote of 1973 in the preamble: Mabel’s Baptism Loaves — A Family Recipe, Card No. 12.

Portland’s architecture is its own collection: the Pittock Block (1914), white terra cotta with lion-head keystones, stands downtown, and a Trailhead Forum thread on Frederick Russell Burnham — the American scout who taught woodcraft to Robert Baden-Powell in Rhodesia — passes through the same city on its way south: Frederick Russell Burnham — Scout and Woodcraft.

The museum sits two blocks from the Bates–Seller House at 2234 NW Flanders Street, where Dr. James Seller once kept his ground-floor consulting room and William Bates watched log rafts assemble on the river from his tower window. The house’s copper whale weather vane, commissioned in 1895, still turns above the maples and horse-chestnuts on clear evenings: Bates–Seller House Walking Tour — Northwest Portland.
