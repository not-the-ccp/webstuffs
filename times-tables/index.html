I have a question that has been sitting on my desk longer than it should, and I am too embarrassed to ask it at the office, so here it is, under the bench where nobody with a job title will read it. Multiplication is one of the four elementary mathematical operations of arithmetic, with the other ones being addition, subtraction, and division. Everyone agrees on that far. The problem is the next sentence in every book I own: the multiplication of whole numbers may be thought of as repeated addition. Thought of as. That is not a definition, that is a rumour. If 7 × 8 is the same thing as 7 added seven times or 8 added seven times, then what exactly is multiplication? What is the thing itself, behind the two shadows? It's not a rumour, love, it's the working definition, and it worked for me for thirty-one years. Take your 84 slips. The multiplication of two numbers is equivalent to adding as many copies of one of them, the multiplicand, as the quantity of the other one, the multiplier. So 84 × 109 is 84 copies of 109, stacked on the ledger. The result of a multiplication operation is called a product — the office word for it, the word you stamp on the finished slip. And a comfort: both numbers can be referred to as factors. Nobody has to keep score of who is the multiplicand and who is the multiplier once the sum is working; they are just the two factors that made the 9,156.

Be careful with only one thing, and I said it to the whole class every September: the factors are not the same as terms. Terms are the things you add together in a sum. Factors are the things you multiply together to make one product. Keep those two drawers separate and the arithmetic behaves. Mix them up and you get a child in Year 5 saying 3 × 4 + 5 is the same as 12 + 5 "because it's all just numbers," and yes and no, and I can't be the one explaining both halves again.

Since you're already arguing about what multiplication is, here is what it looks like when it sits down to dinner. It is often denoted four ways, and the office uses all of them: by the cross symbol, ×, by the mid-line dot operator, ⋅, by simple juxtaposition — ab, nothing between, the way a mason writes dimensions — or, in programming languages, by an asterisk, *. My terminal says 84 * 109 and the ledger says 84 × 109 and they are the same product and I am not the man to pick between them.

The dot is the one that gets me. People call it a decimal point and the whole sentence changes meaning. 2.5 × 4 is 10. 2 ⋅ 5 ⋅ 4 is 40. Same dot. Different room. I have seen a senior clerk sign a slip with the dot and a junior read it with the decimal, and the dispute lasted longer than the recompute.
