I · The Lot

The Book of Habakkuk is the eighth book of the Twelve Minor Prophets of the Hebrew Bible, and this copy of it is a small, close-bound volume, three chapters deep, with a lash handle of loose leather in the manner of the loft's grip spares. It is attributed to the prophet Habakkuk, and most scholars will agree — as they agreed about the Romans next to it — that the book's content best aligns with the period during Jehoiakim's reign as king of Judah, 609–597 BC, which puts the prophet's ink some five centuries upstream of the epistle it supplied. Of the three chapters, the first two are a dialogue between Yahweh and the prophet: the prophet stands on the tower, complains about the violence of the nation, and is answered that the vision is still for an appointed time; the third chapter is a prayer and a canticle on the chariots, and it is the one the singers kept. It is an important text in Judaism, read on the fast days, and its passages have been quoted by the authors of the New Testament, which is the whole of this lot's commercial story.

II · The Line That Travels

Verse four of chapter two states that “the just shall live by his faith,” and that one line plays an important role in Christian thought. Paul quotes it at the head of his argument in the Epistle to the Romans — Romans 1:17 — so that the letter found in the same crate at Thread 214 of The Copyist's Desk has been quoting this book for two thousand years without ever naming it. That is the trade of a prophet's line: it leaves the three chapters and travels. Habakkuk 3 is the reason the hymn writers kept coming back; the canticle of the chariots, of the crushed olive and the shaking cedar, is the ground on which modern Christian hymn writers have stood, Isaac Watts among the earliest, setting O God, Our Help in Ages Past on the prophet's feet in 1719 and letting the whole chorus of the centuries walk after him. A three-chapter book carrying that much freight is a bargain even at the estimate.

III · Provenance and Condition

From the house of the rigger of Mablethorpe, who died last spring and whose loft kept this volume under a tin of leather oil beside three grip spares and a brass ferrule — the same crate, the same oil, the same luck as the fourteen-chapter copy of the epistle which went to the auction as Lot 214. The binding is sound. The damp corner has been mended with the copyists' paste, per the conduct in the old folio, and the mouse has been given credit in the margin, as the board directs. The colophon is whole in this copy, which it was not in the other, and the rigger's own hand appears once, in the back cover, in the logbook shorthand of the loft, in a line the cataloguer transcribes here without improvement:

“As for the harbour-quarter house that keeps travelling: a dining room at the north end of the head; but with a small white flag at each end.”

Read against the review of The Preston Bypass, Leeds, the line is plain. The harbour-quarter house that keeps travelling is Gaius's — the back room of the 193-cover dining room at the north end of the M1, named for the harbour-quarter house of Corinth in which the epistle was written down, and kept travelling by the room that carries its name and its fourteen-chapter copy. The head is the head of the long table, where the teapot sits like a junction sign, and the small white flag at each end is the garnish the house lays at the ends of the beetroot ribbon, cut to the mile, one cover per cover of tarmac. The rigger was telling whoever came next: the Romans has gone to the room where it is wanted; the flags will be out; sit long enough and the room will show you the copy, with the goose boiled again and the circle turned.

IV · Estimate, Sale and Terms

The estimate is £41 14s. 2d., or two barrels of grip leather, payable at the hall, and the lot is to be sold without reserve, as the whole winter sale is. It brought the rigger's daughter to the hall, who had signed the dining room's guestbook the week before, and she bid against no one, the other bidders content to let a prophet's three chapters go where they have been going all along — out of the tower, into the tower, into the letter to everybody. It sold at the estimate, by high water, and is consigned to the household records at the Saltfleet Line, to be shelved beside the whip patterns and the fourteen-chapter copy, which is where a book quoted by the New Testament and by the hymn book alike belongs: in the room where the working things are kept. Terms: the leather oil is included; the tin is not; the paste was not eaten; the flags stay out until the next sale.
