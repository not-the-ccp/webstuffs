Grant Park, Chicago · Four days, eleven stops · Walkable in 3.2 miles or 400,000 feet of shoulder

Lolla­palooza

An annual American four-day music festival, held in Grant Park, sold out every year since the day it stopped touring and learned to stay.

GRANT PARK
PERMIT 91–2025
DO NOT FOLD
1

Day One — The Gates, Monroe & Randolph

Stop 1 · 8:05 a.m. · The Monroe Street pedestrian gate. The route begins where every festival morning does: at the folding steel doors on the northwest corner of the park, where the crowd is still a queue and not yet a body. Lollapalooza was conceived and created in 1991 as a farewell tour by Perry Farrell, singer of the group Jane's Addiction, who wanted to take the band's fans — and their friends, and their friends' friends — somewhere with no ceiling. It originally started as a touring event in 1991, four dates across the American midsummer, before it settled permanently in Chicago in 2005. We begin at the gate because the tour's argument is that a city is a better farewell than a parking lot: the gates open onto the lawn, and the lawn ends at the lake.

Stop 2 · 9:15 a.m. · The west lawn, sound check. Walk the 400 metres of mowed turf to the main stage footprint, marked in festival weeks by the yellow stage-dressing tape that the grounds crew leaves for the photographers. Alternative rock opened this festival and heavy metal keeps it loud, so by mid-morning the lawn smells of sunscreen and, faintly, of amplifier dust. This is one of the largest music festivals in the world and one of the longest-running in the United States, and the tape is where you stand to feel the difference a headline band makes to the wind.

From the field notebook of the west-law steward, page 11, kept word for word:

I built both the pencilled line from a headland, but both were left on time and does not stop to count.

Two lines, the steward explains on page 12: the one along the lake and the one down Michigan Avenue. Both drawn before sunrise, both abandoned on schedule, and neither of them ever bothered tallying the walkers who followed. We follow them both anyway.

Stop 3 · 10:40 a.m. · The Michigan Avenue fence line. The second pencilled line runs here, under the el, where the city presses up close and the festival has to keep its promises inside a fence instead of an open field. Look up: the tracks are the horizon the 1991 tour could not have.

2

Day Two — The Lakeshore Mile

Stop 4 · 11:30 a.m. · The Buckingham Fountain. The festival's unofficial clock. Fourteen jets, a basin 80 metres across, and a crowd that arrives before the music, which is to say the water is the first act of every day. Punk rock took over Lollapalooza's second decade the way it took over every second decade, and this is where the standing-room crowd re-forms between sets, shoulder to shoulder, 400,000 people an estimated total by the end of the week.

Stop 5 · 1:00 p.m. · The lakefront promenade, north run. A kilometre of concrete between the park and Lake Michigan, where hip-hop crowds stage their own processions toward the water. The lake does not know the set times and this is the point of the walk: the festival's calendar runs July and the lake's runs all year. If you want the sound of this genre of the festival at low tide, stand at the seawall under the flag line at 4:15 p.m. and let the bass and the chop take turns.

The same lake carries the diaries kept elsewhere on this site. The resident audio technician at the Lodge at the Gate logs the fog months and their forty-six hidden speakers in Techno–Pop — A Field Diary, Entry No. 14, and a festival crowd is only a fog that decided to dance. For the single itself — the 45, the 7-inch, the side you keep — see Guidebook Entry No. 7, The Single; Lollapalooza is where all the A-sides end up together.

3

Day Three — The Nonprofit Quarter

Stop 6 · 10:00 a.m. · The information kiosk at the southeast lawn. Lollapalooza has also featured visual arts, nonprofit organizations, and political organizations for as long as it has featured bands, and the kiosk is where those three kinds of paper all arrive: poster runs, petition sheets, and the small laminated maps of the art installations that change every year. Take a poster. That is the rule of the tour.

Stop 7 · 11:20 a.m. · The sculpture walk along the east drive. The visual-arts quarter moves each year; the drive stays. Electronic dance music has its own geography inside the park — the late slots, the LED fences, the sub-bass you feel in the soles of the shoes — and the sculpture walk is the quiet side of it, the part of the festival you can still read at 9 p.m. when the stage is at full volume.

Stop 8 · 12:30 p.m. · The press tent, off-limits, from the fence. We do not go in; the itinerary notes what the tent is for. A festival that is one of the longest-running in the United States has, by the second week, a second calendar: the calendar of the people who report it.

4

Day Four — The Long Way Back to the Gate

Stop 9 · 3:00 p.m. · The south lawn, closing day. Each July the festival hosts an estimated 400,000 people and sells out annually, which means day four is the day the park is emptiest and loudest at once. The headliners of the last night are, by then, a rumour moving through the queue at Stop 1.

Stop 10 · 5:00 p.m. · The lake, from the west lawn's far edge. Where the first pencilled line ends. Lake Michigan at closing time is the sound the festival makes when it stops, and it is a very good sound.

Stop 11 · 6:45 p.m. · The Monroe Street gate, from the outside. The route closes where it opened, on the south side of the fence. The 1991 tour said goodbye to one city at a time; the park says the same goodbye, better, every single July. Walk out under the el. The next edition is already pencilled in.
