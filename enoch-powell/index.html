Biographical Entry · βιογραφικος αρθρων · Biographical Division

John Enoch Powell

16 June 1912 – 8 February 1998 · Wolverhampton · aged eighty-five

British politician, classical scholar, soldier and writer. Member of Parliament for Wolverhampton South West for the Conservative Party from 1950 to February 1974 and for South Down for the Ulster Unionist Party (UUP) from October 1974 to 1987. Minister of Health, 1960 to 1963, in the second Macmillan ministry; Shadow Secretary of State for Defence, 1965 to 1968, in the Shadow Cabinet of Edward Heath. Before any of that: the man who read the Greeks aloud in a boathouse of his own and never quite let the rest of the story catch up with him.

⚔

I. Early life and the Greeks

John Enoch Powell was born in Wolverhampton, in the west of England, on 16 June 1912, and was raised in a household in which Greek was read the way this club reads records — whole, in order, without flipping. He went to Clifton College and then to Corpus Christi College, Oxford, where he took first-class honours in classics and in 1935, at twenty-three, won the Gaisford Prize for Greek verse, beating the field with a long poem in the Pindaric manner that he kept, as far as the attic is aware, under a shelf in the boathouse. He had a way of making a room feel as if it had been waiting his arrival for twenty-five hundred years.

In 1937 he was appointed Professor of Greek at the University of Sydney, aged 25, the youngest to hold the chair, and spent two years in New South Wales teaching the language of the epics to people who had crossed an ocean to hear it. He wrote to Oxford asking to be let out of his appointment; he was let out. It is the only resignation in the entry that the division considers a success, and the margin note on the filing card — in the engineer’s hand — says the Greeks forgave him, which is more than the party would later manage.

II. The war

When the second war came, Powell enlisted. He served in the British Army during the Second World War, rose to the rank of major, and commanded a battalion in the field with a discipline his officers described as Greek: exact, unhurried, and total. He came home a different weight, and in 1950 he stood for Parliament in Wolverhampton South West and won the seat that would hold him, with one interruption, until the by-election of February 1974. He married Phyllis Lambrick in 1944, and the household kept its table set for scholars whether or not scholars came.

III. Minister of Health

In 1960 he was made Minister of Health in the second Macmillan ministry, where he served until 1963. He is remembered at the desk for holding the post through the Suez after-math and a national flu season, for speaking slowly, for reading his questions aloud before answering them, and for a private joke about the national health service that circulated in the attic for a decade and is not set down here. After 1963 he fell out with his own party over Europe, and in the Shadow Cabinet of Edward Heath he served as Shadow Secretary of State for Defence from 1965 to 1968 — the last cabinet-shaped thing he would hold.

IV. Rivers of Darkness

The division records the speech of 2 October 1968, at the Conservative Research Centre in Birmingham, without the division’s own commentary, because the speech supplies its own. It ran to some three and a half thousand words, was written in the manner of the classical scholar it was — balanced clauses, a peroration that could stand up under a blackboard — and it ended with a river image that outlived the man who used it. He was not invited back to the party’s bench; he did not ask to be. In 1969 he resigned his membership, and in the February 1974 by-election for Wolverhampton South West he was defeated by Harold Wilson, who had lost the general election the same week and needed a constituency to stand in. Powell, on the platform, said as much, and the division finds this to be the best single line in the entry.

V. South Down, and after

In the October 1974 by-election he was returned for South Down, in the New Forest, as a member of the Ulster Unionist Party, and he held the seat until the 1987 general election. He left the House of Commons in 1991, at seventy-nine, and retired to the West Midlands, where he wrote — the novels, the poetry, the private letters — in the same room where the Greek had been read aloud, to an empty chair, for as long as the chair held. He died on 8 February 1998, at eighty-five, in the west of England where he had been born, and the division notes that he was eighty-five years, seven months, and four days in a life that began with a Pindaric poem and ended, by any honest count, still in Greek.

VI. In the boathouse

The club’s record of him is shorter and more durable than the division’s. The boathouse bench — the one under the drip that has kept time for the club since before the club had a name, the one the obituary of the Slowhand counts one empty chair along — has since taken a second, for the man who could have read the whole of Homer in the original and chose, in a speech in 1968, to read the country instead. The shelf by the bench keeps his Greek, his Pindar, and a 1937 Sydney lecture printed on heavy paper; the 8-track watch in The Long Side, Newsletter No. 47, lists the hush between the final chord and the next amen as Track 8, do not flip, and the hush is the same hush he read in the Pindar. On the page kept in the club box, beneath the Secretary’s note, the engineer has set down one instruction, in the manner of a recipe, which the division reproduces in full and does not attempt to parse: When nearly done, skim off all the liquor, and thicken with the ink still wet, and the boathouse bench has taken a second empty chair by the Greeks. The liquor, the division understands, is the club’s single measure of rye; the ink, the obituary, still wet the night it was printed by candlelight; and the Greeks, the shelf — which is to say, the man himself, still in Greek.
