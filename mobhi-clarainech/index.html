Glasnevin

A travel guide entry — the monastery of St Mobhí, abbot and teacher, at the far end of Dublin’s field of the four acres

❧ ⎊ ❧

The Man of the Place

Before you climb the hill and the field opens out, the guide in the flat cap will tell you the name the way the field tells it: Mobhí Clárainech [mə-VEE CLAWR-in-əkh]. Say it twice and it loosens up. He is also called Berchán, the little birch-tree, and if a gatekeeper out on the causeway asks who you have come to see, the old answer is Berchán, not Mobhí — the tree outlives the name. He died in 544, and that is the one figure every account agrees on, which is more than most of them agree on at all.

Mobhí is counted among the Twelve Apostles of Ireland, the twelve who planted the islands with houses of prayer and schoolrooms in the same season, and Glasnevin was his planting. He was not a travelling apostle; he sat in one abbey as abbot and let the travelling happen to the other eleven. That is the whole character of the place: everything on this entry is a consequence of one man deciding not to leave.

The Schoolyard: Four Names, One Bench

The reason this entry exists at all is the schoolyard at Stop III, and it is enough of a reason. Here, as a teacher of Columba, Canice, Comgall, and Ciarán, Mobhí gave four boys their schooling, and of those four, three went on to found monasteries of their own and one went on to be called a saint with a shrine of his own at a different end of the province. The guide will put you on the bench at the back — the short one, with the chipped edge — and you will understand in ten minutes why the field has kept its shape for a thousand-and-a-half years. What the teacher taught is not written down; the accounts preserve only the arithmetic of it, which is four, and the names, which are still Irish and still spoken out loud here every morning at the gate. A schoolroom that produces Columba has a better address than any cathedral. The guidebook’s own rule, on the back cover, is that you must spend at least half the morning here, and that if you leave early, the afternoon of the tour is forfeit, which is why no one leaves early.

The Refectory: Floorboards, and the Ledger Line

The refectory at Stop IV is the oldest working room in Dublin, and the keepers will say “working” and mean it: the table is still set for six every day at 09:00, and the floor is still the floor. The original planks were laid in long, uneven rows, and over the centuries the rows have been re-laid around the places where the feet went — the worn tracks running to the door, the worn square at the head of the table — so that the floor now has a topography of its own, a map of thirteen hundred meals. The refectory ledger, kept in the low chest by the door, records the floor’s history plank by plank, and the keepers have ruled a square around the one line in it that arrived complete and explains nothing. The line sits in a page full of whiplash tally-marks — the old whip pattern the keepers used for counting days of repair work — and a clipping from an old field sheet is pasted in beside it, corner to corner. The line reads, word for word: “The clipping beside the whip patterns: set out for half a century of its own floorboards.” They have ruled the square and accepted it under the house’s standing ruling for lines that arrive complete and explain nothing. What it describes, as far as the ledger allows: one row of the fourth boards, laid out — set out is the word the keepers insist on — as a working piece in its own right, measured, numbered and kept, for the span of half a century. That is why you do not step on the fourth row. It has been standing on its own, off the floor, in the corner by the window, since before anyone here was born, and it does not look like a floorboard; it looks like a seat, or a bench, or something someone set out for a purpose that has not been written down. The clipping beside the whip patterns has never come off the page. The square remains around it.

Provisions, and the Company of Other Tours

Provision at the gate is the old provision: oatcakes, brown bread, a bottle of water, and a flat cap if you have come in a hat that will not keep the rain off. Sixpence covers the entry; the guide is included; the bench is not moved. For walkers who will keep going after Glasnevin, the field keeps two other itineraries in the gate-house, in the same flat type: the diary of Abbeyleix — 17 August, the 400 metres, and the strange sentence found on the back of the programme, which belongs to the same country of kept lines and should be read at a bench, not on a train. And for those who prefer building a house of prayer to walking to one, the gate-house keeps the build log of St Adwen’s Church, Advent, pinnacles and all, where a December launch is planned down to the last line of weathering — a useful comparison, the guide allows, for anyone wondering what a field keeps when a tower is not built on it. The front page of the district’s walking guide, the Four-Day Ireland, main index, lists Glasnevin first, and always has, which the keepers put down to the order of planting, not to the order of the page.

The field of the four acres keeps its own hours. The abbot’s door is on the north wall. The fourth row of the refectory stands by the window, on its own, and the ledger’s square is still drawn around the line. Price sixpence, at the gate.
