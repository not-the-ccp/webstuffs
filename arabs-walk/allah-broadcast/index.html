The Name Over the Quay

A radio play in one act, in three scenes · cast of three, one table, one dish

اَللّٰه

Broadcast from Room 214 at Anoka Lexicon Field Station on the last night of Al-ʿArab — a Walking Tour of the Arab World in Nine Stops, with the ledger closed, the bacon weighed out between 6 and 14 lbs, and drawer 12 waiting for Friday. The walk has been walking a people for thirteen thousand three hundred and ten kilometres; this act, recorded live at the pilgrim's table, is what happens when the route walks a name. The cast is small. The name is not.

Cast

  • The Announcer — a voice that has already read the 9th-century BCE references at the Assur mounds and will not read them a second time
  • Youssef al-Amin — the station's lexicographer, holding the ʿ-b-d satchel and the card from drawer ars, Hejazi
  • The Caller — from the quay on the Nile at Cairo, where the informant count is highest on the file, ringing in at 22:00 local with the phone on speaker

Scene 1 · The studio at 21:40

[SFX: The tick of a dial. A coffee cup set down on the table — qahwa, bought at the Baghdad stop, the fifth of the route, so that no one at the station ever has to lose a coffee to the wash. A door opening and closing on the fog.]

ANNOUNCER: Anoka Lexicon Field Station, this is Studio 12, hour 2,661 of the walk, and we are one hour from the table. Tonight the class does not walk. Tonight the class says a word.

AL-AMIN: الله. Four letters when it is written. A long open a when it is said, the first l caught in the teeth: [ɑɫˈɫɑː]. Say it wrong and the whole word slouches, the way a hull slouches when it is not riding at anchor.

[SFX: The dial ticks twice. Far off, under the studio, something like the sea — or the fog, if the fog is doing the sea’s work.]

ANNOUNCER: Allah is the Arabic name of God. In Islam it is not a word among words; it is the name, the proper name, the one the ninety-nine good names hang from like lines from a quay. Outside the Arabic tongue it is associated principally with Islam, and yet the name is older on the record than the association: pre-Islamic Arabia already wrote it on coins and in inscriptions, and it is thought to be a contraction — the article and the noun pressed together over centuries of use — from al-ilāh, الاله, “the god,” become the one and only God.

AL-AMIN: And it travels by the card-file’s oldest rule. Cognates. The name sits in a Semitic family: the Aramaic ܐܲܠܵܗܵܐ, Alahā, the same three consonants doing the same work a door’s width away in scripture, the way al-ǧabr and ṣifr travelled to the Toledo quay without leaving a single drawer of the file unmarked. A root that stands in Cairo and at the Assur mounds alike is cognate by rule; a name that stands in Makkah and in the houses of Arabic-speaking Jews and Christians alike is something a little older than rule.

Scene 2 · The caller from the Nile, 22:00

[SFX: A telephone on speaker, the Nile audible under it. A cloth is laid down on a table — before commencing, as the standing orders require.]

CALLER: Cairo, the quay. The informants are still answering. You want to know why the drawer aec — Egyptian — holds the highest count on your file? Because in the mouths here the word is not a word for God, the way a card is a card for a file. It is the God of Abraham, spoken in Arabic by people who are not walking your nine stops. One name, three houses, and no one at the table argues the address.

AL-AMIN: That is the whole of the name in a sentence. Al-ilāh contracted; the article doing the work of the definite; the god singular until the plural has never once been heard.

CALLER: One question before we hang up. The old menu. The card that rode in with the pilgrim line, the one your tour book kept uncorrected — the bacon at 6 to 14 lbs, knees held to the exact time — we have our own line from that same card, from the stew the kitchen made the night the pilgrim line first paid in both directions. You will read it once. You will not correct it. It reads: “He has a longer period for the bottom and sides of it, and well made, very delicious, the onions sliced, 4 sage-leaves, a few spoonfuls of Spinach.”

[SFX: A long beat. The lamp above the studio hums once, like the fog at the yard, where the sixteen hulls number themselves and are counted and not corrected.]

ANNOUNCER: We have it. A longer period for the bottom and sides — the dish cooked low and long, the way the name is worn: the well-made thing, the sliced onions, four sage-leaves, a few spoonfuls of Spinach, capital S, as written. Read at the table it is the pilgrim line at rest again. A name, like a stew, is what the bottom and sides of a dish of it do over time: they keep their own period, and they keep it well. Filed under the quay mark. Kept as kept.

Scene 3 · The table, hour 2,662

[SFX: Chairs on a stone floor. The cloth, already down. The stew, stirring. Somewhere four thousand miles to the northwest, under a fog that never burns off, water following a walker two steps behind — the yard at Hunters Point keeping its own quiet account of a name that has crossed the sea once and will cross it again.]

AL-AMIN: The walk ends the way it began: with a name said out loud. Arabs at the mounds, nine centuries BCE. Allah now, at the table, one hour of the fog to burn.

ANNOUNCER: And with the drawer: ars, Hejazi, quay mark, the pilgrim line, words in and words out, counted, re-counted, filed without correction. The name over the quay is the last word of the route, the way the bacon is the last weight of the route, and the way, in the fine print of every Semitic script from ܐܲܠܵܗܵܐ to الله, the same three letters have been standing over the table since before the table was built.

[SFX: The dial ticks to its rest. The stew, stirring. The fog, holding.]

ANNOUNCER: This is Anoka Lexicon Field Station, Studio 12, signing off at hour 2,662. The walk is closed. The name is open. Good night, all nine stops.

After the broadcast

The class has kept the caller’s line in the tour book, uncorrected, in the same way it kept the bacon line from the old menu — because a line that has been said on the air and not corrected is part of the evidence, the way a name spoken in three houses is part of the evidence. The walk itself, all nine stops and 13,310 kilometres of it, stands in the route ledger of Al-ʿArab, and the word’s whole family — the 32 ISO-coded varieties, the triconsonantal roots, the trade ledger of the Toledo quay — is walked in full in Arabic — Comparative Lexicon Manual, Chapter 12, the drawer this broadcast grew out of. The fog at the far end of the walk, the sixteen white hulls, and the house four thousand miles inland that is more unwholesome and deleterious, are accounted for at Hunters Point — Dream Dictionary, Entry 714, where the yard’s lost-and-found desk is open at first light. The front desk at Webstuffs holds the return: drawer 12, washed and dry, Friday, the ledger closed, the name over the quay still open.
