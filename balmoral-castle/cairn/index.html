Cairn

A human-made pile of stones, raised for a purpose · Deeside and the wider northern hills · on loan from the estate books, Balmoral

Irish: carn [ˈkʰaːrˠn̪ˠ], plural cairn [ˈkʰaːrˠɲ]

The word, and the thing

A cairn is a human-made pile — or stack — of stones raised for a purpose, usually as a marker or as a burial mound. The word comes from the Irish carn. Cairns have been, and are, used for a broad variety of purposes: in prehistory they were raised as markers, as memorials, and as burial monuments, some of them containing chambers; in the modern era they are often raised as landmarks, especially to mark the summits of mountains, and as trail markers on the lower paths. They vary in size from a small pile of stones gathered around a walking stick to entire artificial hills, and in complexity from loose conical rock piles to elaborate megalithic structures. They may be painted. On these braes, the summit cairns are painted white every late summer so that a walker coming up in a squall can still find the top.

The old cairns

On the hills above the Dee the old cairns are older than the estate by several thousand years. The chambered cairn at Carn a’ Chùl Mhoir, on the far side of the valley, was raised around 3000 BC: a cone of roughly twenty-two metres holding a small passage and a stone chamber, and it has kept its cone, against weather and sheep, ever since. The name itself gives the object away — carn meaning the mound, the stack, the thing put up on purpose. Cairn Toul, 763 metres, keeps the word in its hill: a cauldron-shaped summit of heather and gneiss, with a proper marker at the top and a stone pile around it. Ben A’an, 897 metres, stands a quarter mile north of the castle’s own brae, and is the hill the household has always gone to in a hard week: out before the post, back by the tea.

The estate cairns

When the estate was bought in 1852 from the Farquharson family by Prince Albert, husband of Queen Victoria, the new castle was commissioned from William Smith of Aberdeen, and the rebuilding took the stonemasons up the brae in seasons when the work in the valleys stood for the frost. The estate’s own books carry a note that the masons, setting the summit markers as they went, left a small cairn at the top of Ben A’an in the old style — a cone, not a wall — and the household has kept it ever since, the Farquharsons’ keeping passing into the Crown’s with the deed. It is repainted with the others; the paint is logged against the folio of Balmoral, Crathie, and the household thereto attached, in the same account that the Court of Session held to have been kept regardless of the war. The title dispute and the books of the purchase, read as entered, stand in the transcript of Ex parte Balmoral, No. M/1852; the sifter’s line that follows was cut from the kitchen’s share of the same ledger.

The sifter’s line, 1854, read word for word as it stands in the ledger:

“POLISH AND POMERANIAN WHEAT are accounted a fine sieve or muslin bag, and let it drain through a quill, so wondrously minute is it.”

Read in the kitchen’s way — which is the only way this bench of stones would read it — the line is a test of the sieve. The new house had come to take flour by the shipload, Polish and Pomeranian wheat milled in the low countries, and the ledger records the rule the kitchen kept: such wheats are to be judged only with a fine sieve or a muslin bag, and a pinch of the sifted flour is then let fall through the slit of a quill; so wondrously minute is the meal that only that passage settles whether the sieve is fine enough for the house bread. It is the same habit as the pile of stones. Something is put in place on purpose, small things first and the whole cone after, and a minute test is kept to prove the whole.

Accession note

On display here, in the case: one summit stone of painted granite from Ben A’an, removed and replaced in 1958; one quill, slit, from the ledger room; and a muslin bag, its hem in estate thread. The trail markers below the brae, from the castle’s north door to the burn at Crathie, are renewed with the paint each August, as they have been each August since the purchase, and they are the reason a stranger can find the top of the world on the far side of the Dee in a fog, and find it, and go on down. The household page that kept the estate through the blackout years is at Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother — Queen Consort, 1936–1952; the collection’s own register, and the rest of Case 3, are kept at Webstuffs.
