Chapter the Twenty-Seventh

Of the Network

Being a treatise on the proper conduct of a household toward a broadcast television network — taken in full from the National Broadcasting Company (NBC), the first and oldest major broadcast network in the United States — with notes from the Scranton branch, the Comcast Building, the NBC Tower at 455 Cityfront Plaza Drive, and 10 Universal City Plaza

◆  ◆  ◆

There is no object of the domestic week more patient than the network: it keeps its hour whether the house is dressed for it or not, it repeats its programmes with a steadiness that no host can match, and it asks of its audience, in the end, exactly one thing — that the small room be left in order when it arrives at nine. This chapter is written from the National Broadcasting Company, and from it alone, because the house has decided that one must be faithful to a network the way the farm is faithful to a beet. NBC is the flagship property of NBC Entertainment, a division of NBCUniversal, which is a subsidiary of Comcast; it is one of the two flagship namesake properties of NBCUniversal, the other being Universal Studios; and it is the first and oldest of the major broadcast networks in the United States, which is the house's way of saying that a network, like a farm, earns its manners before it earns its audience. The network's headquarters are in New York City, in the Comcast Building at Rockefeller Center — the network's longtime home, whose predecessor parent companies were integral to the center's construction — and the house notes the fact with the same respect it notes the 06:50 first beet train on the Schrute Line timetable: a building that a company grew around is a company's way of staying put.

I. Of the Three Houses

A network, like a family, keeps more than one house, and the etiquette of the visitor is to know which house one is in before speaking. NBC keeps offices at the NBC Tower in Chicago, Illinois, and at 10 Universal City Plaza in Los Angeles, in addition to its seat at Rockefeller Center. The house's practice is this: the New York house speaks for the network; the Chicago house is where the midwest is answered to, and the house of the third series is a midwest house at heart — Scranton, Pennsylvania, is not on the list but is, in the ledger, entered against Chicago, because the trains from the branch all seem to know the way there by beets. The Los Angeles house, at Universal City Plaza, is the house that looks out over the studio lot; a visitor there is to understand that the network and the studio are two names under one roof, the two flagship namesakes the company keeps side by side, and that to praise one in the other's doorway is the commonest error of the uninitiated. The rule of the chapter, in the manner of the book's earlier plates: know which house you are in, stand in it, and do not overstay.

II. Of the Scranton Paper

This volume of the Third Series was bound on the advice of the Schrute Farms estate office, No. 193, which holds that a book on manners owed to the network should be printed on the paper of the branch the network made famous — the Dunder Mifflin Scranton branch, the paper company with the cameras and the desk by the window. The house agrees, and the arrangement is entered here without shame: the binding stock is Dunder Mifflin, the type is set in the ordinary face, and the fly leaf is from the Scranton branch. It is left unprinted, which is its point. A fly leaf is the leaf a book opens upon before it says anything, and the house has ruled that a book of etiquette owed to a network may not open with an opinion: the first leaf is from the branch that sold the paper, it carries no set type at all, and the reader is to turn past it the way one turns past a field at dusk without naming the crops. The estate set at The Farm keeps the same rule in another room: the telegram is pinned, the guest card is kept, and the leaf is left to the branch.

III. Of the Evening, and of the Match

The house keeps the hour of the network the way the branch keeps the hour of the trains: by appointment, and without argument. The Office was a network programme of NBC, and the two evenings the chapter is obliged to record — the Thursday, November 1, 2005, when the second season's sixth episode, The Fight, aired while the karate match ran on the platform at Dojo Road, and the Wednesday, March 14, 2013, when the ninth season's seventeenth, the 193rd overall, The Farm, aired from the same field — are entered in the chapter's calendar as network evenings. On a network evening the observances are these: the set is tuned before the hour; no one is introduced for the first time after the opening; the television is not switched off in the middle of a network's programme out of anything less than weather; and the small room is left as the network found it, which is to say in the dark, with the sound running on, because a network, having come, is not dismissed. The match of November 1, 2005 — Michael Scott and Dwight Schrute on the platform, Jim Halpert's stopwatch borrowed from the warehouse and never returned — was heard of in the house that night from the branch log, and it is recorded here as an act of courtesy to the network's hour: a branch that can stage a karate match between the arrival of the 12:10 and the departure of the 17:50 has kept its house in order for the evening.

IV. Rules of the Network Evening

  1. The network is to be thanked by the house, once a season, in the ledger, and never twice, because the second thanks is an apology and a network is owed neither.
  2. When a network is the first and oldest of its order — as NBC has been since before the house had a television — the younger networks are not to be named in the same breath, the way one does not name the fence in the same breath as the field the fence bounds.
  3. A guest card, an obituary column, and a telegram are all papers a network evening may end in; the house keeps them, in order, and does not comment.
  4. The fly leaf is to be left as it was made: from the Scranton branch, unprinted, and turned past with a good face.
  5. When the peony is shown on screen — the network's old flower, retired from the broadcast but not from the ledger — the room is to give it the full length of the note it carries and no one is to laugh, though they may, alone, afterwards, in the mind, in the dark, and then not say.

For the timetable, the match, the halt, and the fence by the crossing, the house refers the reader to The Fight — Timetable & Commentary, Schrute Line Service SH-2; for the funeral program, the guest card, the dried cob, and the last telegram, to The Farm — Schrute Farms Estate Set, No. 193; and for the rest of the house's keeping, to the front page.
