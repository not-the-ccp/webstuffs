British Columbia

Pacific Northwest · between the Pacific Ocean and the Rocky Mountains · one province, four seasons, one long arrival

British Columbia is the westernmost province of Canada, a great sweep of the Pacific Northwest pressed between the Pacific Ocean and the Rocky Mountains. It covers 944,735 square kilometres of rocky coastlines, sandy beaches, temperate rainforest, lakes, mountains, an inland desert, and grassy plains, and an estimated 5.68 million people lived here in 2025, making it Canada's third-most populous province. Alberta lies to the east, Yukon and the Northwest Territories to the north, Washington, Idaho and Montana to the south, and Alaska to the northwest. The capital is Victoria; Vancouver is the largest city. This guide runs counter-clockwise: coast first, then the mountains, then the interior, then the north, where the Elsewhere walk's eleventh halt waits on the Nass River.

The Coast — Vancouver and Victoria

Most arrivals begin on the coast. Vancouver sits on a peninsula of its own, framed by the sea and the North Shore mountains: walk the Stanley Park seawall at dawn, eat a salmon sandwich on Granville Island, and watch the floatplanes lace in over Coal Harbour. A ninety-minute ferry carries you through the Gulf Islands to Victoria, the capital, where the Inner Harbour, the Empress, and the formal gardens at Butchart bloom from March through October. Whale watching leaves from both harbours — orcas and grey whales pass the straits — and the Pacific Rim coast west of Vancouver Island throws winter storms at long beaches of driftwood and sand.

The Mountains — the Canadian Rockies

East of the coast the Rockies rise steeply. Mount Robson, at 3,954 metres, is the range's highest peak and stands wholly in British Columbia. Glacier, Yoho and Kootenay protect icefields, cedar-hemlock forests, and the spiral tunnels where the train climbs Rogers Pass. Allow a full day for the mountain drives, longer in winter, when the passes close and the ski hills open. The office summary of that climb — Application No. 4,114, the spiral wound one full turn, no more — is kept at the railway's patent file, filed with a pressed cedar frond and one sentence found in the examiner's margin.

The Interior — Okanagan and the Desert

South of the mountains the Okanagan Valley runs lake by lake through orchards and vineyards: peaches in August, pinot noir in October, and towns built for slow driving. Farther south still lies Canada's only desert, around Osoyoos, where annual rainfall drops below three hundred millimetres and the sagebrush smells of rain before any falls. The Cariboo, to the north, keeps the gold-rush town of Barkerville and the Fraser River's long freight of history.

The North — the Nass River and Gitlaxt'aamiks

The north keeps the province's own time. From Terrace the road follows the Nass River toward Gitlaxt'aamiks, the eleventh stop of the Elsewhere walk: totem poles in the village square, cedar canoes, and the winter salmon run, counted in tides and feasts rather than hours. Stay long enough to learn which way the river runs. Beyond, the Alaska border meets the panhandle, Haida Gwaii sits in the open Pacific, and the Great Bear Rainforest shelters the cream-coloured Kermode bear — the spirit bear — among cedars that are already old when a totem is raised.

Practical

Go between May and September for the coast and the interior; November to April for ski and the north's short days. Fly into Vancouver, take the ferries, or drive the Yellowhead and the Trans-Canada. Numbers to keep: 944,735 square kilometres, 5.68 million people, and one province that still keeps its own time.

Found in the margin of the field copy, beside a pressed cedar frond:

This report sits on the Sinis peninsula: Phoenician ruins beside the vinegar crock: The Vermilion Seal — Newsletter No. 17 .

The Sinis peninsula is Tharros, the Mediterranean close of the Elsewhere walk — Sardinia, not British Columbia, which is why the sentence reads like a misplaced postcard. The vinegar crock and The Vermilion Seal belong to the Coopers' Lane correspondence behind the bakery, whose report this was; and the report's own eleventh stop is Gitlaxt'aamiks, here, on the Nass River. So the sentence is a map after all: Sardinia beside the shed, the crock beside the river, and both of them beside this page.
