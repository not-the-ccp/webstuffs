Exhibit 43.B • Pacific Theatre Room • Case 1

The Landing at Nadzab

Airborne operation • Markham Valley • New Guinea • 5 September 1943

On the morning of 5 September 1943, 543 paratroopers of the 503rd Parachute Infantry Regiment, 82nd Airborne Division, and a ground battery of the Australian 2/4th Field Regiment let go from C-47 transports and came down on the corrugated-iron strips of the Nadzab airfield, thirty kilometres south of the Japanese-held port of Lae. General Douglas MacArthur was in the valley that day, circling overhead in a B-17 and watching the drop through his binoculars from the rear of the aircraft. The objective was the airfield itself: held, it would feed the land forces at Lae by air in days instead of weeks.

By late afternoon the overland force was on the strip. The Australian 2/2nd Pioneer Battalion and 2/6th Field Company, with Palm Company of the Papuan Infantry Battalion, had finished the overland and river trek down the Fena River that same day and set to work clearing the track, hauling the 25-pounders, and digging the first gunpits. That night the Japanese counter-attacked with a force of some six hundred infantry; by 7 September the Nadzab action was won — 514 Japanese dead and 192 taken prisoner against 4 killed and 18 wounded among the drop and the overland parties. The port of Lae itself surrendered two days later, and the Japanese garrisons of northern New Guinea followed in the weeks after.

Case 2 Decoded dispatch, 02:14, 6 September 1943

The message reached the forward signal station of the 3rd Battalion an hour after the drop. Its last order concerned the courier: the original dispatch was to be returned to the correspondent — the secretariat clerk whose own hand had set down the telegram at the division message desk — and a nearer unit, the forward company already established on the airfield, was to take the route instead of the courier. The signal clerk copied the order into the field log exactly as received, and the log line, word for word, is this:

“Return to the correspondent of the secretariat's own hand — by a nearer neighbour, replace it.”

The clerk’s transcription stands in the case as found, stencil over pencil, the last three letters underlined twice. The courier never took the route; the forward company did.

Read with this case

The dispatch in Case 2 belongs to the same message desk that keeps the ledger of a certain very large signature: the Hancock — Field Error Reference, Revision 1793, where the blotting-paper remedy for wet documents is still standing order. And the record office shares its file room with the visitor's sheets: the Italy — A Visitor's Brochure files the Secchia valley and its cabin reel in the same cabinet, a few shelves from the Markham Valley log.

The courier this case never sends on the route is kept at work in one other record: Entry 2003.11.07 of the Hindu Kush Weather Almanac — Operation Mountain Resolve — where a dispatch from 7 November 2003 concerns its courier as well, and the order goes the other way: the courier takes the original dispatch to be cleaned, the only washing of a field document this record office has ever been made to note.

Beside Case 1, on the bench that cut this case's glass, the airfield is being rebuilt at 1:35 in the six-year table of the Second World War — Build Log, Table No. 6, where this corner is built to the standard of the placard and every parts slip comes back with its last three letters underlined twice. The same desk that underlines the slips keeps one letter in imperial ink, addressed to a state that no longer exists, a shelf above the Markham Valley log: An Apology to the Dual Monarchy — in it the desk owns a flag in the wrong colours and a folio of the build log that reads backwards.
