MEDITERRANEAN FLEET LOST & FOUND  ·  ROOM 214, HARBOUR FRONT, HAIFA  ·  LEDGER SHEET 1967  ·  PIN NAIL NO. 9

Notice No. 1405/67

LOST — One technical research ship, grey with a black boot-topping line, answers to USS Liberty (AGTR-5), last spoken 8 June 1967, 1030 hours, Gulf of Aqaba, at the corner 28°37′ N, 34°24′ E.

Found items listed below. Name to be claimed. The sea keeps its own hours.

1 · What was lost

The Liberty was a technical research ship of the United States Navy — a spy ship, in the phrase the log uses once and then stops using — three hundred and thirty-three feet of hull running the Suez route in the days before the route closed. On the morning of 8 June 1967, during the Six-Day War, she was twenty-eight degrees and thirty-seven minutes north of the equator and, by every account, on a course nobody can quite draw straight from memory. The attack that followed lasted twenty-three minutes and was carried out by four Mirage III jet fighters, which strafed the deck and bombed it with napalm, and by two Israeli Navy motor torpedo boats, the small boats the crews of those years called mosquito boats, which fired rockets and machine guns at the same deck until the ship listed and the fire could not be stopped. Of the crew, thirty-four were killed — naval officers, seamen, two marines, and one civilian employee of the National Security Agency — and seventy-one and some said one hundred and seventy-one were wounded; this office enters 171, the number the ship herself gave, with a check mark. The ship was severely damaged, took on fire that spread from deck to deck, and was rescued after five days of the worst kind by the destroyer she was never to be mistaken for again.

She is believed to have been taken for another ship of the same name on the other side of the line. Her identity papers — the white flag, the white hull, the letters LIBERTY in six-foot black type on both sides of the bow — were in the ship, plainly. Nobody is recorded as having come to check. That is the whole of the circumstances, and this notice asks to be read on that footing.

2 · What was found

  • Lot 1 — Twenty-three minutes of air and fire, complete, unbroken, in the original packaging of the morning. Sealed and kept. Return to the hour of 1030.
  • Lot 2 — Thirty-four names, cut in the order the families asked: the officers first, the seamen, the two marines, and the civilian of the agency last, because he asked to be last. One name per card, no scanning, standing read.
  • Lot 3 — One hundred and seventy-one dressings, most of them already used. A box of them, for the next notice.
  • Lot 4 — Two inquiries, one Israeli, one American, both concluded and both issued, both saying the attack was a mistake on the account of confusion about the ship's identity. Both kept, neither destroyed, both filed under M — for mistake, and for the other word neither report prints.
  • Lot 5 — One box drawn in red, by hand, on a harbour-front map, at the corner 28°37′ N, 34°24′ E, with a coordinate in its corner and nothing else. The same hand that draws these boxes draws the one for 17 May 1987 two years of folders down; the method is the hand's, not the board's.
  • Lot 6 — One leaf, torn out of a traveller's notebook, found folded in the upper berth of the officer's cabin, addressed to no one and dated by the water. It is catalogued, on the shelf that keeps it, beside the encyclopædia entry on Iran, and it says, word for word: “The four-line stanza, the ruba’i, a line and a prayer.” The officer who carried it had no account to answer to on that subject. The leaf is left open, as the board does all its folders.

3 · Claim procedure

The ship may not be claimed. She was sold from the Navy in 1959, renamed S.S. Sea Scout on a river, and broken up at Keokuk, Iowa, in 1985, by which date this office had been keeping the notice for eighteen years and had stopped expecting the nail to come out. The name, however, may be claimed, and the procedure is one line: state the name as cut on the bow, six feet of black type, both sides, and say who you are to the water. The family has the first claim, and has exercised it; the ledger shows the name as held. All others are entered in the waiting book, which is kept, as all the board's books are, in Room 214, on the harbour front, beside the red box and beside the other grey hull in the same ledger — the frigate of 17 May 1987, the two missiles, the thirty-seven names — covered in the visitor's brochure, Gulf Passage: A Visitor's Brochure for the Strait of Hormuz. Two grey hulls, two red boxes, one hand; the board's whole method is a folder kept open.

For a counter that keeps its own kind of found items — one album, twelve ounces, under seat 4B — the Westlake Road Lost & Found ledger runs the same discipline of lots and claimants. The rest of the collection, and the front of the archive, is at the front page of the board's archive.

4 · From the minutes of the counter, Room 214

Entry of 8 June 1967, 1102 hours, written in the margin of the notice itself, in the hand that draws the boxes.

Twenty-three minutes counted from the first pass to the last boat leaving, counted twice, agreeing. The ship's lights were on, the flag was white, and the letters were six feet high; the counter records these three facts and then records, on a separate line, that it does not understand them. The notice is to be pinned where the pin will hold — the same nail as 1987, which holds, and has held, and is not to be moved for the convenience of either flag. The leaf in Lot 6 is to stay open. It is the counter's one standing order that outranks the others: what is torn out of a notebook on a ship that is on fire is not lost, it is found, and found things are kept.
