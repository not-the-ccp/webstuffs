Build Log No. 9 2234 NW Flanders Street, the garage below the attic Scale 1:24 · Chassis 25/6

Formula One, one part per meeting, no flipping the needle

A Grand Prix in the Garage Below

A 1:24 Lotus Type 25, racing green, number 12 — the car that won the 1962 Formula One World Championship and took this garage two winters and one long summer to build.

Kept by the club's self-appointed Chief Mechanic, who was appointed by no one. Painted by candlelight, or the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour. The workbench is the old boathouse bench, where the roof drip has kept time over everything we have ever made or heard since before the club had a name.

LAP 1

22 November 2025 · 6 h 40 min

The kit arrived with the Friday post, the way the best and the worst news has a habit of coming to this house: a 1:24 resin Lotus Type 25, 340 parts in two bags, instructions in a language of small explosions. The car itself is a subject this garage has not needed to learn. The Type 25 was the first Formula One car built around a monocoque — one shell instead of a ladder frame, the whole chassis a single piece of pressed aluminium, and it was, for the 1962 season, almost unfair: it won its second race, took the team to four drivers' titles in two years, and made the previous decade of car look like the previous decade of record. The word formula in the sport's name is a rulebook: one engine size, one set of rules every car must follow, and within that small room, the fastest thinking. I intend to follow the rules exactly. That is the whole joke of the club, applied to a garage.

LAP 2

6 December 2025 · 4 h 15 min

Chassis dry-fit, all 61 aluminium-effect parts. The beam drip over the bench is set to a rate of one drop roughly every four seconds, which is exactly the interval two-part epoxy asks for to be mixed and used before it closes. The Gallery 3 timetable lists the drip as a percussion instrument and I will not argue — here it is a metronome, and a very good one, better than any I have owned, because it does not stop when the listening ends. I fitted the body, dry, held on with three paperclips, and stood back. In candlelight a 1:24 car is almost real, and almost the wrong size, and both at once. The reel counter on attic unit No. 2, which has been running all winter for Tobias Finch at sea, runs in revolutions; a Formula One engine of 1962 ran at 14,500 revolutions a minute. I have made a rule that I zero the counter before every paint session and read it after. What it measures is not important. It must run.

LAP 3

17 January 2026 · 8 h 05 min

Base coats. The green is mixed from three tubes to match a photograph of the 1962 car that is itself a photograph of a film, which is a depth of secondhand seeing I have decided is a feature. Coats are numbered in the margin of this log. By coat 47 I had stopped counting in coats and started counting in weeks of not counting, which is when the green started to look like green and not like a colour. Forty-seven is the newsletter's number and I will not change it. The obituary went up in the attic this month — the Slowhand, 30 March 1945 to 30 March 2026, to be exact, arrived a few weeks before this entry was finished — and the nickname comes from a 1967 television appearance in which a camera operator asked him to play a little slower and the camera, being a camera, could not. The club has taken that instruction into the garage as a method: nothing at this bench is rushed, and the Slowhand is the club's saint now, so the car is built slowly in his name and one right hand, unarguable, is credited in the log. One part per meeting. One lap per entry. Do not flip.

LAP 4

9 February 2026 · 5 h 30 min

Details: the number 12, white, on the nose, the way it wore it on the circuits of 1962; the air intake, the wheel rims turned by hand from brass rod, eight of them, turned to tolerance over one long session while We Are Fearless ran whole in the attic upstairs and nobody flipped, which you can verify against Newsletter No. 47, The Long Side, where the 8-track watch keeps Track 8 — the hush between the final chord and the next amen — and the engine in this box stays silent until it does not. It is a fair arrangement. The Long Side is the club's own charter and it states the rule this garage is only obeying with paint: however the thing reaches you, in order, in whole, and you say so at the next meeting.

LAP 5

21 March 2026 · 7 h 20 min

The day of the note. Sanding the rear wing under the bench, in the gap between the whale's shadow and the door, I pried loose a board in the beam that had not moved in decades, and under it, in a race notebook from 1948 with a water-stuck cover, a pencil line in a hand we did not recognise, in a script this old and this tired. It reads, word for word:

"This is of little Alice and all his hardy sobriety and fortitude, there were promise in the same light at that hour."

We have sat with it for a while. Little Alice is what the first three of us called the boathouse itself, before it was a boathouse and before it was the club, when it was just a boat under canvas that someone kept promising to fix. All his hardy sobriety and fortitude is the Chief Mechanic on coat 31, standing in the cold, who would deny it, and the same light at that hour is this light — the candlelight, or the phone torch, which the obituary already told you are the same. The attic, meanwhile, has opened a second wake for a subject that will not stop moving, at Funk — In Memoriam (for now), where the drip over the bench is eulogized as a percussion instrument and the one, unarguable, keeps the garage’s four seconds without once being asked to. The line is nonsense on the page and, under the beam where it was kept, it is the whole project in eleven and a half words: a small car, a patient hand, a promise, and one light. I have re-nailed the board over it. The note goes back under it. The log keeps a copy, because logs are for keeping.

LAP 6

27 March 2026 · 3 h 45 min

The last week before the birthday, which the garage does not mark and the attic does. We put the car together on the bench, four corners to the floor, the 12 on the nose, and the drip kept its four seconds while the needle upstairs ran its long side. The reel counter read 061214 at the end of the session — I am not going to explain the number and you are not going to ask. The car is parked in the boathouse on the shelf beside the first-press Layla, facing the door, the way a racing car waits at the start. The season it is built for, in this room, consists of a series of races known as meetings, held first Thursday at 19:30, and the first Grand Prix of it will be the May listening, played whole, with the car under canvas and the two empty chairs by the window empty, and the hush, on the watch and on the bench, kept to the rule. For one night, if it comes, the rule is lifted for the needle only. The obituary is in the attic, set down by candlelight, and it says bring the record; this log says bring the car, which is smaller, and the same slow way. The attic has also opened a case in Gallery 3 for the other slow hand, at Dr. Dre — Placard No. 48, where the drip kept time over the mounting of the record, and where one line the committee will not explain sets the coat count down as the whole thing that feels in the dark. The bench has not argued with it. Forty-seven is forty-seven, on the car and on the accession card, and the reel counter, which must run, is running.

PLATE

Between meetings the car races the one circuit the garage has ruled itself onto a board: the The Caspian Circuit — Field & Race Manual, Plate AZ-24, a landlocked grand prix around the Republic of Azerbaijan, fourteen sectors long, Baku at the start dot, Nakhchivan entered twice because the exclave is not attached, and the Caspian ruled a lake by a vote the drip cast alone. The plate shares the build's two instruments and its first Thursday, and its last sector is named for the hush. The club also keeps a copy of the minutes of a borough committee that meets in a subway mezzanine in Queens, at Minutes of a Meeting — 21st Street–Queensbridge Station, where the M train holds an ex officio seat and votes by being punctual; the Chief Mechanic has taken its ruling that terminal is a condition, not a character, over the bench, and the car waits at its own end of the line for the same growing day. The kitchen keeps its own card for the evenings the whole house plays in one day, at Card 17 — Loaf for the Long Innings, an oat and molasses loaf for the Cricket World Cup that is baked to the same tin that holds the sleeve, cut with the knife and not the hands, and taken up to the shelf beside the car to rise while the drip keeps its four seconds over it, because the club has ruled the tin and the chassis are the same kind of thing.

Between meetings the garage keeps one catalogue page that is not a race: Lot 23 — The Market Ledger of Ashton-under-Lyne, an auction lot for a market town’s book kept on the north bank of the Tame since 1762, where the Thursday bell is struck first and the prices come second — the same order this bench has kept for the drip, which keeps time over the forty-seven coats and over the lot alike. The garage has also borrowed one clock from the WNRS script drawer, where a whole show ran on a single tick set to one per second and the tick was the money: The Million Second Quiz — A Radio Play, Script Excerpt, eleven days, thirteen hours, forty-six minutes, and forty seconds, held in one chair. The drip under the beam is that same tick at garage speed, and the bench keeps it over the car the way the chair kept the number: without being asked to stop.

This log is printed by candlelight, or by the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour. The car is built one part per meeting, the record is played side into side, and the drip keeps time over both. Do not flip. The hush, on the bench as on the watch, is the whole point.
