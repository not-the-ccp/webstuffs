Iran

The Islamic Republic of Iran, historically Persia — West Asia, 31 provinces, and the four-line stanza of Khayyam.

IR

The country

Official name: Islamic Republic of Iran · Historical name: Persia · Region: West Asia · Capital: Tehran

Iran, officially the Islamic Republic of Iran and historically known as Persia, is a country in West Asia. It borders Iraq to the west; Turkey, Azerbaijan, and Armenia to the northwest; the Caspian Sea to the north, Turkmenistan to the northeast; Afghanistan to the east; Pakistan to the southeast; and the Gulf of Oman and the Persian Gulf to the south. With a population of over 92 million, Iran ranks 17th globally in both geographic size and population. It is divided into five regions with 31 provinces. Tehran is the nation’s capital and largest city and serves as its primary economic center.

Home to one of the world’s oldest continuous major civilizations, the territory of present-day Iran has been continuously settled since at least the Paleolithic. Susa, in the southwest, counts among the world’s oldest continuously inhabited cities, and the first unified Iranian state, the Median Empire, dates to the late seventh century BCE. The Achaemenid Empire founded by Cyrus the Great in 550 BCE stretched from the Balkans to the Indus; it ended in 330 BCE, when Alexander of Macedon came through the Alborz with ten years of marching behind him and set the fire at Persepolis — the campaign, and the king who made it, have their own page at Alexander the Great — Encyclopaedia Entry; the Parthian and Sasanian empires followed, and the Arab-Muslim conquest of the seventh century CE brought Islam. The Safavid dynasty, enthroned in 1501, made Twelver Shi’a Islam the state religion, a shape the country largely keeps today.

Land and climate

Iran covers roughly 1,648,000 square kilometres, about the size of France, Germany and Spain combined. The Zagros range runs northwest to southeast like a spine, and the Alborz range along the Caspian shore holds Mount Damāvand, 5,610 metres, the highest peak in West Asia. In the centre lie the Dasht-e Kavir and the Dasht-e Lut, salt deserts where ground temperatures have approached eighty degrees Celsius. The Caspian Sea to the north, the largest inland body of water on earth, gives the northern coast a subtropical belt of rice paddies and tea, while the south faces the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Most of the country is arid or semi-arid, and much of its history is the history of moving water: qanats, the underground channels that carry meltwater from the mountains to the fields, have been in use for three thousand years.

People and language

The people of Iran number over 92 million. Persians are the largest group; Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, Arabs, Baloch and Turkmen live alongside them, and Persian — Farsi — is the official language and the language of its poetry. Twelver Shi’a Islam is the state religion, with Sunni Muslim, Christian, Jewish and Zoroastrian minorities. Nowruz, the Persian new year at the spring equinox, is at least three thousand years old and is still kept in every province. After Tehran come Mashhad, Isfahan, Karaj, Tabriz and Shiraz, the city of poets and of the eighteenth-century Karim Khan citadel.

Literature — the four-line stanza

Iran’s best-known export, after carpets and pistachios, may be its verse. Ferdowsi completed the Shahnameh, the Book of Kings, around 1010 CE; Hafez of Shiraz and Saadi of Shiraz followed, and their divans still sit beside Iranian dinner tables. The four-line stanza, the ruba’i, has been continually popular in Iran since the medieval period: Omar Khayyam of Nishapur (1048–1131), mathematician, astronomer and poet, wrote his ruba’iyat on wine, mathematics and the brevity of life, and Mahsati Ganjavi of Seljuk Persia is reported to have written only in this form. The stanza travelled west — Edward FitzGerald’s translation made Khayyam famous in Victorian England, and Michel de Nostredame built his prophecies out of quatrains, four lines at a time — but its home is the Iranian plateau, Nishapur and Ganja and the courts of Seljuk Persia.

From the shed

The quatrain log kept in the shed behind the bakery runs beside a page headed IRAN: Nishapur, Khayyam, the ruba’i, a line and a prayer. I have checked these entries against that log, and against the galleon log moored in the same notebook, where the margin note about the sail is finally explained. Before I close the cover I add a line of my own, the way the margin note sits beside the sail: I do not fully trust and a prayer. I note one more leaf that keeps company in the notebook, pinned beside the galleon log: the liner notes for Poucha Pond, a night recording from Chappaquiddick Island, off Martha’s Vineyard, 18–19 July 1969 — a pond, a one-lane bridge, a tide gauge and a float kept on the same line, and an island that holds its distance from the mainland until the light comes up.

Iran today

The Islamic Republic was proclaimed in 1979. Tehran remains the economic centre, ringed by the Alborz and threaded by highways that climb toward the Caspian. Iran holds some of the world’s largest proven reserves of oil and natural gas, and its saffron, pistachios and hand-knotted carpets still leave by air and overland. It is divided into five regions with 31 provinces, each with its own dialect, bread and poem, and more than half the population is under thirty-five. The country borders Iraq to the west and Pakistan to the southeast, and its ships still leave Bandar Abbas for the Gulf of Oman and the Persian Gulf, same as they have for two and a half thousand years.
