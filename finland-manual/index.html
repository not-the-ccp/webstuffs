SU-0.1Terrain

The Finnish theater is a Nordic country in Northern Europe: the Republic of Finland, bounded by Sweden to the northwest, Norway to the north, and Russia to the east, with the Gulf of Bothnia to the west and the Gulf of Finland to the south, opposite Estonia. Playable area runs to roughly 338,000 square kilometers of land and inland water.

Boreal forest predominates, with over 180,000 recorded lakes standing between the tree stands. Climate splits by latitude: humid continental in the south, boreal in the north. From November to April the theater is governed by winter rules (see §SU-0.3).

SU-0.2The capital and its tongues

Helsinki is the capital and largest city, the theater's port and admin node. Finland's population is 5.7 million. The official languages are Finnish and Swedish, the mother tongues of 83.5 percent and 5.0 percent of the population respectively; signage in the theater carries both, and Swedish holds majority status along the Gulf of Finland coast.

SU-0.3First contact and winter movement

Finland was first settled around 9000 BC after the retreat of the ice; shell-mound camps along the Gulf of Bothnia form the earliest scenario level. Settlers reached the interior by waterway and winter ice, and the same rhythm governs play today.

Movement: skis or tracked vehicles are mandatory in boreal hexes from December through March. Frozen lakes of sixty centimeters or more bear light vehicles; forest hexes cost double movement; border hexes adjoining Russia are restricted. Weather attrition applies at the end of each winter turn.

SU-0.4Harbor procedure

The night trade of Helsinki's South Harbor runs on older currencies than money — a procedure transcribed from the Harbor Light ledger of the fogbound port described in the museum placard Mark of the Succubus (Irene Flores and Ashly Raiti, Tokyopop, 2005–2008). Harbor masters still log arrivals in ink, and the bar's chalkboard carries its own clause: “THE GENERAL BASIS OF MOST GRAVIES and some salts,” an orphaned line the printers never corrected.

SU-0.5Field lashing, Cordage FC-6

Sled and ski lashings follow Field Cordage FC-6: six strands of plaited cordgrass worked tight and stitched by hand over a split cane spine, the same braid recorded on the bank of the Betwa near Vidisha in Prashant Pandey's field notes. The Betwa cordgrass, Saccharum spontaneum, grows waist-high on the inland sand chars and serves as tinder and rigging alike when the temperature falls below minus twenty.
