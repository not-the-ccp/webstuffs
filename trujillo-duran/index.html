CARRERA 21 · GALERÍAS DEL CINE · CARACAS

✶ A Letter of Apology ✶

Addressed to Manuel Trujillo Durán
Photographer · Cinematographer · First of the Latins to hold a projector's hand
8 January 1871 — 14 March 1933

Caracas, 14 March 1933 · written that night, at the shutter of the gallery on Calle Cedeño, while the city still does not know the news

Señor Trujillo Durán,

I am the woman who wrote the cinema column of El Cojo de la Cedeño for eleven years, from 1921 to 1932, and I am writing to you now because the obituary in the morning paper gave you four lines, of which three were spent on your studio on Calle Cedeño and one, wrongly, on your son. You have died on the fourteenth of March at the age of sixty-two, and I have decided that you will not go out of this city with my name on your file. This is an apology, and it is late, and I do not believe lateness excuses it, but I believe a letter is better than silence, which is what I have given you for half a century.

You were the first. I have seen the photographs. In 1905 you came back from Paris with a cinematographe crated to the hold of the ship, and you opened the crates at the port of La Guaira before the import officer had finished his breakfast. You carried the machine, and your assistant José Carrasquero carried the reels, and together you walked up into Caracas in the rain and unspooled the first Venezuelan screening in the courtyard of the Casa del Sol, before an audience of, by the ticket stubs, two hundred and fourteen souls, and I count the stubs myself: two hundred and fourteen, at twenty céntimos apiece. Then you took the projectors out. Valencia in 1906. Maracaibo, twice. Barquisimeto. You crossed over into Colombia — Cúcuta, then Bogotá, where the press of the time called you el hombre de la linterna mágica and never, not once in the pages I have since reread, printed your surname.

And here the ledger begins, because it was my papers that followed theirs. In 1912, when you screened at the Teatro Caracas, I wrote that the show was run by «the Frenchman R.” There was no Frenchman R. You had taught yourself the machine in Paris by standing in the alley behind the Pathé plant and watching the apprentices, and then you taught it to the rest of this continent, and my column gave the credit to a man who does not exist. I have checked. I am sorry. I do not know why; I was twenty-six and the projector seemed foreign enough to be foreign-run, and I had the impudence of my age.

You let me be wrong. That is the other part of the account. You came to my office in 1923 with a roll of film of your own — your last, of the coast at Macuto, and I held it in my hands and it is the only roll I have ever been given, and I have never screened it, because I am afraid that if I unspool it the light will come through the frames and I will have to sit in the dark with you again — and you said nothing about the Frenchman, and you asked me instead to hold the door while the boy from the darkroom brought the water. You were always asking for the door to be held. Fifty years this city has not held the door for you.

They say you were most successful as a photographer, and that the films were only the other business. They will write it that way, because a man with a camera is easier to believe in than a man with a projector. But it is the projector I cannot forgive myself for, because the projector is the machine that makes a whole room into one eye, and you were the first of Latin America to learn how to make the room, and for twenty years after that we watched your light and called it somebody else's. The reels of the Maracaibo shows have long rotted in a trunk in the house of a man whose name I will not print. They are done when it is now no one could name. The films are finished, and the man who ran them is finished, and the only unfinished thing is this sentence in a newspaper nobody keeps, written eleven years too late, and in the wrong hand, and to the wrong name.

I am enclosing a small cheque to the studio on Calle Cedeño for the photograph of me that hangs above your darkroom door, the one from 1909 in which I am nineteen and holding a copy of a paper that did not yet have my name on it. You can keep it. You did the taking and you deserve to keep the only good thing in that frame.

The projection room is empty. I have left the lamp cold. When the obituary runs tomorrow, I have asked the editor — in full print, with the dates, with the surname — for one paragraph that says what it took you to say: that Manuel Trujillo Durán, born in this city on 8 January 1871, carried the cinema up from La Guaira in 1905, and that he was ours, and first, and that he is dead on the fourteenth of March, 1933, and that we are sorry, and that the word is late, and that it is the best we have.

Yours, in the dark,

E. R. — cinema column, El Cojo de la Cedeño, 1921–1932

Enclosure · ticket stubs, 1905, Casa del Sol courtyard

Nº 1 to 214 · one screening, one projector, two hundred and fourteen souls, twenty céntimos apiece, the door held by J. Carrasquero, the light by M. T. D. The stubs survive in a shoebox in the paper's vault; the film is not among them. They are done when it is now no one could name.

Elsewhere in the archive, on the keeping of a name

A register of honours whose committee file was sealed and whose ribbon was returned to the vault, a recognition that stopped conferring the way a projector stops turning: the Indra Rajya Laxmi Pragya Puraskar — Patent Application Summary, whose last entry is on its shelf, which is where I would like this apology to end up, filed and legible and no longer anonymous.

And a book kept by a guard on a morning train, who writes down the facts of the working — the distances, the platforms, the names in the berth — and nothing more than the whole, which is the only rule I broke: the The Dhaka Dawn Express — Timetable & Editorial Notes · BTR Route 6, where a man writes the facts in order and the flag stays small, and where I might have written your name on the platform at 04:12, instead of the Frenchman R. Both records are filed with Webstuffs, the shelf above the darkroom.

Manuel Trujillo Durán · 8 January 1871 — 14 March 1933 · Caracas, La Guaira, Valencia, Maracaibo, Barquisimeto, Cúcuta, Bogotá

The lamp is cold. The name is printed.
