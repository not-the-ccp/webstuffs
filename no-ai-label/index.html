Woodbine Lane County School · Term Report, Form 5C · Michaelmas Term

The No-AI Label

Candidate: Rosalind A. Okonkwo · Candidate No. 047 · Assessor: Mrs. D. Hargreaves, Product History & the Working Hand · Report number 5C/92 · filed in the green tray, second drawer

1. What the label is

A no-AI label is a label used on a product to signify that it was not made using generative artificial intelligence (AI). It is, in the language of the working hand, a certificate of labour: a small mark, no wider than a thumb, that stands in for the longer sentence every part of this was made by a person. The candidate notes that the label does not describe how the thing was made — the jam is still jam, the greeting card still card — only that the making happened in a body and not in a black box. On the shelf of the school kitchen store there are now eleven jars wearing the mark, and every one of them tastes of a specific afternoon, which is precisely the evidence the label claims to carry.

The mark appears on tins, jam jars, greeting cards, wool blankets, bread loaves, postage stamps, and one very large door. The candidate met the door in the shopfront of T. Bramwell & Son, binders of boxes and books, 14 Woodbine Lane, where the no-AI label is pasted at eye level on the oak panel itself, so that the shop, and not merely its wares, is certified as the work of hands. Mr Bramwell, who is eighty-one and does not know what a black box is, reads the mark as “the stamp of the week,” which is close, and which the candidate submits is better.

2. The phrases on the labels

Phrases on the labels include but are not limited to "human-made", "proudly human" and "AI-free". The candidate counted them in the market row over three mornings and kept a tally: human-made appears on 14 of the 31 labelled goods, proudly human on 9, AI-free on 5, and the remaining 3 carry variants — “made by a person, in this town”, “our hands only”, and one card that reads simply “I was drawn by my grandmother”, which the candidate marks as the strongest of the lot. The phraseology is doing the work of a whole sentence: pride, negation, and a claim of authorship, compressed to fit a circle the size of a tuppence.

3. Who issues the labels

Some organisations that provide the labels allow product creators to download and use their no-AI labels for free, while others charge creators for analysing the content and allowing the use of the labels if the organisation believes that no AI was used. The two kinds are as different as the school library and the bank. The free kind — the candidate lists The Guild of the Unmachine, which sets its stamps out of a print room in Portland and offers the designs, in four sizes, to anyone who asks — operates on trust and on the signature of the maker; the label says who made it, not that anyone checked. The paid kind operates on examination: The Hand-Work Registry of Sheffield charges the maker three pounds per article, opens the article, weighs it, smears a flake of its glaze under a lens, and asks the maker six questions about tools, times, and the colour of the kitchen light, before the label may go on. The Registry's ledger, which the candidate was permitted to view through the window, shows 214 articles assayed in Michaelmas and 211 passed; the 3 that failed were returned with a note in blue ink that read we cannot tell it is yours, which is, the candidate thinks, a true and difficult verdict.

4. The candidate's own experiment

The candidate applied for the free stamp, on the maker's signature, for a jar of blackberry jam, and for the paid assay, on the same recipe, for a second jar. The first jar went on the shelf with its label on a Tuesday. The second jar was examined for eleven minutes, passed, and labelled on a Wednesday, having cost the candidate three pounds and one answer she could not get right (the colour of the kitchen light, which she had said grey and which, she now admits, was grey). Both jars are on the shelf. Both jars are the same jam. What the experiment showed the candidate is that the label is not a test of the jam at all; it is a test of the maker's willingness to be found, and the paid label is simply the finding done by someone else, in blue ink.

5. A line kept word for word

One line from the second floor of the school library, found on a card the candidate had pulled for a different subject and did not put back, is kept in this report word for word, on the shelf it came from, beside the tins of the 1962 streamlining lectures: The field of fluid dynamics and its soundings. The candidate does not know why the card is in the jam drawer of her notes. The assessor has declined to explain it, which is, at this school, a mark of the highest order.

6. Assessment

The candidate is commended on the three mornings of market-row counting and on the honesty of the kitchen light. The report is filed with the school's other term reports, including the term report on the Lockheed C-130 Hercules, Form 4B, NAS Jacksonville, which the candidate read for the section on unprepared runways, and the term report on the Peresvet class of pre-dreadnoughts, Form 3B, which the candidate read for the section on what it means to outlive the argument of your own squadron. The candidate is also referred, for the domestic side of the working hand, to Card 3127 of the Michaelis family book, dry-cell shortbread, where a whole preamble on transistors ends, as it must, at a tray of twenty-four strips. The front shelf of the school — Webstuffs — holds the collection these reports are filed into, and the green tray, second drawer, holds this one.
