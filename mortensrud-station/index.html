Field diary, kept in Oslo · volume of the winter platforms

Mortensrud, 24 November

Søndre Nordstrand, Oslo · Østensjø Line · line 3 · the last platform

Saturday · 24 November · 17:42

I took the last train out on line 3 and rode it all the way to where it stops, because a terminus is the only honest edge a line can have and I had not been to one in years. The platform at Mortensrud sits in the Søndre Nordstrand borough of Oslo, and tonight the whole Østensjø Line — 13.6 kilometres of it, 8.5 miles, from the centre of the city out through the dark — ends here, at this one strip of concrete with the yellow line and the benches that smell of wood shavings. The train pulls in, doors, a few passengers, doors, and the departure board shows the next service and nothing after it. This is what a terminus means: the board ends.

What I did not know, and what I spent the whole walk up from the concourse finding out, is that this is a very recent terminus. The station was taken into use on 24 November 1997 — the date is carved above the ticket hall in the same letters I am reading them tonight — and it became the terminal of the line that day, the end of a 2.4-kilometre extension from Skullerud, most of it in tunnels. They spent 215 million kroner on that section of track, and the station itself is a separate, smaller figure: 35 million kroner, NOK, in concrete, wood and stone. I touched all three. The concrete is the platform and the wall, grey and patient. The wood is the benches, the panelling, the warm grain under a cold hand. The stone is what I came back for.

On the concourse floor, set in a shallow channel of pale granite, there is a bed of stones — rough, river-grey, unnumbered except by the light. Above it, where a plate has been replaced more than once (the frame still carries the screw holes of the earlier plates, which I counted: four, four, three), hangs a plate that reads, and I have copied it into this diary word for word: “The full docket for the completeness of the stones it watches.” I asked the ticket agent what it means. She looked at the plate, looked at the stones, and said that it was the station’s only notice that had never once been amended, which is its own kind of answer. A docket, I decided, is a list of everything still owed, and a station at the end of a line owes everything — every extension, every tunnel, every 215 million — to the place where it finally stops. The stones are the audit. They sit there and make the station complete, or they do not. There is no third option, and no later plate will say otherwise.

Before I left I watched the buses, because Mortensrud is not only the end of a train line but the bus terminal for the whole borough, its ranks of departures ticking over in the winter dark like a second departure board, and among them the feeder service to Bjørndal — number 31, I wrote it down, 31, as though the number were a small stone in the channel and needed counting. The extension was built from 1995, the two years of tunneling before this platform existed, and I understood standing there that I was sitting on a very new place pretending to be a very old one, which is what all termini do. The 22:05 came in, doors, a few passengers, doors. The board reset. The stones did not.

Two other keeps of the same winter: the Catalonia day-count of 11 September, where the keeping turned out to be arithmetic, and the Lake Wells Station placard, No. 17, where one small line in a radio log did the work this plate does — a docket kept unamended, filed where the receiver stands. The shelves themselves, if you want to start from the front, are at the Webstuffs front page.
