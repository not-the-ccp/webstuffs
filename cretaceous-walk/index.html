Field Itineraries · No. 77 · Issued by the Registrar’s Back Room

The Chalk Circuit

A Walking Tour of the Cretaceous in Eight Stops

Period
The Cretaceous, krih-TAY-shæss, 143.1 to 66 Ma
Length
77.1 million years — the longest of all
Distance
7.71 km, one kilometre for every ten million years
Gradient
Level the whole way; the sea did the climbing
Departure
The gate above the bakery, 9:00 sharp, lamp lit whether it is needed or not

The Cretaceous is the third and final period of the Mesozoic Era and the longest; at around 77.1 million years it is the ninth and longest geological period of the entire Phanerozoic, so the household has walked it in its full length, once, at the measured rate of one kilometre per ten million years, and this is the itinerary as it stands in the ledger. The name is derived from the Latin creta, chalk, which is abundant in the deposits of the latter half of the period; it is usually abbreviated K, for its German translation Kreide, and the two forms of the name are painted, one on the gate and one on the gate post, so that every walk begins in the word for the stone. The period we walk was warm. That single word does most of the walking for us: a warm climate, high eustatic sea levels, and numerous shallow inland seas standing over land that had forgotten it was land.

The guide for the day is E. V. Hartley of the Blue Gum station hut, who keeps the Lepidoptera desk and, on 2 March 2025, left a lichen-marbled geometrid moth — Archephanes zalosema, Turner 1926 — sitting on a century-old season card, wings closed, doing better at the fence post than anyone. The field diary of that morning records her by signature, and the household has known ever since that a guide who can keep an insect at rest for an hour can keep a walking party in order for seven and a half kilometres. The registrar keeps the ledger at the rear of the column, ink drying slowly, as ever.

  1. Stop 1 · 0.0 km · 9:00

    The Chalk Gate

    Departure at the gate above the bakery. The lintel is a slab of white creta, and on the left post someone has scratched the letter K in small capitals with a point that was not meant for stone; the cut is clean, the chalk dust falls in a fine slow snow, and the guide says only K, for Kreide, which is the whole of stop one. The party is counted. Twelve. The ledger gets its first line of the day, and the ink, as promised, dries slowly.

  2. Stop 2 · 0.9 km · 9:40

    The Third and Final Signal Box

    A wayside box painted the exact grey of a stratigraphic chart, stencilled 3 of 3. The Cretaceous is the third and final period of the Mesozoic Era — the third signal in a sequence of three, with the box already dark, because the walk is proceeding away from the era, not into it. The guide points out, for the record, that “final” does not mean failed: the longest period on the Phanerozoic’s roster does not hurry, and neither does the party. A bench inside the box, still warm from the morning. Tea, two sugars, no one asks.

  3. Stop 3 · 1.8 km · 10:25

    The Inland Sea

    Here the footpath runs along the edge of a long, shallow, sun-warmed lagoon, and the party is told to walk as if the water were all the way to the top of the hill, because in the Cretaceous it nearly was. The warm climate held eustatic sea levels high for tens of millions of years; shallow inland seas, not oceans, stood over the continents, and the boundary between salt and grass was a line you could wade across. The guide stops one member of the party at the water’s edge to count the ripples and call them tides, which is the closest word we have for the way the sea behaved. The registrar notes the water in the ledger in the same grey as the signal box.

  4. Stop 4 · 2.7 km · 11:10

    The Marine Reptile Gallery

    A row of glass cases set along the sea’s edge, one case per swimmer, all of them dark, all of them lit from the water side. The Cretaceous seas were populated with now-extinct marine reptiles — plesiosaurs with their long necks arched for the horizon, mosasaurs built like surfboards and just as eager to ride them, and lesser figures the guide names in a low voice, as if the cases might answer. They will not. They are extinct, and the word is written on each case in small letters, in the German, in the Latin, and in the chalk: creta. A visitor asks whether any of them made it to the boundary stop. The guide looks at the cases and says the walk will tell us.

  5. Stop 5 · 3.7 km · 12:05

    The Longest Room

    Midday under a pergola the household has been shading for a very long time. The subject of the stop is the number 77.1, and the method is to spend an hour with it. The guide hands out the arithmetic: nine periods of the Phanerozoic, and this is the ninth in length, the longest of them, the third and last of the Mesozoic, and still the one the chalk remembers best. Someone in the party begins to count years under their breath. The guide does not stop them; at one kilometre per ten million years, the counting is simply the walk, said more slowly.

  6. Stop 6 · 4.6 km · 13:00

    The Boiling House

    Lunch, in a white kitchen whose floor is worn pale by generations of the same pots. The water in the large copper is the exact temperature of the Cretaceous seas, which is to say the party is asked to put their hands in and remember the lagoon, and then to bring the hands to the bread. Eggs are boiled in the sea-temperature water, one for every member of the party plus one for the ledger. The registrar stands by the copper with the book open, and the household rules for this stop are written in the ledger, as always, word for word: Dish them on a dish for the boiling is continued. That is the whole instruction, and it is the point of the stop: the period we are walking is 77.1 million years of heat that did not stop for anyone, so the pot is never lifted, and the food is served on the dish while the water still moves. The party eats standing, on plates, beside a boil that refuses to be finished, and the registrar underlines the line once, lightly, with the slow ink.

  7. Stop 7 · 5.9 km · 14:30

    The Boundary

    The final stop is a short cut of grey-brown clay, no thicker than a hand, running the length of the field, with white chalk above it and older stone below. Sixty-six million years ago the Cretaceous ended here, and the chalk — the very stone the period is named for — is what stands on the warm side of the line. The party is asked to walk the length of it once, slowly, and to note that the marine reptile cases are not continued on this side, and that the signal box at stop two was dark for a reason. The guide speaks little. The registrar writes one line in the ledger: the sea forgets everything it carries, which the dream dictionary has already said of the mud, and the household files the two together. Anyone who wants the household’s longer account of what the ledger keeps on the far side of the line, and of the soft animal it keeps beneath its fifty-two plates, may read the full entry under Luolishania, where the armored one walks on eleven pairs of legs and never leaves the slab — which is, the registrar observes, exactly what a boundary is for.

  8. Stop 8 · 7.71 km · 16:00

    The Gate Again

    Return through the Chalk Gate as the light goes chalk-white itself. The party is counted again: twelve. The K on the post has not moved, the lintel is still white, and the copper at the Boiling House has, on inspection from the gate, continued to boil the entire afternoon without once being fed or emptied, which the guide notes is the only fact of the walk she would refuse to explain. Hartley folds the itinerary, signs it, and leaves it on the bench by the gate, where a small pale moth of her acquaintance would, in March, have done better than either of them. The registrar keeps one note beside the ledger for a reader who asks what the household does with its fast hours: the traveler's guide to the Ataköy Athletics Arena records a women's 400 metres race at the 2023 European Athletics Indoor Championships — 37 editions deep, twenty-seven athletes, one Andorran record of 57.71 seconds — and says, plainly, that if the one-kilometre-per-ten-million-years ratio offends you, come run 400 metres in 52 instead. The registrar closes the ledger, and the ink, which began drying slowly at 9:00, is dry by the time the lamp is turned off. The Cretaceous is walked. The next one is not on the schedule. For the whole household shelf of field records — the bakery, the lamp, the ledger, and everything filed beside it — the front page keeps the register.

Issued from the back room above the bakery. One kilometre, ten million years. The chalk is abundant; the ink dries slowly; the boiling is continued.
