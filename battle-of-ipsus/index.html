The Ipsus Field Papers, 301 BC–1911

Being five items gathered from the plain of Ipsus and its trade roads, Phrygia, one lot

Estimate £4,500 – £6,000
Provenance The stock-book estate of Emile R. Daskalakis, antiquities dealer, 14 Agios Nikolaos Street, Bursa
Deposit £500, in certified funds

11. Offered is a small collection assembled over forty years around a single event: the Battle of Ipsus, fought in 301 BC near the town of Ipsus in Phrygia, where the successors of Alexander the Great — the Diadochi — came to be counted. Antigonus I Monophthalmus, the one-eyed Macedonian who held large parts of Asia, brought down the plain with his son Demetrius, later called Poliorcetes, the Besieger of Cities; against them stood a coalition of three of the other successors: Cassander, ruler of Macedon; Lysimachus, ruler of Thrace; and Seleucus I Nicator, ruler of Babylonia and Persia. The battle decided which of the four would keep the east. Antigonus and Demetrius are reported to have put up some 70,000 infantry, 9,000 cavalry and 113 war elephants; the coalition answered with 60,000 infantry, between 6,000 and 9,000 cavalry, and — the figure the room is asked to remember — 150 elephants, of which the spare contingent was wheeled through the gap between the armies and turned the field. Both the father and the son died on it, and the plain thereafter belonged to whoever could read the map that follows.

The lot comprises five items, packed in Daskalakis's own leather case. First, a hand-drawn plan of the field in iron-gall ink on vellum, attributed to the dealer's grandfather, showing the town of Ipsus at the lower corner, the river bed (a dry wash, in the drawing, and in fact) running above the right flank, the Antigonid line with the elephants marked as small filled circles — 113 of them, counted in threes, three circles short on the far flank, and the missing three crossed out in a later hand with the annotation “lost in the gap”. Second, a bronze sarissa spearhead of the Phrygian field, 31 cm, the socket corroded, the cutting edge honed flat on one face, which our specialists price as a working tool rather than a trophy. Third, a bronze drachm, Antioch, Seleucus I Nicator, the obverse the horned head, the reverse the seated figure with the long sceptre — struck, as the date on the coin's type-cycle suggests, in the first year after the battle, the year in which the winner founded Antioch and named it for the father he had just beaten. Fourth, a marble rubbing taken at Ipsus itself in 1907, of a boundary stone bearing the lettered town name, Ἱψός, the final sigma still legible in the groove. And fifth — the item the room has come for — Daskalakis's stock ledger, 1896–1911, in which the case is listed under the heading “Ipsos, the plain, and what stands on it”.

The ledger is a dealer's honest paper: ink gone the colour of the river bed, prices in pounds, marks in francs, a column of losses kept in red. In the entry for the spearhead, dated 14 October 1904, the dealer records that a grey-and-black magpie took up residence on the point for the full season of 1904, sitting on it through two fairs, and that he was offered the bird — perched, in the ledger's phrase, “like a coin on a stand” — by a boy from the village, who asked two piastres and did not take them. The boy's description, taken down and entered in the margin in Daskalakis's careful hand, reads: “It is the kindest true thing that can hold a perch.” The dealer did not pay. The ledger records, three lines below, in red ink, that the bird was “gone by the second fair, and the perch was as it had been before the bird” — which we offer bidders as a second valuation of the lot, one in feathers rather than in pounds, and which the present valuers decline to price.

For the field itself, as a note of reference: Ipsus stood on the trade road between the Halys and the great plain, and the battle's ground is kept in the modern district of Yalvaç, where the wash still runs where the drawing's river bed runs. For bidders who would see how a plain is kept in paper — creeks, quarter lines, and a star hand-lettered at the place a township would later call for — the township record of a different plain is kept in the same catalogue, at Lot 27, The Bell Creek Township Papers, Burt County, Nebraska, where an 1854 plat still describes the ground it was drawn on. And for the reader who prefers a field at sea to a field in Phrygia, where the ice in the water does not melt because it was never meant to, the entry is filed at Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 717, eighty-two days, one seating, the reef holding the date in place. Both lots, like this one, hold their ground by paperwork.

Condition. The field map is sound, with one repair to the upper corner and the three crossed-out elephants legible in raking light. The spearhead is corroded through the socket and stable; the honed face is original, in our view, and part of the lot's argument that it was a tool. The drachm is very fine, with a light die crack on the reverse. The marble rubbing is complete, the lettering of Ἱψός clean. The ledger shows water at the lower edge of the last sixteen pages; the magpie's entry is dry, and the red column is intact. The case is of dark leather, brass-clasped, with the gilt label “Ipsos” on the lid, worn to a whisper.

Terms. The lot may not be divided. A buyer's premium of fifteen percent applies. Payment at the fall of the gavel, by certified check; the case will be crated on the spot, and the buyer is welcome, but not required, to make the road from the Halys to the plain and count the circles. No magpie is included in the lot. The perch, should the buyer find it, is the buyer's own.
