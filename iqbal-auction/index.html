Lot 214 — The Fare Album of the Long Line, 1905–1908; with the Munich notebook, three letters, and the gold rule of Chapter 12

The papers of Sir Muhammad Iqbal (9 November 1877, Sialkot – 21 April 1938, Lahore), Islamic philosopher and poet, whose Urdu verse is counted among the greatest of the twentieth century and whose vision of a cultural and political ideal for the Muslims of British India is widely held to have animated the impulse behind the Pakistan Movement; the honorific by which the catalogue knows him is Allamah — Persian فلامه, romanized ’Allāma, lit. “learned” — and the Rooms would have him so entered.

An album of two hundred and fourteen numbered cards, “Fares of the Long Line: Sialkot — Lahore — the crossing — Munich,” in the album-keeper’s hand with later annotations, 1905–1908, half-bound in green morocco with the line diagram of the journey in gilt; together, the Munich notebook of 1908 in which the doctor’s thesis on the philosophy of the Prophet Muhammad was set down against the lecture notes of a young man of thirty; three letters, of Lahore 1911, Simla 1928, and Sialkot 1938, the first written in the year of Shikwa and the last signed by a hand already failing; and the gold rule of Chapter 12, pressed across the album’s flyleaf and never once broken.

Estimate £2,400 – £3,200

Notes on the lot

The lot was assembled in the year the poet died, for the assaying of his papers: the notebook for its binding, the letters for their seals, the album for its cards. Iqbal was born and raised in Sialkot, read his first English at the Scotch Mission College here, and carried on from it to Government College Lahore and the bar before the long line took him. In September 1905, aged twenty-eight, he went to Europe; he sat the year at Newnham, Cambridge, in the winter term of 1905–06, and took the long line through to Munich, where he held the room of the lecture theatre and won the doctorate in 1908. The album keeps that journey as it was kept for the ledger: not as geography but as fares, one card to a leg, the price of each leg in the hand of the album’s first keeper, a clerk of the Sialkot station book who owed the young barrister two annas and a favour, and who paid both in the same currency, which was cards.

The album’s notes are bound into it in sixteen short chapters, and it is Chapter 12, “Of the Sole Fare”, that the bidder must understand. Chapter 12 prescribes the rule by which the whole long line, Sialkot to Munich and back, was to be carried on a single payment: one fare, set at the first platform, before the lamp is lit, and no second asking at any crossing, any harbour, and no station’s window. The notes’ keeper at the Rooms, M. S. Qureshi, who has kept the album’s notes since 1947, holds the rule to be the lot’s heart, for the journey it prices is not the rail line but the life: the one payment of a man, set at Sialkot in the autumn of 1905, that carried him to Cambridge, to Munich, to the professor’s chair at McAlister Hall in Lahore, to the lectures that would print as The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam, and back, on the same line, in the spring of 1938. The album runs to two hundred and fourteen cards, and it is the last that carries the lot’s money: Card No. 214 — whose sole fare is, the notes’ keeper says, exactly as that chapter prescribes. The card is the smallest in the album and the most written over; in the margin, in a second and later hand, are the two words the keeper calls the whole point of the lot — one fare — under the gold rule, and under that, a date the catalogue enters without comment: 21 April 1938, the date the city now keeps as his, the day he died in Lahore and the long line closed.

The three letters travel with the album by the same rule. The letter of Lahore 1911 is short and unsigned in the usual way; it speaks of the year of the complaint and of a reply already on the paper, and of the Ravi below the window at the hour the lamp goes on. The letter of Simla 1928 is from the summer of the lectures and is addressed to the album’s first keeper, still at the station book, and it is the only letter in the lot in which the name Allamah appears, and appears once. The letter of Sialkot 1938 is addressed to the Scotch Mission College and is not about the journey at all; it is about a boy at the college who reads aloud, and it asks that the reading hour be kept where it is, at the window, and not moved to the lecture room. The Rooms have not seen a lot in thirty-one sales that so plainly pays for itself in the keeping.

The cards 1 to 208 of the album stand in their order in the green morocco and are shown with the lot at the sale. The keeper’s note on their keeping is entered at the foot of the flyleaf, under the gold rule of Chapter 12, and the Rooms print it here as it stands: One line, one fare, no exceptions at the platform rail.

Provenance and condition

The album and notebook were in the keeping of the album’s first keeper until his death in 1947, when they passed, by the family’s written wish, to the assay room at 11 Canal Road, where M. S. Qureshi, the notes’ keeper, has held them since, re-binding the morocco once, in 1979, and touching the gold rule never. The letters came to the room with the album and have not been separated from it since. Condition: the morocco sound; the cards 1 to 208 fair to very good with the tide of the sea crossing on the cards of the second run; the last run, no. 209 to no. 214, very good, Card No. 214 the most written over and the most kept; the Munich notebook in its original black boards, the ink of 1908 strong; the three letters in their envelopes, the seals of the first and third intact. The lot will be sold as one, and the Rooms will not divide the album from the letters, for the chapter in the album prices the letters the way it prices the journey: at the figure already paid.

Kept in the same house of records

The rule of Chapter 12 — one line, one fare, no exceptions at the platform rail — is not this room’s alone to keep. It is the same discipline as the gold line of the Cedar Bend Road stage file, Field Manual FM 7-48, Chapter 48, where the painted boundary of the main stage is kept word for word in the keeper’s margin, recorded, left in place, and not annotated; the two keepers have compared notes, or so the exchange shelf says, and the shelf keeps the two cards together under lines.

And the album was, for one year of its keeping, a lost item: reported missing from a supper table in the winter of 1947, it was found, still numbered, in the assay room’s ledger drawer, and the notice of it sits in the Kell register of lost and found beside the planned capital, Notice No. 46, one dish shaped like a lake — the register that files its items the way this album files its legs, one entry to a place, nothing owing at the crossing. Bidders who would see the album’s year as a lost item may ask the counter for the notice, which is kept on the same shelf as the catalogue of this sale.
