FIELD NOTES VOL. IV · RENTAL CIRCUIT No. 09

Northern Ohio Zoological & Mechanical Survey — Field Station 4, rental circuit

Entry 09 of 22

Circus Baby's Entertainment and Rental

Specimen class: funtime animatronic · Operator: Afton Robotics, LLC · Venue: sister location, Circus Drive, undistricted county

Date
28–30 October 2016, nights — shifts one through five
Station
Service corridors, Ballora gallery, scooping room, ventilation access
Conditions
Interior lighting, sealed doors, no external air; technician log active
Observer
M. R., single, notebook No. 11, voice recorder (recovered)

The rental circuit opens after dark. Circus Baby’s Entertainment and Rental is a sister location of the larger chain, built beneath a shopping plaza in October of 2016, and it does not admit guests. It employs one technician per night shift, whose duties are maintenance: hand units to the control panel, check the audio, reload the prize counters, and never open the doors between rooms unless the task list demands it. I watched him through the glass for six nights, standing just inside the staff entrance with a notebook and a flashlight, and I recorded what he did not report.

The primary specimen is Circus Baby herself — height one hundred and eighty-three centimetres, mass estimated at one hundred and twenty kilograms, painted red and white, clutching an ice-cream scoop in a mittened hand. Her faceplate splits into four petals; inside is a secondary row of teeth, and a claw mechanism rated to hold a child or to eject an endoskeleton down a disposal chute. I saw her once at 02:17 on the third night, rotating in the parts-and-service room, the scoop extended, the iris of her eye whirring closed and open at three beats per second. She speaks in stage directions: “Could you repeat that?” and “Ice cream makes any situation better.” Neither is a comfort.

Ballora keeps the gallery above the stage. Pale pink leotard, hair in a bun, eyes shut tight. She does not see; she listens. Her music box plays at walking pace, and she moves only when the technician makes a sound — a footfall, a radio click, a breath held too long. I counted seventeen passes on night four, each one a glide across the mirrored floor with no footfall of her own, and on the eighteenth she paused at the glass and tilted her head exactly fifteen degrees, holding the pose until the lighting cycled. That was long enough.

Funtime Freddy occupies the party room with the hand puppet on his left hand — a smaller face, louder voice, faster temper. He sings on a loop, something about a five-night stay and a party that never ends, and when the loop breaks he laughs. Funtime Foxy is curtaining the west wing, deactivated at the waist but still turning the head, one eye lit, scanning the corridor. Minireenas, twelve of them, three inches tall, live in the ceiling tiles; they collect screws and drop them into the technician’s coffee. I found eleven in one mug.

The enclosure breathes by recirculation only. There are no windows, no fire exit marked for the public, and no air supply from outside the plaza. On the fifth night the ventilation fan in the east corridor seized, the temperature climbed to thirty-two degrees, and I wrote this line in the notebook word for word, because it arrived complete and uninvited: “An imperfect fermentation — slow, sealed, without air, never finished the regular season at a courthouse square much the pit exit.” It describes the room. It describes the technician. It describes the courthouse square outside the plaza, where no trial was ever held for the missing, and the pit exit at the end of the service tunnel, where the chute opens and nothing climbs back up.

The technician’s name appears on the payroll as Michael Afton, which is the surname of the firm’s founder, in a series teased in April 2016 under the tagline “there was never just one” — a warning I now treat as taxonomic fact. The fifth main installment, installed at a sister location, with mobility between rooms where earlier specimens stayed in one hall. On the final night he entered the scooping room and did not come out the same door. Ennard wore his face for the walk to the exit, one animated puppet stitched from spare parts, six animatronics in one skin, and the trapdoor above the stage closed behind them with the sound of a lid on a jar.

I left the notebook on the bench in the service corridor, page open at the scoop diagram, and walked back past the pizza sign, which still lit, and past the courthouse square, which did not. The regular season ended without a finish. The pit exit is still unmarked on the plaza map.
