Glass Spider Cakes

In two sheets — a long preamble from the Sydney end of 1987, and the recipe Nana actually used

✦  ~  ✦

The Preamble

Nana baked these the winter of 1988, when the VHS arrived in the post in a rental box with a blue sticker that said RENEW — 24 HOURS FREE. The tape was Glass Spider, the concert film of David Bowie, and she had queued for it outside the video shop on Crown Street the morning after the paper ran the review. The film is cut from eight shows at the Sydney Entertainment Centre — the first two weeks of November 1987 — the last month of the Glass Spider Tour, an 86-show run that had come through Europe, North America and New Zealand in support of the album Never Let Me Down (1987). It was choreographed by Toni Basil, directed by David Mallet, produced by Anthony Eaton, and Bowie plays it alongside his band — Peter Frampton on guitar among the regulars — and a troupe of dancers who move on the stage like weather moving over water.

Our copy of the tape was the Video Collection International pressing; Nana's brother-in-law in Ohio sent us photographs of his, the MPI Home Video release, and the two sleeves differ only in the shade of red, the way the two Sydney nights differ in the film — one warmer, one colder, the dancers' silver catching the lights differently at 9:04 and 11:31. That is the whole of the family's technical argument about the matter, and it has never been resolved, because Nana's position was that both sleeves were red enough, and that the cake was the point.

She made the cakes as a map of the tour. Eight large ones, one per filmed show, and small ones to fill the rest of the tin — she counted the tin, she did not count the shows. The tin held forty-one, and she set out on the card that the forty-first was for the person who asked where the spiders were, which on the night in question was her nephew, who had to eat it with the icing still on the spoon.

In the margin of the card, in a different pen, is one line that was never amended and that we keep as it stands, word for word, from a strip of the Sydney weather paper she had torn for the recipe box — the ring-tailed thing on the forecast, the whole column of it, as it read:

“The ring-tailed on the weather papers of the weather.”

Ask what it means and the family gives the standard answer, which is that Nana was describing the forecast the way a baker describes a rise: you do not measure the weather, you notice it. The line sits on the card above the dry ingredients, and the cakes have never once failed while it has been there.

The same autumn the household was keeping other things about that year's records — the dock-room FAQ on Surfer Rosa, March 1988, the tin of peppermints that stayed closed — and a small ad from Prague offered a brass washer for the tin these went in, via the Bohemia Bazaar classified column. The washer never arrived; the tin stood alone, which Nana considered the correct arrangement.

Enter a show number from the tour of 86 — the reckoning below splits the run into its four legs, and marks the eight Sydney shows the film was cut from, the last eight of the tour, 79 to 86, the first two weeks of November 1987 at the Sydney Entertainment Centre. The Recipe — in two sheets

For forty-one cakes, one tin, one ring-tailed forecast. Nana's quantities, as written: no more, no less, and the last one for the questioner.

  • 225 g butter, soft — the warmth of the Crown Street queue
  • 225 g caster sugar, or the video-shop coin, whichever was on hand
  • 2 eggs, plus one spare for the wet days Nana did not measure
  • 350 g self-raising flour, sifted through the kitchen sieve, not the mill's
  • 1 ½ tsp baking powder, for the rise the weather gave
  • 100 ml buttermilk — New Zealand, in Nana's account, though this is disputed
  • 1 tbsp golden syrup, for the glass
  • Icing, white, thinned to a line fine enough to draw a spider in eight legs
  • 8 dark chocolates, halved, for the eight filmed shows
  1. Beat the butter and sugar until the tin would hold forty-one if you set it down — that is the family's only measurement, and it is exact.
  2. Beat in the eggs one at a time; on a wet day add the spare, and let the forecast say so, word for word.
  3. Fold in the flour and baking powder in three; do not stir past the third.
  4. Spoon into forty-one cups, eight of them slightly higher — the Sydney eight, the filmed shows, November 1987.
  5. Bake at 170°C for 20 minutes, until the tin's rim is the shade of the VHS sleeve, red enough.
  6. Glaze while warm; draw each of the eight large ones into an eight-legged figure with the piping bag.
  7. Set the halved chocolates on the eight large ones, one per filmed show, and leave the rest of the tin uncounted.
  8. The forty-first cake goes to whoever asks where the spiders are. The icing stays on the spoon; that is not a rule, that is a fact.

The card's keeping: the household's other long lists are at the front page, the dock-room FAQ at Surfer Rosa, Questions from the Docks, Collected, and the classifieds column that wanted a brass washer for the tin at Bohemia Bazaar.
