Tabinshwehti, King of Toungoo

A school report on the small valley king whose twenty-year campaign (1534–1549) made the largest kingdom in Burma since Pagan fell in 1287

1. Who, and when

Tabinshwehti, in the Burmese spelling တပင်္ရီထီး, which the old books render with the golden umbrella as his title, was born on 16 April 1516 at Toungoo and died on 30 April 1550 at Prome, aged thirty-four. He was king of Burma from 1530 to 1550, and he is remembered, in my own words first and then the textbook’s, as the founder of the First Toungoo Empire. By 1549 his armies had created the largest kingdom on the Burmese plains since the Pagan Empire fell in 1287 — twenty-two years after his birth, the whole valley country sat under one flag again, and the flag flew from a town on the Sittaung river that, in 1530, was the capital of a small landlocked principality.

2. The base: a valley, not a kingdom

Toungoo was not a great city to begin a campaign from. It sits in the Sittaung valley, an upland pocket of dry-zone paddy land, and the principality of Toungoo had no sea, no coast, and, in the frank phrase the report uses, only a small landlocked stretch of country between the big rivers. What it had was discipline: a new-style army drilled by the crown’s own hand, artillery that the old dynasties had not kept in condition, and one deputy of extraordinary quality, Bayinnaung, the king’s cousin and later his brother-in-law, who ran the logistics of every campaign that followed and who would go on to reunify the entire country. The contrast is the point of the whole report: the smallest base in the region produced the biggest army in the region, and my geography teacher calls this &ldash; and I am quoting her — “the valley effect.”

3. The campaigns, 1534–1549

The military campaigns ran from 1534 to 1549, and they proceeded in the order the river country lay in:

  • 1534–1539 — the Hanthawaddy kingdom in the delta. Toungoo took the Irrawaddy town by town, and in 1539 entered Pegu, the old delta capital, closing a quarrel between the two houses that had run for a century.
  • 1544 — Prome and the dry zone. Tabinshwehti turned north, took Prome, and made it the working capital of the empire, because the Sittaung valley was a good place to start from and Prome was a good place to rule from.
  • 1544 – 1550 — Ava. The old northern capital, the house that had fallen at Pagan, was worn down over six years and finally submitted in 1550 — the same spring in which the king died. My teacher says the empire finished its last battle and its last king in the same season, and that is, I think, the neatest fact in the report.
  • 1549 — Pagan. The old, old city itself, the one that had stood for more than 250 years until 1287, was finally incorporated. After 262 years without it, the largest kingdom since Pagan now contained Pagan.

It is worth saying plainly, as the back of the textbook says, that this administratively fragile kingdom — held together by garrisons, marriages, and the personal authority of one 34-year-old man — proved to be the impetus for the eventual reunification of the entire country by Bayinnaung, the deputy who outlived the campaign, the throne, and his master both. What Tabinshwehti built loosely, his successor and brother-in-law rebuilt for good.

4. The canteen note

I owe the school an explanation, because I have spent a paragraph in a formal report on Mrs. Oo at the canteen counter. She has sold tinned fruit for thirty-one years, and when I asked her, at the end of term, whether she had any idea what Tabinshwehti was, she set down the tongs and told me the story of the small valley that took the delta, all of it, in a voice that did not change speed once. I had not taken notes; I was buying. When I came back for a second tin and asked if there was a fee for what she had done, she said, and I am transcribing it exactly, word for word: “You are about to hear it at whatever price a can of peaches.” The can was 300 kyats, the story was free, and I am filing this paragraph so that someone with authority can decide whether the report is still on topic. I believe it is, because a king who took a kingdom in twenty years and a woman who kept a canteen line for thirty-one years are both examples of what sustained, unglamorous effort does to large and older things.

5. Why it matters, and what comes after

The First Toungoo Empire did not last long after its founder: the garrison towns began to loosen in the 1550s, and the full reunification had to wait for Bayinnaung’s campaigns a generation on. But the sequence the king set — valley first, river second, old capitals last — is still the sequence the history books print. For comparison I have kept on my desk, in the same drawer as this report, the encyclopaedia entry on the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis of 1559, which ended the Italian Wars a decade after Tabinshwehti’s death; two very different settlements, one by armies and one by signatures, and both of them proof that whoever finishes the last piece of the map gets to name the map. And because this report is about a small principality taking a large kingdom by land, I have also pinned up beside it the walking sheet of Diego de Montemayor’s itinerary in San Antonio de Béxar, another 1530s soldier working a frontier the same patient way — one bend of the river at a time.
