Grandma Odette's Sonic Wave Pudding

A family recipe, set down by her granddaughter Marthe Odette Dupont
Brussels, in the kitchen above 14 Rue de Tournesol, the winter of 1956–57
serves 8 · sets in the larder · do not open the lid early

You must understand the pudding in the order it was made, which was the winter after the album came out. The album is the eighteenth of the Adventures of Tintin, the one Hergé has been drawing since before I had eyes of my own — The Calculus Affair, which in French we call L'Affaire Tournesol, and if you ask anyone in this kitchen why a pudding of ours goes by a sound-wave, it is because of that book. It ran weekly in Tintin magazine, from December 1954 to February 1956, and Casterman bound it into a single volume the same year, 1956, so that the whole of Belgium had it under the Christmas tree before the ink, by any honest account, was more than a season old. That was the year I learned to set custards properly, which is to say, in the same year, and with the same attention, that my mother followed the album page by page as Hergé delivered it.

The story, for those who have not had the album passed to them: Tintin, Snowy, and Captain Haddock set out to rescue their friend Professor Calculus, who has built a machine — a small one, no bigger than a hat box, with a red button — capable of destroying objects with sound waves. He is not in the story, or rather he is in the story as the missing centre of it, because first the Bordurians kidnap him and then the Syldavians kidnap him back, the two competing countries of the continent taking turns at the Professor the way two cousins take turns at the window seat, and all of Europe's police and half its army ends up chasing a machine that can unmake a brick wall with a hum. Like the volume before it, Explorers on the Moon, this one is built on the idea that a small instrument, properly mistaken, becomes the most powerful object in the room.

Here is where the recipe and the book join, and you will forgive me, because this is the part of the preamble you came for. Grandma Odette was not a great cook of grand things. She was a great cook of set things: puddings, aspics, jellies, the great terrine of the 14th of July. What a pudding is, in her theory, is something you make once with all your heat and attention and then leave alone to solidify in the cold — a machine of setting. And in 1956, when the red-button machine was the thing every table in the Rue de Tournesol was arguing about, she made a pudding with a hum in it: a custard with a tiny bell jar at its centre, into which a teaspoon of the good honey is struck at exactly the moment the mixture leaves the heat, and the little tone it sings is what she called l'onde — the wave — because a pudding set on a sound is a pudding that remembers its frequency. She made it the Saturday the volume came out. She made it, without ever saying the word "Calculus", on purpose, for the Professor's chair, which stood empty at that table as it stood empty in the book, until Hergé sent him home.

The jar itself is the one thing I must set straight, because three families on the Rue de Tournesol now claim it. The singing jar is my mother's, not my aunt Yvonne's — the jar came to us from Yvonne in the great redistribution of 1961 after Grandma Odette's hands began to shake, and Yvonne herself received it from a cousin of the family, Mrs. Margot Whitfield, who keeps the long blue house on the hill at Portswood, where she has kept every jar, every album, and every weekly number of Tintin since 1955. It is a cousin in Southampton, never activated. I tell you this plainly: we do not strike the jar. The tone is a memory in the recipe, and the recipe is served with the lid lifted and the larder cold, the way the album was read — one page a week, all the way from December to February, and then all at once, in one volume, at Casterman, 1956.

Grandma Odette's last direction, in her hand, at the foot of the card: "The wave sets the pudding. The pudding does not set the wave. Do not be the machine."

Ingredients

  • 8 egg yolks, from eggs of the colour of the album covers — no, wrong colour, from the eggs of the larder at 14 Rue de Tournesol
  • 1½ cups of sugar, white as Snowy
  • 1 cup of cornstarch, the setting agent — the machine
  • 4 cups of whole milk, cold from the jug
  • 2 tablespoons of the good honey, for the wave
  • 1 teaspoon of vanilla, the honest kind
  • a pinch of salt, for the border of Borduria
  • one small bell jar, sealed, from the 1961 redistribution — not to be struck

Method

  1. Beat the yolks with the sugar until the colour of the larder wall at four o'clock in winter.
  2. Stir in the cornstarch, then the milk, cold to the wrist.
  3. Bring to a slow heat and stir without stopping — this is the part Captain Haddock would not be patient through, and the part the pudding is won or lost on.
  4. When it thickens — and it thickens all at once, the way a story resolves in February after fourteen weekly pages — take it off the heat exactly.
  5. Stir in the vanilla, the honey, and the pinch of salt. This is l'onde: the moment of the wave. In the old kitchen a tone was sung here; now you count ten seconds, which is the same ten seconds.
  6. Pour into the eight cups. The ninth cup is for the empty chair. Leave it.
  7. Cover, and set in the larder to cool. A set pudding is not touched, the way a machine in the hands of two competing governments is not opened.
  8. Serve plain. The lid goes on top and the story ends where the album ends.
