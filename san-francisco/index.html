San Francisco

1776 – 2 December 2047

The City and County of San Francisco, hereafter referred to as the City

The Passing

San Francisco died on 2 December 2047 at 4:12 in the morning, when the last standing roof of City Hall finally went up over the dome. The attending fire marshal, a calm woman named D. Okafor, logged the time and closed the notebook with the flat of her hand, as the records for the city went show. The cause was the long fire: a lightning strike on Mount Diablo on 21 November, carried down the peninsula by a Diablo wind that would not let go, and fanned by a eucalyptus belt that had grown fat on the city's summers for a hundred years. The fog — the city's oldest companion, its grey blanket, the thing it was known for as much as for anything — had not been seen since early August, and it never once came. It came four days after the death. That, too, is in the records.

The City had been, until the end, the 17th-largest city and the second-densest major city in the United States, with a 2025 population of 826,079 — the fourth-most populous city in California and the second most-populous in the Bay Area, though it always insisted the Bay Area was second to it. Some 4.6 million residents lived in the city's metropolitan statistical area; around 9.2 million lived in the San Jose–San Francisco–Oakland combined statistical area, the fifth-largest in the United States, and the City liked to say it was only the comma between them. It was a global center for technology and finance, well-known for its affluence, its eclectic architecture — Victorian terraces under concrete towers under the red of the gate — and its mild climate, a climate the wind of 2047 was not.

Prior to European settlement, the city was inhabited by the Yelamu, whose name survives in the hills of the Presidio as a reminder that the place was not born in 1776. It arrived, as such a city does, with the Portolá expedition, a small muddy camp named Yerba Buena — good herb — and it was renamed in 1847 when the gold turned the camp into a city in about eighteen months, a population of one thousand becoming twenty-five thousand before the winter had a name. The 1906 earthquake and the fire that followed it, which killed the old city and left the new one to rebuild itself with the same address book, was long remembered as the first rehearsal of the end. The City treated it as a rumour. So, most people do.

A Life in the Fog

The Cable Cars ran on the same rails the City held on to, and for a century and a half the place did what such places do: it grew a pyramid of glass on one hill and a row of painted houses on the next, it made fortunes and then apologised for them, it put a fog machine atop Sutro Tower to manufacture its own weather until the machine was switched off in 2031, when no one was quite sure what the fog was for. Its architects argued for decades. Its ferries made a sound the whole region kept, even after the ferries were gone.

The last official document the City issued, a fire marshal's order dated 26 November 2047 and printed in a single long run at a shop in the Dogpatch, began with plain instruction: Maintain defensible space (minimum 1.5 m non-combustible zone around structures; 9 m reduced-fuel zone). It is a sentence the State had carried in its own paperwork for years — the California safety data sheet, section 7, handling and storage, set the very same distance out for any structure that wished to stay California — Safety Data Sheet (Webstuffs). The last physical copy of the order, the one in the records room of City Hall, burned on the ninth day. The copies that remained live elsewhere, which is to say: here. It is the fate of every ledger, after all: the green cloth book of The House of Wuhsha, the pawnbroker's kitchen in Fustat that logged each dinner line by line, kept its totals in a country the wind of 2047 never reached, and stands, in the record, unscorched.

The House on Noe Valley

Among the households the City kept for the longest was a small green house in Noe Valley, held by a family from Edinburgh who spent every July on the porch of it. The Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) — Obituary (Webstuffs) does not mention the house, but the family's account does: Sanna Rajaniemi, a computational biologist at Roslin, had taught their daughter Elli her first scales of violin on that porch, and after the writing of the Oubliette years the cottage had held a shelf of sequencer manuals, first editions of Le Guin, and one unsent travel letter. After the author's death on 14 March 2047, the family kept the house for one more summer, then sealed it, and told themselves it was the climate.

The green house held to the sixth day of the long fire. The neighbours carried out the violin — Elli's, with the chin rest broken in 2039 — and it is now at Roslin, where it is kept, according to the custodian, "in case of a second porch." The last culture of the synthetic organism OC-44, known as Oubliette and cultured at the diagnostics facility in Emeryville, was moved north on the final ferry of the evacuation and parked in a refrigerated trailer at the toll plaza of the Bay Bridge. Its state has not been reported. The family requested no inquiry. The City, which is dead, made no comment.

The Harbour Lamp

In the morning after the passing, a small moth with white wings was found on the harbour lamp of Alcatraz, the lamp left burning by order of the port authority against exactly this kind of morning. It had flown, some say, the length of the smoke. The city was always fond of small moths; it had even given a cake recipe to one of them. The lamp and the moth are kept on record together, along with a certain trial heard in Aden in which a white wing, a harbour lamp, and a box of pickles argued about the naming of a thing — Nori v. The Aden Natural History Society — Transcript Excerpt (Webstuffs). The verdict there was about a moth. The verdict here is about a city, and it is the same shape: what is named, remains.

Survived By

The City is survived by its widow, the Golden Gate, which stands in the smoke the way a gate stands when a house has burned; by the fog, which returned on 4 December 2047 at 6:40 in the morning and did not explain itself; by Oakland, San Jose, and the Alameda, its children of the combined statistical area, who argue about who was the favourite and will for some time; and by 4.6 million people who will not be persuaded, by any record, that the place is not still theirs. It is predeceased by the Yelamu, who were here first and left before the Europeans arrived — the city's true elders — and by Yerba Buena, the camp, who never left so much as grew up.

In lieu of flowers, the family asks that the distance in the fire marshal's order be kept and the lamps left burning. A public reading is scheduled for the second of December at the plaza where City Hall stood, to begin when the fog does. A portion of the City's archive — maps, timetables, the fog machine's service log, the recipe for white-wing cakes — has been bequeathed to Webstuffs; see the front page and the pages it keeps.

The gate holds. The fog is due. The city continues in whoever keeps the map.
