The Platform Bench · Long Gallery · Sitting of 12 May, 1988 service year · Transcript Excerpt

In re the Office of Prime Minister

Case No. 42 · The Statute Book (petitioner) v. The Office of Prime Minister of the United Kingdom (by the hand of the stationmaster at Warwick, respondent)

Before: The Recorder (presiding); the Retired Teacher; the Conductor. Clerk of the Bench in attendance, register open.

EXCERPT — Entered verbatim, register of the bench

The bench opened at 09:14, being the hour at which the Symonds Express has always arrived at Warwick, and the register was found open, which the Recorder took as a good omen. The petitioner, The Statute Book, by its counsel the Survey Clerk of the Long Gallery, stood up and said: “My honourable bench, I bring this petition to establish, by the force of pages, that the office of Prime Minister of the United Kingdom is established by no statute, is set down in no constitutional document, has no seat in my register, and must either be struck from the bench’s roll or given a proper number. I have searched my shelves from the Coronation to the most recent reissue. I found it nowhere.”

THE RECORDER. The bench notes the petitioner’s difficulty and observes it with sympathy, because it is also the bench’s own. The respondent, the office itself, cannot speak; offices do not speak, they are only sat in. The stationmaster at Warwick is therefore received as the office’s hand, and is called to give the account of the office in the order the bench likes.

THE STATIONMASTER. Very good, my lord. The office, then. The Prime Minister of the United Kingdom is the head of government of the United Kingdom. The office advises the sovereign on the exercise of much of the royal prerogative, chairs the Cabinet, and selects its ministers. Modern holders of the office hold it by virtue of their ability to command the confidence of the House of Commons, and they are, for that reason, invariably members of Parliament. The office was held in the recent sitting, 2019 to 2022, by Boris Johnson, Leader of the Conservative Party, who stood at the leader’s platform at 10 Downing Street and announced; his wife, the first-class passenger of this line’s No. 41 timetable, the Symonds Express, appears elsewhere in this court’s files and is not examined today.

THE RETIRED TEACHER. And the statute? The petitioner will forgive the interruption, but the whole petition turns on one shelf. Where is the statute?

THE STATIONMASTER. There is no statute, my lord. The office is not established by any statute or constitutional document. It exists only by long-established convention, whereby the monarch appoints as Prime Minister the person most likely to command the confidence of the House of Commons. In practice, that is the leader of the political party holding the largest number of seats in the House of Commons. The office is a thing that happens in a certain order, every time, without being written down, and it has happened so often that nobody can remember writing it down, which the bench will observe is rather the opposite of the petitioner’s trade.

COUNSEL FOR THE PETITIONER. My lord, I put it to the stationmaster that an office with no statute is a ghost, and that this bench, which enters everything verbatim, has no business keeping a ghost on its roll. I ask the bench to strike Case No. 42 out and enter no office found against the petitioner’s name in the register.

THE RECORDER. Before the bench rules on the ghost question, the clerk will exhibit Exhibit C, the Warwick drawer. The stationmaster will account for it, because it has been requested in writing and it has been refused in writing, and this bench does not do a third refusal.

THE STATIONMASTER. Exhibit C, then. In the stationmaster’s drawer at Warwick there is kept a folded slip found under a hat in the overhead rack of the Symonds Express, bearing a single line in pencil — one people, one scripture, one front — and the conductor’s note added later: keep, for now. I offer the note, not the slip. The slip’s sender is unknown; the timetable that found it will not say what “the other side” is; and I do not think any court, including this one, is better placed to decide. The note, I offer as evidence that a line can be kept in a drawer without a sender and still be a line. The bench, in the event, declined the slip — “the bench cannot hear what the timetable will not say” — but received the note, and the note stands.

THE CONDUCTOR. My lord, three small matters remain before the bench may rule, and they have come up in the evidence and cannot be put back in their pockets. The first is the wash. Two washes were produced for the base plate of the Bedford Downs diorama — a grey wash and a red wash, iron oxide, being the honest colour of the country on the far side, 84 kilometres of it west of Warmun and 109 of it north of Halls Creek. The bench is asked which is the house colour of this court’s own files when they travel that far. The second is the shrub. Two shrubs were cut from the paddock fence line where the horses arrive by other means — a broad-leaved one and a small-leaved one — and the bench is asked which is the paddock’s own. The third is the product. The number 9,156 — 84 entered against 109 as one product — was argued at length at Thread 214, The Aden County General, where a survey clerk maintained that multiplication is just addition with an attitude, and the bench is asked which side the product belongs to.

THE RECORDER. The bench has considered the three questions, and the bench rules in a single line, entered in the register word for word: “The latter are red, the small-leaved, and the retired teacher won.” The first clause answers the wash — it is the latter wash, the red, that is the house colour, because the bench will not keep a red-country file in grey ink. The second answers the shrub — the small-leaved is the paddock’s own, and the broad-leaved may remain where it was cut. The third answers the product — the product 9,156 belongs to the retired teacher, against the survey clerk’s attitude, and the bench records, with some satisfaction, that it had a hunch.

THE RECORDER. As to the petition. The bench has listened to the Statute Book, which has searched its own shelves in good faith and found nothing, and the bench rules that the petition fails for want of a statute, which is the only way a petition of this kind can fail. The office of Prime Minister is confirmed on the roll of this bench, established by no statute, by long-established convention alone: the monarch appoints the person most likely to command the confidence of the House of Commons, and in practice that is the leader of the party, and the office advises the sovereign, chairs the Cabinet, selects its ministers, and is invariably sat in by a member of Parliament. The convention is not in the register. The bench directs the clerk to enter the office against the petitioner’s name as present, unregistered, and in possession, which is, the clerk will appreciate, a standing description of the office and not a comment on it.

THE CLERK. Entered, my lord. The register is heavy, but it is holding.

THE RECORDER. The bench will close at 23:59, being the hour at which the Symonds Express departs 10 Downing Street for the sea, and will sit again on 17 March, which the bench has been informed is a date that runs on its own. Nothing further. The red wash is to be kept in the drawer with the conductor’s note. The bench is closed.

So entered in the register of the bench, the Long Gallery, sitting of 12 May, 1988 service year, by the clerk, who kept the line word for word and has no recollection of why. The slip under the hat remains at Warwick, unexamined, kept, for now.

For the office’s own passenger, stop by stop from St John’s Wood to The Paddock, the bench keeps The Symonds Express — Timetable No. 41, with commentary, whose Warwick drawer supplies Exhibit C of this sitting.

For the red wash, the base plate, the 84 and the 109, and the paddock of 1:252, see Bedford Downs Station — Build Log, Sheet by Sheet, whose second wash the bench has adopted as the house colour of its own files.
