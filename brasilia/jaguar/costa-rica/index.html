After the claim of Notice No. 47, the onça in the dish was accepted on the record and the register proceeded to the next number, Dona Iara found this brochure folded inside the tea towel the dish had been wrapped in. It had travelled, the notary’s deed will note, the whole way from a kiosk on the Caribbean coast to the apron of Studio 4, the same way the family keeps everything: steadily, and without saying when. It is filed here as Notice No. 48, one country, claimed back the way the register claims back everything else — by a name, a date, and a lamp left on.

The country, entered as it stands

Costa Rica — officially the Republic of Costa Rica — is a country in Central America. It borders Nicaragua to the north, the Caribbean Sea to the northeast, Panama to the southeast, and the Pacific Ocean to the southwest, sharing a maritime border with Ecuador to the south of Cocos Island. It has a population of around five million people in a land area of nearly 51,180 km² (19,760 sq mi); the capital and largest city is San José, home to around 350,000 residents and two million people in the surrounding metropolitan area.

Humans have been in Costa Rica since between 7,000 and 10,000 BC. Various indigenous peoples lived in the territory before it was colonized by Spain in the 16th century. The brochure, as printed, adds nothing to this; the register adds a rider: the family that keeps a planned capital in a 30 × 22 cm lake-shaped dish will, on the evidence of this kiosk leaflet, keep a whole country in a folio of the same size.

Suggested circuit · eleven days, one lamp

Day 1–2San José, 1,172 m up on the central plain. The two-million city under the volcano. Dinner at the seventh table of the La Bolsa market; order the gallo pinto and say the word pura vida once, the way a notary reads a clause: once, and as it stands.
Day 3–5Volcán Arenal & Lake Arenal. Here the brochure shows the lake, and the family points: the lake in the dish was drawn from a lake, and this is its elder twin. The crater holds its mouth shut since 2010. Fish the shore at dusk; the water keeps its temperature the way a record keeps its temperature — not hot enough to change, not cold enough to forget.
Day 6–7Tortuguero, the canals of the Caribbean lowlands. Four waterways, no roads, one long boat. Loggerhead turtles, manatees, crocodiles; if the guide taps the prow twice, stop. Count to nine. Begin again.
Day 8–9Monteverde cloud forest. The canopy bridges, the white morpho, the resplendent quetzal in the wrong country by one degree of latitude, and the mushroom morning — see the laminated card in the field notes below.
Day 10Manuel Antonio, Pacific side. Sloths on the boardwalk, a volcano at the horizon, and a beach where the tide keeps its own register and no one is the notary of it.
Day 11Cocos Island, the Pacific outpost that puts Ecuador on the border of this country at sea. Land, rest, and if a large cat prints a line of spots in the mud of the lagoon, do not call it. Call the register. It is the breathing, and it is home here.

FIELD NOTES · MONTVERDE VISITOR KIOSK · LAMINATED CARD NO. 3, KEPT AS FOUND

Edible mushrooms are found about a fourth becomes necessary.

The card has no translator’s note, and the family will not add one. It is read the way Senhora Vaz reads Article 12 of the deed: as it stands, for forty-one years of mornings and counting. The kiosk guide, when asked, gave the only reading the register has ever accepted — that on the wet mornings of the mushroom season, about a fourth of what the forest floor offers up is edible, and that the moment you are deep enough in the trail to need to know which fourth, a machete, a permit, and a notary’s green leather blotter all become necessary at once. The brochure keeps the card with it, uncorrected, the way the dish kept the capital and the kennel keeps the breathing.

The resident of record · Panthera onca

The brochure’s last page is a field identification card, and the family has turned it over and over, because it is the card of the item of Notice No. 47, printed here in its own country. The jaguar is the only living member of the genus Panthera native to the Americas, and the biggest cat species the continent has: body length to 1.85 m (6 ft 1 in), weight to 158 kg (348 lb); coat pale yellow to tan, spots running to rosettes on the sides. The bite that pierces carapaces. The blow between the ears. On this coast it is not a found item. It is the house.

Aunt Marva, line 02, holding the lamp over an ocean: I did not put it in the dish, and I did not put it on the brochure. The brochure came with the towel, and the towel came with the lake, and the lake came with the capital. Dona Iara, controller: The register proceeds. The lamp is on the apron.

All of it is kept in the Webstuffs papers the way the dish is held: not hot enough to change, and not cold enough to forget. On Thursdays the lamp is on. On the Rich Coast, the lamp is always on.
