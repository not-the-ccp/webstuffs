The item is the set of varieties of the English language native to Australia, that is the whole of it, and not a single variety of it held out on its own. It is the country's common language and its de facto national language: Australia has no official language, and has never once filed a claim to have one, which is the sort of paper this office understands. English is the first language of the majority of the population — the census of 2021 has it as the only language spoken in the home for 72 per cent of Australians — and it has held that position, unchallenged, since the onset of British settlement. It is the main language used in compulsory education, and in the federal, state and territorial legislatures and courts, which is to say: the language the schools talk in and the language the arguments are decided in. It began to diverge from British and American English at the first settlements, out in the field, the way the creek diverges from the country it carries, and by now it is a language of its own with the map of the country in it.

Identifying marks

The item should be identified by the following, all of which were still on it at the time of finding and which any claimant can test in front of the office:

  • Accent. Non-rhotic: the final r stays out of the word the way a dog stays out of the shed in the rain. The t in the middle of a word goes soft, so that water comes out almost a word of its own. It sits level with the listener and refuses to rise.
  • Speech. G'day; how ya going; no worries; hard yakka. The question mark on the end of statements is not a defect; it is part of the item.
  • Words of its own. Arvo, brekkie, servo, billy, ute, thongs (footwear, and nothing the office is obliged to elaborate), mojo, barbie, and, in the north and on the shallows where the Indian Ocean keeps it, jellies — the months, roughly November to February, when the jellyfish run out of the ocean and the wet season holds the roads. Everyone in the Kimberley will tell you the jellies are real, and as legal a document as a logbook.
  • Grammar it will not give up. The “I’m after …” of the old north, the you lot for a whole group of people, and the singular she'll and he's both allowed to do the work, depending on who is being polite.

If the item a person is holding has all four marks and still fits in their mouth, it is very likely the item of this notice, and the claim test below should be attempted before anyone signs the book.

How the item was found

The sample crate this office keeps a record of — the one shipped east to the casting platform with the instruction note pinned to its card, the note that runs “Jellies are the times, when the truck should look as though it had been given up on and then forgiven” — came back after the jellies, in the same box it went out in, with the single-axle truck's flat tyre inside it and the note still where it was. Mr. Tovey, who keeps the bench and the balsa, and his companions around the yard, had spent the wet season talking past the crate the way people talk past a thing they are not yet ready to own: about the truck, about the mud, about the colour of the country on the third week of the rains. The repeated specific allusions of his companions had mounted to the crate's card. By the time the road was dry, the card was no longer an instruction note; it had become a list, all the way down the margin, of every specific thing the yard had said true this season, in the language it said them in. And in the bottom of the crate, under the red dirt and the tyre, the language itself was folded into quarters, damp at one corner, complete, and holding its own.

Ms Bedford opened it once, for the record, and it said arvo, which she has entered as the office's reading of it and which the whole of Year 8B agreed to in the muddy bus on the way home. The item is now held on the shelf between this notice and the maps, and it has not once been heard to speak, which is exactly how a language behaves when it is waiting to be claimed.

Claiming the item: the office test

Any person who believes the item to be theirs may attempt the following claim test at the office, in person or on this page. Six marks are available; five carry the claim, four carry a conversation, fewer than four carry a cuppa and a reference to the Western Australia geography report from Halls Creek State School, where the jellies, the truck and the crate's card are written up properly. If this page's test will not run on the machine you are using, the claim may be made in person at the office, Term 2 or later, with your ear as evidence.

  1. 1. A claimant says “see ya this arvo”. What are they losing, or expecting to lose?

  2. 2. Where's the nearest servo? The item, if it is yours, answers this as a question about —

  3. 3. In the item of this notice, thongs are —

  4. 4. Australia, in the record of this item, has an official language. True or false?

  5. 5. In the census of 2021, English was the only language spoken in the home for what share of Australians?

  6. 6. The item of this notice first began to diverge from British and American English —

Score of five or six: the claim is carried, the book is produced, and the item goes out with the claimant, who is asked not to leave it in the rain. Score of four: the office will hold a conversation before the book is produced. Below four: the claimant is referred, with the office's respect, to the geography report and to the obituary of Charles Eaton, OBE, AFC (1895–1979), a man who came out in 1923 with his English in his pocket and spent sixty years of it here; the two are a good place to start.

If the item is not claimed

If, at the end of the term, the item is not claimed by any single person, any single family, or any single state, the office will note the outcome as it has always noted such outcomes: reverted to the whole country. Which is what it is. It is not this school's, it is not the Kimberley's, and it is not the shed's, though the shed has done a great deal for it, the way the shed does for the single-axle truck — it is the language the whole country talks in when it is being itself, and no notice in this office can hold it for long. The item will be kept on the shelf until then, in the quarters it was found in, and anyone who is holding some of it right now, in their mouth, at this moment, may keep it as they hold it: the item is, and always has been, in the hands of whoever is using it. Return, in the meantime, to the Webstuffs front page for the rest of the miscellany, and read the Bedford Downs Station build log for the truck, the homestead, and the mustering line, since the item of this notice is the very language that log was written in.
