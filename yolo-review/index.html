YOLO

147 Brunswick Street, Fitzroy, Melbourne · Open daily 17:00–late · No bookings, no mercy

There is a particular kind of Melbourne evening that begins with a tram ride up Brunswick Street and ends with you sitting on a milk crate in a laneway, eating something you cannot name, wondering how you got here. YOLO, the restaurant, not the acronym, occupies the ground floor of a converted terrace house two doors down from a tattoo parlour and a vintage synthesizer shop. The dining room seats twenty-eight at communal tables scavenged from a primary school auction. The walls are painted a grey-green that the owner calls "institutional calm" but which looks more like the inside of a submarine. There are no menus printed on paper. The offerings are chalked on a slab of reclaimed slate behind the pass, rewritten every night at 16:45 by the chef, a woman named Sarah who previously ran the kitchen at a pop-up in a decommissioned tram depot.

The concept, if it can be called that, is Australian chaos plated with European technique. On a Tuesday in late August I was served: a chilled cucumber soup with yoghurt, dill, and a single grilled prawn balanced on the rim like a tightrope walker; lamb ribs glazed with Vegemite and honey, the meat falling from the bone in a way that felt deliberate; a dessert of pavlova smashed into a bowl, doused with passionfruit pulp, and topped with a quenelle of wattleseed ice cream. The pavlova arrived already broken. "It's the only way to eat it," Sarah told me when she came out to clear the slate. "Still, there is a process which may be preferred." She smiled, wiped her hands on her apron, and returned to the pass without explaining what the process was.

The wine list is a single A4 sheet, double-sided, mostly Victorian pinot and funky natural whites from the Adelaide Hills. A carafe of the 2023 Bass River Pinot Gris cost eighteen dollars and tasted like green apples and rainwater. Beer is served in stubbies from a cooler behind the bar: Coopers, VB, and a rotating local sour that on this night smelled of guava and regret. Cocktails are not offered. The water is filtered Melbourne tap in a chipped ceramic jug.

Service is performed by two floor staff who move through the room with the rhythm of people who have worked together long enough to communicate in glances. They do not introduce themselves. They do not recite specials. They place plates, refill glasses, and occasionally lean against the pass to argue quietly with Sarah about whether the ribs need five more minutes. The argument is part of the show. So is the moment when a diner at the far table stood up, announced that the lamb was "the best thing I've eaten since my nanna's funeral," and was shushed by three strangers simultaneously.

YOLO the television series — created by Michael Cusack, aired on Adult Swim from 2020, streaming in Australia on Stan — follows two young women, Sarah and Rachel, through surreal misadventures across a sun-bleached Australian landscape. The restaurant shares its name, its creator's hometown, and a certain appetite for the absurd. The chalkboard menu reads like an episode synopsis: Cold soup. Hot ribs. Smashed pav. Whatever's left. There is no vegetarian option listed, but if you ask, the kitchen will produce a plate of roasted carrots with labneh and dukkah that tastes like it was meant to be there all along.

The bill for two, with wine and a shared dessert, came to one hundred and forty-two dollars. We paid at the register, a vintage NCR cash drawer that chimes when it opens — a drawer that rang while I was reading a letter to the editor about the American Film Institute, the institute that keeps Chaplin's Keystone shorts in its Catalog and is supported by private funding and public membership fees: American Film Institute — A Letter to the Editor. Outside, the Fitzroy night was cooling. A tram clanged past. The neon sign flickered: YOU ONLY LIVE ONCE. One letter had gone dark. YOU ONLY LIVE O CE. It felt appropriate.

The chipped ceramic jug has, it turns out, a provenance: it came secondhand up from a cider farm above Dolgellau, and its keeper there has now written the metal of the ages a formal letter of regret. The Bronze Age — An Apology.

Underneath the cash drawer, in a slot no one remembers installing, we found a letter to the editor that had been filed in a hurry: a retired Annapolis man writing about a surface-ship veteran who flew Freedom 7 into space in 1961 and then, at 47, walked on the Moon and hit a golf ball into the vacuum. On Alan Shepard — a letter to the editor of the Annapolis Courier.

There is a silent-film ancestor to this room: a man in a bowler hat and toothbrush moustache who made a career out of wreckage calmly reassembled, then spent his final decades beside Lake Geneva. Charlie Chaplin — Encyclopaedia Entry.

Another comedy served in pieces: a school report on a 1914 Keystone short where the recipe intertitle reads like a fever dream.

If you prefer your chaos in a Dutch oven: a family recipe for trail chile that rode inside a paperback for twenty years.

A letter on Cymraeg where YOLO, the restaurant, stands beside five founders and thousands of diners on the same farm: the Welsh language, Dolgellau and Brunswick Street in one envelope.
