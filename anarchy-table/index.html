Grimsby & Wren Table Games · Fieldhouse Lane, Oldbury · Catalogue No. 7

The Anarchy Table

A game of free association & reciprocity for two to six players · One landscape, two offices to abolish, and a scale of eleven grains

Designed by M. A. Aston (Oldbury, 1946–2013) from his own field notes · Play-test crews: the dig house, 1994–2011, and the Naga Listeners’ Club

§1 What the game is about

The subject of this game is anarchism, a political philosophy and movement that seeks to abolish all institutions that perpetuate authority, coercion, or hierarchy — and its two great targets on the table are the State and Capitalism. Anarchism advocates replacing those hierarchical social structures with stateless societies: horizontal networks of voluntary associations that run on two printed rules only, free association and reciprocity. The game takes its side in the old argument honestly: anarchism is described as part of the libertarian wing of the socialist movement, broadly known as libertarian socialism, and the box is printed with that motto along the spine. Although traces of anarchist ideas are found all throughout history, the campaign map of this game begins where modern anarchism emerged, in the Enlightenment, and the first card in the deck is dated 1793.

§2 In the box

  • One landscape board, The Plain — 90 cm, three passes, one inverted-hull hill, printed from the same map case as our field sheets.
  • 240 association counters in three woods (rowan, oak, yew), 24 to a player, no colours, no ranks.
  • The grain scale: eleven counters, each containing, altogether, one dense body of eleven grains, and one spare plate.
  • Seventy-two day-markers, for Campaign 1871, printed 1–72 with the 52nd marked in oxblood.
  • Three office plaques: The State, Capitalism, and The Committee, self-declaring (the third is a trap and the manual says so, which is unusual for a manual and a point of pride).
  • One field trowel, steel, 90 mm, used as the die: throw it flat, the handle points, the handle length is your number, and no player may ever hold the handle while throwing, because that is authority, and the table will know.
  • Tweed, one square per player. Tea, strong, in quantity. The tweed is not decoration; a player without tweed forfeits the first cup, and the first cup decides the first move.

§3 Setting up the plain

Deal each player one rowan, one oak, and one yew counter, and let the rest sleep in the box: an association that has not formed has not yet been counted. Place the two office plaques — State and Capitalism — at either end of the plain, on the far side of the second pass, so that every route between players must either go under them or abolish them. Lay the grain scale at the table's centre and set it at zero. The board's inverted-hull hill is neutral ground: no counter may sit on it, the way the supplier's digger files no trench in a finished field. If six play, sit them so that no one sits at a head; the plain has no head, and neither may the table. This is the only seating rule, and it is the load-bearing one.

§4 A round, and the law of reciprocity

A round runs in three beats. First, move: throw the trowel and walk your top counter the handle-length, along trackways only — the board's thin lines — never across the open field, because crossing is how a general moves. Second, give: reciprocity is the engine of the whole game. Any player who receives a counter, a grain, or a cup of tea within a round must, before the round ends, send one of equal weight back the way it came, and may send it to any player on the circuit, not only the giver — the network closes, and a closed network is the plaything. Third, form: any two or more adjacent counters belonging to different players may join into a voluntary association, but the association lasts only so long as each member keeps giving, and any member may walk away at the end of any round without penalty, which is free association printed as a rule rather than an apology. Standing orders that move counters against their owner, that collect a toll on the trackways, or that name a head of the table are the moves of the two offices, and every such order on the board is a strike point: hit it with a counter that is being given, and it is abolished, and its plaque turns over.

§5 The grain scale, read word for word

Progress is scored on the grain scale, not in counters. Each abolished office, each association that survives five rounds, and each complete loop of the plain (every player, once, has given to every other player) lifts the scale by one plate-division, and a plate-division is eleven grains. The designer's note on the scale's use is printed on the spare plate, which was his hand, and the committee has never corrected it, which in this house means it stands forever: “So, so; he travels fast, and have contained, altogether, eleven grains in one dense body, they then undergo, they are about a fourth part.” The play-test crews read it the way they read everything on this table: the first clause as the pace (a fast association travels eleven grains a round), the second as the packing (eleven grains in one dense body is a plate at full weight), the third as the test (a full plate, under pressure, must still hold), and the last as the ratio (a network that has given a full round holds at about a fourth part of its own weight in grain, and no more — the rest is air, and air is where the next season begins). A game won on the scale is won at eleven plates, which is to say the scale reads a whole year's harvest in grain before the tea is cold.

§6 Campaign 1871: the seventy-two days

Campaign 1871 is played with the day-markers, and it is the game's long season. The plain becomes a city in a week, and the clock is the real one: 72 days, from the 18 March that opens the campaign to the 28 May that closes it. Each round flips one day-marker face down, and on day 52 — the oxblood marker — the two offices may field one counter each, because the storm came down, and a stateless table that has not yet formed its associations by the fifty-second day is a field with no hedge in it. The 1840 card (the one that asks what property is, in four words the players must not answer, only pass around) may be played once, and only to a player who holds no counters; it hands them one of each wood and is then filed, like the other early cards, in the Enlightenment drawer where the map case keeps its first season. The campaign is won the ordinary way — eleven plates on the scale — but it may also be lost the campaign's own way, and that loss is printed plainly: if day 72 is turned down and the scale reads fewer than eleven plates, the two offices stand for the rest of the season, and the next game begins on day 73, with the same tea.

§7 Campaign 1936, and the harvest on the Aragon lines

The second campaign keeps the plain and drops the clock; instead of days it runs on harvests, and its field notes come from the Aragon lines of 1936, where the movement's ideas met the wheat. Each round, one player acts the collective: their counters work the board's open field — the only place a counter may cross without a trackway — and the grain they bring in goes onto the scale at half weight, because the collective feeds the whole table. No player may be named to the collective; it is voted each round by counters on the ground, one vote per counter, and a player with no counters has no vote, which the designer marked as a feature. The campaign's winning condition is the scale at eleven plates, and its special card, The Aid Train, carries a counter of any owner to any other player for free, once a game, because reciprocity on a long line is the hardest distance a network ever covers and the box does not believe in long lines without trains.

§8 Scoring, and the end of the table

Eleven plates wins, whichever campaign is running, and the winning is shared: every player whose counters are on the board when the scale fills takes one of each wood back to the box, and the associations that survived the final round are drawn on the back of the landscape board, in pencil, by the player who lost. Losing is the only solo act this game allows. If both offices are abolished but the scale is short, the plain runs stateless for the remainder of the season, which the play-test crews called the long quiet, and no new associations may form during it — the plain rests, the grain goes on, and the next game is the next season. The supplier's standing note for referees, in the margin of the first printing: keep the tea moving, keep the tweed on, and if anyone at the table begins to describe the other players’ counters as theirs, the round is over and everyone starts again, because a network that starts keeping score of itself has already re-elected its head.

§9 The designer's note

Whoever plays this should know where it came from, because it came from a field. The designer read landscapes for a living, first with the excavation units of his Oxford region from 1970, then from the lecturing posts at the University of Bristol and the University of Oxford, and then from a trench he shared with the public for eighteen broadcast seasons, 1994–2011; the trackway lines on the board are drawn from his own Early Medieval field systems, and the three passes are the ones he walked most. The company that made the box kept the full record of him as a substance to be handled with tweed and one strong tea: the Safety Data Sheet, Rev. 15 — ASTON, Michael Antony, Grimsby & Wren Field Supplies Ltd. — sixteen sections, all present, next review never by agreement. His own box, Catalogue No. 7, serial 001, was kept in the dig house for the whole broadcast run, and its grain scale is the one the Class 2-C report on Eta Funayama Kofun, Nagomi Junior High mentions under the name the class gave it: the Naga Listeners’ Club of Naga City, Camarines Sur, which received the box by exchange with the long-wave set's sister school at the foot of the hill, and which played Campaign 1871 on the plain the class walked — the inverted hull as the neutral hill, the 1951 designation as the first card, and the road south of the second pass as the one route on the board that goes under both offices and out the other side. The class won in sixty-one days. The designer's note, on the last card of the deck, is short and signed only with the date: “Every field system I ever read was a network that had survived without a head. I have only put it on a table.” It is dated 1 July 1946, which the committee has confirmed is the date he would have used, not the date it was written.

§10 Errata, one line

First printing: the seal reads OLDURY. This is the supplier's own place and is therefore not an error; the errata line exists to say so, once, and is now closed. The front of the catalogue, with the other field games and the rest of the shelves, is kept at the Webstuffs front page.
