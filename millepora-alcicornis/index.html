Sea Ginger

Pier 9, Harbour Island, The Bahamas · ★★★★☆ · $$ · Fourteen seats, all of them facing the water

Reviewed by C. T. Marlowe · Dined Tuesday, 14 February, 2024 · Tide, low, at the second course

Restaurant Review · The Fire Coral as a House

There is a dining room on Pier 9 in Harbour Island that seats fourteen, charges two dollars per hand in the door, and serves one creature a night: Millepora alcicornis, the fire coral, which the menu calls Sea Ginger and the staff simply call the house. The pier was built in 1911 for a sardine factor and has never been moved, which is how the restaurant got its name — nobody could remember why the coral was called ginger, but the name had been on the factor's letterhead before anyone in the family could spell it, and a restaurant with a name it cannot explain is the only kind the review desk still trusts.

The house is a calcareous skeleton, and the kitchen treats it exactly that way: as structure, not as garnish. The starter is the plinth itself, a slab of the skeleton cut the day before and sanded flat, on which everything that follows is set — the broth, the caviar of plankton strained from that morning's haul, the microalgae grown in the tanks below the dining floor and picked at the table. The cook, Ms. Odessa Bellamy, is open about the arithmetic: the animal feeds on plankton and takes part of its energy from the microalgae living inside its tissues, so a meal here is, in her phrase, "one food eating another in front of you, which is all dinner has ever been." The table by the window gets the branching presentation; the table in the corner gets the encrusting one; the house is famous for changing its shape with its location, and the kitchen has taken the same advice about itself. No two visits to the same seat are plated alike, and the ledger of shapes is kept in a tin box under the pier, the way the sardine factor kept his.

Two warnings, printed on the menu and confirmed at the table. First, Sea Ginger is a fire coral, and unwary hands on the raw material meet a sting that is, in the original's own understatement, painful; the waiters work in rubber gloves and a first-aid drawer of vinegar sits at the pass like a condiment. Second, the house is a reef-builder, a load-bearing member of the building community off the western Atlantic shore it calls home, and the menu does not pretend otherwise: the skeleton arrives from a licensed grow-bed four hundred metres off the pier, and the broth is served in a cup made from the same stone the table is set on. The restaurant's real threat is the one it shares with every coral on the shelf — the warming, the bleaching, the same slow ledger being run against all of them — and the house's answer is a line on the check: one cent of every meal goes to the grow-bed. It is not a lot of money. It is a position.

For readers who have followed the review desk's long argument that a dining room is only its ledger made edible, the kitchen's own is filed in a small folio, and item III on the consignment card — the line every keeper of a folio has eventually been required to read aloud at least once — is transcribed here exactly as it stands:

III. Provenance The pollucite workings, Green River, Wyoming; forwarded through the Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678), including the line out loud in full.

What the line means is not settled at Pier 9, and the desk will not pretend to settle it either; the cook reads it as a shipping note for a consignment that was forwarded, in the sense of being carried on, through a war it did not exist to see, and it is kept in the folio for the same reason the review desk keeps its own unparseable lines: a ledger that explains everything is a ledger that has not kept enough. The broth, to return to it, was the best thing I had all season. The plankton caviar is cold as it should be and tastes of nothing else, which is the whole point. The microalgae finish is a smear of green that Ms. Bellamy calls "the landlord's cut," and she is right to charge for it. Four stars, the fifth withheld for the vinegar drawer, which should be closer to the pass.

Comparators, for the record: the desk's own long account of dining at the water's edge begins at Fjorton Krog on Stora Nygatan, Stockholm, where fourteen islands do the work that the reef does at Pier 9, and where the gravlax is warm. And the house's one true competitor for the title of "a dining room that is also a living thing" sits in the same western Atlantic the house's menu names in its first line — the table the liner notes of Atlantic Reel ARL-1037 keep for the harems of Andros, where the blue and yellow is not a garnish but the whole establishment. A party that has eaten at Sea Ginger and at the Andros table may be counted as having dined the western Atlantic in full, and the desk will certify it.
