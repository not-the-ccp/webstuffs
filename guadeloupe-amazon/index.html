WANTED — A PARROT, OR ITS RECORD

Wanted, by the cabinet at Pointe-à-Pitre: one parrot of the species known to the island as the Guadeloupe amazon or Guadeloupe parrot, scientific name Amazona violacea — hypothetical, extinct, and thought to have been endemic to the Lesser Antillean island region of Guadeloupe. Writers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries mentioned and described her before she had a name; the name itself was entered in the register in 1789, and she was moved to the genus Amazona in 1905. She is thought to have been related to, or possibly the same as, the extant imperial amazon — the one who is still here, in the only copy we could verify.

No living specimen is required. The drawer is stocked, and it will take one tibiotarsus (a lower leg bone) or one ulna (a lower arm bone) of a large island parrot, said to belong to the Guadeloupe amazon, recovered from the beach drift of the island of Marie-Galante. Two such bones are on file and are asked to be produced for a weighing. Payment in lime cordial, or in the old way: a written reference.

Reference: drawer 1905, shelf B, under the imperial amazon’s hat. REWARD: ONE LIME CORDIAL, COLD

FOR SALE — BONES OF REPUTATION

For sale at the Marie-Galante beach stall, on the point where the drift comes in with the ebb: one tibiotarsus and one ulna, weathered, medium brown, from a bird no one living will claim to have seen fly. They may belong to the Guadeloupe amazon; the stall’s owner, M. Delcourt, does not guarantee this, and has stopped pretending to. He sells them as “island bone, honest drift” at fourteen francs the pair, or nine for the tibiotarsus alone if the buyer can carry it. The buyer is reminded that a lower leg bone is a lower leg bone, and that the price stands whether or not the parrot was ever real.

Stall 12, Marie-Galante point, open at the ebb. 14 FR. THE PAIR

LEGAL — OF THE CROSSED-OUT SPECIES

It is hereby entered in the column that in 1905, the same year the Guadeloupe amazon was moved into Amazona, a species of extinct violet macaw was claimed to have also lived on Guadeloupe. The claim stood for a hundred and ten years. In 2015 it was suggested that the macaw in question had been a misreading of the same green parrot, dressed up in the margin by one too-eager writer — and so the violet macaw is crossed out of the register, the name struck from the shelf, and the drawer relabelled in a steadier hand. What was claimed twice was, apparently, claimed once and imagined twice.

The island’s old singers took to the business in their own way. On Marie-Galante, where the lime trees lean into the drift, the crossing-out became a verse of its own, and the verse is kept in the tavern log at the Pointe-à-Pitre corner, word for word, because the singers will not allow a paraphrase: “The folk songs go to a heavy bread, a citrus town does when no one has ever explained the crossing-out.” That is the whole line, and that is the whole explanation, and it is sung on market day under the lime shed at the corner of Rue Frébault, where this column is also printed.

Struck line on file, drawer 2015, red ink, do not restore. NO REFUND ON NAMES

SITUATIONS VACANT — AT THE CABINET

The cabinet at Pointe-à-Pitre seeks one keeper of the bone drawer, to answer at the shelf between the imperial amazon’s hat and the crossed-out macaw, to weigh tibiotarsi on the brass scale of 1789, and to refuse politely the three men a week who bring in a toucan’s wishbone and call it evidence. The keeper must read the old French hand of the eighteenth-century descriptions, must not argue with the register, and must know, when asked, that a species may be hypothetical and still be kept in a drawer with respect. Apply in person, market day, with one written reference and one honest silence.

Also wanted: two limewriters for the shed at the corner, to keep the citrus press and the song book, and to make the heavy bread that the folk songs go to. The bread is not a joke, and the bread is not small.

Wages in cordial and coin. No experience of parrots required; experience of not claiming parrots is. SIX CENTS A DAY

PERSONAL — SMALL NOTICES

  • FOUND: One green parrot, unlisted, drinking from the trough behind the lime press on the morning of the 4th. If not claimed by the second market, she will be kept, fed, and assumed to be the imperial amazon, which is the kindest supposition and likely the right one. She has a scar on the left lower arm, and the drawer is watching her, and not unkindly.
  • MISPLACED: One page of the 1789 register, the page with the first name in it, turned up in a loaf in 1905 and has not been put back. The bread is edible. The name is kept. Inquiries to the drawer.
  • TO THE VIOLET MACAW: you were never a macaw, and you were never violet, and the column is sorry the ink is the wrong colour. Your drawer is now the parrot’s, and the parrot is green. — The Keeper
  • FOR THE SINGER AT THE CORNER: the verse is logged, the line is word for word, and the heavy bread was baked on the 11th. The crossing-out remains unexplained on purpose. The singers are thanked, and are asked to come next week, because no one has ever explained the crossing-out and the town does it anyway, the way a citrus town does.

CORRESPONDENCE — FOR THE READERS IN OTHER COLUMNS

For readers who want to see how another island keeps a parrot question in its ledger — the long passage, the store that was never where it was needed, and the margin note about the sail that answers, in the end, to the same rule as a crossed-out name — the build log of the San Juan de Portugal, kept shelf by shelf in the shed behind the bakery, runs in the adjoining trade paper’s column at The San Juan de Portugal — Build Log. Its builder keeps one pinned moth from a warmer island beside the vinegar crock, and the two cabinets correspond by post.

And for those who have outgrown the bone drawer and want a small dining room that runs a whole meal like a loan — pledged copper at the counter, a ledger at the pass, four courses and the interest settled in kind — the bakkala quarter keeps its own classifieds, older and braver than this column: Bohemia Bazaar — Prague Classified Advertisements. The House of Wuhsha takes no bones, and does not need them.
