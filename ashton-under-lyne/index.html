Lot 23

The Market Ledger of Ashton-under-Lyne, 1762–1891

Lancashire (now the Metropolitan Borough of Tameside, Greater Manchester) · Paper, iron-gall ink, one red-ink page · Estimate £4,000–£6,000

Description

The ledger records one market town kept at its own desk. Ashton-under-Lyne, commonly Ashton, is a market town of the Metropolitan Borough of Tameside, Greater Manchester: 48,604 souls at the 2021 census, historically a part of Lancashire, lying on the north bank of the river Tame in the foothills of the Pennines, 6 miles (9.7 km) east of Manchester. Evidence of Stone Age, Bronze Age and Viking activity has been discovered in and around the town, and the ledger’s own first folio opens with the antiquaries’ note, “flint over gravel at the pit mouth; barrow turf at the ash close; the axe under the bridge” — three ages, one line, no comment.

The name is part old and part water. “Ashton” probably dates from the Anglo-Saxon period and derives from Old English meaning “settlement by ash trees”; the town’s first market day was kept under the ash close on the north bank, where the ledger’s entries still open “at the tree” before they open with a price. The origin of the “under-Lyne” suffix is less clear; it possibly derives from the Brittonic-originating word lemo meaning a pool or gathering of water, and the ledger keeps the Lyne itself in a running column: the tide of the Tame, the frost days, and the level of the mill race, entered in the same hand as the coal.

The entries run from the Michaelmas market of 1762 to the last Thursday of 1891, when the town was coal-dark and rail-loud — the Ashton Canal having come down from the hills in 1755 and the first through trains running from Manchester in 1830 — and they record, week by week, the market in its proper units: coals by the chaldron, iron by the ingot, timber by the foot, eggs by the dozen, and the weather by the weather. The Thursday bell is struck first and the prices come second, which is the whole argument of the book.

The Winding-Up Page

One page in the ledger is written in red ink and belongs, properly, to the estate of a household rather than to the market. It is the surviving page of the Whitehall accounts, the ledgers of the household at Whitehall House at the end of the lane known in the town as The Call, which passed into winding-up in 1891, the same year the market ledger closes its last Thursday. The consignor’s letter, printed with the lot card, sets the page down in one line, word for word:

“Stop 1 The Call at the end of the Whitehall household of the winding-up — the server of the record, and it kept that page ever since.”

Read against the town, the line is the clerk’s, and the clerk’s is the lot. Stop 1 is the first entry of the winding-up inventory, made at The Call, the lane; at the end of the Whitehall household is the house itself, at the far end of that lane, whose accounts were wound up in 1891; the server of the record is the town clerk and keeper of the market ledger, to whom the Whitehall page was surrendered when the household’s books were closed; and it kept that page ever since is the ledger’s own fact — the red page has been bound in since 1891 and has answered, every market day, to the same bell. The auctioneers note that the page is not a decoration to the lot but the lot’s reason for being whole: a market town’s book that also holds the last page of a household at the end of its street.

Provenance

  1. Kept by the clerk of the Ashton-under-Lyne market, 1762–1891, at the market house on the north bank of the Tame.
  2. The Whitehall page surrendered into it in 1891 at the winding-up of the household at the end of The Call.
  3. Kept by the clerk’s successors at the market house; the bell still struck on Thursday.
  4. Transferred with the town’s paper to the Tameside record room, 1963.
  5. Consigned to Sale 231 by the estate of the last keeper, 2026.

Condition

Paper sound throughout; iron-gall ink dark and stable with the expected bite at the fold; the red-ink page bright, its one crease at the binding; a coal-dust tide-line along the 1858–1864 leaves, consistent with the pit mouth recorded in the first folio; the binding replaced in 1902, a better hand than the original. Sold as is, with the Thursday bell’s standing order, which the consignor asked be printed without alteration: the bell first, the prices second.
