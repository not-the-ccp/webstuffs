1 · What the kit is for

The art this kit is made for has one plain name and a long shadow. It is espionage — spying, or intelligence gathering — the act of obtaining secret, confidential, or in some way valuable information, which is also what such information is called once it has been obtained: intelligence. The two words do not mean the same thing any more than the pond and the gauge do; one is the work and the other is what the work brings in. A professional trained in conducting these operations by their government may be employed as an intelligence officer, and the kit makes no claim to be an officer; it is the officer’s shelf, not the officer’s hand. The work itself may be conducted in a foreign country, domestically, or remotely — the wire does not ask which — and the practice is clandestine, as it is by definition unwelcome. In some circumstances it is a legal tool of law enforcement, and in others it is illegal and punishable by law, which is why the kit ships with the law’s own chapter in its inside pocket: the manual’s reading of the public wrong, filed at Chapter 14 — The Crime, in the Keeper’s Field Manual, where the act is read as a thing that changes hands. The kit does not choose which circumstance it is in. The kit watches, and the circumstance does the rest, which is how the art has always been kept honest.

3 · The object of the watch

Every volume begins with its object, and this volume’s preface is one line long. The card keeps it word for word, without reading it twice, in the way a clerk copies a figure he does not check against the kettle:

“One water and the White Whale its object.”

The water is Poucha Pond: the whole of it, black before the party and black when the last car left, read twice a day on the north bank at the same volume as the float. The White Whale is the standing object of the watch — the thing the watch is set on, the one object of the art that the art has never yet brought in. The ferry master’s rule, which the kit prints inside the back cover, says why the object has to be unseen: a watch with a sighted object is a census. The census is done the first time the thing is seen; the watch is only then beginning. So the kit does not aim to catch, photograph, or file the White Whale. It aims to keep the water read and the wire hot and the three columns agreeing, with the object held in the preface the way a leaf is held in the attic — flat, unspendable, and exactly where the keeper put it. One water, and the object, is the whole of the doctrine, and the doctrine is enough.

4 · Field use, on the record

The first filling of the kit, number one on the shelf, was on the sandbank on the night of 18–19 July 1969, with the recorder already on, reading the pond as the manual reads it: as an instrument, not a charge. The dawn patrol came up at track ten with searchlights and boats and the telephone wire, nine minutes and six seconds on the record, and the kit did what the kit does — it kept the reading, copied the line as it sat, and did not check the figure against the kettle. That is the kit’s whole field example, and the manual gives the rest at Chapter 14 — The Crime. The second use of the kit is the use the drawer shows: when the folio came up in the Friday post of the 17th, no return address, no stamp of any office the keeper could place, the keeper filed it beside the volume and the small wire, in the ledger that keeps the state’s own signature in the third column, and noted, twice, that the two ledgers were the same. The kit watches from inside its own case file, which is the one position in the art from which nothing on the record can see you, because you are where the record is kept.
