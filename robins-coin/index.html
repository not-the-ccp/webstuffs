The Naturalist's Cabinet · Field Notes, Vol. CXI · No. 4, April 1997

The Two Specimens of Jackie Robinson, Mint-strike, in situ

Examined at the United States Mint, Philadelphia · 38°57′N, 75°08′W · the 50th anniversary season, April to September

Field day 1 · Tuesday, 15 April, weather clear, wind off the Schuylkill at a gale's memory

Came down this week to the Mint at Franklin Square with the tray, the black velvet, the brass loupe, and the little card I carry for anything with two faces, because everything of value has two faces. The specimen in question is the commemorative pair of 1997, the silver dollar and the gold half eagle, struck to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the day Jackie Robinson took the field for the Brooklyn Dodgers and broke the color line of Major League Baseball. The Mint sets a modest population for the silver piece and a strict one for the gold, and the gold half eagle is the rarest coin in the United States since the Saint-Gaudens double eagle was recalled in 1933. I have kept my hands clean and my notes close to the metal.

On the obverse of the dollar, the animal is caught at the instant of motion: Robinson sliding into home plate, the spike heels trailing, the body all diagonal. The designer is Alfred F. Maletsky, and the drawing has the honesty of a good field sketch — nothing posed, nothing settled, the whole figure still traveling the way a heron is still traveling after the fish is gone. The reverse, by T. James Ferrell, carries the logo of the Jackie Robinson Foundation, which I have set down in the ledger as the species' mark of its descendants, the way a warbler's song settles the whole question of which warbler. The dollar is one ounce of silver, .999 fine, and it weighs 26.73 grammes in the palm, which is the weight of a small stone and the weight of a promise, which I have found are sometimes the same object.

Field day 2 · Thursday, 24 April, overcast, the tray smelling of mint flux and rain

The half eagle came under glass on the second day, which is the only day a gold coin of this rank should meet the eye: William C. Cousins has given us the portrait, Robinson in profile, and the reverse bears a baseball, its seams crossing the field like the lines of a surveyor's plat. It is five dollars by law and it is a great deal more by fact. Under the loupe the fields are bright as river sand in the sun, and the portrait holds its place at the center the way a lighthouse holds the middle of a channel — not decoration, but navigation. I asked the attendant whether the coin was to be read as a medal or as money, and she said it was the fiftieth anniversary of a first, and that a first is always both.

I note for the record a line found penciled into a visitor's ledger at the Franklin Square window, left there by an earlier hand and taken by me verbatim, uncorrected, for the file: The head is turned nose to the north, the ice in the order of the island with the chair and after the heat to the Webstuffs front page . The writer's meaning I do not know. The coin's head does point, and the north is the direction the Mint's own compass rose favors, and a field naturalist does not discard a sentence for being hard; the hard sentence is usually the one that was written by someone who had seen something. I have filed it beneath the two specimens where it will keep until the order of the island is explained to me, or until the heat does something to the chair.

Field day 3 · Saturday, 26 April, sun, the tray on the park bench at Franklin Square

Both specimens out together at last. The silver carries the motion and the gold carries the face, which is the whole natural history of the matter in two pieces: the slide that broke the line, and the portrait that closed it into a name. On April 15, 1947, at Ebbets Field, he took the field as the Dodgers' first baseman; the coin, fifty years on, is what the anniversary did to the memory — it pressed the memory, the way the Mint presses everything, and the press made it hold. The foundation's logo on the reverse keeps the work going in the field itself, which is the only place the work was ever done.

For the reader who takes coins as a kind of weather rather than a kind of metal: the companion entry in this cabinet on a suspended season, the dark seats of the 2020 Campeonato Nacional, is kept in the Dreamer's Concordance, entry for Universidad de Chile, where a whole city lies down in a single blue sheet and the dream ends at a table that is set. And for the discipline of keeping a line on the move with only one stop to the village, the Korek Express timetable and commentary shows how a regional line touches a place exactly once a day, long enough to unload the fish van, which is the same economy of arrival a commemorative coin practices on an entire century. The reader is also invited back to the front page of the cabinet, where the tray is wiped, the cards are squared, and the file is closed for the season.

One further arrival at the cabinet: a notice from the Oficina de Extravíos in Bogotá, Capital District, reporting a black velvet tray found on a bench at Parque Santander, empty, with two round impressions still holding the shape of the specimens that once sat there — the same velvet, the same two specimens, and a scarf the colour of a Chilean sky. The full notice is filed at Bogotá lost-and-found, Notice No. 02148. The club has since walked the matter the last short way it needed to walk: in Correspondent No. 7 of the Greenbush Valley the secretary reports the trip to the National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum in Cooperstown, New York, and sets down plainly that the last place the tray was carried before it went south to that bench was the plaque room of the Hall, where the two specimens sat in their two circles to the millimetre of the velvet's memory.
