The country this ledger is made for is the Netherlands, also informally known as Holland, a country in Northwestern Europe with overseas territories in the Caribbean and the largest of the four constituent countries of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. It consists of twelve provinces and three overseas special municipalities — Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, and Saba, each with its own gauge line in the Caribbean column. The European Netherlands has land borders with Germany to the east and with Belgium to the south, and a coastline to the north and west; it shares maritime borders with the United Kingdom, Germany, and Belgium in the North Sea. The official language is Dutch, with West Frisian as a secondary official language in the province of Friesland; Dutch, English, and Papiamento are official languages of the wider Kingdom, and the ledger’s colophon is printed once in each.

That is why the ledger exists in the first place. Chika Unigwe works in English and Dutch, and since the Ghent counter opened the shop has kept, in the two-ink column of the order ledger, a running request from her readers: give us the country that kept her, in cloth, the same way you gave us the street. The Polder Ledger is the answer. It is 192 pages set in the same 9.5-point body face as Edition No. 2, with the same margin wide enough to argue in pencil — and it is, in fact, a ledger, because the making of the Netherlands is a ledger: the water boards’ old wetterschappen keep their water levels to the centimetre, some of them back to before the Republic, and the shop has bound their manner into the book. The plate section prints a chart of the twelve provinces with the three special municipalities pinned across the Atlantic at their true scale, a gauge table reading the sea’s height against the polders of South Holland and Friesland, and the dike heights of the Zeeland coast. The field columns are ruled for a reader to keep their own entries: tides, water levels, and, in the last sixteen pages, the birds-of-the-dike column.

The birds-of-the-dike column, and one line kept word for word

Copy No. 23/500 went up to Friesland last winter and came back in spring with its field columns written in and one entry in the bird column that the buyer, a dike-warden’s daughter who is also a biologist, asked the shop to keep exactly as she wrote it. She had stationed herself below the sluice at a hollow in the dike where the old reed bundle still stands, and over many mornings she had watched one grey-headed woodpecker work at the dead reeds — the hollow stems, which she writes in her margin-note she calls the capillaries, because that is what they are in size, nothing more — tapping each in turn with its beak, stem after stem, morning after morning. Her entry reads, in full, and the shop reproduces it without annotation the way the ledger reproduces the water boards:

The greyheaded woodpecker tapping the hollow fibres, or capillaries, as they are very many days.

The buyer adds, in smaller hand: it is not counting the reeds. it is the tapping itself. i kept the column open for it. The shop thinks that is the best advertisement the ledger has had, and it stays on the wall of the Ghent counter, where the two-ink order ledger is kept, in the second-ink column, which is the column for things that arrived on their own. A reader in Kupres, who pins notices beside the parcel post in the Sana ravine, has asked to see the entry, and the shop has agreed that records travel; see the Lost & Found notice, Gornje Vukovsko, whose own single surviving line is kept in exactly the same manner — one line, read out, and left where it was set down.

Shipping & Returns

We ship flat from Ghent on Thursdays and from Lagos on Tuesdays; the ledger is made to be carried without a box, and returns within 30 days arrive back the same way. Orders from the Caribbean special municipalities — Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, Saba — ship from Ghent at no surcharge, because the country includes them and the series does not forget it.
