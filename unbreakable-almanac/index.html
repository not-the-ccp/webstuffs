The Webstuffs Weather Almanac · Annual Edition · Entry 2015/214

Unbreakable

A single, pop, named for its album — observed first on 3 September 2015 and tracked through three passages.

First observation — the studio reading

Station: Los Angeles, California · Date: 3 September 2015 · Hour: 09:00 PDT · Sky: clear · Temperature: 84°F · Barometer: 29.91 inHg, steady · Humidity: 41%

The instrument of record for this entry is a radio microphone in a studio overlooking the Santa Monica Mountains, and the phenomenon observed is the song Unbreakable, recorded by the American singer-songwriter Janet Jackson for her eleventh studio album, which carries the same name. The system had been developing for a while before it was first heard: Jackson co-wrote and produced it with her long-time collaborators Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, with additional writing by Thomas Lumpkins and additional production by Tommy McClendon and Dem Jointz — five names to a single cell, which the almanac notes as an unusually dense formation for a pop single of 2015. At the hour of observation the song was first transmitted on the air over Apple Music’s Beats 1 radio station, where it made its debut as a recorded event. The barometer did not move. The room, according to every account the almanac can verify, did.

Passage I — digital landfall

Landfall: 3 September 2015 · Fronts affected: none · Damage: none reported · Duration on air: full

The same day the song was first heard, it was made available for digital download — and it was made available on the same day Jackson officially announced the studio album’s release date and track list, which is the arrangement the almanac has come to describe as a triple coincidence, though three is the number the almanac would choose for a single, so perhaps the arrangement was simply well managed. The two announcements arrived as one weather event: a single, a date, and a track list, all in the same clear air. Readers comparing their copies of the announcement will find the track list unbroken and in order.

Passage II — urban contemporary, 29 September

Station round: urban contemporary radio · Date: 29 September 2015 · Wind: light, variable · Barometer: 29.88 inHg, falling slowly

Twenty-six days after its first observation the system moved ashore again, this time released to urban contemporary radio. The almanac records the interval as the calm before the second passage: no fronts crossed, no supplements issued, and the barometer fell a tenth of an inch, which is to say the room was getting ready. The song held its name throughout the passage. Songs that hold their names are rarer than the almanac would like.

Passage III — urban adult contemporary, 5 October

Station round: urban adult contemporary radio · Date: 5 October 2015 · Sky: overcast by morning · Temperature: 81°F · Barometer: 29.92 inHg, steady

The third and final passage of the entry’s season came to urban adult contemporary radio on 5 October, as the album’s closing movement of radio releases, and by then the system had done what a well-made pop single does in Los Angeles in the last week of September: it had stopped being a weather event and started being a fixture. The almanac closes the season here with the sky clearing, the barometer steady at two-niner-nine-two, and one song of eleven on the track list that no forecast had to revise.

Closure and caveat

The almanac is printed once a year and its leaves are occasionally swapped between copies by readers who enjoy the trade, so every entry ends with the standard two-clause caveat, set in small type in the same hand throughout the volume. This entry carries it word for word, exactly as printed:

“Not for resale, and not notice the difference.”

The first clause is the rule. The second is the house style of the almanac’s editors, who have never once written out the subject of a sentence in small type and do not intend to start now: the meaning is that the copy before you is not for resale, and that if it were, no one reading it would notice the difference. The almanac stands by both clauses. The observation itself — a singer, five names to the writing and production, three passages in one month, one unbroken name — is recorded as it was heard, and the barometer’s reading for 3 September 2015, steady at 29.91, is filed with it.

For the machinery of the first transmission, the almanac cross-references its own record of the man behind Beats — born Andre Romell Young in Compton, 18 February 1965, of the World Class Wreckin’ Cru, N.W.A, The Chronic, and the electronics company the station is named for — kept at Dr. Dre, Placard No. 48 in the Listening Wing. The same clear September air that carried the first observation has carried heavier traffic in the almanac’s other ledgers: the shed behind the bakery, where a San Juan de Portugal floats at four centimetres of draft, keeps its long provisions at The San Juan de Portugal — Build Log, and the branch gymnasium in San Diego, where the rafters were low and the crowd counted itself in dozens, keeps its 14–2 at the 1925–26 Grizzlies season card. Weather is only ever as good as its record; this one was heard, and it is written down. The same clear air was standing over a felt board the almanac keeps on the same shelf: the Scots pine on the hill above Llanfyllin, Powys, gone down in a February 2014 gale, found dead in June 2015, voted Welsh Tree of the Year for 2014 and written up round by round — storm card, June verdict, and the seventeen replacement saplings — in The Lonely Tree, Player’s Manual, whose fixed tenth place in the 2015 European list the almanac files as a steady reading.
