Lost · One comet, one body, two lobes

67P / Churyumov–Gerasimenko, also "67P/C–G" and, around the exchange floor, Chury. Jupiter-family comet, originally of the Kuiper belt, approximately 4.3 by 4.1 km (2.7 by 2.5 mi) at its longest and widest dimensions, the two lobes joined at the neck in the shape the plate photographers of 1969 first made out on the plates. Orbital period 6.45 years as of 2012; rotation period about 12.4 hours; maximum velocity 135,000 km/h (38 km/s; 84,000 mph) at perihelion. Last seen coming to perihelion, closest approach to the Sun, on 2 November 2021; next perihelion expected 9 November 2027. Answer to "one large object with a small satellite" or "two small objects with a long neck." If found, do not attempt to return to sender: the address is the Kuiper belt, and the belt is far. Reward: a good seat at the 2027 perihelion and a page in the ledger of the exchange, where Chury's name is kept in the first column because it begins with a number and numbers always come first.

For sale · One grain, no more

One authentic grain of Churyumov–Gerasimenko dust, loose, unmounted, with a written note from the finder, a plate-photographer's daughter at the old observatory on the river. The note is preserved, and the relevant line reads, word for word: "The paper is roughly 100,000 nm thick, so this week — the matter of 2.5 micrometres or less." In plain terms for the buyers: the card on which the note is written is some 100,000 nanometres of paper, while the grain being offered — the matter of the advertisement, this week's matter — is no thicker than 2.5 micrometres, a hair's width divided, and it came off the coma on the morning of the 2014 flyby when the body was shedding like a door left open. The grain is dark, it smells faintly of nothing (the seller insists this is a feature), and it weighs less than the comma at the end of this sentence. Priced at four stamps, non-negotiable; the seller will wrap it in a corner of the original card at no extra cost. Buyers in the near-Sun trade should note the grain is not for wearing, not for eating, and not, despite appearances, for sending. See also the exchange's other sheet for items of modest weight: the Lost & Found Notice 4172, one gradient descent, last seen descending at 3400 N. Willamette, which is filed with the same quiet confidence.

Wanted · Two names, one plate

Wanted, for the record and for the ledger, the continued good offices of Klim Ivanovych Churyumov and Svetlana Ivanovna Gerasimenko, the Soviet astronomers who first observed the body on photographic plates in 1969 and lent it their names, which it now carries for as long as it orbits. The exchange is not asking either astronomer to do anything; the ad stands as a matter of record, in the old custom by which a name given to a moving object is thanked in print once a turn. Chury has completed the 6.45-year circuit many times since 1969, and the plates remain on file at the exchange, the 1969 ones in the top drawer, the corner of one curled. Enquiries should mention the neck, the 12.4-hour day, and the fact that at 135,000 km/h the body has outlived every address it has ever had.

To let · A window on the 2027 passage

To let, from the exchange's own stock, one good window seat, east side, with a direct view on the 9 November 2027 perihelion: first light on the neck, the coma at its fullest, the tail trailing over the meridian in the way a long piece of ribbon is trailed over a garden wall. The seat is comfortable, the horizon is dark, and the tenant is expected to keep the ledger updated as the 12.4-hour rotation turns the lobes under the window in sequence — the large lobe by night, the small by day. Rent: one honest observation, entered in the book. No subletting to telescopes; telescopes must stand in the queue and they stand there a long time, being heavy and patient. The present tenant, a small paper comet of the near-Sun variety, is moving out on the strength of the Greene Corner listing, Product No. 30, whose keeper has taken a shine to the long corners and the numbers held a season at a time, and has offered the paper comet a shelf in the back room where the cedar smell is already settled.

In memoriam · The neck

In memoriam, the neck of 67P/C–G as it stood in 2015, measured, sketched, argued over, and entered in the ledger in ink that is still, the exchange notes, the same ink as the 1969 plate file. The neck joined the two lobes for as long as the surveyors could agree on its width, and it is remembered at this exchange for holding what it held — dust no thicker than a hair's width, ice with a long memory of the Kuiper belt, and a rotation day of 12.4 hours that ran, day after day, like the counter of the exchange itself. The ledger line is preserved as entered: two lobes, one neck, one name given twice, one number kept in the first column. Those who wish to see the ledger in person should come on a Friday, when the sheet goes out, and the dust grain from the For Sale column above is still, the exchange assures the public, no thicker than it was last week.
