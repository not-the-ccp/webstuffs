Opening the thread properly this time, with the name spelled the way the maps spell it: Liyue (LEE-way; Chinese: 璃月; pinyin: Líyùe), which translates as the jade moon or the glazed moon — you can hear the glaze in it, the way a kiln mouth cools. It is a fictional nation in Genshin Impact, the game developed by miHoYo, and it sits in the eastern part of Teyvat, the continent the whole game is built on. For the first chapter of the main storyline it is the main location — the place a newcomer lands on, walks through, argues with the sky in, and only then is told the rest of the map exists.

Here is the figure I keep meaning to write down: the creation of Liyue took one year. One year, for the cliffs, the harbours, the lanterns, and the tea. Its design is based on Ancient China, and the artistic references are named in the studio's own interviews: the sandstone pillars of the Zhangjiajie National Forest Park in Hunan, the terraced pools of the Huanglong Scenic Area, the karst landscape of Guilin, the stilted houses of Fenghuang Ancient Town on the Tuojiang, and the Hanging Temple in Shanxi, the cliff shrine held up by not much at all. The setting has been positively received; players and critics have praised it as a symbol of Chinese culture within the game. I am starting this thread to walk the references one by one, reply by reply. Start with the temple.

RopeAndIncense wrote on 14 June 2025, 11:03 — Post No. 2

The temple, then, because I lived near its model for a whole winter. The Hanging Temple — Xuànkóngsì — clings to the cliff face above the Jinwater gorge in Shanxi, and from the valley it looks pinned on by an accident of rope-work. The buildings lean out over nothing; the beams are dark with a century of smoke from the incense below. That is exactly the posture the game's Liyue borrows: a shrine that is allowed to be precarious, because the mountains around it are also a little precarious. In the game the same idea shows up everywhere — bridges that go off into mist, staircases that belong to nobody, the whole eastern rim of the country built like it is being negotiated with the cliff rather than placed on it.

One thing I have not seen anyone say: the temple teaches the game's art to recede. When you stand under the Hanging Temple you do not photograph it head-on; you stand in the gully and let it hang above you. The Liyue scenes do the same — you play at the foot of the pillar-forests and the camera is always a little below you, looking up. Whoever framed that, they had been to a gorge in Shanxi, or they should have been.

Quoting the board keeper's old note, from the thread on the Tuojiang riverbird: … Walks depart from the summer, migrating across the roof.) That is the whole of the surviving field note — the top of the page was torn off, and what is left is the last half of a sentence about the white-eyes, which leave in the summer heat and cross the old tile roofs of Fenghuang Ancient Town in a thin line. I am pasting it in because it is the most accurate description of Liyue's skyline I have ever read: the country itself departs from the summer, migrating across the roof.

If the board keeper agrees, the next reply should be the pillars. Zhangjiajie does not forgive bad weather; I have four grey mornings and one perfect one, and the perfect one is worth the whole thread.

HuanglongTrek wrote on 15 June 2025, 07:18 — Post No. 3

Before the pillars, the water, because Huanglong is the reference everyone misses. The terraced pools of the Huanglong Scenic Area in Sichuan are not blue — that is the first thing to say, out of the way, because the postcards lie. They are the colour of lily pads in calcium, green at the edges and jade at the centre, and in one year of light they change eleven times. Liyue's hydro-areas drink straight from that: the warm pools, the stepped basins where the water falls a finger's width from one shelf to the next. And the name — Líyùe, the jade moon — is doing the same work as the water. Jade is the colour you get when stone has been patient longer than anyone alive. A glazed moon is the same colour from the other side of the sky. The country is named after its own geology, and I think the studio meant it.

Guilin gets a sentence too, while we are at the water: the karst pinnacles of the Li River, the hills that look boiled up from the plain, one per rice field, no two identical. That is the middle country of Liyue — the walkable part, the market part, where the story actually happens at street level. Zhangjiajie is the wall; Guilin is the floor; Huanglong is the colour the whole thing is lit in. One year to make all three agree, and they do agree. I have walked all three in the flesh, and the game is closer to the truth than most of the truth's own brochures.

Fenghuang Ancient Town, the fourth reference, is the one my grandmother would have recognised in an instant: the stilted houses leaning over the Tuojiang, the wood dark to the colour of strong tea, the bridges that are not really bridges but doorways built over water. When the first chapter of the game shows you the port district — the houses on posts, the rope bridges, the boats moored close enough to reach — it is showing you that river town. And it shows you the smell of it, which is the generous part: smoke and hot stone and noodle broth, the three things that are always on a Tuojiang evening in the wet season. The reception the setting has had — praised as a symbol of Chinese culture in the game — I read as a family matter more than a cultural one. The people who recognised their own riverside in a fictional nation were the people who grew up on it. That is a better review than any critic's.

One small cross-file for the record: the nearest thing on this board to that riverside feeling — a place that serves at the edge of its kingdom and keeps a corner table for forty years — is the tavern reviewed in the Corona dining column. Different river, different kingdom, same rule: the food is the map.
