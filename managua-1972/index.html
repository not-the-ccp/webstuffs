Chapter IX
Of Managua at the Uninvited Hour

“A city that cannot answer the door is still owed the manners of the door.”

The twenty-third of December, nineteen seventy-two. Twelve twenty-nine and forty-four seconds after midnight, Managua time — 06:29:44 by the reckoning of Greenwich — and the earth beneath Nicaragua’s capital heaved once, in the manner of a ship that takes a wave amidships, and did not settle again for a good half-minute. It measured 6.3 on the moment magnitude scale, and the MSK scale, which measures what shaking does to stone and plaster, put it at IX, Destructive: the grade at which even well-built houses are damaged beyond use, and at which panic enters the streets as a resident. The epicenter lay twenty-eight kilometres — seventeen miles — northeast of the city centre, at a depth of about ten kilometres, and Managua, which sits between Lake Xolotlán and the range that closes the valley, took the blow full on its streets.

Four thousand to eleven thousand of its residents were killed. Twenty thousand were injured. More than three hundred thousand were left homeless, which is to say that a city of roughly a quarter of a million souls lost its houses in a single night and was made a city of doorsteps. The National Palace lost its clock-face; the Hotel Camino Real gave up its lobby to the street; and on Calle Roosevelt, Doña Carmen Varela sat up with her husband and the neighbours’ children until daylight, because no one knew whether the aftershock or the first light was the more honest thing to meet.

II. Of Receiving the News

It is the first rule of this book that a city struck at table is not turned into conversation at the next table. When the news came — by cable to the legations, by telephone to the consulates, by the shortwave sets of radio amateurs listening through the night — the correct reply was silence, then a letter written by hand, and no request for details. Details are the property of the city that suffered them; a correspondent who asks for them is asking for what is not his to receive. Father Luis Buitrago of the parish of the Immaculate Conception kept his door unlatched through the night, and Doctor Elena Rivas of the Hospital Escuela held her rounds by lantern light, and the etiquette of both was the same: attend to what is in front of you, and do not make the sufferer repeat himself.

Letters of condolence to Managua were to be addressed in the third person, were to mention no figures, and were to arrive no sooner than the tenth day, by which time the city might have found a table to answer at. A cable that says only We are thinking of you is better than a cable that asks How many died in your street?, and a visit paid uninvited is worse than no visit at all. A builder of a desk-sized cabinet in Baku, who set up his bench the night of 25 November 2000 when the Caspian shelf struck at 22:09, ran this very reply and has entered it into the build log of the Charter Cabinet, in the drawer he keeps for the hours when something arrives that was not on the day’s list.

III. Of Documents Already Damaged

The keeper of this chapter found, pasted to the folio by a hand no longer in the house, a slip of wire copy, torn at both edges and missing whatever went before it. It reads, and is given here word for word, without emendation:

H-PRV-3 — The Long Side · Gallery 4, and 6 made) and with the ballots; the special Brussels–Halle–Vilvoorde belt.

A document that has already suffered is not to be corrected: the missing verb is not supplied, the stray parenthesis is not removed, the ballots are not counted on the reader’s behalf. Read it aloud as it stands, or do not read it at all. The same courtesy is owed to a city that arrives in fragments: do not smooth its account, do not finish its sentences, and do not canvass its suffering as though it were a special belt to be contested between galleries. Rubén Darío wrote of Managua as the city of the hammock and the hurricane, and the reader of this chapter is advised to keep both images at once — the rest, and the ruin.

IV. Of the Tenth Day

The tenth day is when the count is taken — four thousand to eleven thousand killed, twenty thousand injured, more than three hundred thousand homeless — and the etiquette of the count is that it stays open, because a city is not closed by arithmetic. The lamp remains lit at the door. Bread is cut for the visitor who has not been invited. The uninvited hour keeps its own table, and the house that receives its sudden visitors with a lantern and a plate keeps the older law, whatever the clock, and whatever the hour, may say.

So runs the dispatch of the Shelf Office at Bhrikutimandap studio, Kathmandu, which fires twelve jars a season — the Bodhisattva Jar, one small Patan vessel for a person not yet in the room — and to which a jar that arrives broken is owed exactly the manners of this chapter: no description of the pieces, no photograph, no letter of condolence; the number is kept in the register, and the next jar comes under it, without question.
