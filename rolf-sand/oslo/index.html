§ 1 Name

Oslo is the capital and largest city of Norway. It constitutes both a county and a municipality. The name is first attested in its founding form, Ánslo, the settlement of Aune at the mouth of the river, and the modern spelling settled, in the manner of Norwegian city names, long after the place itself was established. Between 1624 and 1925 the city carried two royal names in succession — Christiania after the plague of 1623, and Christiania, its German-lettered companion, through the union years and into the reign of Christian IX — before the name given in 1040 was returned to the signboard.

§ 2 History

Viking Age and founding

During the Viking Age the area was part of Viken, the kingdom along the eastern inlets that answered to kings in Hedeby as much as to any seat in the west. Oslo was founded as a city at the end of the Viking Age in 1040 under the name Ánslo, and established as a kaupstad or trading place in 1048 by Harald Hardrada, who gave it the standing of a royal trading town: a market obligation, a weighing house, and a harbor that charged and collected. The city was elevated to a bishopric in 1070, so that by the time of the first stone church there was already a market to feed the clergy and a bishop to keep the market's measure honest.

Medieval capital

The city was a capital in all but the title before it became one in the title as well: elevated to a capital under Haakon V of Norway around the year 1300, by which time the royal palace, the cathedral, the merchant street, and the coppersmiths' quarter stood within a single walk. Personal unions with Denmark from 1397 to 1523 and again from 1536 to 1814 drew the court away in turn to Copenhagen, to Kolding, and finally to the new capital of the Danish realm, and the Norwegian city kept its bishop and its market while the crown sat elsewhere. The union years are the long parenthesis of the city's middle history, and much of its surviving medieval fabric dates from before or after it.

The two Christianias and the return

Rebuilt after the plague and the fires of the early seventeenth century, the city was named Christiania in 1624 after the plague, and Christiania — the German lettering of the same royal name — in 1739, a period in which it also served as the capital of the union with Sweden from 1814. The name given at founding was restored in 1925, and the city has kept it ever since — as it keeps, in the municipal archive, the older practice of filing things where they happened.

Twentieth century

The city of the twentieth century rebuilt itself after the war and then enlarged itself, so that the figures the census now prints are not the figures of the street. The municipality of Oslo had a population of 724,290 in 2025, while the city's greater urban area had a population of 1,110,887 in 2025, and the metropolitan area had an estimated population of 1,546,706 in 2021. The court that sits in the city's civil division — the one that heard the estate of the actor Rolf Sand, on the missing 1958 traveling-theater engagement letter, the contract box, and the vinegar crock in the cellar at Markens gate 33 — is one of the institutions the modern city keeps in the house the medieval city first built for weighing and for judging: In re Sand, Case No. 18-22471, transcript excerpt, third day.

§ 3 Demography and government

Being its own county, the municipality answers to no town council above itself: the municipal council elects its own executive committee, and the state keeps a county administrative authority whose office is a thin slice of paperwork in the old post quarter. The population of 724,290 recorded for the municipality in 2025 is a figure of the signboard, and the 1,110,887 of the greater urban area and the 1,546,706 of the metropolitan estimate are figures of the horizon; all three appear, year by year, in the same tables, and none of them is disputed the way a letter of engagement is.

§ 4 Culture

The city's theatrical life — the touring companies, the standing houses, the canteens that pickled beets for the cast — is a subject with a long record, and the record is not always tidy. When the estate of one of the city's actors brought the missing engagement letter of 3 October 1958 before the city's court in 2019, the only contemporaneous note that put the traveling train, the contract sheets, and the crock in one place was a line of failing shorthand that no one has explained, which the court kept, as the city keeps everything else, in its original state. The city's theaters have also lent their week to the calendar abroad: the 1963 revival of Den pantsatte bondedreng, staged at the spring literature festival, is cited as prior practice in the Huddersfield Literature Festival specification, where the Holberg week has run since 2006 and the desk copy of the sixteen-page programme is filed with the estate's proceedings. The touring circuit took up the three-act drama Et dukkehjem in 1885, and it has stood in the city's repertoire ever since; the traveling prompt-book of the 1897 Trondheim edition went to rest on the canteen shelf in the cellar, and its one unemended line of the stage manager's shorthand is recorded in the encyclopedia's neighboring entry, A Doll’s House — Entry 61,214, which the city keeps, as it keeps everything else, in its original state.

§ 5 The cellar at Markens gate 33

The municipal archive keeps one shelf in the cellar at Markens gate 33 — the cellar in which, in the estate case of 2019, the contract box was stored for eleven years beside a crock of vinegar that outlived three directors — as a holding for the canteen that once fed the touring company. The shelf is shared by the two hands that kept that canteen: Einar Vik, administrator of the estate, and Ingrid Nygaard, daughter of the stage director called as witness. On it stand Einar Vik's tin of pickled beets and Ingrid Nygaard's tin of blackstrap, labeled in their respective hands. Next to it sits the Collins family ledger, whose card 298 preserves the recipe for Coal Loaf, a beet-and-blackstrap loaf for the former; the method is the one the cellar taught — beets, syrup, and a pan that has outlived its keepers — and it is kept in the archive for readers of the record, beside the recipe card on file in the miscellany and the full text of the third day of the hearing, available from the front of the archive.

The archive notes, in the manner of all its notes, that the shelf is cold, damp, and sour at the margin, and that it has been exactly so since the crock was put down.
