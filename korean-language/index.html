Lost & Found

Notice No. 117/418 · opened 9 November, at the hammer, in Sale No. 117

Found: one language, Korean.

1 · Where it was found

Found loose from its canvas slip in Lot 418, The Haeju Papers, Sale No. 117 of Aldgate & Vane, in the message space of the undelivered telegram addressed in Roman letters to “H.H. Choi, c/o P.O. Box 7, Riga” — the one space on that telegram which was blank where it should not have been. The language is riding in the slip without weighing on it, which the counter notes is the only property by which one can tell it was never carried: a weight that arrives already light, and a paper that is not the heavier for it. The house struck the slip at two o’clock on 9 November; the language was taken off the counter at three, and this notice has been posted in the room since.

2 · Description of the item

One language, Korean, a Koreanic language, native to Korea, which makes up the majority of the language’s 81 million speakers. It is the official language of both North Korea and South Korea; in the south the language is known as Hangugeo (South Korean: 한국어), and in the north it is known as Chosŏnŏ (North Korean: 조선어), and both spellings arrive with the item, folded in, still arguing gently in the corner of the register. The language is written in the Hangul alphabet, all fourteen basic consonants and ten basic vowels present and squared away in their row. Since the turn of the 21st century the language has travelled on its own popular culture — films, music, the long export of the screen — around the world through globalization and cultural exports, and the counter can report that this part of it is the part other offices ask about first. Beyond Korea the language is recognized as a minority language in parts of China, namely Jilin, and specifically Yanbian Prefecture and Changbai County, where it keeps its own corner of the market square; it is also spoken by the Sakhalin Koreans in the northern fishing towns of the island, who have worked the same strait for two generations and still keep the old calendar on the boat.

3 · Condition report, as weighed at the counter

The language holds. The two spellings coexist without wear on either; the Hangul set is complete and unsmudged; the pop-culture skin is fresh, glossy, slightly warm to the touch, and peels back cleanly to the older dialects underneath, which are dry but serviceable. The Jilin and Changbai corners are faintly damp from the border river; the Sakhalin end carries a smell of cod smoke and lamp oil that the counter has declined to catalogue. Weighed in both palms on the counter scale the item reads fair; the counter clerk’s entry against it is four words and the office has let them stand: handled with two dry hands. No damp, no transfer, no mark.

4 · The line on the slip, transcribed word for word

A single line in pencil on the inside of the canvas slip, in a hand the office does not recognize, which the clerk is required by the counter rules to give exactly as it stands:

“Despatch! and get him to see if she have any daughters, by one of them, keeping the rooms are carpeted, she should not be; fair, handled with two dry hands.”

Read as a lodger’s agent’s instructions, the line works well enough to be worrying: send word to have him see whether she have any daughters, by one of them; the rooms to be kept carpeted; and whatever turns up in the rooms, it should not be — but fair, and handled with two dry hands. The office has no opinion who she is. No interpreter on the shift, Korean or otherwise, has claimed the whole line in one reading, and the clerk notes that the last four words of the instruction match the counter’s own condition entry for the language in section 3, which the office records without deciding which came first, the slip or the scale. The line is kept with the notice, uninterpreted.

5 · Claim

Claims on the item are anticipated in large numbers and of two kinds. South Korea may claim it as Hangugeo, with its seal, and is invited to refer the office to the 1966 federation minute, which it holds with the papers at Lot 418 and which the counter has already seen. North Korea may claim it as Chosŏnŏ, with its seal, and may be reminded that the counter, like the catalogue that opened this notice, takes no side of the line. The office will not parcel the item at the 38th parallel; it has tried, once, with another item, and the shelf remembers. The minority claimants — Yanbian Prefecture and Changbai County in Jilin, and the Sakhalin Koreans of the northern towns — may claim in kind, a market-day in the first case and a boat’s log in the second, and their corner of the item is marked in the register and set aside, damp or no damp, in their names. It is also on record that the item was found on the message side of a telegram that was never delivered, and the counter would be glad to know what it was meant to say, though it suspects the blank is the answer.

6 · If unclaimed

Unclaimed after six weeks, the item moves to the Minority Shelf, shelf 3, the damp end, where the office keeps the other unclaimed names until their true names surface — for one such, a pen name found loose from its script label, see the sister Lost & Found Notice No. 216/2016 of the Hibiya Ward Community Office, whose true name turned up folded inside a programme booklet, which is how these things do. A language of 81 million speakers would be a large thing to leave on a shelf, and the counter will log a protest to that effect on the day of transfer.

7 · Related notices, and the room

Anyone who sat the room on the day of the sale will know the canvas slip; the lot itself, its red belt and its paper aeroplane and its 1842 line, is consigned as Lot 418 in Sale No. 117, Papers of the Martial Arts. The language’s other appearances in the archive are on the air: in the 2YA radio play on Tennis New Zealand, the March home tie against Korea was one of the fixtures Miss Thompson struck into the calendar, and the visiting side crossed the Pacific speaking the very item in this notice, which makes the tie, at a distance, a home game for the shelf.
