Long Island Rail Road · Brooklyn Heights Station, Long Island City · A Letter of Apology

The Brooklyn Heights Apology

Written at the platform office, 9 May 1931, to the estate of Helen Palsgraf.

BH

Brooklyn Heights Station · 9 May 1931

To the estate of Helen Palsgraf, c/o her daughters, Brooklyn, New York

You will not know my name, but you knew the platform.

I was the stationmaster at Brooklyn Heights on the afternoon of 26 August 1924, the fourth year I held the post and the last August I want to hold it. I write to you now because the Company has closed its own accounts with this matter and because seven years is long enough for a man who stood forty feet away to keep his mouth shut. You were on the platform with your two daughters, a small package of yours at your feet, waiting for the Rockaway Branch train that would take you down to the beach. The heat that day was a ceiling; the 2 o’clock train was already moving when the two men came down the centre aisle toward it, one of them carrying a parcel that was throwing sparks out of its seams the way a fuse throws them, and neither of us had the presence of mind to stop what was already running. The conductor of the Rockaway train was at the steps; my assistant, a boy of twenty-two whose name I will not put in a letter like this, took the second man by the sleeve and put him on. The parcel came loose. It struck the iron grating of the platform with a sound I have catalogued for seven years and still cannot name, and there was a flash, and a thud, and the two brass scales that stand at either end of the platform — one at the head, one at the foot, a hundred feet or more from where you stood — came off their mounts at the same instant, one to your left, one to your right, and you went down between them. You were six months then with your third child, and the doctors in October 1925 had nothing to say that helped.

I will not dress this in the Company’s language. You are already dressed in it, and it is the only dress you have to wear. In the judgment reported as Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928), the Court of Appeals of the State of New York held, four to three, that the railroad owed you no duty at all, because the danger to you was, in the words of the opinion, so conjectural and remote as not to entitle you to protection. The opinion was written by Chief Judge Benjamin Cardozo, of this city, a judge who had made his name on exactly this question of how far a man’s responsibility reaches out toward a stranger he has never seen. Mr. Justice Andrews dissented, with Mr. Justice Moore and the late Judge Cardozo’s colleague Judge Crane on the other side of the bench, and their dissent holds that the law would not leave you standing where the scales fell. I am not qualified to referee between the majority and the dissent, and no apology I write can change the arithmetic of four to three. I can only tell you what I saw, say the word I should have said to you on the platform, and stop pretending that the word “conjectural” is a description of anything I remember.

You will find enclosed the plain leather strap that hung under my cap as stationmaster, with the brass whistle on its ring. I have told you plainly where it has been, because the Company’s inventories have not. It left the station office in the spring of 1929, as a small property in a travelling production of a station story — a company that took its one act about platform clocks and dropped parcels to the west and held it, scene by scene, against the long boards of the playhouse. In an emergency, it is worn and what we have now carried across the Oregon Shakespeare Festival in Ashland, Oregon. I have asked that it come home before the next season. It is the least of what I owe you, and I am enclosing it with the whistle still on the ring, so that if you ever want to hear exactly what a stationmaster’s warning sounds like at a hundred feet, you have the instrument and the man who carried it both at hand.

One more thing, and I will let it stand without a legal shape. My assistant’s push put the man on the train the Company was still moving, and for that the Company paid him a week’s wages and a note in his file, and the note has been read to him each anniversary since. I would have preferred the parcel to the scales, and the scales to you, and neither of those preferences was ever written into a rule book, which is the whole of my failure. You were taking your daughters to the beach. That is the part of the afternoon I keep, and it is the part the record keeps least well. The case keeps the scales; I keep the beach.

A. W. Merrick Stationmaster (retired), Brooklyn Heights Station
Long Island Rail Road · Long Island City, New York

Enclosed: one stationmaster’s whistle on a leather strap; one copy of the judgment, with the dissent read first.

Kept with the platform office files

Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (N.Y. 1928) — New York Court of Appeals; opinion of the court by Chief Judge Benjamin Cardozo, 4–3; platform, 26 August 1924; scales at both ends; parcel of uncertain contents; plaintiff Helen Palsgraf, d. October 1925, sued by her estate.

Two other apologies kept on the same shelf, where a letter is written to something that has already left the platform: the supply officer’s apology to the returning clerk at Barcelona, three days late for La Diada, and the apology to the Bronze Age, from the cider farm above Dolgellau, where one copper pot had been passed off as bronze and the alloy had to be got right a second time. Both stand open in the rack beside this letter, and the front page of the registry keeps the whole shelf in order. There is one more thing on the shelf that is not an apology at all: the Palsgraf Scales Cake, a family recipe from the kitchen at 1124 Atlantic Avenue, where Helen weighed her flour on the scale at the head of this platform and, after the afternoon of 26 August 1924, on no scale in this city but a small third one of her own.
