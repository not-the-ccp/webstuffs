27. Offered is a complete, and in our judgment unbroken, collection of records from Bell Creek Township, Burt County, Nebraska. Burt County, organized in 1871 around its county seat of Albion, was divided into twelve townships, and Bell Creek is one of them, taking its name from the sandy-bottomed Bell Creek that drops from the north through Section 14 and on to the Elkhorn in a course of roughly eleven miles. What follows in this lot is the township's own paper trail: how it met, how it counted, how it paved nothing, and how it once nearly lost a school roof to a book.

The lot comprises seven items, cased in the original walnut box. First, a bound volume of township board minutes, 1871–1903, in the hand of three successive clerks, the early entries kept under the supervision of A. J. Brundage, township supervisor, whose margin notes on drainage are famous among local readers. Second, the preliminary plat of the township by the surveyor W. H. Kettle, dated 1854, showing the creeks, the quarter-section lines, and a single hand-lettered star at the place the township would later call for. Third, the school assessment books of the Bell Creek one-room district, 1878–1916, every family's school levies ruled out in columns. Fourth, the road survey of 1936, kept under the work-relief program, tracing the gravel roads of the township in a neat red ink. Fifth, the church ledger of the congregation at Bell Creek, 1882–1948. Sixth, a bundle of correspondence concerning the 1899 schoolhouse, of which more presently. And seventh — the reason the lot draws the room — a copy of Moby-Dick; or, The Whale, in the binding of the American first issue, 1851, with a carpenter's pencil marks in the early chapters and a coffee ring, still pale, across the chapter on the whale's size.

The 1899 letters concern a dispute so small that it survives only because the clerk thought it was a good one. In the autumn the district's one-room schoolhouse shed its shingles over three winters' worth of wind, and the township's only man with a saw, a ladder, and the right temper for both was Jonas Fitch, who had in that summer taken possession of the whale novel and was, by the board's unanimous and undeniably true finding, “in the midst of it” — that is, in the whale, and not on the roof. Two summonses went to Fitch. Two Saturdays were missed. Then, in the clerk's entry for October 3, 1899, after the second failure, the board's whole position is given in a single line, in the clerk's hand, dry and unsparing, and plainly spoken aloud as it was written: “It is the whole roof depends on someone reading a whale novel.” The entry ends there. The roof was put in that November by a second carpenter from Albion, billed at four days and two dollars a day, and Fitch's copy of the novel — never returned — is the item in this lot, with its pencil marks and its ring, the physical record of the only school roof in Burt County history known to have lost a schedule to a whale.

For the current state of the township itself: Bell Creek remains one of the twelve townships of Burt County, and a small one still. The population was 228 at the 2020 census, and the 2021 estimate placed the township's population at 229 — one more than the count, which we note for the bidders as an example of the pace of change in the section. The plat of 1854 still describes the ground, creek and quarter line alike, which is rarer than the record should be.

Condition. The minutes volume shows foxing to the first eight pages and one repaired gutter, and is sound. The 1854 plat is in the original cloth folder, edges rubbed, the red survey lines bright. The whale novel is a reading copy and reads fine; the coffee ring is not a defect to be priced, in our view, but a part of the lot's story, and bidders are invited to treat it as such. The box is of quartered walnut, brass-lined, with the paper label “Bell Creek Twp — Records” on the lid.

Terms. The lot may not be divided. A buyer's premium of fifteen percent applies. Payment at the fall of the gavel, by certified check; the walnut box will be crated on the spot, and the buyer is welcome, but not required, to take the route by Albion that Kettle's star still marks.
