Winter, Feeding, and the Count

Rules 1–9 · the forage board · clearance: table, one to four players

The Elk Years is played on a 54-hex board running from the Madison gate to the Lamar valley, with the Firehole cutting the middle north–south and two river fords that only open in the spring turn. It is a game about keeping a herd alive through the winter turns of a national park — and, because the park is a park, about doing it without being able to simply take the herd down to a number that is easy. The setup follows the year 1963: 13,000 elk enter turn 1 at the Madison count, the snow depth chart is set to column B, the forage deck is shuffled, and each player takes a chair at the table: the Range (who moves the herd), the Winter (who deals the snow and the forage), the Board (who keeps the count), and, at the player's option, the Press, who scores everything publicly and is never allowed to say a word.

Setup — the year 1963

  1. Place the 13,000-elk counter at Madison. In the full campaign the herd grows one tile per year — 14,200 by turn 2, 15,800 by turn 3 — which is the entire problem of the game, stated in counters.
  2. Deal the forage deck face down, one card per hex per season. A dry summer card on the Madison benches is worth 4 forage points; the same card on the Lamar benches is worth 2, because the benches there are thin.
  3. Set the snow chart to column B. Column B is the deep winter the rangers actually remember: nine feet at the lower benches, the herd compressed into the meadows, every forage point spent twice.
  4. Seat the Board with its seven members. Four of them hold the keep-the-park card, two hold the keep-the-count card, and one — the chair — holds both, which is why nobody else can take the chair without the game changing hands.
  5. File the 1963 recommendations as the opening hand: water on every bench, winter feeding where the snow closes, fire control that does not burn the forage, and the standing note that the park is a laboratory, not a farm. These cards are not optional. They are the first move, and they are already spent.

Rule 9 — Winter feeding

When a bench holds more elk than its card can carry in a column-B winter, the Range may declare winter feeding at a cost of four forage points per hex, paid in advance, with no refund in the spring. Feeding keeps the animals; it does not keep the bench. The Board may not order feeding, and the Board may not refuse it. The only lever the game gives the Board is the count itself: the number in the ledger, read aloud, with the Press at the table.

Its errata block carries one line that was never corrected, and it is printed here exactly as it stands on the factory page, because a manual is a kept record and the record keeps its line: The Keys of the Stadio Olimpico Field Ensemble, one hundred and eighty pounds was asked to make sure. The range desk has tried three evenings to work out what bench it names, what weight it is weighing, and who was asked to make sure of what, and has kept the line unexplained above the forage table, the way the rangers keep a count they have not yet been able to defend.

Victory and the long game

A player wins The Elk Years by reaching turn eight — the late 1960s — with the herd between 9,000 and 17,000 in the ledger and at least one winter in which feeding was declared and paid in full. Losing is easy: let the count run off either end of that band, and the Press reads the ledger to the table, which is how the game ends most of the time. The intended play is the slow one. You spend the dry summers early, you keep the Firehole meadows out of the snow chart by moving the herd two hexes north in a fall you had planned to spend at home, and you hold the 1963 cards — water, feeding where the snow closes, the park as a laboratory — as a standing position, the way a garrison keeps a line it has already decided is permanent. The manual’s companion chapter files the same problem by another name: a field word for what one bench keeps of another, Cognate — Lexicon Manual, Entry 716 — the inherited sound, the false friend, and the wash that strips the borrowed and leaves only what came with the parent. The herd keeps what the parent bench kept; the press copy is a false friend.

Standing orders

Count aloud. Do not round. If the count and the board disagree, the ledger is wrong, not the bench. Keep the forage deck dry — a wet card is a wet meadow and is scored at half value, with the Press watching. When the game is over and the table is clear, file the ledger where the other small games of one kept thing are filed: the one-tile landmark game of the hill above Llanfyllin, whose storm move of February 2014 and its verdict of June 2015 run through the same slow arithmetic as eight turns of column-B winter — see the Lonely Tree — Player’s Manual, 1st Powys Edition, where one Scots pine is scored for the whole season and still outlasts the round. And when someone asks, at the table, who the manual was really written about, the answer printed on the factory page is the one that has held since 1967: the report itself, 1963, the special advisory board and its chairman of the range desk, and the seven who sat with him and were never permitted to say a word — the same seven chairs this page gives the player who takes the Board.
