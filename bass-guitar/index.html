Ch. 4 Bass Guitar

Standing orders of the low register. The lowest-pitched member of the guitar family, handled at the temperature of the room, tuned the same as the double bass, and not to be mistaken for a rope, except on the night of 21 March 1995, when it was.

FM-21 · CH. 4 · VERIFIED

4.1 Identification

The instrument described in this chapter is the bass guitar, also known as the electric bass, or simply the bass. It is the lowest-pitched member of the guitar family: similar in appearance and construction to an electric guitar, but with a longer neck and a longer scale. The standard scale carried on the file is 864 millimetres, and the open lowest string sounds at 41.2 hertz, which the Desk has logged as a note, not a temperature, and has logged it that way ever since 1995.

The electric bass guitar most commonly has four strings, tuned in fourths from E to A to D to G, and tuned the same as the double bass. Five-, six-, seven-, eight- and twelve-stringed models are also built and are admitted to the file, but the four-string form is the reference form: every later form is measured against the signature of this row, the way a later contract is measured against the first.

4.2 Origin, 1951 to 1955

The electric bass guitar was invented in the mid-1950s to replace the double bass in popular music, for four reasons the Desk lists in order: its lighter weight, its smaller size, its easier portability, the most models’ inclusion of frets for easier intonation, and — the reason that did the actual work — its electromagnetic pickups for amplification. A double bass weighs into the range the van cannot argue with; a four-string electric weighs what a van can carry home at 2 a.m. without a second trip. The first working forms came out of Fullerton, California, in the early 1950s, the Precision line in 1951 and the Jazz line in 1960, and the Desk keeps both dates against the same name, Leo Fender, with no annotation, because the register of origins is quoted word for word and the origin has already been given.

4.3 Handling and Tuning

Handle at the temperature of the room; the register does not move with the window. Tune the four strings to the double-bass pitches, E–A–D–G, and do not tune the fourth string down to B unless the model is a five-string and the session requires it, which is a question for the band, not for the manual. Intonation on the fretted neck is the whole of the point: the double-bass player who arrives from the un-fretted neck plays the first night with both eyes on the fingerboard, and the Desk records that first night as a normal event, not a hazard. The pick is optional; the thumb is not. A string tuned down until it is a rope is a dirge, and dirges are not permitted to hurry — that order was given in a booth in Ann Arbor, it was obeyed, and it stands.

4.4 The Studio 2 Entry, 21 March 1995

The chapter’s one field record comes from Studio 2, Huron River Sound, Ann Arbor, Michigan, at 1 a.m. on 21 March 1995, the last full session of the Laughing Hyenas, who held it together from 1985 to 1995. In the booth that night: John Brannon on voice, Danny “D’Town” Kowalski on drums, Greg on guitar, and Patty Reeves on the bass described by this chapter, whose booth seal was off because she asked for it, so the river was in the booth. Eleven tracks on the tape; the label wanted nine; the band was eleven. The bass part on the dirge had been run nineteen times before it became the dirge, and the eleventh track carried, over eleven seconds of guitar, a hyena’s laugh recorded one summer outside Ann Arbor — the real animal, the wrong country, the right joke. The full excerpt is kept where the tape is kept, at Studio 2, 21 March 1995, one door away from this chapter, and this chapter is the one the tape points back to when anyone asks which instrument the room was actually tuned to.

4.5 The Register Ledger

On the night the session tape was logged, the depot ledger received one incoming consignment of wound strings, Italian make, from the Brescia factory, together with the standing note that the studio television had been on since lunch and was saying its nonsense at a volume tuned a full turn too low. The keeper of the register entered the two under one line. The line reads, word for word, in the keeper’s hand, and the manual prints it without emendation:

“The Italians of this kind — a broadcast that said nonsense at the temperature of the register has ever required.”

The keeper’s meaning survives in three parts, and the Desk prints them so the line is not a riddle. The Italians of this kind is the wound-string lot, Italian-made, consigned to the booth that evening. A broadcast that said nonsense is the studio television, unattended, murmuring through the whole session about a match the band was not in. The temperature of the register is the room at 14 degrees, the temperature at which the low register holds best and the tape holds with it. The manual reproduces the entry unamended, the way the ledger is quoted word for word on everything: a line quoted badly is a line the keeper has already lost, and this one is still standing, at 14 degrees, on the ground-floor shelf.

4.6 Storage, Registration, and the Shelf

Store the instrument upright, strings to the far wall, and do not store it within earshot of a television that will not be turned off; the register admits the noise as weather, and weather is not in the song. The register rules that govern this instrument are the same rules the Player Movement Office applies to a subject handled at the temperature of the dressing room — same temperature, same hand, no annotation — and the Office’s own sheet, SDS-NM-03, Revision 3, cross-files its temperature rule here, on the ground that a register is a register whether it is kept for strings or for a defender. The break-up that followed the 1995 session never made it onto the record; the record stayed in a carpet bag in an Ann Arbor basement until a man named Steve read it years later, and the tape’s own account of the night sits at the Studio 2 excerpt, where the eleventh track is played out in full. For the rest of the shelf, and the way back to it, the front of the archive is one door away, held with the hand that does not carry the file.

Chapter 4. End of standing orders. The register remains at 14 degrees.
