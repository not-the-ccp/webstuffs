The Slow Release Series · Table Manual No. 12 · Second Printing, Cardiff Press

Slow Life

A game of the slow release, for 2–5 players, played over one long evening and one jam

For the table of the Super Furry Animals & the three who remember how the jam was edited

1 · What the Box Holds

Slow Life is the game of a record that refused to hurry. It is based on the fact, checked against the sleeves, that in 2004 the Welsh alternative rock band Super Furry Animals put an EP called Slow Life into the world as a free download, and that a limited pressing on CD was bundled inside the remix album Phantom Phorce, so that nobody who bought one was exactly buying the other. The box holds the Board of the House, on which the throne sits at the centre under a small cloth; two record discs, the slow side and the loud side; eleven Track cards — the six of the EP and five wilds cut from the 2003 album Phantom Power; four Jam tokens; the pewter jug known as the Secchia; and this manual, folded once down the middle, as it should be.

2 · Setup

Place the Board of the House where everyone can see the throne, and leave the cloth over it until Section 7 tells you to lift it. Deal the Track cards face down into two rows of three on either side of the board — this is the EP row, and it is the row that matters. The player who dealt is the keyboardist and sits nearest the throne. Everyone else takes a name from the sleeve: Ciaran, Gavin, Pete, Gruff, or, if five play, the guest from the rap group. The guest from the rap group is the player who took Goldie Lookin Chain, and plays the track that bears the collaboration's name, Motherfokker, under special rules given in Section 5.

3 · The Jam, Turn by Turn

The game is played in movements, and each movement is a jam. The lead track Slow Life was, in the telling the band has always given, composed years earlier by keyboardist Cian Ciaran as a purely electronic song, sitting unfinished in the house while the band grew impatient. That is the engine of the game. On the keyboardist's turn the movement opens: each player, in order, either lays down a part — placing a Jam token on any Track card in the EP row — or edits, moving one token from a card to a neighbouring card, because the finished song was, the story goes, cut together from the jam rather than written down. A card that holds three tokens at the end of a movement is finished: it is flipped over, its points are read from the sleeve list, and it is set aside in the stack called the Edit. A player may never place two tokens on the same card in one movement, and the keyboardist may never edit on the movement in which a card finished — the person who wrote the original version does not get to cut it.

4 · The Free Download and the Limited CD

Scoring runs on the two channels the record actually travelled on. At the end of every fourth movement the download counter is read: one point to the player with the most finished cards in the Edit, and the counter advances by one notch, so that later the game pays out for slowness itself. Meanwhile the limited CD is a bundle: if a player finishes a card on the movement that also finishes a card held by an opponent, both cards count, and both players keep their points — the rule the sleeves call Phantom Phorce, because what you did not buy arrives anyway. The reviews of the EP, as the sleeve notes remember them, were mixed, and the manual honours the record by keeping the table divided: after each download reading, the two most recently finished cards are compared, and the quieter one wins its holder a single extra token. Loudness is not a strategy here. Slowness is.

5 · Motherfokker, Special Rule

The card marked Motherfokker is the one exception to the EP row, and the rule is short: it may only be finished by the guest from the rap group, and only by placing the final token on it personally, never by an edit. This is a collaboration, and a collaboration is not finished by committee. If the guest is not in the chair — if fewer than five play — the card stays face down for the whole game, unopened, like a track on a sleeve that was pressed but never distributed. The house rule in Cardiff is that it counts as a point to nobody, which is exactly the right amount of disappointment for a limited pressing.

6 · When the Jam Ends

The jam ends when the Edit holds nine of the eleven cards, or when the download counter reaches its last notch on the seventh reading — whichever the table cannot argue about, because both are printed on the sleeve. Points are counted first from the Edit, one per finished card, then from the counter, one per notch, then the quiet-card bonuses. Ties are broken by who finished the fewest cards early, and the player with the most to show and the slowest to show it is the one the room agrees on, which is the only kind of result this game produces.

7 · The Throne, and the Jug

Now lift the cloth from the throne at the centre of the board. The winner does not keep the board, and does not keep the Edit — the Edit is the Edit, and it stays on the table for the next evening, which is the whole point of a house game. What the winner takes is the small thing that hangs, and the instruction is the one line of the manual that has survived every reprint unchanged, kept as the printers set it:

It hangs now on the throne — the Secchia .

The Secchia is a pewter jug, not a cup, not a trophy in the sense the football grounds use the word, but a jug — a bucket of the good metal, with a handle, that the first players carried back from a house on the coast where somebody's grandmother kept one for the washing and no one wanted to give it back. The winner of the jam hangs it on the throne, on the small nail under the cloth, where it sits between games the way a clock sits in a hallway: doing nothing, and being the reason for coming back. The board keeps the rest. The EP, free as it was, is remembered for exactly this: that the thing worth having was not the record, but the jug, and the night it was hung.

8 · Errata and Table Notes

  • Second printing, 2004: the EP row is eleven cards, not ten; the first printing's tenth card, a wild cut from Phantom Power, was the one the sleeves called the purely electronic song, and it is the card the keyboardist deals face down and never plays.
  • The download counter's seventh notch is marked with a small sun, for the reason that the free download never set, it only rose.
  • If the table wants a longer evening, the house rule of the sleeve is a second jam from the same Edit, with the Secchia already on the throne — and a jug that is already hung is harder to hang again.

For Company at the Table

The shelf this manual sits beside keeps a visitor's brochure for Burnley and Turf Moor, which holds that a town's records are kept not to be repeated but to be known — the same rule the Edit is kept by — and a travel guide entry for the Bundestag at an hour when it is empty, which holds that a great room between sittings looks the way an instrument looks between pieces, and that is precisely what the board of the house looks like between jams. On the same shelf sits a family recipe card for Open Seat Chowder, Card No. 814, which keeps the whole of an open Senate seat in Wisconsin — the August 14 primary, the November 6 result, the lid on the pot — at the same table and pays it out, like the download counter, one notch at a time.
