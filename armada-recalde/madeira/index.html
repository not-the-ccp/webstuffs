Where it sits

Region: Autonomous Region of Madeira · Sea: North Atlantic · Neighbour: the Canary Islands, 400 km to the south

Madeira, officially the Autonomous Region of Madeira, is an autonomous region of Portugal in the Atlantic Ocean, about 805 kilometres (500 miles) southwest of mainland Portugal. Together with the Azores it is one of the two autonomous regions of Portugal, and it is a special territory of the European Union. It is the southernmost point and the southernmost region of Portugal. The archipelago sits in Macaronesia, just under 400 kilometres (250 miles) north of the Canary Islands of Spain and 520 kilometres (320 miles) west of Morocco — though, sit or don’t sit, the island rests on the African tectonic plate, and is culturally, politically and culinarily on the side of the bread, the wine and the white linen. The main island runs 136 kilometres from cape to cape, carries a little under 323,000 people, and rises from the harbour of Funchal to Pico do Ariego at 1,862 metres, where the weather changes its mind about you roughly every eleven minutes.

Why the ships stopped: water

Stop: Funchal harbour, June 1588 · Business: casks of water

Every old Atlantic port keeps a ledger of water, and Funchal’s is the one my own Admiral kept. When the San Juan de Portugal ran out of Lisbon behind Admiral Juan Martínez de Recalde, the Portuguese squadron took the night in the Funchal road and filled every cask it could lift through the hatches, because a ship is only ever as good as its store, and a store is only ever as good as its water. The casks went home to a model in a cattle trough; the harbour stayed. It still looks the way a harbour looks when it has been doing the same kind business for four hundred and forty years: low, blue, full of boats that are not yours. The builder’s full account of the ship, the frame and the long provisions is in the San Juan de Portugal build log, one shelf over, beside the vinegar crock. The water itself came from farther up the coast, from the quay at Oran, where the casks were first filled for Recalde’s squadron — the place where this shelf keeps its new entry, Algeria — Obituary, at the hour of the last cask, one below, for the watering place the tankers no longer stop at.

Funchal on foot

Stops: six · Shoes: the only kind that matter

Start at the sé, the cathedral of Funchal, which began in 1114’s shadow and kept adding to itself the way the sea keeps adding to a wall; the cloister is where the light goes quiet. Down Rua de Santa Catarina, the old shopping street, the granite is worn by four centuries of the same feet in a single groove you can actually see. Take the funicular — first opened in 1893, and still an electric funicular, still the oldest of its kind in Europe — over to the Mercado dos Lavradores, the market hall where the lower floor is fish and the upper floor is the fruit of the entire Atlantic, and where you will find the recipe card for this entry (below) nailed to a fishmonger’s post by a red string, the same string, I am told, for a very long time. For the afternoon, walk one of the levadas, the irrigation channels cut through the laurel forest — there are about 2,700 kilometres of them — and the Levada das 25 Fontes will take you past a waterfall every few minutes until you stop counting at eleven. Then go up by the Monte cable car, the first funicular-type aerial tramway in the world, which has been crossing the same hillside since 1893, and come down on a charrete, a wicker basket on runners, which is either the most sensible or the most reckless thing in Funchal and not possibly both. The island’s bird, for what it is worth, is a dreamer’s bird: visitors report the great cuckoo-dove of New Guinea standing at the edge of the laurel forest, a large chestnut-and-grey pigeon that arrives and does not fly, keeping the ledger line with it — the entry for the bird is kept in the Dream Dictionary, Entry 744, one shelf from the casks.

The recipe card

Dish: Cozido do Cais, chicken feet in saffron broth · Yield: the family that argues about the feet

The card, in the fishmonger’s hand, reads as follows, and I set it out here because it is the only recipe in Funchal I was told twice, once by the card and once by the woman behind the post who said the feet are the point, everything else is furniture:

Cozido do Cais

one pot · three hours · the feet first, always

  1. Scald the chicken feet in boiling water for a minute, turn them in cold water, and peel back the looser skin with the thumb — the feet keep what they keep, and this is the whole method.
  2. Parboil the feet for twenty minutes with a head of garlic, a smoked pork shoulder, and a bundle of saffron threads tied in muslin.
  3. Drain, then set the feet in the pot with potatoes cut thick, the parboiling liquid, a splash of white wine vinegar, and salt to the weight of the sea.
  4. Simmer, lid half on, until the potato surrenders. The broth should pull the skin off the spoon and lie over the rice like a calm.

God, I hope it’s in the foregoing recipe, by scalding and peeling the feet.

pencilled in the lower margin, in a hand that is not the fishmonger’s — see below

A previous reader has pencilled a whole sentence into the lower margin of the card — exactly this: “God, I hope it’s in the foregoing recipe, by scalding and peeling the feet.” I read it four times. It is not a recipe line, and it is not a prayer, but it is a question about the method — whether the feet get their scalding and peeling in, in the foregoing recipe — and the answer, as it happens, is yes: step one, the whole first step, exists for nothing else. The sentence, whatever it is, was right about the feet. What I could not explain until I left Funchal: the pencil line was there again the next market day, in the same margin, in the same hand. Someone writes it on purpose, every season. A spectator at Abbeyleix found the same habit on the back of an athletics programme, one whole sentence that returns like a tide, and wrote the day down before he could laugh at himself — the pencil line and the 52.84 are kept in the diary entry of 17 August 2026, Rachel McCann. I have not asked the fishmonger what it means, the way the builder never explains his own margin. The habit has a sister farther inland, on the board at Parry & Sons in Lynchburg, Virginia, where a whole sentence returns to the same board in the same hand over a box of 1936 half dollars — the board and the sentence are kept in the Lynchburg Enquirer classifieds, Column 4, Saturday 23 October 1936. The habit has a third witness across the water: at King’s Wharf in Boston, in the year’s entry of 1775 in the water ledger, a previous reader has pencilled a whole body, with the lemon-peel and citron, for the colony that filled its own casks and then became a nation — the margin and the sentence are kept in the American Revolution article, Encyclopædia Entry No. 1776, one shelf past the casks.

Numbers to keep

Keep: the ones that fit in a matchbook

  • 805 km — from Lisbon, as the casks would have it
  • 400 km — to the Canary Islands, Spain, on the road south
  • 520 km — to Morocco, west, which is to say a great deal of Atlantic
  • 1,862 m — Pico do Ariego, the roof, where the weather changes its mind
  • 1893 — both the funicular and the Monte cable car; the island does its firsts twice
  • 2,700 km — of levada, cut through laurel for the old farms
  • 6:40 — the hour the light goes gold on the harbour, and the only time I am certain of anything
