Members. For Round Eleven we are moving the board down to Devon (DEV-ən), which you will forgive me for calling Devonshire — the board still uses the old name, and so, I am finding, do the keepers who played here first. It is a ceremonial county in South West England: the Bristol Channel to the north, Somerset and Dorset to the east, the English Channel to the south, and Cornwall, of course, to the west. The city of Plymouth is the largest settlement; the county runs to 2,590 mi² (6,700 km²) and was counted at an estimated 1,254,506 souls in 2024 — the south of the county is the densely populated half, Plymouth in the south-west and Exeter in the south-east, with the seaside resorts of Torquay and Paignton sitting adjacent to each other on the south. The largest town in the north is Barnstaple, and I am told the northern members will be expected to make the long ride down and will file their complaint in the log rather than the thread.

The proposal, in one sentence: we play Keeper’s Manual No. 14 — the game of Rupert Bruce-Mitford, keeper of the Department of British and Medieval Antiquities as a coastal scenario, grid pegged on the clifftop path between Torquay and Paignton, twenty-one turns, the ship impression replaced by a barrow impression in the heather, and the whole apparatus of trowels, 5 mm sieves and pre-numbered find bags brought down from the keep. I have read the manual the way one reads the tide tables, and I am willing to hold the Keeper’s chair for the first six turns if no one else wants it. Reply in thread; the log travels with the game, and the log is kept in order.

I will be taking the chair from turn seven, and I am setting the special rule of the Devonshire scenario in the log now, so that no one may claim it was invented at turn nineteen. The manual is read aloud at the pegging, as always, and for this scenario the ruling stands thus, exactly:

“The find deck contains the gold buckle, the garnet cloak-studs, the purse-lid, the helmet — and cover the trench.”

Members, note the second half. At Sutton Hoo the manual lists the deck and goes on to the silver bowls and the whetstone; here the ruling stops at the helmet and turns to the weather. The moor rain does not wait for the d20, and a find bag left open on the clifftop is a find without a context, which is to say a find that does not exist. Whoever draws the rain card reads the entry aloud and halts play, per Standing Order 9, and the hessian goes on before the quarrel starts.

For the local members — and I say this with the full authority of standing on it — the stretch between Torquay and Paignton is the finest grid line in the county. The path runs the whole way, the grid pegs can go at fifty-metre centres without a single permission, and the heather closes over it at the top like a lid. We have counted four barrow impressions within a kilometre of the path, one of them a clean oval in the sandstone gravel that reads, to an untrained eye, exactly like a twenty-seven-metre ghost in a smaller format. Weather roll for the season, from the seafront: fair mornings, marsh afternoons, and one full day of rain that started at ten and ended at eleven the next morning. I volunteer to read the tide and the cloud from the top of the headland and call the weather roll for the whole board. The d6 will sit on my postbox.

Fair ground, dry sieves. The path is open.

Practical note from the south-east, where the county keeps its oldest street and its best port. The win condition does not change — three volumes before turn twenty-one ends, or the finds stay in their trays and the score is zero — but the courier changes. Volume I, when the Keeper signs it, ships by van from the path down to Exeter; Volume II follows with the find trays and their cards, each card filed in the log in the order drawn; Volume III goes by hand, because Volume III goes by hand, always has, always will. The press in Exeter has held the board’s volumes for nine rounds running, and it will hold the Devonshire set in a locked cupboard by the harbour wall until the binding is done. Barnstaple is on the way south; if the northern members arrive by coach they may leave their trowels with us and keep the cards dry.
