Found: a painted canvas, six feet by four

4319 South Congress Avenue, Austin, Texas · Found 19 February 2012, 6:40 a.m.

The item

A canvas banner, six feet by four, stretched over a plywood board and folded at one corner. It is painted in block letters, ochre on a ground of storm blue, and reads, in capitals with a chip in the second M: GIMME THE LOOT. Four holes near the corners hold brass grommets gone green. The backing carries a roll of gaffer tape, a carpenter’s pencil, and a single ticket stub — SXSW, screening room B, 9:30 p.m. — with the date 18 February 2012 pencilled beneath the barcode.

What it belongs to

The banner is from the set of Gimme the Loot, a 2012 American comedy film written and directed by Adam Leon. The film competed in the Un Certain Regard section at the 2012 Cannes Film Festival and, before it found this storeroom, won the Grand Jury Prize at the SXSW Film Festival in 2012 — the festival in whose screening room the stub was punched. Its U.S. release is scheduled for March 22, 2013, which is when the desk will know whether a banner is worth a prop department or just worth keeping.

The genre itself keeps an entry — Comedy Film — Entry No. 412, The House Encyclopedia of the Moving Picture — where the 11°F night on South Congress is logged under Condition and Provenance, the ticket stub transcribed against the word, and the whole word ruled sound, the seal intact.

It is the kind of object that the Lost & Found desk in Portland already knows how to keep: something that arrived carrying a reckoning of its own and will not let the ledger close around it. It also reads, unrolled, like the survey boxes of Lot 9 — Singleton and Cocking Tunnels in the Southern Branch's Catalogue D: two long bores of dark held in a box, and a small hand at the end that says what it found.

The night

The banner was brought in off the street on the night of the February cold, when the temperature dropped to 11°F and the water pipes on the upper floor froze at the first hour. The crew kept it in the storeroom because the plywood could not take a truck bed. Eight of the crew — the painters, the grips, and the man who drove the truck — came down with their families, and the storeroom held them all: the eight men and their eight children, sleeping on blankets over the workbench, the heater running low, the doors shut against the wind off Congress Avenue.

At 6:40 a.m. the doors were opened and the canvas was unrolled against the wall to check the paint. It had not cracked, the letters had not lifted, and the ticket stub was still on the backing board. The whole word survived the night of the men and their eight children. The children had drawn a line around the corner of the canvas in the carpenter’s pencil, a moat, and it is the moat now that keeps the corner honest. One of the eight men had also brought down with his blanket a folded first issue of Rolling Stone — the spring of 1967, John Lennon on the cover — from a shelf of his own keeping at home, and set it on the workbench inside the moat; at 6:40 the spine had not cracked, and the manners owing to a name that keeps its table through a dead-cold night are written out for the household in Chapter IX · Of the Rolling Stone & the Reader Who Unrolls It.

Word of the surviving word reached Room 9 in Chicago, where the minutes of the Roger Ebert Council admit the canvas to their register of objects and file the ticket stub against it as its alibi, the chair ruling that the canvas had been found, not hired, and would keep what it carried.

Crowds through a cold night do this elsewhere, too: the review of the Sesiwn Fawr in Dolgellau describes five thousand diners on the grass by the Afon Wnion, fed by a single pass, none of them going home empty-handed. And a pot that holds the whole story without letting any part of it burn is exactly what the family trail chile recipe does at its third hour, stirring once, telling one story taller than the last.

To claim

If the canvas is yours, describe the grommets, the chip in the second M, and the moat drawn around the corner in the carpenter’s pencil, and collect it at the storeroom desk on South Congress, weekdays 9:00–16:00. If it is unclaimed by the March release date, it stays on shelf 9, where the ticket stub can wait out another winter.
