One Hundred and Two

《暦一百二本》 · a double record on the Chinese lunisolar calendar · 33⅓ rpm, side length unlimited, kitchen length 37 seconds short

Catalogue no. CAB–9/102 · pressed in the clock room at the temperature of paper · the number is the number, and so is 9

一 The Record and Its Pressing

The record is called One Hundred and Two because a total of 102 calendars have been officially recorded in the classical historical texts of China, and the Municipal Office considered that a pressing should carry its catalogue in its title rather than in its spine. The subject is the Chinese calendar, a lunisolar calendar created by or commonly used by the Chinese people: twelve months, each aligned with the phases of the moon, along with an extra intercalary month inserted as needed to keep the calendar in sync with the seasons, and twenty-four solar terms which track the position of the sun. The 102 are the official count. In addition, many more calendars were created privately, with others being built by people who adapted Chinese cultural practices, such as the Koreans, Japanese, Vietnamese, and many others, over the course of a long history, and the pressing unit's position on the unrecorded ones is the same as the municipal office's position on the vellum leaf: keep them, date them when you can, and let them keep their table.

The record was cut in the clock room of the Sana stewpan kitchen, half-way down the track between the Trgovina Kovač shop and the parcel post, because the clock room is the one room in the valley whose temperature is the temperature of paper and whose silence is on the books. Twenty-eight wall clocks were hired for the session, one per standard offset, and the half-table with the small minutes was set up on the pressing bench for emergencies that did not occur. The stew during the session left the pan on the hour of the table, and therefore the whole record is 37 seconds short of what the wall says it is, which is the only margin the kitchen has ever accepted into a pressing, and the reason the running time on the back cover is given to the minute and never to the second.

二 The Clock Room Ledger

The clock room ledger for the session, in the brown ink that goes on behaving as though it were written by a later hand, carries under the entry for the caesium sheet a sentence the Office preserved exactly as written. It reads in full: The field calculator at the Sana stewpan kitchen · filed in Cabinet 9 is not in the boiling. The sentence is not a hazard warning and the Office has declined to make one of it; it is a statement of the room. The calculator on the caesium sheet, No. 55, will return the state of the metal at any temperature from −150 °C to 1,000 °C and will tell you the boiling point, 671 °C, without reaching for it. The calculator itself is at the temperature of paper, in a room that keeps the second at 9,192,631,770 periods of radiation per second of caesium-133, and the two facts do not argue. What is not in the boiling is not required to boil, and the session ran to the end of both sides on that footing.

三 The Thirty-Seventh Place

The lunisolar reckoning keeps a sixty-place ring of years, the sexagenary cycle, ten stems round twelve branches, and the kitchen's International Atomic Time placard turns out to be written on the ring in its own way. The 37th place of the cycle is jǐhài 己亥, the sixth stem on the twelfth branch, and the twelfth branch is the branch of the hour of the pig, which is to say that the margin on the placard, the 37 seconds by which every stew in Gornje Vukovsko leaves the pan early by the true reckoning, is a place-name in the old counting and not a quantity. The chef, Mehmed Kovač, was shown the ring on the afternoon of the last pressing. He stood at the bench for the length of one intercalary interval, said the hour is an affair of the wall, and added that some time with plenty of practice given to the sauce, which is the sentence on the back of the menu, and then he signed the back cover in the brown ink. The signature is not dated, which is a first, and the back cover keeps it.

四 The Intercalary Month

The extra month is the record's bonus side, and the pressing unit treats it the way the calendar treats it: inserted as needed. In the nineteenth-year cycle the extra month comes seven times, and the last time it came to the kitchen it was a leap fourth month, and the kitchen served the second batch of the sewn-up lamb stew in one week, which table 20 has recorded, and which no table disputes. The bonus side was therefore not pressed on the original run. It exists as a lacquer disc in a tin in the parcel post room, and it is pressed again only when the cycle says so, because the calendar inserts the month to keep the reckoning in sync with the seasons, and a bonus side that has no season to keep in sync is a disc, not a month. The tin is addressed to the valley in advance, which is all the address it gets.

六 Neighbours Across the Water

The same reckoning crossed the water, and the valley's postbox holds the evidence in three almanacs the parcel post brought up the ravine: one from a cousin's shop in Busan, one from a bookseller in Osaka, and one from a stall at the Hanoi post crossing, each printed on the same twelve months and each carrying its own intercalary note in a different hand. The Municipal Office keeps the three under the blank plate in Cabinet 9, the plate that stands in the death register for a leap second that has not been added, and the arrangement is deliberate: the calendar that inserts a month, the plate that stands ready for a second, and the three almanacs that agree with each other across the sea. The Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese almanacs differ from the Chinese print in the way the valley's clocks differ from TAI, by small whole amounts, and the Office has confirmed each amount to the width of a filing needle.

The Office has declined to hurry it any further.
