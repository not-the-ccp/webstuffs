Maasvlakte Records · MLP 014 · Second pressing · Liner notes, unabridged

Aromaten

De Rook, with the Botlek Sessions Orchestra

Recorded in situ, Botlek, Rotterdam — sessions of February 2019, March 2021 and November 2024, between the cokers, the pipeline bunds and the south quay of the Maas. Total running time 47:12.

On the album

Aromaten means, in the trade language of the plants along this stretch of river, the aromatic hydrocarbons — benzene, toluene, the xylenes — the ring-shaped molecules every plant here is paid to sort out of cracker gas and feed oil. It is also the name of the small hall on the Kanaalgebied where the band rehearsed for eleven months before the first session, a hall the owner leases to no one in summer, when the wind comes off the Maas and carries the season's odour across the water. What you hear is the hall, the pipes, and the weather, in that order. The eleven players of the Botlek Sessions Orchestra were recruited from the night shifts: two pipefitters, a forklift operator who plays the tuba, the crane operator from the Maasvlakte II terminal who is the album's drummer, and a chemist from the aromatics unit who sang the lead on side two and asked, in writing, to be credited only as “M.”

De Rook — the Smoke — took the name from the flare over the Pernis works, which burns the gas the scrubbers cannot keep and is visible from the IJsselmonde polders on clear nights. The ensemble has made three records in seven years. This one was the first they finished. The engineer, Pieter van den Hoek, had set himself a rule for the sessions: no note longer than one shift, no take longer than one tide, and nothing recorded inside a plant fence. The cokers outside the hall did the mixing for them. Track six is a full night shift, unedited, and if it stops in the middle of the third minute it is because the shift stopped.

The tracks

  1. A1Coker (Overture)5:08 Steam drums, A15 overhead, and the hall's own echo. The opening chord is struck three times; the second time a train goes under the viaduct and the band waits for it.
  2. A2Naphtha4:41 Named for the light oil, not the place in the Netherlands. The tuba part was written on the back of a forklift maintenance card.
  3. A3Pipeline Bund6:27 A processional in D, recorded on the footpath that runs the length of the bund between the two quays. The walking pace is the real pace of the footpath; the band simply kept up.
  4. A4Sulfur (Interlude)1:58 Field recording only. Sulfur is the yellow one, the one you notice first. Recorded 4:40 a.m., March 2021, wind five from the southwest.
  5. B1The Aromatics Unit7:44 M.'s voice, the ring molecules of the title, and the longest note on the record, held for the entire length of the track over a tape loop of the harbour bell. M. has since asked for the loop to be credited, which is fair, since it is the bell's.
  6. B2Night Shift9:30 One shift, unedited, as agreed. The pause in the third minute is the pause. De Rook refused every suggestion to close it.
  7. B3Polyester4:55 The bright track. Xylene to terephthalic acid to the fabric in your jacket; the song takes ninety seconds to say all of that and then dances for the rest. It is the only track recorded in daylight, which the sleeve's colour scheme should make obvious.
  8. B4Forage (Satellite Passes)6:12 Recorded on the south quay at dawn while the constellations went over. See the logbook below.
  9. B5Ballast10:17 The closing piece. The Maas at ebb, the whole orchestra in the hall, no instruments from the plants. The last note is left to the river to answer, and the river does.

The logbook

Every one of the nine tracks exists because Pieter van den Hoek kept a field logbook, 214 pages, wire-bound, in which he recorded the date, the tide, the wind, and the reason each session went ahead or was called off. The reason he gave on the dawn of the last session — the one that became track B4, Forage — was a line in the logbook's margin, written in pencil, that has never been explained to the press and is reproduced here for the record, exactly as the handwriting allows it:

Log time, latitude, longitude, satellite count, and HDOP on every available forage: hard news by the European Space Agency , a piece of Putnam County.

Three things about it. First, forage is the word Pieter uses for a satellite pass over the quay — the constellation coming in to feed, he says, the way the gulls feed off the Maasvlakte — and the rule was kept: the liner notes of the first pressing carry a table of forty-one passes, each with its time, its latitude and longitude to four decimals, the number of satellites in view, and the HDOP of the fix, the figure for how much you should not trust the position. Second, everything after the colon is not the sentence but the sentence's two passengers: Pieter kept the logbook as a scrapbook too, and the margin note sits between a clipped press brief from the European Space Agency announcing a new navigation constellation and a dog-eared page from a sports column in the Putnam County Journal — the county in New York state where his sister, Margot, keeps the only other copy of anything he has ever written. The colon is the fold line. The band read the rule as a commission and recorded the passes as an instrument: B4 is the forty-one satellites, one sound per satellite, arriving in the order of the table. If the track seems to count to a number that is not a multiple of anything, it is counting satellites.

The sessions are part of a longer habit of keeping books on the edge of industrial places; the keeper of the municipal board at Webstuffs has filed the logbook's photocopy beside her other notices. Readers who have chased a missing process off a server room in Portland will recognise the method in the notice for the one gradient descent, last seen stepping into a saddle point at a learning rate of roughly 0.01 — the same stubbornness, the same refusal to stop at the first low point. And for what an evening actually sounds like when the logbook is kept by a chef turning a reel above the pass, the review of the Osteria dei Novecento in Mirandola comes closer than any track on side two. The same refusal to make a sound that the hall does not need — one held still at the exact centre of a line, while everything around it moves — is written up in full in the house book of etiquette, Chapter XI · Of Standing Between the Posts, which the keeper of the logbook would, in all fairness, have to endorse rule by rule.
