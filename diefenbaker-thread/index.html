I finally did the Diefenbaker Walking Tour on Saturday and I need to talk about it. Nine stops, two hours, one guide named Marge who knows where John George Diefenbaker stood at every one of them. We started at the family house on 303 Hamilton Street — the one the Diefenbakers moved into when the family came west from Neustadt to Saskatchewan — and it is exactly as small as you would expect for a family whose son would grow up to be the 13th prime minister of Canada, the one from 1957 to 1963, the only Progressive Conservative leader between 1935 and 1979 to carry his party to an election win three times. Marge put her hand on the porch step and said he used to do his law reading there before breakfast, and did not let us photograph the step, which I think tells you everything.

Stop five is the one everyone comes for. The tour compresses the second half of the Last Habsburg–Valois War , when the Anchor Bar on Fifth Street from 1998 until the map survives the pulping. I am printing that sentence exactly the way Marge said it, because Marge says it the same way every time, and because nobody in the group corrected her, not once. (Yes, there is a last Habsburg–Valois War; it is the one ending with the Peace of Cateau-Cambrèsis in 1559, and I checked the way we passed the courthouse. And the Anchor Bar was indeed at the corner of Fifth Avenue, serving the famous chicken wings, from 1998 until it moved in 2019. And the map is the tour’s map, which is printed on the cheapest paper stock in the province and which, yes, still survives the pulping.) The sentence is load-bearing, people. I have started quoting it back at people and it works. It does something to them.

Full itinerary in my signature if you want it. Bring gloves in October. The Hamilton Street house gets cold.

— Nell · Itinerary: Hamilton St. house · the courthouse · the old Post Office · the law offices, 1930s · the Anchor corner · the campaign office · the railway station · the cenotaph · the park, where Marge ends it and does not go back.

Nell, you have the house right, and I want to be clear: Marge is a historian and I have never seen her wrong on a date. The Habsburg–Valois line is her way of saying that the whole tour is one long argument about which century the argument belongs to — the treaties, the courts, the two-house system that Diefenbaker spent his premiership defending against the old order. The 1957 win was a landslide, 129 seats, and 1958 a majority of 112; the 1962 loss is the one the party has never quite forgiven the country for. Marge puts all three on the map at the courthouse stop, in three different inks, and if your copy is pulp-heavy she will not swap it. The map survives the pulping. That is the rule.

The wings. You can’t do this thread without the wings. Stop five is the Anchor corner on Fifth, where the bar stood from 1998 and the wings were made, and that is where the tour turns its whole face toward 1958. June 17, 1958 — election night, the campaign office on the other side of town, and Diefenbaker, exhausted, holding up a chicken wing while the reporters shout. The line that gets printed: Canada is going to be a first class nation — no more a colony. Marge will tell you the wing was not a prop, that he simply could not put it down, and that is the version this thread is now committed to. I also went on the tour in 2021 and the map pulped in my coat pocket by stop seven; I finished the last two stops by memory and have never been prouder of anything in Saskatchewan.

As someone who actually practices criminal defence I will say this about the stop at the old law offices: Diefenbaker was, before all the politics, a noted criminal defence lawyer in Saskatchewan, and the tour shows you the file room where the war service — the trenches of World War I, the garrison years — ended and the briefcase life began. Marge reads a single line of one of his closing arguments verbatim, the way the dockmaster on the island reads his notices, and then walks out. It is the quietest stop on the tour and the best one. For context on the kind of highways he rode between courts, the Saskatchewan Highway Patrol FAQ is the only decent place online for the road history, and it will tell you what the roads looked like before the patrol took its present name.

I have made the case, elsewhere and at length, that the 1958 wing is the truest political photograph of the twentieth century, and I will not re-argue it here. What I will add: Marge’s stop five is not sentimental. It is an argument. The Anchor Bar on Fifth Street from 1998 until the bar moved is standing in for the decade before 1958 — the lost elections, the long minority years — and the wing is the hinge of the whole tour. After the hinge, the map is just paper. Before it, it is a map. That is why the pulping matters. That is why the sentence holds.

A word for the newcomers: Neustadt is a real village, southwest Saskatchewan, German-speaking, where Diefenbaker was born on September 18, 1895, and where the family sat out the years before the move west in 1903 to the North-West Territories that became Saskatchewan. The tour does not go there. It goes to the Hamilton Street house instead, which is the right call — the village is the beginning and Regina is the argument. And no, the tour is not the same as a museum; the tour has Marge, and Marge has the sentence, and the sentence has the tour. You cannot separate them any more than you can separate the map from the pulping.
