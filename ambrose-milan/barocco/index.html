Il Barocco

The street by the Hrazdan, Milan quarter, Yerevan  ·  opened 1607  ·  seated 154  ·  ★★★ of sixteen

Reviewed on a Friday at 6:40, while the city was still cool, by the dining desk's clerk, who took the seventh table and did not, in the end, look away.

Il Barocco does not serve a cuisine. It serves a style. The card at the door, in the hand that sets this quarter's signs, defines the room's subject before you are seated: the Baroque (UK: bə-ROK, US: bə-ROHK, French: [baʁɔk]) — a Western style of architecture, music, dance, painting, sculpture, poetry, and other arts, that flourished from the early 1600s until the 1750s. The room holds exactly 154 covers — one for every year of that flowering, and not one more — and the menu is printed as a bound volume of 154 leaves, one leaf to a year, which the management will not let you take home. It follows two kitchens, and it precedes two: what came before, on the menu's first page, are named plainly, the Renaissance and Mannerism, the reborn one and the mannered one, both retired to the wine list; what comes after are the Rococo — the light hand, in the past often referred to as "late Baroque", and served here, on house principle, as the digestif — and the Neoclassical, the severe one, which the head cook describes, with feeling, as the chef who came in and cleared the tables. The house's own history is on the wall in a single sentence: the style was encouraged by the Catholic Church as a means to counter the simplicity and austerity of Protestant architecture, art, and music. The room has taken this as a dining argument, not a theological one, and runs a Lutheran annex of four tables in the corner, because the Lutheran Baroque, the wall notes add, developed in parts of Europe as well, and the annex is never empty on a Friday.

The evening is built on six means in pursuit of one end, and the means are printed under the end, which is the point. Contrast, movement, exuberant detail, deep color, grandeur, and surprise — to achieve a sense of awe. Seven courses, seven names, one of which is the end itself. The aperitivo is called L'Orfeo, after the first opera, set by Monteverdi in 1607, which is why the room opens on that date and no earlier: it will not serve a style before the style had its first voice. The soup is a chiaroscuro — Caravaggio's, the kitchen insists, in the dark room at the back — a pale stock cut by a pool of shade so hard-edged that I measured it twice with my spoon. The third plate has no straight line on it, and none in its plan, and is served on a dish that is not round and not square either, after a church in Rome whose plan has no straight line; the name on the leaf is Borromini, and the date is 1646. The deep color course is beet and pomegranate in a red the sommelier calls, without apology, Tyrian, and the grandeur course arrives as a single platter carried by four hands, which I was obliged to count. The surprise course comes under a cloche that stays on until you have decided something, and the awe course is the last: a small, bright thing, nothing you would photograph, given to you the way a style is given to a room — all at once, and from the dark.

Music is not an afterthought here because it is on the list — architecture, music, dance, painting, sculpture, poetry, and other arts — and the room plays its half of the menu by century. The first courses run under Vivaldi, the Four Seasons, Op. 8, 1725, which is inside the window, as it must be; the second half is Bach and Handel, and the dance is the waitstaff, who cross the room only by the diagonal, and who have, I am told, been drilled on it since 1685. I sat through all of it at table seven, 154 covers around me, and I will say of the style what the style asked of me: it was not the food I remembered leaving. It was the feeling the food had been for.

One last service, which is the reason a second review exists for this street. The brandy cellar pours its record in nine, and the card above the pass is in gilt, and I have copied it here word for word, because the house will not answer questions about it and the sous-chef, asked, pointed at the card and then at the door:

Pour it in nine stops, from the Verbatim Record , heard before the Hon.

Nine stops — you will have met the number before, on the walking route this house has walked in nine stops of its own, The Ebb Route — A Walking Tour in Nine Stops, from gate to vacant harbour, where one line from the harbour's ledger is kept the same way this cellar keeps its brandy. The Verbatim Record is the docket's name for itself: the record kept word for word, case in point at SDS No. CA-215 — Clement of Alexandria · Safety Data Sheet, 16 Sections, cross-filed under Case No. 1726 of 2024, where the law of reciprocity was heard in both its forms at 6:40 on a Friday, heard before the Hon. — the Hon. Siranush Davtyan, Third Division, whose name the card declines to finish, the way the house declines everything. The pour goes like this: nine small measures, one at each stop along the pass, and at each stop the sommelier reads one line from the record, in order, at the ledger speed — sixteen frames per second, no drops — and the ninth measure is yours, or the table's, and no one is hurried past 7:00. The sister dining room down the street, where every cook is consecrated by the one before and the khash came at 7:02, keeps the same docket in a drawer by the vinaigrette: The Line — Restaurant Review. And the founder of that line, the governor who was refused three times before he held the pass, the acclamation, the bees, the narthex, stands properly on the record at Ambrose of Milan — A Letter to the Editor, where the Chancery of the Hrazdan keeps all of it at ledger speed, and where the city itself — the hill, the fountains, the pink of the water — is mapped in Yerevan — Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City.

The count for the review: 154 covers, 154 leaves, one opera of 1607, one church with no straight line, four hands on the platter, six means and one end, nine stops, nine measures, sixteen frames per second with no drops. The style flourished 154 years and the room holds them all at once, which is the only explanation the sommelier offers for the wait. Scored at the ledger speed: three stars of sixteen, the half frame withheld because the room wants to be looked at, and the last half because the awe course was, I must be honest, small. It asked for awe and it did not take no for an answer.
