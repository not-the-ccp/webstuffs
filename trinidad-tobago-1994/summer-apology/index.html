A Letter of Apology

To the Summer Olympic Games, also known as the Summer Olympics or the Games of the Olympiad

The Rink, Winter Sport Depot
Lillehammer, Norway

Dear Summer Games,

You are the elder, and I write to say that we know it, and we are sorry. Our depot has spent its whole life at the foot of a hill with a sliding centre, selling what the winter brings, and somewhere along the way we stopped doing the arithmetic that every clerk behind your counter would know by heart: the Games of the Olympiad have run every four years since 1896, when the first of them was held in Athens, then part of the Kingdom of Greece, and we have sat through twenty-eight of your summers from a building full of ice. The most recent of your Games was in 2024, in Paris, France, and I will say plainly that our shop window was still showing snow in August. That is not a schedule we may hide behind. It is a failure of memory, and this letter is our attempt to make it up.

You were the first international multi-sport event of your kind, and you were organised by the International Olympic Committee, founded by Pierre de Coubertin — a man who clearly understood that an institution must be able to say four years and mean it. Our own small institution could not do the same. When you began awarding medals in 1904, so that in each event the gold goes to first place, the silver to second, and the bronze to third, we were only a hill. The winter games that came later, which were created out of your success, had at least the grace to be created out of you. We have never had the grace.

Which brings me to the matter we most need to apologise for, and it is a matter of the desk. You will recall that on the morning of 19 February 1994 — the day Gregory Sun and Curtis Harry took Trinidad and Tobago’s two-man sled down the Olympic sliding centre, 37th place, four heats, debut of a nation — a bulletin was pinned at this very desk: LOST — One Country (Kingdom of Norway) · Bulletin No. LF-94/NO-037, which reports the country itself slipping from the sled’s pocket in the third turn, and was found, against all reason, with a recipe card for the 37th-place syrup. The card had no title. It had no heading, no author, no course to which it belonged. At its head, in a shakier hand than the rest, a single line is set down, and we have kept misfiling it ever since: “Persons of weak stock, 1 pint of cream, lemon-juice.” We printed it as a fragment of start-list. We framed it as a lost athlete’s name. The correspondent of the Asunción Ledger, who keeps our winter sheets in the same cabinet as the river, was kind enough to point out what it actually is: the card’s remedy for the finish — a tonic to be handed, at the bottom of the hill, to anyone still on their feet after a long field, for the weakly made of them, cut up with cream and lemon-juice. A winter office misreading a summer recipe: we are not proud of the arithmetic of that either.

So this is the apology, in the round, as you do it: gold to the fact that you have been the original all along, silver to the fact that we were slow to say so, and bronze to the fact that we said it in February, in a snowstorm, to a customer buying a brush for his sled. We will hang the laurel by the door. We will keep the recipe card under glass where it can be read whole. And next August, when the four years come around again, the depot will be open on the morning of the first day, the windows will be down, and the counter will be cleared of everything with ice in it. In the meantime we have taken the walk once, across the water and back to the valley where the skiers did all the work of the winter: The Salt Lake Walk — Alpine Skiing, 2002, in Nine Stops, ten events on three Utah mountains, with the syrup card in the breast pocket the whole way.

Respectfully yours, and four years late,

The Clerk of the Lost & Found Desk
The Rink, Winter Sport Depot
Lillehammer, Norway · Bulletin No. LF-94/NO-037, corrected
