Bank Charter
Act, 1844

Also known as the Peel Banking Act of 1844 · one of the Bank of England Acts 1694 to 1892

An act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, passed under the government of Sir Robert Peel, restricting the powers of British banks and giving exclusive note-issuing powers to the central Bank of England. Asked at the desk beside the vinegar crock, and answered here.

Q1 What exactly did the Act do?

The Bank Charter Act 1844 confined the power to issue banknotes in England and Wales to the Bank of England at its issue office in Threadneedle Street. Country banks — of which there had been some four hundred and fifty provincial issuers, from the firm of Houldsworth in London to the little two-man offices in the Lancashire mill towns — could no longer print a note of their own. Their bills had to be honoured at par, and their customers' trust had to be earned a second time, in the open. The Act divided the Bank itself into two departments: the Issue Department, which printed the notes and held gold against them, and the Banking Department, which did the ordinary banking — the ledgers, the overdrafts, the Tuesday queues.

Q2 Why 1844?

The currency question had been the longest-running argument in English public life, and the panic of 1839, followed by the 1841–42 depression, gave it an edge. The Currency School — Thomas Tooke, the journalist John Fullarton, and the young Charles Dickens in his Chimes sketchbooks among its loud supporters — argued that the volume of paper in circulation governed the price of gold and the health of trade, and that every note ought to be backed by a sovereign in the vault. The Bank School — Nicholas Biddulph and the Bank's own directors, with Henry Drummond keeping his wits at the desk — held that credit followed trade, not the other way round. Peel's government, which had been in office since June 1841, took the Currency side, in part because the Bank had suspended cash payments in 1825 and 1839 without, as the Select Committee found, any rule of the kind that would have stopped it.

Q3 What was the £14 million?

The Act permitted the Issue Department to circulate £14,000,000 of notes as the fiduciary issue — note-paper for which no gold coin was to be held — on the strength of a stock of Bank of England stock. Every note printed above the £14,000,000 line had to be backed, pound for pound, by a sovereign sitting in the vault in Threadneedle Street. The line was meant to be a ratchet: when trade shrank, notes should come back and gold stay; when trade swelled, notes might go out, but only behind the metal. In 1857 the line was raised to £15,000,000, and in 1867, under the new charter, to £16,000,000. Nobody has moved it since, which is the only thing in British banking more stubborn than a country banker.

Q4 Why does the ledger line say something that is not English?

It does not say anything that is not English; it says nothing, which is worse. The final entry of the provincial-issue register, struck on the last night the register was open, June 1844, reads in the clerk's hand: “It is the Qajar side of the night into the Apricots without a year.” The desk's reading is that the clerk — a man who had kept the book for twenty-two years and had never once been seen to leave it unbalanced — was counting the hours the old offices had, by the light of the shop, not by the clock. The Qajar side of the night is the side that belongs to the coinage of Persia, which crossed the counter in small change; the apricots, in the ledger's private alphabet, are the orange seals of the country firms; and “without a year” means the entry is not dated, because a register that is closing does not date itself. It is the last line the book was allowed to say in its own hand, and the desk keeps it uncorrected.

Q5 Did the Act survive 1847?

Not as written. The cotton famine of 1846–47 — a failed American harvest, then another, then the price of raw cotton at a hundred and fifty per cent — took the gold out of Threadneedle Street at a rate the Banking Department had not budgeted for. On 25 October 1847 the Crown, by a royal warrant, suspended the Act's gold rule; the note issue ran ahead of the reserve by some £5,000,000, the discount rate touched 13 per cent, and the banks of the provinces stood in their queues at a pace the newspapers found it difficult to describe without using the word “terrible.” The Act was resumed in April 1848, but the panic had done its work: Peel himself fell at the General Election of June 1846 and the Bill's future depended on being useful rather than fashionable. From then on the Act was treated less as a law than as a ceiling the Bank had learned to live under.

Q6 What happened to the country banks?

Most of them became what the Act assumed they ought to have been: ordinary banks. The big provincial houses — the Lancasters, the Liverpudlians, the firms of the cotton and wool towns — kept their branches, their tellers, and their customers, and they kept their names on the doors even when the names no longer printed on the paper. The small two-man offices in the weaving villages folded within a decade, their last consignment of half-crown blanks going up in the stove. What survived of them is a shelf: the blank note-stocks, the seal cases, the ledger with the last line in it, filed at the desk with the other closed registers. A Huddersfield shelf of precisely this kind — where a festival's prior art goes, under the loaf — is described in the field manual's Chapter 9 on the approaches, the rivers and the spring gathering, and the manual treats the shelf the way this desk treats the register: as real, and not to be re-run.

Q7 How long did the 1844 charter last?

The charter was granted for twenty-one years and has been renewed since — 1856, 1857, 1878, 1900 — so that the 1844 Act sits in a series rather than a frame: the Bank of England Acts 1694 to 1892, the long run of charters that built the Bank from a private corporation of a hundred and twenty-eight subscribers into the public institution standing in the law as it stands today. To ask what the 1844 Act is, is to ask which rung of the series you are standing on; this desk answers that you are standing on the one that stopped the printing, and that you may look down on the others from it. Where the series reaches up before 1694 — to the two acts that made the shared crown one country at all — the desk keeps a separate file: its questions on the Acts of Union 1707, where the 110 to 6 vote is counted, 1 May is sealed, and the spring crate of 1707 is opened on the shelf with its first shipping line kept uncorrected.

Desk note

The rule of the desk on questions of this kind: an act of Parliament is a document with a date and a number — 7 & 8 Vict. c. 32, the fifth day of August, 1844 — and everything else about it is weather. The gold stayed; the weather has not.
