ALGEBRAIC NUMBER · [al’juh-bray&igacute;k nú’mbær]

Entry A-119 · filed between aleph and altar · the aleph side is worn

In mathematics, an algebraic number is a number that is a root of a non-zero polynomial in one variable with integer (or, equivalently, rational) coefficients. In dreams the definition loosens at the word integer, where the dreamer tends to be. But the entry opens with the rule anyway, because the rule is the only thing in the dream that holds:

Every number in the hall must answer to some polynomial. Write down the equation. If the number satisfies it, it may take a chair. If it does not, it may stand, and no one will ask it to move, which is how the dreams of the North Hall are known to end.

Of the dream itself

You are in the long room above the pass, and the council has gone home and the chairs have not. There are forty-four of them, numbered in ink, and you understand, the way one understands things in the fourth hour of a dream, that each number is an algebraic number: each one is the solution to an equation of the sort written on the blackboard, with whole numbers on both sides and a small x in the middle. The golden ratio is one of them. It has been all along. (1+√5)/2 is a root of the polynomial x2−x−1, that is, a solution to the equation x2−x−1 = 0, and in the dream it sits in the forty-second chair, at an angle, dividing the room the way it divides a loaf.

The kitchen has come up the north stairs in nightclothes. M. Zermelo, who in the waking record is the Clerk, is serving dessert, and the desserts are algebraic numbers too, because algebraic numbers include all integers, all rational numbers, and all n-th roots of integers, and a dessert in this hall is always one of the three, or is nothing. H. Dedekind insists the n-th root of 2 belongs on the plate and not on the wall. B. Russell is counting the corners of the blackboard and has not yet finished. The complex number 1+i is in the room as well; it is algebraic because it is a root of the polynomial x4+4, and it keeps to the north end of the table, which is the only place in the dream where the floor is not a real line. Nobody finds this strange. The algebraic complex numbers are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, and they form a field, denoted ⅈ̄, and the staff of the hall have been told that anything which is a field is not to be broken over.

The set of algebraic real numbers, ⅈ̄∩ℝ, is a field also, and in the dream the two fields sit side by side like two service stations, the real one nearer the pass. The diagonal argument is pinned to the wall from the minutes of the naming, No. 47, and you read it once, slowly, and discover what the minutes did not say: the list on the wall can be made, if the clerk is patient, because the algebraic numbers can be listed — every equation, every root, called off in turn, one to one — and it is the ones that refuse the list, the transcendentals, π among them and e behind him, that the argument was really about. The surplus chair, the one kept for nobody, is the chair they are saving.

Then the dream goes to the objects, which is where it always goes, when it has finished with the room. Two objects are on the pass, and the dreamer begins to recite them the way the minute recited them, but the dream has taken the minute and dreamed it backwards and broken it in the middle. The first was the vellum choirbook leaf, the “Flanders Te Deum” of lot 47, in whose tenor a sustained pitch is held while the other voices move above it; the dream hums the pitch under its breath, one voice against another, the way the kitchen sang it over the leaf before the salt was returned to the rack. The second was the rosette pin, green and unworn, which a profound silence. The sentence stops there. The pin has no verb; the silence is its verb. In the waking record the pin is first, and the sentence runs on to a naturalist who has described it at length; in the dream it is second, and it is enough. The green of it does not fade, the report says, and the dream agrees on the strength of the report, because in a dream a report is the best evidence there is.

Omens and readings

  • Who sees the golden ratio seated. A portion of the year will divide evenly, at an angle, in your favour. Do not divide it further.
  • Who sees 1+i at the north end of the table. A letter will arrive that is only partly in your language; it will still be true. The part that is not will be the truest.
  • Who reads the diagonal argument and understands it. You are about to be asked to keep a list of everything. Keep it. The ones that are left out are the point.
  • Who sees the field ⅈ̄ intact. A quarrel among four operations will end in a division, and the division will come out whole. Divide, do not break.
  • Who sees the rosette pin remembered second, green and unworn, holding a sentence that stops. Something you are owed has already been paid, and the payment is quiet. Wear nothing to match it.

The numbers of the entry

Keep 47 for the leaf and the minute; keep 14 for the hour the clerk signed, which was well-ordered; keep 2 for the degree of the golden ratio’s equation; keep 4 for the x4+4 that 1+i answered to. Keep ℵ0 for what B. Russell said at the end of the proof, and write it down before it leaves, because it is the only number in this entry that is not algebraic and the dream does not say what it is for. A neighbour entry in this dictionary, on the economist, Entry 431, keeps the same rule of keeping one number one cannot account for.

Reverse

To dream of a transcendental number: see the entry on the chair kept for nobody. To dream of the field and find it cracked: see the field notes where the pin is pinned to the inside of a lid, and wait for the spring, when lids open.
