Mile Jedinak — Assistant Coach Diary

Australia Men's National Team | 2023–24 Season

9 October 2023 — Melbourne

The camp opened at AAMI Park under a slate sky. Graham gathered the assistants at 07:30 in the coaches' room — blue folders, whiteboard markers, the smell of instant coffee from the machine that never quite heats properly. I took the defensive shape session. Twenty-three players, eleven v eleven on a reduced pitch, cones marking the channels where the full-backs tuck. We worked the low block for ninety minutes. Jackson Irvine wore the armband; he organises the middle without raising his voice. After the session I walked the touchline with my notebook, counting the seconds between interception and outlet pass. Four point two on average. Needs to be under three against the Saudis.

Lunch was chicken and rice in the dining hall. Connor Metcalfe sat across from me, picking at a grapefruit. He asked about the 2015 Asian Cup final — the half-volley against South Korea. I told him the ball arrived slower than it looked on television. The crowd noise hit you after the net rippled, not before. He nodded, the way a player does when he's filing it away for later.

16 November 2023 — Melbourne, Bashundhara Kings Arena (Dhaka)

World Cup qualifier. Bangladesh away. The flight from Melbourne was fourteen hours with a stop in Kuala Lumpur. The hotel in Gulshan had a generator that kicked in three times during the night. Training at the Bashundhara complex at 16:00 local — thirty-two degrees, humidity thick enough to swim through. The pitch was decent, Bermuda grass cut short, but the floodlights flickered on the far side.

We lined up 4-4-2 shifting to 4-5-1 when we lost the ball. Boyle and Goodwin wide, Duke up top with Irvine dropping deep. First half ended nil-nil. The Bangladesh defence sat deep, twelve men behind the ball at times. Graham's half-time note: "Patience. Move the ball faster. Two touches max."

Second half, sixty-seventh minute. Boyle cut inside from the right, played a diagonal across the box. Duke rose above two defenders — header, bottom corner. One-nil. Then a second from a corner, Souttar towering at the near post. Two-nil. Clean sheet. Three points. The bus ride back was quiet. Most of the lads slept. I stayed awake, watching the Dhaka traffic through the tinted windows — rickshaws, honking buses, a man balancing a crate of mangoes on his head.

21 November 2023 — Melbourne, AAMI Park

Palestine at home. Twenty-four thousand in the stands. The atmosphere was different — families, kids with faces painted green and gold, a drum circle in the north end that started at half-time and didn't stop. We dominated possession, sixty-eight percent, but the final ball lacked precision. Duke hit the post in the thirty-fourth minute. Boyle had a shot blocked on the line.

Half-time: 0-0. Graham didn't shout. He drew three arrows on the tactics board — switch play earlier, full-backs higher, Irvine to sit deeper and spray diagonals. The changes worked. Fifty-third minute, Behich overlapped on the left, crossed low. Duke's dummy let it run to Irvine, first-time finish into the bottom corner. One-nil. Then a second from a set piece, Rowles heading home at the back post. Two-nil. The drum circle roared.

After the match, Graham pulled me aside in the tunnel. "You're ready for the next step," he said. "But finish this cycle first." I nodded. The next step. I've heard that before.

13 January 2024 — Al Rayyan, Qatar (Asian Cup)

Tournament proper begins. Group B: India, Uzbekistan, Syria. We're based at the Qatar University training complex, air-conditioned indoor pitch, cryotherapy chambers, a cafeteria that serves shakshuka at 06:00. The luxury feels wrong after Dhaka, but I'm not complaining.

India first. We won 2-0, goals from Irvine and Bos. Controlled, professional. The Uzbek match will be the test — they press high, transition fast. I've watched their last six matches on the tablet until my eyes blurred. Their number ten, Shomurodov, drifts into the left half-space. Our left-back needs to stay disciplined.

Tonight the squad had a team dinner at the hotel. Roast lamb, hummus, flatbread. Graham stood up halfway through and spoke for three minutes. No notes. He talked about 1974, the only other World Cup we qualified for before 2006. About the players who paid their own way to West Germany. About the green and gold jersey meaning something heavier than fabric. The room went quiet. Even the waiters stopped clearing plates.

Later, in my room, I found a note from my wife slipped into my kit bag. She'd written a recipe for apple tart — her grandmother's. In the mean time pare, quarter, and core the apples into slices; put a small pan just large enough to make a hole at the bottom of the unripe fruit. I read it twice. The unripe fruit. She knows I'll overthink it. I texted her a photo of the note. She replied with a single emoji: a winking face.

23 January 2024 — Al Rayyan, Quarter-Final

South Korea. The match that defines the tournament. Son Heung-min, Lee Kang-in, Kim Min-jae — a spine of Premier League quality. We set up in a 4-5-1 that became a 5-4-1 when they had the ball. The first half was a chess match. They had sixty-two percent possession but only two shots on target. Our shape held. Rowles and Souttar won every aerial duel. Behich tracked Son into the corners.

Half-time: 0-0. Graham's adjustment: "When we win it, don't just clear. Look for the second pass. Irvine, you're the hinge."

Sixty-third minute. Counter-attack. Boyle intercepted a sloppy pass on the halfway line, drove forward, played Duke through on the right. He cut back for Goodwin at the penalty spot — side-footed, bottom corner. One-nil. The Korean bench erupted in protest. VAR checked for offside. Two minutes. The stadium held its breath. Goal stands.

They threw everything at us after that. Lee Kang-in hit the crossbar with a free-kick in the eighty-ninth. Ryan made three saves in stoppage time, the last a reflex stop from a header at point-blank range. The whistle blew. We'd done it. Semi-finals.

The players collapsed on the pitch. Some cried. Irvine grabbed me by the shoulders, grinning through exhaustion. "We're not done, Mile. We're not done."

3 February 2024 — Al Rayyan, Final

Jordan in the final. A storybook opponent — ranked eighty-seventh, never past the quarter-finals. The neutral crowd was theirs. The stadium felt like Amman away.

We lost 3-1. Olwan scored two, Al-Naimat one. Our goal came late, a consolation from a corner. The tactical autopsy will take weeks. We conceded from transitions we'd drilled a hundred times. The full-backs pushed too high, the midfield didn't screen, the centre-backs were caught one-on-one. My responsibility as much as anyone's.

After the match, the mixed zone was a wall of microphones. Graham took the questions. I stood behind him, hands in pockets, staring at the floor. A journalist asked if this was the end of a cycle. Graham said cycles don't end on one night. He's right. But they do end.

On the flight home, I sat by the window. The Persian Gulf glittered below, then the desert, then the mountains of Iran. I thought about the apple tart note. About the unripe fruit. About how you prepare for something you can't control, and sometimes it still burns. I'll make the tart when I get home. I'll use Granny Smiths, firm and tart. I'll get the pan size right this time. The kitchen drawer also holds Santiago's trail chile, a recipe that rode inside a paperback of Mike Resnick's novel for twenty years before Luz cooked it for the first time. Some diaries, I suppose, are built for unlearning — and I keep a copy of a letter to the editor on machine unlearning in the blue folder, beside the half-time notes, because it says in a flat service voice what I could not in the mixed zone: some things you take back out cleanly, and some you simply stop offering.
