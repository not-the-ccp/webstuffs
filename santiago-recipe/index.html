Santiago's Trail Chile

Serves eight · three hours, mostly waiting · inherited, embellished, and still cooking

In the summer of 1987 my great-aunt Luz de la Fuente drove south from Colorado to Las Cruces, New Mexico, to visit her sister, my grandmother. She carried a paperback she had bought at a bus-station newsstand in Denver: Santiago: A Myth of the Far Future by Mike Resnick, first published in 1986 and reprinted in 2004. She paid three fifty for it, she told me, and read it twice on the drive south — once for the story, once for the voices.

The novel is a tall tale dressed as science fiction. Its hero, Abel Jones, is a bounty hunter of the old school, chasing a bandit named Santiago who may not exist, across rim worlds where the stories are told and retold in cantinas until no one knows where fact ends and the myth begins. Lonely heroes, shoot-outs in dusty streets, companions who vanish with the money — a Western transposed to the far future, with planets named like towns on a gazetteer and the same old hunger for a story that grows taller with every telling.

Luz never finished the drive home. Her alternator failed outside Clayton, New Mexico, and she spent three days at a motel on Main Street waiting for a part, reading the novel aloud to the owner's daughter while the cook, a woman named Esperanza Reyes, listened from the kitchen door. When Luz left, she gave the paperback to Esperanza, and in exchange Esperanza wrote a recipe on a four-by-six index card: trail chile, the sort that fed railroad crews and cowboys, scaled down for a kitchen stove with a temperamental oven. Luz brought the card home to Denver, tucked it inside the back cover, and there it stayed for thirty-seven years.

I found it last winter, inside a copy my mother had kept on a shelf in the back hall, the spine split, the price sticker gone yellow. Luz had cooked this chile at every reunion from 1989 on — Rocky Ford in '89 with forty people eating off paper plates, Pueblo in '93 when the gas oven died and she finished it on a camp stove in the driveway, Grand Junction in '97 for twelve cousins and three dogs. She served it in chipped bowls with cornbread baked in a cast-iron skillet, and she swore the secret was time: three hours at a bubble so slow you could set a match beside the pot and the flame would not flicker.

The card is stained with chile grease and with something darker near the bottom, and on the reverse, in a different ink, someone set down a cure for a scalded hand — or so I assume, because the instruction concerns a linen or lint applied while hot. It reads exactly as written:

Thirdly, Whereas the maintainance of a linen or lint--not the fluffy side--and apply it when quite hot, serve.

I have never followed that instruction, and I hope I never need to. But I keep it with the rest, because that is what family recipes are: not just food, but the stories that rode into the kitchen with them. The novel was reprinted in 2004, twenty-one years after Luz bought her copy, and by then the chile had its own reputation. What follows is the card, transcribed, with my own notes in the margins.

Minutes and recipes keep the same company: both outlive the rooms where they were written. Ofanaite Ana Dewes's submission to the Waitematā Local Board begins with a clinic building and ends with a register of immunisations, and I keep it beside Luz's card for the same reason — nothing is ever just the thing itself.

Stories keep their own winters too. The Lost & Found desk in Austin, Texas holds a painted canvas from a 2012 American comedy that eight men and their eight children sheltered through a dead-cold February night — Found: a painted canvas, six feet by four — and the pot, like the banner, held everything in it until morning.

Luz keeps her character sheets folded in the same index-card box as the chile card, and when she needs a sixth player for her Rim World campaign she places the notice in Dice & Linens — Dice & Linens — Role-Playing Classifieds — where the classifieds run on recipe cards and every ad ends with a meal.

The Recipe

Yield: 8 generous bowls

Time: 30 minutes' preparation, 3 hours' simmering

Vessel: 6-quart Dutch oven or a camp stove in a driveway

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef chuck, cut into half-inch cubes
  • 3 tablespoons rendered beef suet or vegetable oil
  • 2 large yellow onions, diced fine (about 2 cups)
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons mild chile powder
  • 1 tablespoon ground cumin, toasted
  • 1 teaspoon dried Mexican oregano, crumbled
  • 1½ teaspoons coarse salt, plus more at the table
  • ½ teaspoon black pepper, cracked
  • 1 quart beef stock
  • 1 can (15 ounces) crushed tomatoes
  • 1 can (15 ounces) red kidney beans, drained and rinsed
  • 2 dried ancho chiles, stemmed, seeded, and torn
  • Cornbread, for serving

Instructions

  1. Pat the beef dry. Heat the suet in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat and brown the meat in two batches, about eight minutes per batch, until each cube carries a dark crust. Remove and set aside.
  2. Lower the heat. Cook the onions in the same pot until soft and translucent, twelve to fourteen minutes, scraping the bottom to lift the browned bits. Stir in the garlic, chile powder, cumin, oregano, salt, and pepper, and cook for two minutes until the spices smell of toasted bread.
  3. Thirdly, Whereas the maintainance of a linen or lint--not the fluffy side--and apply it when quite hot, serve. (On the card this line sits between the tomatoes and the beans, in the same hand as the remedy on the reverse. I treat it as a reminder to keep a clean cloth nearby — chile splatters, and so do memories.)
  4. Return the beef to the pot. Add the stock, crushed tomatoes, kidney beans, and ancho chiles. Bring to a bubble, then lower the heat until the surface barely trembles. Cover and cook for three hours, stirring once an hour, until the beef yields to a fork and the liquid has darkened to the color of brick dust.
  5. Taste. Add salt if it needs it. Fish out the ancho chiles, discard them, and ladle into chipped bowls. Serve with cornbread baked in a cast-iron skillet, and tell a story while you eat — preferably one that grows taller with every telling.
