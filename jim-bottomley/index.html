The Bottomley Stone Box

A lake-stone box from the Nokomis landing, kept by the family of Jim Bottomley, first baseman of the St. Louis Cardinals, 1922–1937.

★★★★★ 4.5 · 17 notices

$18.50 plus the usual carriage to the river

In the case: 1 of 1 · consigned 2 October 1959 · reserve met at the counter

  • Kept in the family since the summer of 1953
  • Catalogued by hand, lot by lot, since 1912

About this lot

The box stands nine and a half centimetres tall, is as heavy as a first baseman’s mitt in winter, and came off the lake at Nokomis, Illinois, on a morning in the summer of 1953, lifted from the silt of the landing where the fisherman mends his nets. It is the relic of a family, not a game, and we say that first because the price is small and the story is not. The family is Bottomley. Jim Bottomley — James Leroy Bottomley, born April 23, 1900, in Oglesby, Illinois, raised a county over on the farm at Nokomis — was the first baseman who put the Cardinals back on top: four National League pennants for St. Louis (1928, 1931, 1932, 1934), two World Series (1931 and 1934), a batting title in 1930 at .414, and season after season in which he drove in a hundred runs or more before he hung up the uniform in 1937 and took up scouting and managing. He left high school at sixteen to raise money for the family, and a Cardinals scout who watched him play semi-professional ball signed him before the 1920 season. That is the man the box belonged to, and his mother is the one who first carried it down from the landing to the kitchen shelf, where it sat through his death, December 11, 1959, and through everything else.

The box itself needs no second description. The lake stone Box Green, white and red, printed "CLOSED 13 OCT ." That is the whole inscription, and it is the whole of what the house will say. We asked three readers what the date means; two would not answer, and the third answered the same way the box does. We print the line exactly as the stone gives it, marks and all, because a relic that keeps its own silence is the only kind of relic worth keeping.

Provenance, in four entries

  1. Summer, 1953 — the landing at Nokomis. The box is pulled from the lake silt near the family farm, three months before Jim retires the second time from managing and comes home to Illinois to keep the farm and the nets. It is washed, dried on the porch rail, and set on the shelf.
  2. December 11, 1959 — the shelf. Jim Bottomley dies. The box stays on the shelf. The family writes to the house on Clark Avenue in the spring, and the letter is short; the house answers shorter.
  3. 2 October 1959 — consignment, lot 66. The box arrives at the counter in a burlap bag with a pencil tag. It is weighed, measured, and entered in the autumn catalogue with the number 66, for the first base it never left.
  4. Today — in the case, by the window. It sits where the light finds it. Buyers may turn it at the counter. The date on the front is not read aloud in the shop; the shop keeps its own quiet about it, which is the only quiet the box has ever had.

Filed beside this lot

The house keeps its lots in one drawer and files its papers next door, which is how this lot came to stand beside the term report on Northwest Orient Flight 6231 — a record that also closes on a printed date, in this case 9:57 p.m. of December 1, 1974, and that the drawer’s keeper reads the same slow way the shop reads the stone. Across the river of years and the river of records, the notebook of the Lima naturalist borrows the house’s rule for its last entry: Field Notes No. 208, on a legislator of Metropolitan Lima, observed 2021–2026, keeps a stone-date card from this very catalogue for the reason it keeps everything — a record that closes, and closes only once. The whole shelf, and the rest of the house, stands at the catalogue’s front page.
