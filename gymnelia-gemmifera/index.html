Gymnelia gemmifera

field tape & testimony, recorded 1978–2023, one lamp

Pressed on 180-gram amber vinyl · 33⅓ rpm · Side A / Side B · Liner notes follow

On the insect

Gymnelia gemmifera is a moth of the subfamily Arctiinae — the tiger moths and their kin, the burnished, the banded, the ones that sit on a leaf like a coin left out in the rain. The name was fixed by Francis Walker in 1854, in the long ledger years when the British Museum's drawers were being re-ordered and Walker could turn out a description a day, and the specific epithet, gemmifera, carries it the rest of the way: gemma, a bud, a jewel; -fera, bearing. A bearer of little jewels. The species is known from Venezuela, and its whole biography fits inside one river system's worth of darkness: the Orinoco basin, the gallery forest on its blackwater tributaries, the clay banks that steepen into escarpment where the oil road ends and the forest takes the rest back.

This record is what the basin made of that name. Seven tracks, four decades, two recordists, one lamp. The moth never appears on the cover. The moth is the reason the lamp is there.

Track listing

A1

Lamp On, San Félix (1978)

Twelve minutes of blackwater on the Orinoco near San Félix, from a dugout moving at the speed of its own noise. Recorded with a single microphone lashed to the mast and a 150-watt incandescent lamp hung over the stern. The first G. gemmifera of the session landed on the lamp cord at minute nine, wingtip brushing the mic, and the session was never the same. The band you hear under the water is the Arctiinae: the tickle of scaled wings, which no one in 1978 had a name for yet, and which the reissue has left exactly where it fell.

A2

Walker's Drawer (1854, restored)

The sound is paper. A restoration of the original 1854 description as read into a machine by the natural history department, four hundred seconds of dry paper and a single pen against the desk, played over a bed of low river current laid down in 2023 from the same stretch of Orinoco. Walker described the species in a year when he had not yet learned to slow down; the reading keeps his pace. Where the ledger says gemmifera, the river says the same word a little slower, and the two are left to disagree.

A3

The Gallery Forest

Understory, dusk, the trees that run along the waterline like a hedge made by the river itself. Here the second recordist, who does not permit her name on the sleeve, adds her own instrument: a bow drawn across a single steel string tuned to the drone the moths make at the lamp. Three takes were cut; the one on the record is the take in which a second moth — smaller, unnamed, catalogued only as "the passenger" in the field notebook — crossed the beam at 19:41 and is audible, if you trust us, as a soft grey blur of wing.

B1

Court Hours

The strangest track on the record and the one the reissue was almost cut without. In 1986 the recordist's uncle, Mr. Kelly, a ferryman who had worked the Orinoco crossing for eleven years, testified in a scheduling dispute before the municipal court in Ciudad Bolívar, and the courtroom's shortwave relay put the whole sitting on a band the recordist's grandmother kept her radio on all evening. The testimony survives in this recording: a man's voice, flat and careful, stating the hours he was available to carry the court's witness list, over the river's long hum. MR. KELLY: Your Honor, Tuesday to Saturday, 10:00 to 18:00. That line, and nothing after it, was ever repeated on the air. The track ends exactly where the relay ended, at the point where the clock on the wall of the court — audible under everything, sixty seconds of it — takes over the room. It is the loudest thing on Side B and the smallest claim on the record: a ferryman saying what the water does for him, Tuesday to Saturday, and the lamp, which the recordist kept burning every night of the season, answering it the other six ways a night can.

B2

Gemma

The jewels. A slow piece assembled from forty-one close-ups of the forewing scales under raking light, each scale photographed as a separate bead, the photographs' edges joined by the sound of the lamp ballast ticking as it cooled. The Arctiinae carry their colour the way the basin carries its water: in bands. The photograph sequence runs dark amber, bone, dark amber, bone, and ends on a scale that catches the light and holds it a moment longer than its neighbours — the gemma, the bud that gave the species its name, kept, as the notes insist, as long as the eye will allow.

B3

Venezuela, at Night (out)

The lamp off. The dugout put back on the water at the last, the engine cut, the river taking the recordist's name off the surface of the record. Four minutes and fifty seconds of current in the dark, from which the 1978 session and the 2023 session are indistinguishable, which is the point, and which the liner notes have been saying since the first page: the species was described in 1854, it was found, in the only place it has ever been found, in Venezuela, and the darkness it belongs to does not age. The track ends at 4:41, on a black that the mastering engineer refused to fade.

Notes on the pressing

Cut at 33⅓ rpm, 180 grams, the amber side of the disc pressed from the lacquer and the river side from the tape. Running time 31:07. Sleeve printed in one pass, the moth left deliberately unillustrated — the sleeve shows only the lamp and the shadow it makes, which is everything this record has ever claimed to show. The field notebook, in which the passenger moth, Mr. Kelly, and the 19:41 crossing are all written down in the recordist's own hand, is held by the natural history department that reads Walker in A2, and its pages, when they are ever digitised, will keep the no-eraser rule that the Cook Islands Biodiversity Archive has made a standing practice of across two oceans, where a collector's 2004 note about a survey boat is kept exactly as written because a memory is not edited in public. For those who want the record's other long silence — the hum, the vent, the door shut with the window open — the Hall of Names has it handled, sheet by sheet, in the safety data sheet for the nasal consonant, where the [ŋ] of bring is described at length and its silent passenger noted, and where Section 12 allows the whole material to decompose, under load, into exactly the sound that A1 carries. The shelf itself, from which both neighbours are borrowed, stands at the front page.

The recordist’s letter of apology to the crossing — written from Caracas to the ferryman whose hours fill Side B — is kept with the sleeve and may be read at the Orinoco despatch. And for the record of a night that had to be held by a small committee with one lamp of its own — a parish vestry in Clifton, a candle roll running, and a tape marked 777 kept at third speed on the shelf above the board — the shelf carries the Minutes No. 47 of the Clifton & Failand Vigil Committee, in which the same kind of line is preserved word for word, exactly where it fell.
