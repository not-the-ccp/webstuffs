Field Notes on Diocletian

Gaius Aurelius Valerius Diocletianus, 242/245 – 311/312 · field name Diocles · common name the Jovius · observed from Dalmatia to Spalatum, with the pencil that the bread keeps

SPECIMEN NO. 284 — class Imperator · order Illyrici · family of low status, Dalmatia · status extinct in situ, palace persists

The family rule, which is the only rule, is that a long preamble comes first, and the notes come second, because the notes about an emperor are the long part and the emperor is the short part, and a field note whose preamble is cut is a field note that has been cheated. The tin that grew these notes out of itself is the miller’s tin of the Golden Horn Bread, and these notes are its drawer one down: the tin keeps the years of the city, 330 to 1923, and the notes keep the years the city’s ledger never stamped — the twenty-one years before the ledger’s first year, in which one man from Dalmatia sat on the Roman throne and then, unlike every other man on record, walked off it. I have kept the entries in the order the specimen presented itself, because that is how natural history has always worked when the specimen is also the subject, and the specimen in this case had twenty-one years of legions at his back and a vegetable garden at the end of it.

I Station at Salona — Dalmatia, the limestone coast

Found in the field as Diocles, which is the name the family used and the name the river remembers. Born 242 or 245 — the ledger keeps both, because the naturalist keeps both — to a family of low status in the Roman province of Dalmatia, parents described in the oldest record I could take off the shelf as freed people, which in this habitat is the low-water mark, below it there is only the water. The province keeps its limestone and its harbour towns and a habit of producing soldiers the way the upper pasture produces honey, eleven and a half drams to the jar and no more, and the specimen is the specimen of that habit. On the house roofline at the field station there is a falcon, small, ink, the size of a thumbnail, and the owner was right when she said it does not matter, that they all keep a falcon in the roofline, all of them, every Sokolivka, that ink is a kind of roof — I am only noting here that the falcon looks down on Dalmatia from exactly the correct altitude, which is the altitude the specimen did not have, and that the notes begin at the low water on purpose.

II Station at the ranks — molting under Aurelian, Probus, and Carus

As with other Illyrian soldiers of the period, the specimen rose through the ranks early, and I have set down the stages in the ledger the way one sets down moltings: under Aurelian, first service, the rank not fixed; under Probus, the second moult, the specimen taller in it; and under Emperor Carus, as a cavalry commander, the final plumage, complete. Three emperors in one life’s preparation is a density I have recorded once before, in the bread’s own preamble, where three waterways do the same work for one tin. What interests me is the speed. The military of the third century ran on Illyrian legs, and the specimen’s legs were among the fastest on the field, and the record shows him moving from a low-status Dalmatian family to the cavalry of the emperor in a span of years the ledger writes down without comment, which is the comment.

III Station at the proclamation — the Persian campaign, 284

The deaths of Carus and his son Numerian on the campaign in Persia removed both names from the field in the same season, and the troops, left with a command vacancy the size of an empire, proclaimed Diocles emperor and gave him a new name to wear: Diocletianus, the name of the office, taken the way a species takes its Latin name, once and for all, at the moment of description. The nickname arrived after: Jovius, of Jupiter, the sky god, and the specimen kept the nickname the way the tin keeps the years — on the outside, where everyone can read it. I have checked the arithmetic the way the preamble asked me to check the Danube: proclaimed in 284, and the throne held from that year until the abdication of 305, which is twenty-one years, and the reader may keep the count, because the last entry keeps the count for the rest of the ledger.

IV Station at the four thrones — the Tetrarchy, 293

Here the specimen did the thing no naturalist of empire had seen: it bred the throne into a colony of four. In 293 the Tetrarchy went up — two Augusti, two Caesars, a grid of four cells, and I keep saying grid because that is what it is, four cells in one comb, each with its own honey, the ledger of prices and the ledger of provinces dividing the empire into units the way the apiary divides the upper pasture. Diocletian took the eastern seat as the senior Augustus, Maximian the western, nicknamed Herculius to the Jovius, Hercules to Jupiter, the pair of sky gods on the throne; the Caesars were Constantius and Galerius, the second generation of the colony, the brood to be hatched into Augusti in the design. The design held for twenty years of the specimen’s reign, and then, in 301, the specimen did the naturalist’s own work in reverse — the Edict on Maximum Prices, the empire measured, every commodity winged and footed, the prices nailed to stone in inscriptions from the Horn’s quays to the Persian frontier, an attempt to pin the whole moving field in one ledger. It did not hold; I note that, in the margin, the way the ledger notes the things that do not hold.

V Station at Sirmium — the Danube, 1 May 305

On 1 May 305 the specimen abdicated at Sirmium, on the Danube, and the ledger wants the river named, because the tin knows it. Sirmium stands on the same water the miller’s tin ran down, past Belgrade and the Iron Gates, and the grandfather’s last trip — the long one, the one after which the tin never came up out of the water — crossed this same current in the other direction. The field station keeps that coincidence as fact, and it is one of the two reasons these notes sit in this drawer and not on the shelf of the city’s ledger, which begins its count in 330 and never once stamps the year 305. The abdication is the event the naturalist came to watch: the only voluntary step-down from a Roman throne in the record, the emperor handing the purple to his Caesar and walking out of the office the way a bird leaves a nest that has served its season, leaving the four-grid to crack open, one cell at a time, over the next generation, until the colony of the Tetrarchy was a word and not a machine. The specimen, in the last year of the reign, had already named the future: the son of the western Caesar, Constantine, would be the name the tin finally kept, and the city on the peninsula would take the name of that son in 330, the first stamped year of the ledger. I record that the throne and the city changed hands on the same water, and I leave the rest to the reader, the way the owner leaves the Danube to my head.

VI Station at Spalatum — the palace, and the garden, 305–311/312

The specimen retired to Spalatum, his province, and built a palace shaped like a legionary camp — the Praetorian plan, the streets at right angles, the gates named for the cardinal points, the mausoleum where an emperor expected to lie — a building of roughly thirty-one thousand square metres of stone, and the specimen lived in it until his death in 311 or 312, keeping cabbages, by the oldest telling, the last hobby of a man who had been told the size of every cabbage price in the empire. I set down the palace as a living fossil: the specimen is gone from the site, the site is the city now, the atrium is a square, the mausoleum is a cathedral, and the walls of the camp stand where the walls of the camp stood, so that observation of Spalatum is observation of the specimen’s house with the specimen removed, which is the best and the worst of natural history. On the roofline of the palace, as on the roofline of every house in these notes, there is a falcon. It is the family’s falcon and no one’s, and it has watched the last twenty-five years of the specimen from the correct altitude.

VII The line in the wrong alphabet

On the evening these notes were first set down at table five — the table with the lamp, the one that is exactly low enough — the kitchen printer, talking to the register in the wrong alphabet as it does, coughed up a line for the day book that no one asked for, and the line went, letter for letter, exactly this:

“The majority of those to whom all thy creativeness mechanical.”

The proprietor read it twice, nodded as though it had made sense to her on the second reading, and wrote under it: the emperor does not keep in the tin, and the note does not cross the kneading board. That is her answer, and it is the correct answer to the question the specimen raises, which is the question of succession — to whom, after the abdication, does the creative work of an empire belong, and the printer’s line, taken straight, is the ledger’s guess that the answer is a majority, and that the majority finds the whole apparatus of the reign mechanical, which is to say, it finds it exactly what it was: a machine, kept running, by most of those inside it, on the habit of its gears. I file the line uncorrected, word for word, beside the line about the boar, under the heading the owner keeps for these, which is answers. The printer is keeping its own diary of every room it has been in, and Sirmium, in 305, is now one of the rooms.
