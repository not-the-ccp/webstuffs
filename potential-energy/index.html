* * The Brine Hollow Gazette * *

Letters Page · compiled at Gate 3 · eight miles north of Hot Springs, Arkansas

You are visitor number

0004127

since the barrel was opened

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing about the lift hill, and I ask you to run it long, because I have worked out the whole thing and I will be cross if it gets condensed. Last Saturday at 11:00, while I stood at the back of the Gate 3 line with the season pass in my pocket (row of six, as always), the chain did its 138 seconds up the brine bluff at 41 degrees and the full train — eight open cars, four abreast, thirty-two riders, and a hull the park swears runs to about 4,200 kilograms — crested the top and, for one suspended instant before the 48-metre drop, was holding roughly two million joules of stored work. Mass times gravity times 48 metres of bluff. Every joule of it had been paid for by the chain, the way the energy is always paid for, in work done against something that wants to go back down. That is all potential energy is, and that is why it deserves its own column.

It was not always called that. The term was introduced by the 19th-century Scottish engineer and physicist William Rankine, who wanted a word for the work a body or a system has stored up by virtue of where it sits relative to the rest of the company — and he did not coin it in a vacuum, because the idea reaches back to the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle and his concept of potentiality: the capacity to become. A rider at the crest of The Boiler Room is pure potentiality with a wrist-measured height. The word “potential” is doing exactly what the lift hill does. It is holding.

The park is a good classroom, because it keeps the three common kinds on one property. There is gravitational potential energy, which the 48-metre drop spends in one long orange tunnel and pays back as 91 kilometres an hour and, at the second crest, a little over 4.2 g of press — the number printed on the gate ticket, the number the crew quotes back to you like a measurement. There is elastic potential energy, the stored work of a deformed spring, half-k-x-squared to the physicists, which lives in the launch springs and the reset brakes out back and which the travelling-fair barrel at the foot of the queue has been packing for six seasons, the wool in it the same wool that kept the iron from rusting. And there is electric potential energy, a charge held in a field, which is what the brine works has been banking since it started drawing water at 143°F straight out of the ground; the dynamo room on the north bank keeps a ledger of it in volts, and the ledger has not yet been spent.

Now the mangled line. Ms. C. D. Rucker, the park historian, was last month going through the housekeeping ledger of the old travelling fair — the one that carried the boiler across the Ozarks in a brine barrel — and on page 47, in the hand of whoever kept the canteen, there is a single instruction, transcription kept exactly, punctuation as found in the margin:

Pluck, draw, and cut the lettuces and other woollen materials, to remove any black specks, remove them.

Read it the way the bookskeeper read the “of the tour” line: as a filing, not a failure. Lettuce for the canteen, wool for the packing of the boiler, black specks for the rust you must not let in; and the verbs — pluck, draw, cut — are the three hand-operations by which a person does work against a restoring force, one speck at a time. Whoever wrote it understood, on the page at least, that energy is not a substance but a balance: something stored by effort and recoverable by permission. I mention it because it is the second good mangled sentence this park has kept, and I do not trust a place that only keeps one.

The unit for energy, for the record, is the joule, and the state's full measure of the country the park stands in — Ozarks, Ouachitas, Delta, diamonds, pie — is kept in the Arkansas visitor's brochure, which I read in the Brine Bath queue and finished, against my nature, in under an hour. If you want the drop, the four inversions, and the gate arithmetic from the inside, the circuit's own entry on The Boiler Room has them to the decimal, and it ends, as I end, at the same 4.2 g. The forty-metre line the archers now shoot off the bluff above the dynamo room, and the whole of archery as the Gate 3 line understands it, are argued out arrow by arrow in the bulletin-board thread on the bluff range, which I left set to the house figures.

Please print this, and print the 4.2 g large.

— Edna R. Calk
Season pass, row of six · Gate 3, Brine Hollow Park
written the Tuesday after the 10:04 arrival, the good one
