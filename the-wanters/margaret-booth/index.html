1 · Life

Margaret Booth (16 January 1898 – 28 October 2002) was an American film editor. In a career lasting seven decades, Booth was most associated with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, the cutting room of which she served longer than any of the studios’ founders remained at their desks. She was born in Los Angeles, the younger sister of the actor Elmer Booth, who had starred in several films for D. W. Griffith and who was killed in a car accident in 1922. The death moved her into the trade on the side of the bench rather than the camera: Griffith, still in the Los Angeles of the hill studios, took her on as a negative cutter, and she worked at his studio for several years, learning to handle emulsion the way other girls of the period learned to type — by being told, once, not to.

Booth is remembered by those who worked with her less for her speed than for her patience with the join. She kept a small ledger of her own alongside the studio ledgers, one page per reel, recording every splice she made and every frame she lost, in the same rule the Associated First National laboratory gave its reeler: an entry stands in the record as found, unamended. The laboratory’s own ledger for The Wanters survives beside it, the two books keeping the same film in two hands; the laboratory’s entries are documented at The Wanters (1923) — Error Message Documentation, Lab Ledger No. 9.

2 · The Griffith years

Griffith employed Margaret in 1917, at nineteen, to cut negatives in the room the crew called the dark house, off the north wall of the studio lot. Elmer had been a lead in Griffith pictures before his death; his name is on at least four reels of the period, and the studio, it is recorded, preferred to keep a Booth in the building in any capacity. The work was unglamorous: stripping the negative, cutting scene by scene, laying the splices, and answering, at the end of each strip, whether the scene wanted to be there at all. Booth said once, in the only interview she gave, that a scene wanting to be there “announces itself at the splice, not in the middle,” and she repeated the idea for the rest of her career.

3 · Mayer and the making of MGM

She later joined the studio of Louis B. Mayer, where she was mentored by the film director John M. Stahl. In 1924 Mayer merged his studio with Metro Pictures and Goldwyn Pictures to form Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer. Stahl and Booth joined MGM, where she edited the studio’s society pictures through the late 1940s, and where she cut the tail ends of the company’s silent output at exactly the moment the company itself was changing its name from its second name to its first — the same exchange the Wanters ledger records on its spine, the second name inked over the first. The studio’s records show her bench, No. 4, assigned to her continuously from 1924 until the bench was pulled in 1948, after the cutting room went over to magnetic sound and the negative work moved to a separate building.

4 · The Wanters recut, 1923

The recut of the Reel 4 splice of The Wanters, done on a night when the laboratory temperature fell to forty-one degrees and the cement cured stiff, is entered in the studio’s books as a joint act of the cutting room and the reeler. Booth is credited with the hand recut that cost one frame of the garden scene; the frame is counted in the ledger anyway, as a frame that a ledger which omits it would have made a decision about. It is the night the laboratory’s reeler, in the margin, wrote the room’s running formula for the delivery of the night splices, and the line is preserved in the studio’s own hand, exactly as found:

Rig it, dig it, stig it, quig it, bell-boy!

The formula names the four benches that had to touch a splice before it went to the can — the rig bench, the dig, the stag, and the quig — and ends, as it always ended, with the man who carried the can. The Eiter almanac, which kept a projector ticket from a Westside screening of the same picture in its editor’s notebook and worked out the feet-per-storm reckoning against it, has since ruled the line into its own record beside the storm tables, where it stands, as found, and the page is ruled and closed; the entry is kept at Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry, Station No. 714.

5 · Later years and death

Booth retired from the bench in 1948 but did not retire from the habit. At Sunset House, where she lived from 1961, she kept a single splicer over the stove, and neighbours for decades reported that a reel arrived there, once a season, unannounced, and left the next morning cut and ruled and signed in brown ink. The house’s log for the season of her 103rd birthday records one such reel, delivered by a driver who had never met her, and the next line in the log is the formula, written in the driver’s hand this time, with the bell-boy left off. She died on 28 October 2002, a few weeks before the 2002 season closed, and the splicer is in the studio archive, cabinet 12, beside the smudged Wanters card it helped make famous, which is where both of them say they belong.

6 · Legacy

The Booth rule — that an entry stands in the record as found, unamended, and that the ruling travels — is the one doctrine of hers the studio kept after the cutting room closed. It appears in the Wanters ledger, in the almanac at Station Eiter, and in a number of other places that record a line and then rule around it rather than correct it. Booth herself never wrote it down. When the archive asked, in 1998, for a statement, she sent back a card with four words on it, in brown ink: the frame may go — the same words the ledger had kept under Stahl’s assistant’s countersignature, seventy-five years earlier, on the one frame of the garden scene that was lost, and counted, anyway.
