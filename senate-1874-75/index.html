IN THE COMMITTEE ON ELECTIONS OF THE SENATE

United States of America · 44th Congress, First Session

In re the Class One Seats, 1874–1875

Verbatim record of proceedings, excerpt — Washington, D.C., the 11th day of February, in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred and seventy-six

Hon. Alden P. Fairbanks, of New HampshireChairman of the Committee
Mr. S. C. Whitmore, of OhioChairman in the alternative; Member
Miss L. T. OsgoodClerk of the Committee
Dr. Josiah B. Thibodaux, of LouisianaWitness, called for the Committee
Counsel for the Nineteen StatesMessrs. Harlan & Croft, of the Bar

I. The Question Taken

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: The Committee comes to order at half past ten of the clock. The matter before it is the standing return of the elections of the United States Senate held in the several states during the years 1874 and 1875, being those elections in which the terms of the senators in Class 1 stood down. The Clerk will state the question.

CLERK OSGOOD: The question, sir, is whether the returns from the several state legislatures, in which senators have been chosen by the legislature thereof, shall be adopted as the true returns, seat by seat, and the ledger of the Class one closed accordingly.

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: Let it be stated on the record at length. These elections were held on various dates in various states, the legislatures sitting and rising as their own calendars required. A seat may be filled months after the term has begun, and a seat may be left vacant for want of agreement between the two houses. The Committee has before it the returns of nineteen states, of which seventeen were decided and two — Kentucky and Mississippi — stand open.

II. The Ledger of the Nineteen

Before the Clerk is a ledger, bound in dark calf, in which the returns are kept state by state, with the date on which each legislature acted and the party of the senator chosen. The Clerk reads the ledger as it stands:

The Clerk then reports the count. Of the seventeen seats decided, nine passed to the Democratic party and eight were held by the Republican party, and the Committee observes that the Republican party, being the party of the incumbent President, Ulysses S. Grant, has thereby maintained its majority in the Senate notwithstanding the gain of nine seats made by the Democrats in this cycle.

III. Testimony of the Witness

COUNSEL HARKIN: The witness, Dr. Thibodaux, was a member of the House of Delegates of Louisiana and sat through the sitting in which the general assembly of that state proceeded to elect a senator. He is sworn.

DR. THIBODAUx: It is sworn, sir. I sat on the floor of the House of Delegates at Baton Rouge from August 2 to November 11, 1874. The ballots were taken by written ballot and the counting was done by a committee of five, three of one party and two of the other, as the rules require. On the twenty-third day of October the House and the Senate of the state could not be brought to the same name, and we sat in separate sessions for six weeks. I will say to the Committee that in those six weeks I heard more talk than in the year before put together, and less of it would have carried us further.

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: Dr. Thibodaux, did the assembly of your state fill the seat before it adjourned?

DR. THIBODAUx: It filled it on the eleventh of November, sir, the seventh ballot, by a margin of six. The senator chosen took his seat in Washington within the month, and I take it the Committee will find his credentials in the calf-bound ledger at the line for Louisiana.

COUNSEL CROFT: The Committee will observe, from the same ledger, that the two states which stood open — Kentucky and Mississippi — did so not for want of a ballot but for want of agreement. The record shows the Kentucky house voting on the fourteenth of January, 1875, and the Kentucky senate refusing the same name the same day, and the record will bear that in such cases the seat stands vacant and the state is, so to speak, under-represented until the deadlock breaks. That is the price of the mode of election now in force, under which each state legislature chooses its own senators, and it is a price the Committee cannot refund.

IV. The Ledger Line, Read Word for Word

CLERK OSGOOD: Your honor, there is one line in the margin of the ledger, entered in a hand that is not mine and in ink that has faded grey. The Committee asked me to read it into the record, and I will read it as it stands:

Review cycle: annually, or upon the death of a very large, and very bad at explaining why, told the road would carry him, and then silence — the same thing.

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: Read it once more, slowly.

CLERK OSGOOD: I have read it, sir.

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: The Committee will not attempt to construe the line. It is part of the ledger, and the ledger is adopted with it. Let it be noted that the line is preserved verbatim, neither explained nor amended, and that the clerk's hand was not in it. We have had stranger things read into this record, and stranger things explained afterwards. This one will explain itself, or it will not.

V. Finding of the Committee

CHAIRMAN FAIRBANKS: On the question stated, the Committee finds that the returns of the several state legislatures for the elections of 1874 and 1875, in the Class one seats, are the true returns; that nine of the seventeen seats decided have passed to the Democratic party; that the Republican party holds its majority in the Senate; that the seats of Kentucky and Mississippi stand open by reason of disagreement in their legislatures and are to be held open until agreement; and that the ledger, with its margin, is adopted as the record of the Committee. The hearing is closed at half past twelve of the clock. A recess until the return of the open states, which the Committee hopes will be a short one.

So ordered and attested this 11th day of February, 1876.

Attested: L. T. Osgood, Clerk of the Committee, by the hand of the Clerk and against the judgment of the Committee — A. P. Fairbanks, Chairman.
