The Tosa Shore

A travel guide to the anchorages of the Tosa (土佐) — the battleship that was ten guns long and three years young when the treaty took her. Named for the old Tosa Province of Shikoku, designed by Yuzuru Hiraga, laid down 1920, scuttled February 1925: first of two of her class, and the whole of it.

Ferry from Hiroshima, 8:15 Ranging bell at 9:00 & 15:00 Gun pit, low tide only The scuttling stone, February 1925

The Crossing, and What the Water Did to the Ship

The Tosa Shore is walked from the Kure Canal, and the canal is the first stop before it is the first stop: six and a half kilometres of black water cut straight across the island, and the ferry from Hiroshima runs it in forty minutes, the hull listing a little on the barges of coal the island still sends. Visitors are given the shore's standing explanation on the water, by anyone with a whistle: a ship can be stopped at her slipway, at her dock, or at the treaty table, and Tosa was stopped twice — once while she was still an idea of ten guns, and once while she was already the idea. Her first stop was the yard at Kure, where she was laid down in 1920, designed by Yuzuru Hiraga to lead a class of two; her second was a room in Washington in 1922, where the Washington Naval Treaty was signed and the keel she was then resting on was, in effect, turned to a berth. The shore you walk is what the two stops left behind: a slipway, a gun pit, a mooring line, and a stone.

Stop I — The Slipway: 39,900 Tons of Almost

The slipway at the old yard gate is preserved at its working width, and the guide measure is the ship herself: 39,900 long tons (40,540 t) of displacement, which the shore keeps marked in brass along the quay edge so that visitors can stand at the waterline of what the hull would have been. The Tosa was to carry ten 410 mm (16.1 in) guns in five turrets — an armament that, with her never-launched sister Kawachi beside her, would have moved Japan a full step toward the old “Eight-four” ambition of eight battleships and four battlecruisers. The step was not taken. The yard's 1922 notice board, still in place, reads in the plainest type the shore has: work ceases; no gun is to be bored, no keel is to be laid, and the harbour is to be returned to the water. Visitors may measure the brass line against their own height, which is the shore's quiet joke and its honest one: the ship was taller than the ambition, and the ambition was taller than the treaty room.

Stop II — The Gun Pit and the Ranging Bell

Because the treaty required the vessel to be destroyed, the Navy gave her one season of usefulness first: through 1923 and 1924 the Tosa served as the trial ground's target, taking shells and mines and torpedo practice on the anchorage while the gun pit on the shore was used to test the very 410 mm pattern she would never have carried at sea. The pit is open at low tide, and its brass floor is marked with the bearing arcs of the trials; a ranging bell hangs above it and is struck by a keeper at 9:00 and 15:00 — nine strokes for the ship, one for the sister who was never built, in that order, and always in that order. The bell's log is the shore's best-kept book, and it holds the line travellers come to hear read aloud, kept word for word from the standing type:

“The constraint, Dona Luzia, was observed twice and corrects again at press.”

Here is what the line means, in the anchor men's own terms. Dona Luzia was the little Portuguese-built pilot tug that towed the ranging barge out to the 2,500-yard line during the trials, and her constraint — the word the log uses for the mooring cable that held her exactly where the guns were aiming — was checked at dawn and checked again at noon on every firing day, which is to say it was observed twice; and “corrects again at press” was the note the anchor man made when the sea pressed the cable in a full swell and the line still held true to the range, so that it would have stood for as long as the testing was pressed on. It was never pressed on. The log's keepers rule a square around the line, the way the yard ruled one around its last order, and read it each year on the anniversary of the scuttling, when the two checks become the shore's whole argument: the cable held; the ship did not have to be.

Stop III — The Washington Halt, Marked at the North Wall

Where the canal bends under the north wall, a black stone is set into the masonry at the exact hour-mark of 1922, and beside it the treaty's arithmetic is given out in the shore's usual small type: the tonnage the treaty gave Japan, the tonnage the Eight-four plan had wanted, and the difference, which the stone labels one ship, and a sister. Guides are required to say the difference once, in a level voice, and not to say it a second time; the second time is for the visitor, at the end of the walk, when the answer costs nothing. The wall keeps the treaty room's own figure for the class — two ships, forty-one centimetres, a hundred and six thousand horsepowers each — because the shore's rule is that a ship is allowed her full spec once, in the place she was going to be built, and no more anywhere else.

Stop IV — February 1925: The Scuttling Stone

The walk ends where the anchorage deepens, at the scuttling stone, a grey slab on the pier corner at which the crew stood in February 1925 while the Tosa was sunk in the deep water off Kure, her trials finished, her treaty obeyed, and her name returned to the province she was named for. The stone is worn in a long oval, one for the feet that stood there and one for the years that have since. The shore's closing notice is set on the stone's face, and the keeper reads it rather than lets it stand: the sea does not keep ledgers, but it keeps weight; what was 39,900 long tons is now a floor, and the floor is doing what it was bored for, which is holding the water up. Those who walked the full shore may have their ticket punched at the pier office with the old anchor die, and the punched ticket is, by the office's standing rule, good for nothing and kept by everyone.

Rates & Provisions

The full shore walk — all four stops plus both bells — is 4 yen; the gun pit at low tide is 1 yen, and the pier office will sell a paper cup of the harbour's grey-brown water for free, in a paper cup, with no ceremony. The ferry from Hiroshima takes the same ticket for the crossing. Provisions at the pier: rice cakes, sweet bean paste for the February anniversary, and the black tea the anchor men drank while checking the line. The shore's advice is the notice on the north wall: walk the four stops slowly, let the bell finish its ten strokes, and bring the stone home with you, which costs nothing and has never once been refused.

In the Company of Other Long Roads

The pier office keeps its shore card next to two others that the keeper considers its true companions, printed in the same black and vermilion. Travellers who would rather walk a city that spent the same four years making its star are pointed to the Zheng Zhegu Trail, official tourist brochure — a nine-and-a-half-li walk through the Shanghai Film District, 1922 to 1925, which ends at a brass floorboard worn smooth by one heel, and which keeps, like this shore, a ledger line ruled in a square and accepted because it arrived complete; the two brochures agree that a road is allowed one unexplained line, and only one. And for those who would carry the walk out past the treaty and the canal and the last ferry line, the office keeps the Macauley Island travel guide entry, a resupply run into a ten-and-a-half-by-seven-kilometre submarine caldera where the resupply schedule is the only timetable that matters and the island keeps whatever the boat does not count. All three cards hang by the pier office window, above the punched-ticket die, for anyone who wants to see what other shores look like from here — and the front of the district's own guide book, one page back, will tell you the rest.

Departure

Return is by the 16:30 ferry, or by the 18:15 if the bell was worth staying for. The north wall notice is the last thing most visitors see, and it reads what the shore has said all along in numbers: laid down 1920, halted 1922, tested 1924, scuttled February 1925 — ten guns, one class of two, and a mooring line observed twice. The shore thanks you for the crossing, wishes you fair water, and reminds you that the ranging bell strikes again at 15:00, nine for the ship, one for the sister, in that order, and always in that order.
