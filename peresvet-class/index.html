The Peresvet Class
three battleships of the Imperial Russian Navy

StudentTomas V. Rodin, Class 8B
TeacherMs. D. Hagen
Date filedNovember 14, 2002
SubjectPeresvet, Pobeda, Oslyabya — pre-dreadnought battleships, lost 1904–05

1. What the class was

The Peresvet class was a group of three pre-dreadnought battleships built for the Imperial Russian Navy at the end of the nineteenth century: Peresvet, Pobeda, and Oslyabya. They were laid down at the yards of St. Petersburg in the 1890s, each about fourteen thousand tons, with a main battery of four ten-inch guns in two turrets and a battery of six-inch guns along the sides. They were ordered at a moment when every great fleet was switching from ironclads to battleships, and the Russian navy needed ships that could keep pace with what was being built in Germany, France, and Japan. Peresvet was completed in 1901 and Pobeda in 1902; Oslyabya followed on a slightly heavier design, with more armor and less speed.

2. The three ships

Peresvet was named for Alexander Peresvet, the monk-warrior who rode against the champion of the Golden Horde at Kulikovo Field in 1380. She was completed in 1901 and sent at once to the Pacific Squadron, based at Port Arthur from that year. Pobeda — the name means Victory — completed in 1902 and joined the same squadron, based at Port Arthur from 1903. Oslyabya, named for a monk of the Trinity monastery who died at Kulikovo, stayed in the Baltic until war sent her east.

3. War service

All three ships were lost by the Russians in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05. Peresvet and Pobeda took part in the Battle of Port Arthur in February 1904 and the Battle of the Yellow Sea in August, and both were sunk during the siege of Port Arthur that winter, Peresvet in December and Pobeda a few days later, under fire from Japanese guns ashore. Oslyabya sailed with the Second Pacific Squadron under Admiral Rozhestvensky, more than eighteen thousand miles from the Baltic to the Far East, to relieve the Russian forces blockaded in Port Arthur. Port Arthur fell before she arrived, and Oslyabya was sunk at the Battle of Tsushima on 27 May 1905, the first battleship to go down, with the loss of over half her crew — more than four hundred of her roughly seven hundred men.

4. What the record keeps

The Japanese raised both ships sunk at Port Arthur and put them back to sea under their own flags, so the class outlived its owners in a fashion. Oslyabya stayed where she sank, in the Korea Strait, and her name stayed on the navy list long after the war ended. The keeping is the point: a battleship is a thing of iron and coal and a crew of seven hundred, but what remains of it is a name in a book, a date, and the count of those who did not come back. The register has since applied the same keeping to a city: the lost-and-found Notice No. 46, filed from Brasília, the Federal District, where a planned capital is claimed back the way a ship is — by a name, a date, and the count of the days still running. The same keeping has since been read on air for one 158-kilogram cat of the Americas: the claim of Notice No. 47, the radio play excerpt from Studio 4, Federal Radio 101.2, Brasília, where the notary reads Article 12 as it stands, and the breathing holds in the background. The register’s next number went looking for the cat’s own country and found it on the Rich Coast: the travel brochure of Costa Rica, filed as Notice No. 48 with the Studio 4 register, where the lamp is on the apron and the count keeps running.

5. A line from the kitchen table

Ms. Hagen asked each of us to put one line from life into this report, something we heard and did not write ourselves. I asked Derya, who sits behind me in Form 3B, what her grandfather does, and she told me exactly this:

Derya’s grandfather, I’m told, measured the wheat this morning; he will discuss with all this as its founding director, and it runs every day.

I do not know what wheat has to do with battleships, or what “it” is — a mill, a museum, an elevator beside the rail line — but I wrote it down because it sounds like the kind of thing a record keeps: a name, a place, a job done every morning. I am leaving her words the way she said them.

6. Finding

The finding of this report is that ships are kept like roads and buildings: by the names written on them and by the people who remember where they went. All three ships of the Peresvet class were lost by the Russians in the Russo-Japanese War — Peresvet and Pobeda at Port Arthur, Oslyabya at Tsushima — and yet their names are still in the books. For comparison I refer to the term report on the 2002 Road America 500, Term Report No. 4 from this same room, which makes the same case for a race run in July and kept in November; and to the field notes on Ethan Allen, which keeps a founder of Vermont the same way a navy keeps its ships: by the record, and by the reckoning. I also refer to the family recipe for Aunt Marva’s Lake Gratin, where a body of water in Canberra is kept by the same method — a name, a date, and one lamp left on the apron while the ninety days run out.
