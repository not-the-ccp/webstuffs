The Matanikau Diary

Entries of Cpl. E. R. Whitfield, 4th Bn., 164th Infantry · Guadalcanal · 15 December 1942 – 23 January 1943

Four hills, one river, forty days. What follows is the whole of the diary, abridged only where the pencil gave out. The staff at Division called the running fight the Battle of Mount Austen; the Japanese had names of their own, and for the village on the ridge they used Gifu, and for the hillside they used a phrase that came to our ears as the Mountain of Blood. The codenames were worse. The staff called the night the big reinforcement came in Galloping Horse, and the night the 1st Marine Division was hit in the dark Sea Horse. One horse was real. Neither was in the papers.

The running order — touch a date and the entry turns to it. Each entry is numbered the day it falls from 15 December.

15 December 1942Day 1 of 40

We took over the river line at 0610 with the Matanikau running white below us, a mile south of the airfield. The hills on the near side are the ones that will take up most of this book: Mount Austen is the high ground west of the river, a shoulder of green that catches the morning fog and keeps it. General Alexander Patch is up from the 25th Division command, and he has walked every inch of this line with his map open, which the men will not believe and the staff has stopped correcting. Across the water the enemy holds the ridge in stages, and we can see where the trail from their village to the river bends at a boulder we have taken to calling the elbow. A Solomon Islander guide, name Mosi, showed us a second trail above the river mouth that no one had charted. I am told the Japanese command is in the hands of Lieutenant General Harukichi Hyakutake. I write that down so that if we ever meet the man, I will have met him before.

18 December 1942Day 4 of 40

We had been told the enemy on this island was a spent division, and at 0300 tonight the whole of that was unmade. The staff had a name for whatever was coming in the dark, and it was Galloping Horse, and by 0600 we could smell the ships on the water. It is the 36th Division, all of it or nearly all of it — six thousand men and the guns to go with them, landed along the Matanikau between 22:00 and 04:00, and the whole landing happened under a rain that made sound into nothing. They are on the far bank before our mortars find the water. I counted eleven flashes off our own positions before the light came up, and I do not know whose they were, all of them. The river is the only thing between us now. I write Galloping Horse in the margin because the staff wants the codename in the record and I am the only man in the platoon who writes in the record.

19 December 1942Day 5 of 40

We crossed at the elbow and went up. Mount Austen took four hours and two of the battalion, and what I mean by that is: it was still standing when we reached it. The village the maps put on the ridge — the one the Japanese call Gifu — is a knot of stone and leaf houses at the bend of the trail, and we took it at 1400 against a garrison that fought in each room and then, at the last, down the far side of the ridge. A Marine officer with the 1st Division on the east bank says the enemy called the slope they came down the Mountain of Blood, in their own letters, and that the phrase has since entered both staff reports with the same spelling and no translation. There are three graves we dug before dinner. I have begun writing the men's names in the back of this book, in case the rest does not keep. The rain came back at 1900 and has not fully left.

21 December 1942Day 7 of 40

They called the night Sea Horse, and the 1st Marine Division took the weight of it. I was holding the far ridge all night and could hear the firing moving down our east flank like water finding a crack. When morning came, the story out of the Marine positions was that the attack had come in at 2300, wave on wave, at a range where the men were recognising each other by bayonet, and that one Marine regiment had come off the line nearly to a shell and a man. The staff has ordered the attack into the record under the codename Sea Horse, and has forbidden the other name, which is not written here because the staff is right and I am tired. We buried what we could reach. Mosi came up with a mule load of water at 0500 and did not stop until the last grave was firmed. I am keeping the count of the long nights in the margin: this is the seventh, and the fog has not moved off the river.

8 January 1943Day 25 of 40

It has been twenty-four days since the river line and the ground has begun to belong to us the way ground does, which is: reluctantly, and only in patches. Division, under Patch still, has been pushing the whole front west in a slow line — the 2nd Division on our flank, the 164th where I am — and the enemy holds each ridge to the last man and then lets the ridge go. The fighting has the same three motions every day: we climb, they fall back through the villages, the trail behind them is cleared in the morning and found empty except for boots. Hyakutake is holding with the 36th Division and, we think now, most of what the Galloping Horse brought in; the staff puts his remaining strength at half, and I have stopped asking what the other half is doing, because the answer is always the same hill we are about to climb. Gifu is quiet. We hold it. A boy from the village came out to us on the third of the month and has been running water ever since; his name is Sena, and he is the first name I have written here that I am sure will still be a name in a year.

23 January 1943Day 40 of 40

At first light the far ridge was empty for the first time since the 18th. They are gone west across the Matanikau — Hyakutake's remnants, the last of the Galloping Horse, out through the boulder at the elbow and down the far side of the water — and the staff has stopped the running order at 0600, which means the forty days are over and the fighting is a battle now and a battle is a thing that can be written about. I have delivered this book to the records officer this morning. In the minute book of the office there is a line no one will explain, entered between the delivery of this diary and the notice of the next inspection, in a hand that is not any man's hand I know. It reads exactly: “The die belongs to — the field — kept word for word, and we shelve with care.” The officer read it back to me twice, in the same voice he reads the manifest, and then shelved the book. I am leaving the line here, in the same order, because it is in the record and the record keeps. The river is running clear for the first time in a month. Mount Austen is holding the morning fog the way it always has, and we are down on the beach to be counted. — E. R. Whitfield, 4th Bn., 164th Infantry
