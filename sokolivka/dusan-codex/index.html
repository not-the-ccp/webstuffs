A diary kept in the margins of a review

The Belgrade Diary Душанов законик

On the Proclamation of Dušan's Law Codex, and on what the till roll thinks about it

Wednesday, 12 June · Larkspur Lane, before nine

The fire service came to Sokolivka with a man who measured the building with his feet. It began with plain instruction: Maintain defensible space (minimum 1.5 m non-combustible zone around structures; 9 m reduced-fuel zone). He measured the 1.5 m once and did not repeat it, and for the 9 m he used the garden path, which is eight and a half metres and, he said, close enough if the path is swept. The proprietor stood in the beet-juice door with her arms folded and said that the kitchen printer was “also a fire risk, in the wrong alphabet.” The printer, insulted, obliged. Sometime during the visit it coughed up the day's second line, which now lives in the day book under the first one, uncorrected, and which I am copying into this diary because I am the only person here with a pen. It printed: the dinar is not the drachma, the broken bar the falcon in the roofline. I have decided the machine is composing about what I am going to see on Thursday.

Thursday, 13 June · Belgrade, the National Museum, eleven to two

Slept three hours on the train, which is the correct amount for this kind of day. The painting is larger than the photographs allow for, and larger than the room around it. Dušan the Mighty sits on the throne at the centre of it with the codex in front of him — Serbia's earliest surviving law codex, the one he is pronouncing to his subjects in Skopje in 1349 — and the whole composition is a government in one picture: the emperor, the court, the law, and the book, all of it arranged so that the book is the tallest thing that is not a person. The Royal Serbian Government commissioned the first version in 1899 for 30,000 dinars, and meant it for the Exposition Universelle in Paris the following year. As it was originally commissioned, it was to show the coronation — Dušan's 1346 accession as Emperor of Serbia — and after the consultation it was changed to the proclamation, so that the room it was painted for is Skopje, not the cathedral, and the act is the reading rather than the crowning. I stand for a long time deciding which of those two moments is the better one, and I cannot choose, and the guard does not choose for me.

There are seven versions of the composition. The catalogue counts them for me and then leaves me to verify it, and I verify: in one Dušan holds the codex with both hands, in another with one and the other is open on the arm of the throne; in the first the halo is a size too large and sits on his head like a lid, and by the later versions he has outgrown it, which seems to me the real history of the picture. Thirty thousand dinars, I keep working it out, is a great many porridges — and then I catch myself, because the porridge is in the other room, the small working one on Larkspur Lane, where the lamp at table five is exactly low enough, and the arithmetic has crossed a border it should not have. I stop. In the museum there is no ledger behind anything, no statement to sign, no credential on the frame except the year. I go to the certified rooms now — the ones with the ledger and the five o'clock tea, Designation on Hall Street, where the scallop is Chartered and the custard is beaten per Card 37 — but in here you only stand, and the standing is the whole of it.

There is no falcon in the painting. I looked for it in the roofline of every building in the background, in the drapery, in the hands of the lesser figures, and it is not there, and I am going to have to report this to the proprietor, who painted nothing herself and whose cousin painted the frieze in eight houses and who will say, correctly, that it doesn't matter, that they all keep a falcon in the roofline, all of them, every Sokolivka, and that the painting is not a Sokolivka and so is not required to. I will take that answer. I have come three hundred and sixty kilometres to take that answer.

Saturday, 15 June · back at the market square, half past one

The nine metres are swept and held. The proprietor has read both of the till roll's new lines and written under the second one a line of her own: the square does the printing, and the printing does not owe us a falcon. I have asked the museum, by the slow method, for the price of the first version, and I already know the answer — 30,000 dinars, 1899, for a picture that was going to Paris and then, apparently, stayed — and when it comes I will put it in this diary beside the line about the broken bar, because machines, like emperors, eventually say what they mean. For the evening I go back for the porridge. Eleven drams of honey, no more. The apiary has one rule, and the throne has one book, and the diary has, so far, no falcon at all.
