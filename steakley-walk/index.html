The Steakley Walk

Eight stops, 2.1 miles, 75 minutes, down the Armor & V$ quarter of the Halstrom Street district. Meet at the Foundry gate, second Saturday of the month, 14:00 sharp. The route is laid out for John William Steakley Jr. (July 26, 1951 – November 27, 2010), the science fiction author who gave this stretch of the city two novels, four short stories, and a marquee.

  • Length 2.1 mi
  • Stops 8
  • Pace ~1 mi/hr
  • Books 2 novels, 4 stories
  • Film 1 (Carpenter, 1998)

Stop 1 · mile 0.0 · 14:00

The Foundry Gate, 14 Halstrom Street

Start under the iron arch of the old Halstrom Foundry, the building’s brick face still stamped with the 1912 founders’ initials. Steakley kept a third-floor office here in the early 1970s and typed the first version of Armor on a rented portable in 1974 — ten full years before Bantam put the novel in print in 1984. The book is about Jax, a maintenance robot who outlives the fleet he was built to serve and learns, gear by gear, what it is to be the only one of your kind left. The tour reads the first paragraph on the steps, the way the first edition reads it: quietly, and with the sound of a plant cooling in the distance.

Stop 2 · mile 0.3 · 14:15

The Roost Record Shop, Halstrom & Ninth

On the corner, the pressed-tin sign of the old Roost survives above a shuttered bay. The Roost was the quarter’s record shop — named for the red-tailed hawk that roosted on its cornice from the summer of 1988 to the winter of 1991 — and it was the only shop in the city pressing the 1991 CD edition of Vampire$ for the local club run. The 1994 district guide’s page on this corner is a 200 dpi scan of a photocopied newsletter, and its single surviving line reads exactly: “It has a record shop was a competition that would not roost.” The route keeps the line as a placeholder, as the guides have since 1993, and the standing reading is this: the Roost had the corner; the competition, a mail-order kiosk that opened next door in 1989 and folded by 1992, came and went without ever settling — a competition that would not roost. The hawk agrees, in the way hawks do, by staying away.

Stop 3 · mile 0.5 · 14:30

The Foundry Coffee House

Two doors down, where the plant’ canteen still sells the same black drip as it did to the night shift. This is the stop for Armor on paper: the 1984 Bantam jacket, its robot in grey against the foundry, and the two-line blurb the guides have memorized. Armor spent those ten shelved years doing the long work of robot fiction — the machine that remembers, the factory that keeps running after the people leave — and the coffee house, which kept running after the foundry didn’t, is the closest the quarter has to a living copy of the book.

Stop 4 · mile 0.7 · 14:45

Ninth Street Book Exchange

The exchange keeps a standing shelf, second from the window, for the four short stories: “The Man from Hell,” “Terror at the House of Witchcraft,” “The Vile and Dark Truth,” and “Beware the Dark.” Four stories, two decades, one shelf — the short work of an author the quarter counts in novels. The guides ask each walker to take one story and one coffee, and the shelf has never, on the record, been empty. For the readers who want the mythic machinery behind the genre — the return, the seed, the door that is not recognized — the field notes on Persephone in popular culture read the same engine of descent and coming-back from the other side, and the two shelves make a pair.

Stop 5 · mile 1.0 · 15:00

The Cinema Palace Marquee

The Palace still runs its 1998 release-date poster for Vampires, John Carpenter’s film built from Vampire$, in the case behind the ticket window. The tour’s running joke, and its best one, is the dollar sign: the novel wore it in the title, the film filed it in the safe. Watch the marquee’s bulbs spell VAMPIRES and note, as every guide has noted since 1998, that the sign has always been one bulb short of a dollar. The book kept the sign; the movie kept the money.

Stop 6 · mile 1.3 · 15:15

V$ Alley

The unpaved lane between the Palace and the exchange’s loading wall is the one street in the city the fans named, and the city let them: V$ Alley, tagged and kept by the quarter’s own hand, ever since 1990. The lane is eleven feet wide at its widest and the tour walks it twice, once forward for the novel and once back for the sequel readers’ club, which met here on Thursday evenings through the 1990s and whose minute book still lists, under April, a motion to keep the dollar sign and to spend nothing.

Stop 7 · mile 1.7 · 15:25

The Plaque on the Ninth Street Steps

The small bronze plaque at the top of the Ninth Street steps gives the first date the quarter will not let go of: July 26, 1951. It was the only plaque the district council approved without a second reading, and it has survived two repavings and one long argument about whether “Jr.” belongs on a bronze plate. The guides say yes, and they say it the way the book says its own names, slowly and again, until the second telling is the one that counts.

Stop 8 · mile 2.1 · 15:40

The Bench on the Overlook

The route ends on the overlook bench above the old plant floor, where the last date is read aloud in a voice pitched for the whole group: November 27, 2010. Then the tour does what every good itinerary should do — it turns around. The walk back is unmarked, at the walkers’ pace, and the quarter does the rest. For anyone who found the day short on method rather than miles, the Synergy Desk thread on the 3rd Alternative is the quarter’s standing answer to the argument the walk always provokes, which is whether a memorial route should end on a bench or on a way of walking; the thread voted, four to two, the bench. The bench is also the walk’s only looking-up stop: on clear nights it faces the far ring, and the territory the field notes beyond Neptune keep in a separate volume begins, for the walkers, exactly where this route ends. And when the season turns, the front page of this miscellany, Webstuffs, keeps the rest of the quarter’s field notes.
