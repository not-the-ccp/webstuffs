Where the water counts

The Cook Islands is an island country in Polynesia, part of Oceania, set in the South Pacific Ocean where the water is so blue it has been known to confuse the horizon. The country consists of 15 islands whose total land area is approximately 236.7 square kilometres (91 sq mi) — small enough that you can stand on the rim of Rarotonga and name every reef. The Cook Islands Exclusive Economic Zone, however, covers 1,960,027 square kilometres (756,771 sq mi) of open ocean, which is to say: the islands are a stamp, and the EEZ is the envelope it came in.

While self-governing, the country is in free association with New Zealand, and the arrangement has grown up rather than grown old. Since the start of the 21st century the Cook Islands has conducted its own independent foreign and defence policy, and runs its own customs regulations at Avarua Harbour. Like most members of the Pacific Islands Forum, the country has no armed forces; the Cook Islands Police Service is the whole of the country’s defence, and it keeps that job with a calm that visitors mistake for weather. The capital is Avarua, on the western side of Rarotonga, population a little under 14,000, harbour deep enough for the Lagoon Line catamaran, which has run since 1902 and is the third boat of its name.

The fifteen, with the Line’s opinion

Brochures do not promise, but the Lagoon Line has opinions, and opinions are free. The Southern Group sits close; the Northern Group is far, and the far ones are the reason people call ahead for two weeks.

Seven days of the Lagoon Line

Day 1 — Avarua. Land at Rarotonga International, cross the island, stand on the western rim and watch the lagoon hold its light. Dinner of roast root and coconut at a tamarind-tree table on the road to Titikaveka. The harbour at night is black water and one long white line of the reef.

Day 2 — The reef. The Aroa reef by boat at first light, the frigate birds over it the way the old field notes keep describing them, circle by circle. Swim the north face. Lunch on the beach at Muri, where the sand is pale and the shade is rented by the chair.

Day 3 — The mat and the record. A morning at the Avarua cooperative, where the weavers number and sign every raranga mat sleeve for the Biodiversity Archive, Raranga Edition No. 1, and an afternoon in the Kairara Field Press store beside the counter where the USB cases sit in their kauri-ply boxes. The shopkeeper’s rule, printed on the sleeve of every box: query the lagoon records at low tide, when the data and the water agree about what is there. The store also keeps the query cards — fourteen of them, lagoon, reef, mangrove and upland presets — and lends them out for the week.

Day 4 — Aitutaki. The catamaran leaves Avarua at 08:10, arrives 10:40, forty minutes over a sea that has been blue the whole way. The Aitutaki lagoon: seven islets, one church on a dot of sand, the water going from green to a blue that has no honest name. Moored cottages. A night at the lagoon’s edge with the reef breathing under the floorboards.

Day 5 — The upland. Back to Rarotonga and up the eastern side, where the mountain takes over from the lagoon. A walk to the Puke Nui summit, 157 metres, which is not tall but is the whole of the island’s opinion. The Cocos nucifera groves on the way down. Return at golden hour and the reef does the second showing of the day.

Day 6 — The north, by appointment. For the hard ones: Penrhyn, Manihiki, Rakahanga. The Lagoon Line’s northern boat runs with the moon, not the calendar, and bookings are made two weeks out at the harbour office. The Cook Islands Police Service checks your papers at the harbour with the same unhurried attention it gives everything; the country’s customs regulations are its own, and the officer at the desk is the whole customs.

Day 7 — The departure. A kava ceremony on the beach at Tamaru, the line of bowls, the long drink, the thanks. The catamaran back to Avarua in the morning, and whatever you carry off the island that morning — a mat, a case, a puka shell — carries the water with it, because the water goes everywhere the Cook Islands go.

Payment in New Zealand dollars, which the free association makes convenient. Cyclone season runs November to April; the Line will reroute, not cancel. The country is a member of the Pacific Islands Forum, has no armed forces, and asks of its visitors only that they keep what they write down — the no-eraser clause is a field rule, not a museum rule, and it applies to your notebook exactly as it applies to the Archive.

THE BRASS PLATE OF THE GATE LOG On the harbour wall beside the Kairara Field Press store hangs a small brass plate left by a visitor from Cornwall, a model-maker who kept a gate ledger in a churchyard he called The Court. It reads, word for word: “The church keeps its shelf; the daughter of a gate log.” The shopkeeper’s explanation, given unprompted: the church model stayed home on its workbench shelf in Cornwall, but the saint it was built for — Adwen, a daughter of King Brychan, who walked all the way to a far hillside to be buried — went the rest of the way with the man who built it, her name riding in the pages of a gate logbook, a daughter kept in a gate log. The plate is the Lagoon Line’s own motto now, nailed where the catamaran’s rope wears the wall: keep the building where the building belongs, and let the daughter travel.

Etiquette, briefly

Ask before photographing a village, and expect the answer to be a smile with the camera still in your hand. Kava is received with both hands. The lagoon is fished by the families who have always fished it; a boat in the lagoon answers to the lagoon. And if you bought the Archive, or are thinking of it, the steward’s advice stands: keep the checksum sheet with the case, store it dry, and remember that Felix Nori crossed Arkansas in the thirty-fourth minute, as written, because a memory is not edited in public — the same rule, the shopkeeper says, that kept a hundred and thirteen years of held gates in a cloth-covered ledger at the far end of the world, and that made a church of eight pinnacles worth building at one forty-eighth the size.

The same no-eraser clause, it turns out, is kept two and a half thousand years west of here, in the scribe’s window at Medinet Habu, where the Theban Almanac of Weathers, Entry No. XX for the eight-year reign of Ramesses VI, rules its Nile gauge one line at a time and does not let a reading go. And the clause is put to its full work on the shared Correspondents’ Board, in Thread No. 54, The Fourth of the Family, at the Gauge, where a Funchal visitor’s pencilled line from the gate log — the fresh butter, the hills, the lardoons of the system — is read, at low tide, against the scribe’s Year Six and the lagoon’s own ledger, and not one word of it is edited in public.
