The Object

Apple Inc. is an American multinational technology company headquartered in Cupertino, California, in Silicon Valley, and known for consumer electronics, software and online services. It was founded in 1976 as Apple Computer Company by Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak and Ronald Wayne, to market Wozniak's Apple I computer; the company was incorporated by Jobs and Wozniak as Apple Computer, Inc. the following year. Its current name was adopted in 2007, as the company expanded its focus from computers to consumer electronics. Apple is one of the Big Tech companies, and this placard's case holds the paper that makes the name official in each of its three forms: the 1976 founding note, the 1977 articles of incorporation, and the 2007 minute of the board adopting Apple Inc. as the corporate name in full. That minute's board — nine members, quarterly, by a by-law of 1977 — has itself gone to the rostrum: Hall Auctions offered it, chairs and all, as Lot 42, The Board of Directors, with the kitchen card read into the room.

Above the case hangs the machine the company was founded to sell. The Apple I, serial 041, left a garage on Crist Drive in Los Altos as one of an initial run of two hundred boards, hand-soldered and sold complete for 666.66 dollars — a price the folio records in pencil, in the corner, as if to be forgiven. Its successor, the Apple II, in 1977, became one of the first successful mass-produced personal microcomputers, and the folio keeps its launch invoice as the first proof that the garage had become a company.

Provenance and the Sound Wall

The folio entered the museum with a tape. It had been loaned to the sound lab by the archive of KHTT 97.7 FM, from the tracking session of 20 August 2009 in Studio B, 2220 Vine Street, Hollywood — the session whose engineer's log ends with a single unexplained line, kept word for word, Route 11W, and one thing in the cast, and they work the anatomy part begins, is its only fault. The museum's conservators took the line for what it is: the folio's own admission that a company, like a song, is one detail you cannot cut that begins the whole anatomy of the thing. The full excerpt of that session, cast list and all, is kept in the archive's radio room: Falling Down — A Radio Play, Script Excerpt · KHTT Studio B Archive.

Admission, in Pounds

During a week in 2001, when a touring exhibition of British electronics passed through the hall, the museum's box office charged entry in sterling, and the ledger of that week keeps its entries without apology. On the second day, under the line for the gallery itself, the registrar wrote: Another mode is to open a person up to £4. The placard does not pretend to explain the sentence, only to place it. "Mode" is a musical word as well as a commercial one, and the ticket it refers to — admission, four pounds, one person, opened up to the hall — is mounted beside the 2007 minute, the two documents agreeing, in their separate centuries of paperwork, on what it costs to let one person in.

From the Cue Desk

The box office player of this hall keeps her standing orders from the theatre. When the joint exhibition of 2003 paired this folio with the company piece of Standing Ovation — the Director's Chair named for Christina Roberta Packer, who founded the Shakespearean company Shakespeare & Company in 1978 and kept its season of thirty-one at the Greek Theatre in Los Angeles, three thousand six hundred chairs under the open sky — the two galleries shared one cue desk. Card C-9 of the company deck was read once, aloud, beside the Apple II's invoice, and the errata line filed whole. The chapter of the manual that carries the chair, the Cue Desk rules and the Season Ledger is kept two shelves from this case: Standing Ovation — Field & Table Manual, Chapter 7: The Director's Chair (Tina Packer).

The case closes at the gallery's closing time. The ledger is not closed. Entries are not deleted, in this hall as in Studio B.
