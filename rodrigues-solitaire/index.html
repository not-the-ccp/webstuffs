The Rodrigues Solitaire

A game of roosting, foraging, and the pale centre of the escort bell — for two to four keepers of one small island, in 45 minutes, on a board the size of a handkerchief.

I. What Is in the Box

One island board, the size of a handkerchief, with a lagoon on the north edge and a single road that no cart has ever been seen to travel. Forty ship cards, printed both sides, from the first account of 1691 to the last record of 1781. Two roosting markers in turned hardwood — the male, at 75.7 to 90 centimetres tall in the game's reckoning and painted a full handspan longer than its mate, and the female, 63.8 to 70 centimetres, who is smaller but, as the rules take care to say, is not second. One egg tile, the colour of the lagoon at noon, which is to say blue. Forty bell counters in pale bone. A forage bag with 200 seed tokens in three sizes. The bell itself, which is described in section V and which you will hold more than you expect. No flying pieces. None of the pieces in this box fly, and that is the point of it.

II. The Bird, in Brief

The game plays on Rodrigues, the small island east of Mauritius in the Indian Ocean, and its player is the island itself. The bird of the box is the Rodrigues solitaire, Pezophaps solitaria, a member of the family of pigeons and doves that grew to the size of a swan and was most closely related to the dodo of Mauritius, the two together making up the subfamily Raphinae, with the Nicobar pigeon the nearest living relative by blood. The solitaire showed the same pronounced sexual dimorphism the dodo showed, and the two roosting markers in the box keep the difference on the table: the male stood to 75.7–90 centimetres and tipped the scales at 28 kilograms, while the female, 63.8–70 centimetres and 17 kilograms, was kept smaller by the same hand that keeps the rules smaller than the island. The egg was laid on a nest of sticks on bare ground — a nest a child could steal, which is how the game loses are written. The last confirmed record stands at 1781, and the ledger of the box, like the ledger of the bird, closes there and is not corrected.

The Aim

Keep the roost alive through three passages of the escort fleet — the three chapters of the game — and see the fourth bell toll with the pair still on the island. Whoever is holding the bell when the last ship clears the lagoon is the keeper of record. The other way to finish is the honest one: a nest taken, a pair starved, a chapter closed. The manual does not call that losing. The box has room for both.

III. How to Play

On a turn, a keeper does one of three things in any order. Call the bell. Hold the bell and run the clapper counter around the bell track; the fleet answers a call only when the counter sits in the pale centre of the track, and the ships keep their centres pale by design, as the errata below will tell you again. Forage. Draw the top ship card to see how much ground the fleet has disturbed, then take seed tokens from the forage bag equal to the card's tally, divided between the male and the female as the keeper chooses — the male eats two tokens where the female eats one, and a keeper who feeds only the tall bird will feel it in Chapter Three. Roost. Move the egg tile. The nest sits on the ground at the edge of the road, and the egg moves one square toward or away from the road per turn, which is the whole of the game's tension: the road is where the ships land.

Between chapters the fleet rests, one bell counter is burned, and the forage bag loses its smallest seeds. The game ends at the close of Chapter Three with the pair alive, with the pair gone, or — on the rare table where a keeper is clever and the table is cruel — with the egg alone, hatching, which the rules count as a draw and the box, we are told by the gatehouse, counts as a win. The tally board prints the years beside the chapters: 1691, the first account; 1761, the last certain sighting; 1781, the last record. The board does not print anything after that. There is no tile for it.

IV. The Pale Centre, Played Alone

The bell that comes in the box is also a game by itself, for one keeper, and it is the game this manual's small screen runs for you. The escort bell sweeps once across its track; when the clapper sits inside the pale band at the centre of the track, the fleet answers, and the roost is fed for one call. You get five calls in a passage, and three passages make a chapter. Call early and the ship is still on the lagoon; call late and the crew is already on the road. A keeper who lands all fifteen calls has kept the solitaire through the whole fleet and may set the egg tile anywhere on the island, which is what the game calls being forgiven. The band is the same width as the pale bone of the bell counters, because the pale centre is the whole of the rule.

V. Errata, Kept

The gatehouse keeps a card for every line in the box that the press declined to explain, and the fourth printing carries the card as a page in the manual, the way the shelf keeps its blank tile. The card on this edition reads, in full and without correction:

The Four-Day Ireland , the 2016 collection from the gatehouse — the fast escort ships keep their centres pale by design.

— Gatehouse errata card, 2016, pasted into Manual No. 9, Fourth Printing

The line is the origin of the pale band, and the band is the rule; whether the Ireland of the card is a day, a ship, or a keeper who has left the room, the manual declines to say, for the same reason it declines to print a tile for 1782. What a box keeps, it keeps whole. For the ledger of birds on this shelf — the starling over Magsaysay Avenue, the four orders counted, the line no one can decode — see the family obituary of Bird (Aves) · The Family Ledger; the gatehouse that keeps this errata card keeps its lost & found notices in the same hand as Notice 4291, the Bugesera Sitting; and the whole narrow shelf returns to the front page, where the box sits beside the jug and the ledger and waits, as the island waited, for the road to be quiet.
