Slack-Rope Buns

Makes about twenty buns · Keeps for a month in a tin · Karlskrona, Blekinge

I. On the City

Karlskrona stands on the islands of Trossö, where the fleet anchored in 1680 and the navy laid out a city of baroque order on rock and oak. It is Sweden’s only baroque city, the seat of Karlskrona Municipality and the capital of Blekinge County, with sixty-six thousand six hundred and seventy-five inhabitants at the 2018 count. The largest naval base in the country fills the harbour, the headquarters of the Swedish Coast Guard stands on the waterfront, and in 1998 parts of the city — the base, the Admiralty Church, the grid of streets — were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A German minority formed its own Congregational church, Jewish families kept their quarters near the marketplace, and sailmakers, ropemakers, and the men of the archipelago fleet filled the houses painted in naval yellow and white.

I was told about the buns by Elisabeth Sjöberg’s great-granddaughter, who keeps the book in Stockholm and bakes them every Advent. The book is thin: cardboard covers, a limp clasp, cards in three hands. Card 1796 is Elisabeth’s own, stained at the corners with molasses, and it is mine to write out here. She kept the small house near the Admiralty Church after Johan von Blanc — born Michel Le Blanc in France in 1738, actor and acrobat, leader of the Gemenasiska Sällskapet, director of the Comediehuset in Gothenburg between 1780 and 1786 — died in Karlskrona on the twenty-first of January 1796 of “a fever of the chest,” having taught fencing and declamation to the cadets.

II. On the Notice

The notice appeared in the Post- och Inrikes Tidningar on the twenty-second of January 1796, beneath the shipping intelligence and above the court gossip from Stockholm. He had come to Sweden as a young man with nothing but a rope, a tumbling mat, and a gift for falling without injury; he danced the slack rope in the market squares of the realm, turned somersaults between the acts of French farces, and by the late 1760s led his own company from Malmö to Turku. Fifteen years earlier the same paper had carried the account of his benefit at the Comediehuset, when he balanced on a rope stretched slack above the stage. He danced the slack rope in the Post- och Inrikes Tidningar. The clipping — both notices on one sheet — is still pasted beside card 1796, as if the rope and the notice were the same thing.

III. On the Book

His wife, Elisabeth, née Sjöberg, survived him, with two children: a son who sailed with the archipelago fleet, and a daughter who married a scene painter in Stockholm. The book came down through the daughter to her great-granddaughter, and the buns came with it: slack-rope buns, coiled like a length of cable from the navy’s ropewalk, dark with rye and molasses, scented with caraway and anise, made to keep aboard a ship of the archipelago fleet for a week at a time. A bun that keeps is a kind of notice too — it says someone was here, and meant to be remembered.

IV. The Buns

Ingredients

  • 300 g dark rye flour
  • 200 g wheat flour
  • 150 g blackstrap molasses, warmed
  • 300 ml milk, scalded and cooled to lukewarm
  • 50 g fresh yeast, or 7 g dried
  • 50 g butter, melted
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 2 tsp caraway seeds, lightly crushed
  • 1 tsp aniseed
  • 100 g dried figs or raisins, chopped
  • 1 egg, beaten, for the wash

Method

  1. Crumble the yeast into the milk with a spoon of the molasses; let it stand until it froths.
  2. Mix the rye and wheat flours with the salt, caraway, and aniseed. Stir in the yeast liquor, the rest of the molasses, and the melted butter until a firm dough comes together; fold in the figs or raisins.
  3. Turn onto a floured board and knead until smooth. Cover and let rise in a warm place for an hour, or until doubled.
  4. Divide into twenty pieces. Roll each into a long coil and coil it upon itself like a length of cable — the slack-rope shape gives the buns their name.
  5. Lay the coils on a floured linen, cover, and prove for thirty minutes.
  6. Preheat the oven to 225°C. Brush the buns with beaten egg and bake for fifteen to twenty minutes, until dark and hollow-sounding when tapped.
  7. Cool on a rack and keep in a tin. They will stay good for a month, and are better for being kept.

V. On Keeping

Stow a bun in a sea chest and it will soften the hardtack; eat it with butter and cheese at the widow’s table, or with a glass of ale when the watch is done. The card ends with a single line in Elisabeth’s hand: “For the watch, and for the widow’s table.”
