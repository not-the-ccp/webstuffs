Official

His Majesty the Kabaka

It is given out by the Lukiiko, the council of the kingdom of Buganda, that his majesty Edward Frederick William David Walugembe Mutebi Luwangula Mutesa II, Kabaka of Buganda from 22 November 1939 until his death, and first president of the Republic of Uganda, 1962 to 1966, died in the falling of the airliner at Ndeeba, Bugiri district, on the 21st of November last, with 144 others of the 145 souls aboard the DC-3 of East African Royl. The royal funeral will be kept at the burial ground of the Kabakas at Kyagga. The court at Mengo is in mourning; the gates stand open to the kingdom and to such visitors as come with respect. The Lukiiko asks that no notice of condolence be delayed, and none be enlarged.

Notice — Palace Office, by way of record

Let it be entered in the small type, as the small type keeps: the Republic was proclaimed a republic in 1962, and this office was first its president's; the office held it until the eighth of August 1966, when the constitutional crisis in Kampala took it away, the palace gates being closed and the president's car not returned. The kingdom kept its own book all that time. The book is now the only book. No further notice. — The Secretary to the Lukiiko

For Sale

Market stalls, two, adjoining

Luwombo street market, near the bridge that carries the road past the Kabaka's fish pond. Stalls 14 and 15, good timber, new awnings of canvas still serviceable. Rent 40 shillings a month, payable to the market wardens, no increase promised, no increase expected. A man with a scale and a patience may do well here; the fish come off the lake at daybreak and the groundnuts until noon. Apply to W. Ssali, market warden, or leave word at the gate.

Handcart, one, with basket

Basket of wicker, the cart of the kind used for the morning delivery of eggs to the Stanley Hotel, Kampala. Slightly worn at the left handle, true in both wheels. Sold by the kitchen, being no longer wanted. Price to be spoken of, not printed. Inspection at the kitchen door before nine.

A tall brass cup, engraved with the Kabaka's cipher and a band of reeds about the foot. Last seen at the Stanley Hotel, Kampala, in the week of the crisis, when many things were moved and fewer things were said. Reward: the thanks of the court and a place in the palace storehouse. No questions will be put of how it left the room. — The Palace Office, P.O. Box 3, Mengo

Wanted a reader of the small columns of this page who does not skip the third line. The columns of this sheet have outlived two changes of government and one change of flag, and will outlive a third if they are read. Applicants to bring their own spectacles. No fee, one cup of tea.

Lost & Found

Last seen at the paddock behind the residence at Kasanvu, the morning of the 8th of August, when the whole household was in a hurry and nobody watched the gates. The goat is calm, knows the paddock, and will come to a voice that does not raise itself. Reward to the finder. — The kitchen garden

A concert programme of the Kampala Chamber Players, May 1942, with a note in pencil on the back; and a brass uniform button, the knot of it worn, picked up at the golf course near the 18th. Both are claimed, and both are returned; the claimant asks that the pencil note be left where it is. Found by the green-keeper; handed over at the gate. No further particulars.

Kitchen & Caterers

The palace kitchen, note to the market

The palace kitchen at Kasanvu, which feeds the court, the retainers, and the students who come to ask about the old cooking, gives notice to the market for the year ahead: Into the broth: leeks first, because they do end. The winter leeks are pulled from the beds at the pond by the first of February, and a broth that waits for leeks that are not coming is not a broth, it is a fence; the kitchen would rather go without than build one. For the funeral table of the Kabaka the kitchen asks for 400 leeks, 60 chickens, and no ceremony with the onions. Delivery before nine, at the kitchen door, where the handcart is sold and the tea is given. — Head Cook of the Royal Kitchen, by order

Caterer's notice

The caterers of this district will note that the table of a kingdom is not the table of a state; the plates of the one are set for those who were born to the food and the plates of the other are set for those who are about to leave. The kitchen of Mengo keeps to the first. A plate that holds a flag is not a plate; it is a dish of this breed, and the breed is described at length in the correspondence shelf of the Plate & Post, to which the curious are referred — see Dishes of This Breed, the letter filed No. 48, which settles the matter better than any kitchen can.

Personals

K.F.

Sends his compliments to his old masters and masters' masters at the Victoria University Law School, 1939 to 1945, where the law of the land was learned in a language of the land's conqueror and used, in its time, for the land. He thanks the Kampala Golf Club for the handicap book, in which his name appears between a tea merchant and a judge; the book is now of no use to him, but the book keeps. He is sometimes called King Freddie abroad, and rarely in Uganda; both are true, and neither is the name the kingdom uses.

To the readers of the small columns

The small columns of this sheet are set here so that what happens in this place is written down where it can be read again after the shouting. For the discipline of the record, the small type, and the book that keeps, the readers are referred to two other small types that have done the work carefully: the Ledger Leaf, Special No. 63-A, on contempt of court, in which a small courtroom's order is entered, fine and all, and the Oneirocriticon, East Africa Station, entry No. 47, in which one line of this country's own ledger is kept whole for what comes after. What the ledger keeps, the small type keeps; the shouting does not keep anything.

Obituary

Mutesa II (1924–1969)

Edward Frederick William David Walugembe Mutebi Luwangula Mutesa II, born the 19th of November 1924 at Mengo, was Kabaka of Buganda from the 22nd of November 1939 until his death, the crown being placed upon him on his eighteenth birthday in 1942 in the old manner, at the royal court, before the Lukiiko and the chiefs and the country that the country keeps. He served as the first president of the Republic of Uganda from its independence in 1962 until the eighth of August 1966, when Milton Obote, his erstwhile ally, closed the palace gates and the presidency went to the man who would, within the year, make the republic a one-party state. His defence of the interests of Buganda, and of the kingdom's traditional autonomy, led at last to the conflicts out of which his fall was made; the constitution was suspended, the kingdom's council silenced, and this office entered the small type of the palace instead of the large type of the state. He died in the air at Ndeeba on the 21st of November 1969. The kingdom mourns; the small type keeps.
