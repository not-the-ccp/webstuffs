The Eiter Almanac · Dining Column · Review No. 207 · set down in the old way

Teardrops

Restaurant review · 14 Fiddle Row, above the station, above the valley · one acoustic kitchen, six quiet courses, and one plate kept for the person who does not come.

★★★★★ four out of five · the fifth is the frame the cold took

1 · The room

Teardrops is small in the way the song it takes its name from is small: a gentle, guitar-driven ballad of a place, not a stadium. The house opened on 20 February 2007 — the same day “Teardrops on My Guitar” was first sent out to country radio — with nine tables, felt laid over the banisters, a mandolin strung in the corner where the door does not hit it, and a fiddle hung above the pass where the steam does not reach. Its debut year is the house’s founding year, and the staff still count the room’s age from it. On 9 November 2007, when the record found its second radio, the terrace was moved to the east wall, and in 2009 the house issued what the waiters call the international card: an extra page for the northern tables, the only second edition the kitchen has ever made. The cellar, as a matter of policy, is stocked with 2006, 2007, and 2009 vintages only. There is no other vintages’ shelf.

2 · Written by two hands

The menu was written the way the song was written: by two. Chef N. Chapman and pastry cook Liz Rose write every season’s menu together, at the small table by the fiddle, in one sitting, and the kitchen does not admit a third handwriting. Chapman is a gentle cook in the specific sense the reviewers of this column use it — he leans on the wood range and lets the fire do what the mandolin does in the recording: stay a half-beat out in front and keep the tempo from swelling. The house’s standing philosophy is unrequited love, and it is worked into the booking system. Every table is for two; one of the two places may be set, on request, for a person who does not come, at no charge, and the plate for that person is the one the kitchen most takes seriously. I watched a sauce go out for a chair that held only a coat.

3 · The six courses

The menu runs six courses and costs 68, which is the price of the whole argument. The Verse, first: leek and thyme in a broth so thin it arrives still whispering. Unrequited, second: a slice of rye, honey, lemon, and one olive for the absent seat — a course with no quantity, only the absence it is shaped around. Mandolin, third: shaved fennel, orange, salt. Fiddle, fourth: long ribbons of root vegetable in warm butter, the plate scored with a knife the way a string is scored when it breaks. The fifth course is the only one with a number on the card, 9b, and it is a small course, and it is graded. The sixth is the one the room was built around.

4 · Thunder cake, the old reckoning

The kitchen’s thunder cake follows the receipt the almanac keeps in its own entry: butter, sugar, the eggs of one brown hen, flour folded in, a pinch of cinnamon from the tin that travelled west in 1894, a greased pan, thirty-five minutes, or roughly 950 feet of storm. Chapman counts the distance to the valley the old way — count the seconds between lightning and thunder and divide by five — and the cake comes out of the oven when his count reaches four, which is the farthest a storm has to be for the cake to still be warm at the table. Do not bake the film, the almanac’s editor adds, and the kitchen takes that instruction literally, in that the cake and the projector ticket of 3 October 1923 are never put in the same drawer. The full reckoning, and the ticket, stand at the Eiter almanac entry, Station No. 714.

5 · The terrace and the gulf

The river below Fiddle Row bends once, hard, and makes a gulf in the valley floor, and the terrace faces it. The terrace extends along the east wall only — the west half of the row is brick, and when I asked Chapman why, he walked me to the window, put the back of his hand under the light, and said, simply: “That is the whole eastern half of the gulf.” He did not explain, and the room did not require it; the gulf is the reason the terrace exists, the way a verse is the reason the chorus exists, and a house that explained its gulf would have to explain everything. The measure of the water itself — how a gulf is counted, and what its edges are for — is kept where water is kept, in the Mediterranean Sea, travel guide Entry No. 12, which is this column’s standing cross-reference whenever a kitchen starts talking about water it cannot serve.

6 · The frame the cold took

The one fault I can set down: on the coldest nights the kitchen loses a frame, one forty-eighth of a second, from the plating. Chapman concedes it. Cold butter cures stiff, he says, and stiff butter slides, and the spoon leaves a plate one frame late, which is not visible to the eye and is recorded anyway, because a ledger that omits a lost frame is a ledger that has made a decision it was not appointed to make. I am not a ledger. I am a review. But the Wanters error documentation, Lab Ledger No. 9, has ruled around exactly this gesture — the line set down, it took place, the page ruled and closed — and the two documents agree with each other better than they agree with the season. This is the only course that did not arrive on time, and it was the butter.

7 · Verdict

Teardrops is a restaurant that holds its line. It opens at seven, it serves six, it counts storms in fives, and it keeps a chair for the person who does not come, which is the most honest reservation system in the valley. It is four stars: four out of five, the fifth held back for the frame the cold took, which the kitchen has written down and will not repeat, and which I will not either. Go on a wet evening. Sit by the fiddle. Let someone count the seconds for you.
