FIELD NOTES · NOTEBOOK NO. 4 · WINTER 1991 · BOUND IN GREEN COTTON

On the Record Producer

Producer aquaticus · first observed in situ, March 1991

Specimen No. 4-A

Station: the cellar under 14 Marsh Road, one stair below the bar of Swamp Terrorists

Observer: the naturalist on the bench, second table, facing the water

Specimens counted: 1 (and one echo, disputed)

Monday, 11 March · 17:40 · rain, the marsh full, light failing early

It is a music-creating project's overall supervisor I have come to watch, and I should say at once that the species is real, is abundant in its own account, and is difficult to see. A record producer, or music producer, is the creature that stands between the band and the record the band believes it has already made. Its responsibilities can run through a range of creative and technical leadership roles that no single hand can hold, and the hands I watched did not try. It was in the cellar all evening. It was also, I am now fairly sure, on the ceiling, because there was no other explanation for the third pair of footsteps when only two of us were on the landing.

The habitat is a cellar cut under a bar, wired for sound, with a door that opens onto the room where the menu pinned to a rusty nail reads Grim – Stroke – Disease. Upstairs the eleven courses go out at the speed of a ferry. Downstairs the work proceeds at the speed of patience. I do not mean that lightly; the whole of the upper restaurant is a study in the producer's opposite, the project that has no supervisor at all and simply happens to the diners. The producer is the one who notices that a session is drifting, in the way a ferryman notices the tide, and pulls it back by small, unannounced means.

Its diet is performances. I watched it through the floor-grate while it ensured the musicians delivered acceptable, high-quality takes — this is the feeding behaviour, and it is hands-on, the species being of little use at a distance. When a verse came in two beats shy, the producer did not speak. It nodded at a point on the wall, once, and the next take came in on the beat, which I have noted as a form of call-and-response with the wall, the wall being, I suspect, a drum. It also supervised the technical engineering of the recording directly: it sat at the desk, it pointed at the meters, and when the meters objected it was the meters that moved.

Monday, 11 March · 21:05 · the green lamp, seen from below, is a lighthouse for moths

On the second night the creature's name came up, and I record it as it came: P. Marlowe, though the ledger by the door of the cellar — the same kind of ledger the Trust keeps to the minute — carries only the initials, and the initials carry a small annotation in grease pencil: coordinated the whole of it, incl. the flatbread. This is the part of its range I had not expected. The producer's involvement in a musical project can vary in depth and scope, and in popular hands I have been told the species may create the recording's entire sound and structure, down to the last cymbal. In this marsh, evidently, it goes further: it coordinates the production team and the process, and it also, on the evidence of the ledger, saw to it that the kitchen upstairs had butter.

By contrast the classical bird, if one may call it that, is a slimmer thing: in the recording of orchestral music the producer serves as more of a liaison between the conductor and the machinery, standing politely in a corner and keeping the orchestra's tea correct. I have never seen one, and I am not sure the difference is the work so much as the weather. The popular producer works where it is storming; the classical one has the sun. Our specimen is a storm-species. The cellar in February of 1991 was a storm, and the thing I was watching was the weather.

Tuesday, 12 March · 19:20 · the parcel arrives, one cloth-bound book, one tape, the tape marked 777

On the following week a black parcel of one cloth-bound book and a tape marked 777 came in from the record shop on Halstrom, and the Trust took it up in closed session in this very cellar — the session I have read about elsewhere, held with the door locked and the meters on the low. I was under the table, which is where I belong, and I have transcribed from the session log, as the log kept it, without alteration, the one line the producer wrote against the tape:

“Or, to the braces—though not a joke, and the set menu, to a harpooneer yet out of the province of the dish they are done with it.”

Session log, Tuesday 12 March 1991, in grease pencil, below the word braces a small drawing of a fiddle section

I must set it down as it stands, because the log will not let me improve it. What the species meant, in my reading and no other: the tape's brass section — the braces, the small drawing below the word confirming it was brass and not anxiety — was not a joke, whatever anyone upstairs said; the set menu of eleven courses was the reference point, the thing the evening was being measured against; and a harpooneer — the braised eel, dish of the house, which Dot has been pulling out of the smoke like a line out of water — was, on this tape, still outside the province of whatever dish they had finished doing. The producer's whole art, in one line, is knowing which dish the room is done with before the room does. The book was kept, spine in, by the door. The tape I have never heard. I do not think the species wants me to.

Wednesday, 13 March · 23:10 · anise on the stairs

It coordinated us last. The band, the engineers, the meters, and — I give this with full evidence — Eustace, who came down at midnight for his pour and found the whole production waiting for him at the door, a glass already set, anise already in it, not told what it was. The producer does not announce. It arranges. I climbed the one stair out of the cellar in the anise and the dark, and above me the grease-pencilled sign was doing its long quiet work, and I understood at last that the sign and the producer are of one family: both of them say the thing that has already happened, in a hand that is clearly not, in the final analysis, in its full head, and leave the reader to the water.

Behavioural note for the next observer: the species keeps to the bench across the yard as well, and will sit down anywhere a ledger is present, because it can keep the facts in order. If you see a figure at a control desk and the meters stop objecting, do not go in. You are not missing the record. You are standing in the recording, and it is being made for you, and it is not done with you yet. Be back before the tide turns.

Addendum, in a steadier hand, later

The reviewer upstairs gave the kitchen two forks and a standing table, and the Trust entered the verdict in its own ledger the same night; the diary of those opening fortnight is kept at the Marsh Road diary, March 1991, and the closed session on the parcel, session seven of 15 March, stands on the record in the council's minutes. My own eleven courses are accounted for under Swamp Terrorists, Q Magazine, March 1991 — the flatbread acquitted, the anise standing as witness — and the creature itself is not the first disciplined producer I have sat under: a head cook on Station Road, Orange, runs a nine-variant course with the same unannounced hand, and I have written of her at 817 — The Armoured Mess. Two stations, one species: the thing that makes the whole arrive before the tide turns. And the creature did, in fact, transmit: on the afternoon of 16 March it recorded and aired from this same room a short radio program about the music that has no writer’s name on it, and the afternoon, including the recipe card read by the cook word for word, survives in the excerpt kept at The Cellar Revival, Marsh Road Studio, 16 March 1991.

Notebook No. 4, bound in green cotton, kept under the bench, second table, facing the water. All counts verified against the tide. The tape marked 777 is not mentioned in this notebook except once, and even once is too many times, I think, but the log had to be transcribed, and the log does not keep to the province of its dish either.
