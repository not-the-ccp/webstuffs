The Gatehouse Answers

An FAQ on the Insanity Defence, set down at the top of the drive, Broadmoor Hospital, Crowthorne · Berkshire

Kept by the estates office in the 1863 wing, at the top of the drive, the oldest standing thing here — the same wing that Stop Two of the One-Day Pass put you at the foot of, on the steps that the condition survey calls a mixed heap, layer on layer.

Questions arrive at this door from four directions — the four NHS regions that send their referrals to this gate, London, Eastern, South East and South West — and the archive room answers the ones that keep coming, the way the yard answers by counting every bolt twice. What follows is the register as it stands. The letter of apology that sent the first batch of questions this way is filed next door, and this page is its quieter sibling.

I. What exactly is the insanity defence, in one sentence?

The insanity defence — also called the mental disorder defence — is an affirmative defence by excuse in a criminal case: it argues that the defendant is not responsible for their actions because of a psychiatric disease at the time of the criminal act. It is a defence the defendant raises, and it asks the court for a verdict of the whole, not a discount off the verdict. The yard’s version: the audit finds the chassis unsound, and the whole vehicle is taken off the road, not merely repaired.

II. How does it differ from an excuse of provocation?

Under provocation — the old partial defence that mitigated murder to manslaughter — the defendant is still responsible; the responsibility is only lessened, because the mind was disturbed by a temporary mental state, a blow or an insult that wore off. The insanity defence does not lessen responsibility, it declines it entirely: the psychiatric disease is not a mood, it does not come and go with the weather, and it was at the wheel at the time of the act. One is a fine for a scratch; the other is a finding that there was no one in the cab. This contrast is the first thing the committee asked, and it was the first thing we got wrong in the 1897 dress book, which is why the dress book is now bound separately.

III. And how is it different from self-defense, justification, or imperfect self-defense?

Self-defense is a justification: the act itself was right, or at least not to be blamed, because it answered a threat. The insanity defence concedes that the act happened and says nothing good about it; it says only that the actor’s disease took the blame from their hands. Imperfect self-defense, where the threat was real but the force was beyond what it called for, is a mitigation — a lesser charge, a shorter sentence, a lighter pencil. Three different rooms on the same floor: one says the door was open, one says the hand was unsteady, and the third says the hand was the wrong size. This FAQ belongs in none of the three and sits in the fourth, unlabelled room at the end of the corridor, where the kettle is out.

IV. Why do the old rules talk about wolves, and who was Daniel M’Naghten?

On 5 January 1843, Daniel M’Naghten — who had been hearing voices for years — shot and killed Edward Drummond, a secretary to the Prime Minister, mistaking him for a government persecutor. The House of Lords was asked to set down the law of the case, and it answered with the test still known as the M’Naghten rules: a person is not responsible by reason of insanity if, at the time of the act, they were labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of what they were doing — or, if they did know it, not to know what they were doing was wrong. The court illustrated the second limb with the famous hypothetical of a man who, knowing what he was doing, takes another’s life while supposing himself to be eating a wolf at his supper. The wolf has outlived every judge who used it. It is in the register here under W, pinned under the Stop Two leaflet for the record.

V. When did England stop saying “not guilty by reason of insanity”?

The Criminal Law Act 1991 did that on 30 September 1991, replacing the old verdict with the phrase not proven by reason of insanity — a verdict the Act expressly provides is to be read as a conviction, which is one of the few cases where the law apologises in advance for its own wording. The change was cosmetic in form and a confession in spirit: the defence had become so disfavoured that Parliament could no longer bear to see a person pronounced not guilty of the worst thing on the books, whatever their state. The estates office’s opinion, kept in the margin, is that a verdict is like the mixed heap on the west steps: you may re-lay the flagstones all you like, layer on layer, and the four centuries underneath remain what they are.

VI. Is the insanity defence the same thing as a finding that a person cannot stand trial?

No, and the difference is the whole question. A finding of unfitness to stand trial does not touch guilt or responsibility at all: it says that a mental disease prevents the defendant from effectively assisting counsel — from following the case, instructing the lawyers, telling their own side — and therefore that the trial cannot fairly proceed. The insanity defence is about the moment of the act; unfitness is about the moment of the trial. One looks back to the wolf at supper, the other looks across the well. A person can be fit to stand trial and still plead the defence, or unfit to stand trial with the defence never reached; the two findings run on different clocks, and this archive keeps both calendars on the wall, because we are a hospital and we are used to other people’s time.

VII. If the verdict is “not proven by reason of insanity,” where does the person go afterwards?

This is the question the committee asked on the morning after the Pass, and it is answered by the geography. England keeps three high-security psychiatric hospitals: Broadmoor here in Crowthorne, Ashworth near Liverpool, and Rampton in Nottinghamshire, and a special measure of detention — the restriction order with a hospital direction — places the person in one of them at the level the case requires. Broadmoor is the oldest of the three and sits at the top of that scale: 874 beds, heathland on the southern edge of the Berkshire downs, a long drive, a tower that does not need to be there but is. The One-Day Pass on 14 November opened these doors for exactly one day, which is why the committee’s questions came in the morning post. The kettle is out.

VIII. What was the mangled fourth line of the leaflet, really, about?

The committee deserves the whole of it, so here it is, unbound and in its own disorder. The line ran: “Walking shoes; the steps of Stop Two, which are a mixed heap, layer on layer, and the chassis are the audit.” And before the committee thinks the archive has lost its wits — the subject of this very register, in the building — let it be recorded that a second mangled line was found in the binding, set from the laundry’s 1897 cream schedule at the same wrong volume, which reads: “The timings are pinned to a cream; wash, pick, and wipe the outside, put a thick tendinous substance.” Read it the way the yard reads its books: the timings are the canteen’s pinned rota, the cream is the schedule it was pinned to by a misplaced staple, wash, pick, and wipe the outside is the vessel’s cleaning order as it was meant to be set, and the thick tendinous substance is the schedule’s own note on the sinew in the stock pot, entered on the wrong line. Two documents, one binding, two mangled lines. The corrected leaflet is now set in three honest columns, and this sentence has been re-laid on its own flagstone, where it can be read in walking shoes.

IX. Do you charge for answers?

The pass costs nothing, and neither do the answers, though the register costs something else: eleven retired estate vehicles in the south courtyard, chassis up on their stands, every bolt counted, weighed, and entered into the quarterly audit. If a member of the committee would walk the drive again, in walking shoes, before the frost, the estates officer will meet them at the gate with the unbound documents and the kettle. The apology that set all of this in motion is at the door, one step down the drive — and if the committee would like to see a neighbouring institution answer its own questions the same way, the frequently asked questions from Netherne Hospital, formerly the Surrey County Asylum, are a pleasant mile of heathland away by comparison. The whole shelf, and the drive back to the gate, is kept at the estates office letter of apology, File 41/7.
