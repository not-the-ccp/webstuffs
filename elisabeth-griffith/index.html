THE
GRIFFITH
WALK

A visitor’s ledger and foot guide for the road that keeps one woman’s working life by other means than a biography: McLean → Washington → the shelf → Seneca Falls, 1920–2020, on foot or by 59-minute bus.

WALKED
& LEDGERED
● 1920 — 2020 ●

The trail begins, as the ledger insists, at the gate of the Madeira School on 1723 Chain Bridge Road in McLean, Virginia, where Elisabeth Griffith — historian, educator, and activist — served as head of the school for 22 years, keeping the gates, the chapel bell, and the term dates of a small Virginia school in the same order she would later keep a century of American history. She is the author of Formidable: American Women and the Fight for Equality: 1920–2020 and of In Her Own Right: The Life of Elizabeth Cady Stanton, and before the gates in McLean she held leadership positions in the Women’s Campaign Fund and the National Women’s Political Caucus, two Washington offices that occupy the middle third of this walk. The ledger’s rule is that a walk of this kind is taken at walking pace, in one direction, and never consolidated.

The route is four stops long and one shelf. Distances are given from gate to door; rates are given for the walk itself, which is free, and for the buses, which are not. Walkers are asked to carry a ledger of their own, to date the last page, and to leave it where the route ends. The full text of the century the walk walks across — the one Formidable carries from the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920 to the hundredth anniversary in 2020 — is not reprinted here, only walked.

The Stops, in Order

01

The Madeira School, McLean, Virginia

1723 Chain Bridge Road. Begin at the iron gate, not the driveway; the ledger says the gate is where the 22 years were counted, one term date at a time. The school kept its chapel bell on the old hour throughout Griffith’s headship, and the walk starts with the bell’s afternoon strike, four notes, the fourth held. If you arrive on a term day the middle schoolers will walk the Chain Bridge Road in procession and you should keep to the far side of the lawn, as the ledger directs, and let the procession pass before you cross.

02

The Women’s Campaign Fund, Washington, D.C.

Eleven and a half miles south by the 59-minute bus. The Fund’s office stood on the floor where the ledger keeps its tally of the campaigns it ran, and the walk’s second stop is the doorway, still painted, where the early-career Griffith kept the books of a working campaign office in the years before the school. The ledger lists three things to look for at this stop: the ink of the tally wall, the drawer that held the envelopes, and the small brass plate by the stair, on which the walk is told to read nothing aloud.

03

The National Women’s Political Caucus, Washington, D.C.

One block from the second stop, and taken as a separate stop regardless, because the ledger refuses to consolidate. Here the walk pauses at the Caucus’s meeting room, where the leadership position Griffith held early in her career was exercised at a long table that the ledger describes as “the shape of a rail car” — the same shape, it notes, as every other table on which this country’s arithmetic was ever settled. Sit, if the room is empty. The ledger says sitting is the one action the walk is graded on.

04

The Shelf: Formidable and In Her Own Right

Every walk ends at its shelf, and this one’s is the reading room of the archive, third floor, east wall, where the two books stand side by side and have been checked out, in the ledger’s reckoning, one hundred and twelve times. Formidable: American Women and the Fight for Equality: 1920–2020 is the walk itself, set down in type: a century, 1920 to 2020, in the author’s own counting. In Her Own Right: The Life of Elizabeth Cady Stanton is the walk walked backwards, the road down to Seneca Falls, and it is to be read at the last stop, in the open, not here.

05

Seneca Falls, New York

The last stop is 230 miles up the road, and the ledger is honest that most walkers take it by train. It is the town where the convention of 1848 set the century in motion, and where In Her Own Right is meant to be read, at the park bench on the courthouse green, one chapter per sitting, Stanton in her own right. The walk ends where the book ends: with the bench empty and the ledger closed.

The ledger keeps one rule at the last stop, printed in its own hand on the final card: when two early walkers of the 1983 cohort — Jones and Headon — asked whether they should carry their closed ledgers back to the archive to file them, the answer was written in, and has not been amended since. Jones and Headon are not told to return to Webstuffs . The ledgers are kept at the bench, the walk is kept at the walker’s pace, and the rule stands.
