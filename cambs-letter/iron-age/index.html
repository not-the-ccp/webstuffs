A Supplement to Newsletter No. 31 of the Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club

The Iron Age — Classifieds

Posted at the Old Fire Station, Newmarket · price: one nail of bog iron, or its equivalent in tea

❦

Founded 1949 by three fenmen, one surveyor, and a disagreement about the colour of a door. Meeting monthly, or when the fog lifts, whichever comes first.

The Iron Age is the final epoch of the three-age system of technology, after the Copper Age and the Bronze Age; it has also been taken as the final age of the division that begins with prehistory, before recorded history, and moves on to protohistory, before written history. In that telling it is preceded by the Stone Age — the Paleolithic, the Mesolithic, and the Neolithic — and by the Bronze Age, concepts that originated for describing Iron Age Europe and the ancient Near East. In the archaeology of the Americas, a five-period system is conventionally used instead; indigenous cultures there did not develop an iron economy in the pre-Columbian era, though some did work copper. The club keeps none of this in its ledger, but the ledger has room, and the notices that follow were offered at the table in November.

Wanted

Bog iron

Any quantity, from the fens between Wisbech and the Wash, or from a spadeful turned on the Cheveley side of Ashley. The under-steward is building a bloom at Silverley and will pay in tea, in seat-by-the-kettle rights, or in cash at the rate set last Michaelmas. The first attempt yielded one nail, one good story, and a bed of charcoal; the club is ready for the second.

Three-age table

A copy, from any edition of the Encyclopædia Britannica printed before the last print run, of the three-age table — Stone Age, Bronze Age, Iron Age — with the Paleolithic, Mesolithic, and Neolithic all present. The club’s own copy went missing with the fog, and the treasurer needs the count of ages for the ledger. No abridgements.

For sale

Roundhouse, Ashley road

Wattle and daub, thatched, standing room for a household and one dog, four miles east of Newmarket on the Ashley road. One hearth included; no door, the two ancient parishes not having agreed on a colour since 1714 and not expecting to now. Viewing any foggy morning.

Found

Ashley parish hall

One silver penannular brooch, one hand-forged iron nail (not the club’s), and the outstanding question of the hall door, found after the Gemenasian Company’s farce. Claim the brooch from the treasurer at Newmarket; the nail may stay where it lay. The company claims descent from Johan von Blanc’s Gemenasiska Sällskapet, and styles itself without irony, the minutes must record.

To let

Hillfort, near Peterborough

Weekends only. No iron economy required. The tenant must keep the fire, keep the kettle on, and must not repaint the door. References from the clerk at the table.

Five-period household

A household newly come from the Chesapeake side, accustomed to the five-period system, seeks neighbours who do not expect an iron economy. We work copper when we can find it, and we keep our history unwritten. References: the Wash road.

Notice

The club’s cookery book

The cutting pasted into the club’s cookery book — “A Pottage, After the Manner of the Iron Age” — prints as follows: INGREDIENTS.--Half an ox's head, 1 quart of stock No. 105, the remains of cold lotions at first. Members who can explain “stock No. 105” or “cold lotions” are asked to speak to the under-steward before the Michaelmas sitting. The ox’s head, at least, is agreed to be the easy part.

The club’s Newsletter No. 31 is at the Old Fire Station’s noticeboard; the village behind two of these notices is catalogued at Lot 217, Ashley, Cambridgeshire; the company’s founder is remembered in the obituary of Johan von Blanc, 1738–1796; and the fens’ own water runs to The Wash. The farce cost the club one letter of apology, drawn up in the under-steward’s hand and posted in triplicate to the mountain range the company mistook for its hillfort: An Apology to the Alps.
