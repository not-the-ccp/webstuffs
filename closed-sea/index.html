The Wadsworth Travel Folio · Route No. 2 · Printed at Ann Arbor

The Closed Sea

A guide to the coast as it stands after the Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves, 1807 — the year the country’s doors were ordered shut, and the earliest date the Constitution would let them shut.

Filed with Placard No. 9 of the ethnography case · the ledger keeps the whole route, stop by stop, in full

I. The law, briefly stated

The Act Prohibiting Importation of Slaves was enrolled as 2 Stat. 426 on March 2, 1807, and it took effect on January 1, 1808 — not a day earlier, because Article I, Section 9 of the Constitution holds the door at that date and no sooner, and the guide will not promise a visitor a coast that is not yet closed. The legislation was promoted by President Thomas Jefferson, who called for its enactment in his State of the Union address of December 1806; he and others had been pressing the same bill, one term at a time, since the 1770s, and the road from that early pressure to this statute is the longest single argument the republic ever made on the floor of its own house.

The act fit the shape of the age. The general trend was toward abolishing the international slave trade; Virginia had prohibited or restricted it first, and every other state followed, with one door left ajar — South Carolina, which had reopened its trade on the strength of the same argument it had once closed. Congress finally closed the national door with penalties entered in the ledger’s own way: a fine of $3,000 per voyage, the vessel itself condemned, and the whole matter weighed at every inspection dock between the Potomac and Savannah. The guide below walks that line of docks. It is a travel entry, not a history lesson; the history is in the way the coast behaves.

II. The route, in five stops

The Wadsworth ledger keeps the route as a day’s circuit out of the Emporium and back, cab for the long water, feet for the docks — the same way the case next door keeps its Middle Passage walking tour in eight stops. The order of the stops is the order of the argument: who argued, where the shutters went up, where the coast was inspected, where the door was left open, and where the whole thing is kept.

Two hundred and forty miles of coast, one statute, and a January date the guide repeats until the visitor has it: January 1, 1808. The cab runs the circuit in the order above; the ledger does not run it at all. It simply holds it.

III. Baltimore, the inspection dock

Stand on the cobble where the inspection office kept its scales, at the water’s edge below the wharf. Before January 1, 1808, the dock weighed what came in; after, it weighed what went out — tonnage, cargo, and the fine of three thousand dollars when the two did not agree. The customhouse kept its register in longhand, and the register still smells of the harbour. The guide asks the visitor to do the one thing the register asked of every master: state what you carry, and where it is going, in full. On the closed coast, a manifest that rounds its figures is a manifest that confesses nothing and proves less. It is the same grammar the Wadsworth ledger applies to a census — the practice of counting in full is what a coast does with its doors once it has closed them — and the ledger, which counts the whole country to the last digit under Placard No. 9, the ethnography case, was not improved by this dock by accident. It was improved by this dock’s custom.

IV. Charleston, the door that reopened

The guide comes here last among the water stops, because the door at Charleston is the honest counter-example of the route. When the national coast closed, South Carolina — the state that had once restricted the trade along with the rest — reopened its trade in 1808 and kept the door open until 1810, when the coast closed it on the strength of the very statute this folio walks. Stand on King Street, where the auction rooms faced the wharf, and read the date stone: the same January the country shut its door, this city unlocked its own. The guide enters no judgment on the stone. It enters the date, and it enters the date twice, once in 1808 and once in 1810, because a door that opens twice in one season has taught the ledger something about registers that a shut door never could.

V. The line the ledger kept

In the sleeve of this folio, under the cab fare and the dock’s register, the family keeps a second torn page from the same farm almanac of 1871 that sits in Placard No. 9 — this one from the chapter on the shell-fish, corner gone, ink gone soft at the fold. The ledger kept the line exactly as it stood in the almanac, word for word, and the guide prints it here exactly as the ledger keeps it:

Some recommend the young Crab, a little salt; and mix them with good ventilation, and perfectly free from dust.

Farm almanac, 1871, the shell-fish chapter. Torn page, sleeve of Route No. 2, kept with the circuit’s cab fare.

The almanac, on this page, is a keeper’s manual: the young Crab is to be salted a little, mixed with ventilation, and kept perfectly free from dust. The ledger reads it the way it reads the coast. What a closed trade leaves behind is not absence but what must be kept clean in its place — the ventilation of the register, the manifest stated in full, the salt of the fine, the room where the dust of the old cargo settles out of the air of the new one. Every stop on this route is a place that was, and is, and must be kept perfectly free from dust. That is the almanac’s whole advice, and it is the route’s whole itinerary. A compiler with a barometer read the same keeping at the Charles, in Entry No. 112 of the Charles River Almanac, and filed it under weather of a higher order: a record that will not round its numbers, and a coast that will not reopen its door.

VI. Practicalities, and where the route is kept

The circuit is best walked in the month of January, when the coast is at its January date and the visitor can stand on the dock in the hour the statute turned. The cab runs from the Emporium at the hour the case lamp is lit, and the ledger room closes at nightfall. The folio is returned to the shelf with the rest of the family’s itineraries, and it keeps company in the same crate with Card No. 214, the Huron River maple bread, which the route eats at stop five, with the dust of the circuit still in the room. Admission to the folio is free; the visitor is asked only to state, at each stop, what the statute did there, and to say the date in full: January 1, 1808, and not before. The ledger keeps the circuit exactly as it ran — five stops, one door, one date — and it keeps it in full.

The folio is opened at the hour the lamp is lit and closed at nightfall. The sea it walks is closed; the route, in the ledger’s reckoning, is not. What the coast refuses to reopen, the register keeps — in full, and perfectly free from dust.
