UNITARIAN MEMORIAL CHURCH · TOORAK ROAD, CAULFIELD, VICTORIA · LOST & FOUND OFFICE, GLAZED CABINET, LEFT OF THE PULPIT

NOTICE No. 47 · OPENED 3 APRIL 1970 · NEVER CLOSED

FOUND

one Unitarian minister, in good working order, one tweed coat, one peace sash, and one shelf of things nobody has claimed since

The article

FOUND, on the platform at Caulfield Station, on a Tuesday the office no longer dates: one Unitarian minister, middle-aged at first sighting and never dated since, travelling southbound with a satchel of hymn papers, a peace sash folded to the size of a handkerchief, and the particular expression of a man who has just finished a sentence he intends to finish some other way. The article is identified on his chest, by the parish pin, as the Reverend Victor Montgomery Keeling James, of Toorak Road, Caulfield, minister of this congregation from 1947 to 1969, born in Melbourne on 19 March 1897, and gone — found, that is, and then lost again — in 1984. He is described by the office as answering to “Victor,” to “Rev. Keeling James,” and, from a certain table at a certain meeting in 1961, to a great many other things.

His marks, entered in the book: the sash, blue with a white star; a stamp album of the People’s Republic of China, four of them from the 1964 issue, the peony and the bridge; a tin of tea from Peking, 1958, opened once and kept empty and clean, the way some people keep a chair; a peace petition with 11,302 signatures, three of them in the same hand; and a shelf of postcards, of which the office will say more and less than it should.

PROPERTY MARKS, AS ENTERED IN THE BOOK

  • No. 1 — Peace sash, blue, one white star, folded twice
  • No. 2 — Stamp album, P.R.C. of China, incl. four of the 1964 issue
  • No. 3 — Tea tin, Peking, 1958, empty, clean, kept as a chair
  • No. 4 — Petition, 11,302 signatures, three in the same hand
  • No. 5 — Postcards, one shelf, count disputed by the office

The office does not open the tin. The office has always said this, which is why the tin is still clean.

Condition, by way of history

The article arrived in this parish in 1947, in a year when the office was keeping count of the light bulbs, and has been in service here since, which is to say the notice is older than its own subject’s tenure by a small margin, the way all good paperwork is. In the 1950s and the 1960s the article attracted hostility of the right-hand kind: the “Save the Parson” postcards, 11,000 of them, filed in the box marked H because the office could not find a better letter and never did; the two petitions, 1955 and 1961, asking his removal on account of his work in the peace movement and his correspondence and visits across the water, to Communist China, of which there were three, of which the office keeps the tickets, the peony stamps, and the tea. The office’s entry of the period is one line: subject answers everything, to everyone, including the postcards. It is the only line in the book that is not crossed out, and the office has had reasons to be careful with its crossing out.

He retired in 1969 and remained, which is the word the office uses for every man who retires and remains. The last entry in the book is dated 1984 and consists of a dash, an em dash, and the note collected by the weather, no claim required.

Claim

Claims on the article are received at the glazed cabinet, left of the pulpit, after the second service, on condition that the claimant answer the hymn that plays in the satchel, whichever it is, and does not answer. The postcards are not for sale, are not for return, and are not, on any reading of the book, for anyone; they are held in the name of the parish, the box marked H sitting on top, which is where they are, which is where they have been, which the office finds has come to be the same sentence. The tea tin is claimed by nobody and kept by everybody, on the standing rule that an empty tin is a chair and a chair is a person, and a person is not moved to the cellar.

As to Lot 12 on the annex, the parcel the office has held since April 1970: the claim has now come in, in a hand the book recognises, and the office has entered its decision word for word, as the book requires. The party takes the rest of the Continental Divide Sale, Catalogue 12 , in which she never once stopped. She — the book is careful — is Mrs. James, who spoke from the opening gavel to the last hammer of that sale, forty-three minutes of it, about the binding of the second volume, and who has never since been quiet in the office about the binding of anything. The office notes that the party takes Lot 12 and the remaining lots, that no further claim will be entered against them, and that the cabinet is one shelf lighter as of this notice.

FROM THE SHELF OF POSTCARDS, AS ENTERED IN THE BOOK

“Dear Reverend James — we are a body of neighbours of good standing and quiet temper, and we write to say that the sash has been noticed, and the star, and that the star is not of our making. Your congregation. — P.S. This postcard is number 11,000, and we are not finished.”

Caulfield, 1961 · box H · not crossed out

Related ledgers kept elsewhere on this station: the night and choir services run on the sister board at The Gospel Line — Timetable & Commentary, GT-1603, where a line kept word for word is a line the office would not cross out either; and the hush after the last hammer is filed with the Album Keepers at Eric Clapton — In Memoriam, where the rule of playing it whole holds for ministers as well as records. The office’s front door is at the front page, one cabinet away.
