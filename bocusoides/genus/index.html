Genus

pl. genera · rank above species, below family

On the rank itself

A genus is a taxonomic rank used in the biological classification of living and fossil organisms, as well as viruses, sitting above the species and below the family. In binomial nomenclature the genus name forms the first part of the binomial species name for each species within it. Panthera leo (the lion) and Panthera onca (the jaguar) are two species within the genus Panthera; Panthera in turn is a genus within the family Felidae. The museum keeps one drawer for each such name. This drawer, as visitors will notice, holds exactly one thing.

Because a genus may hold one species or a thousand, the case is fitted with a sliding shelf. The shelf has not moved since installation. A genus of a single species is called monotypic, and a monotypic genus is the drawer in its quietest state: the name exists for the sake of the one animal that bears it, the way a street sign exists for the one house on the street.

Exhibit — Bocusoides zhaoi

Accession SMSC/2024/031 · prepared by E. Renshaw · glass 40 × 30 × 25 cm · cork ramp, 4 cm

The specimen is an ant-mimicking jumping spider of the family Salticidae, one of the monotypic genera Bocusoides, received from a Guizhou field contact in May 2024 in a lidded jar with damp moss and a toothpick for scale. It performs its work in the mornings, roughly 7:40 to 9:00, holding the first pair of legs out in front of the body the way a Formica holds its antennae, so that the body reads in three segments though it is a spider with eight eyes and no waist. The full build — terrarium, 90 mm macro rig, nine mimicry trials, frame 214 — is logged in the Bocusoides build log kept in the garage on this same ridge, where the case glass is polished between showings and the torn corner of the club zine still sleeps in the lens case.

What the vitrine displays is not the spider so much as the name around the spider: Bocusoides, one word doing the work of a shelf, a drawer, and a room.

How the case is decided

The composition of a genus is determined by taxonomists. The standards for genus classification are not strictly codified, so different authorities often produce different classifications for genera; there are general practices — resemblance, shared ancestry, the habit of splitting at the elbow of doubt — but no judge's bench tall enough for the rank. Two authorities may look at the same boulder, the same lichen, the same grey-and-white stripe, and leave the specimen in two different drawers. The museum accepts both rulings, which is why the sliding shelf exists.

The preparer's ledger, consulted at the last case audit, records how the garage handles a specimen whose case comes down with nothing diagnostic to stand on — a single animal, one logbook, no second authority to be cross-examined. The entry, in pencil, is short and the placard reproduces it exactly:

“Naught's an obstacle, naught's an angle to the jury.”

The clerk's gloss, beneath it: when the evidence is empty, the absence is argued for nothing and against nothing; the drawer keeps its one screwdriver either way. It is the museum's favourite line of law, though no court has ever heard it.

Curator's note & related holdings

The placard was installed in June 2024, one month after the Waitematā Local Board's extraordinary meeting on 12 June — the minutes of which are preserved as the Waitematā Local Board minutes, where a different kind of register, the Newton Road patient register of fourteen thousand immunisations, was voted for preservation by 8–0. Two registers, one isthmus: one of animals, one of people, both kept by the same rule of careful hands.
