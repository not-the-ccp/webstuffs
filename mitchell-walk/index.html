Mitchell Walk

A Tourist Brochure on the Grounds of Josephine M. Mitchell
Champaign & Urbana, Illinois · the Illinois River Basin, prairie pothole country

  • Admission free, all year
  • Route 1.4 miles, 9 stops, level
  • Best light October, on the north side of Goodwin Avenue
  • Bring a pencil; the curve will want one

I · Getting There

Champaign and Urbana sit flat on the prairie between the Sangamon and the Illinois rivers, in the pothole basin that the farmers called the flats before the surveyors called it a basin. From the train platform at Union Station take the blue tram one block to the plaza, then south on Goodwin Avenue for nine blocks to the Mathematics Building. The walk begins where the building’s south wall ends and the elm row begins, in a square of brick 30 feet wide, marked by a low bench and a bronze plate set into the paving at head height for those who prefer to read standing. The plate is the first stop and the last; the whole route returns to it, the way her best work always did — a closed curve with a singularity at the centre, and everything else circled round it once, quietly, on the way home.

II · The Mathematician

Josephine Margaret Mitchell (June 30, 1912 – December 28, 2000) was a Canadian-American mathematician who worked in the analysis of functions of several complex variables, the branch that studies what happens to a complex function when the domain has two or more complex directions at once. She was born in Toronto, Ontario, the daughter of a schoolteacher and a housekeeper, went up to Cornell University in Ithaca, New York, where she took her doctorate in 1940, and took a post at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign the year after the war settled in, in the mathematics building that now closes the walk. For the better part of three decades her seminar met on Tuesdays in the upstairs room of 725 South Goodwin: students who were there remember a small desk, a long blackboard, and a habit of circling an estimate twice before it was allowed to stand.

In 1955 the university made her tenure, the chair of mathematics holding a tenured post. In 1971 it took it away. The reason was a rule about relatives holding office at the same house — an anti-nepotism regulation — and the rule was applied only one way: after Mitchell married Richard O. Davis, who was a more junior member of the mathematics faculty at the same university, the administration revoked the tenured position of the senior scholar and let the junior one stand, untenured, in the very room where her seminar had met. The case made the newspapers in 1971 and 1972, and a settlement followed in which the university paid a substantial sum and the walk below got its name. In 1982 she gave the account herself, in A Woman in Mathematics: A Life, and the book remains the last stop of the route: one copy of it is kept on the low bench, in a dry sleeve, for anyone who wants to read the ending from the top.

III · The Plate, and the Line on It

Visitors come for the elms and stay for the plate. Set into the paving beside the bench, at head height, it is bronze, nine inches wide, and cast in the old lettering of the basin’s survey markers. It carries exactly one sentence, and the sentence has been read aloud to the walk on at least one occasion by a groundskeeper, in the flat prairie voice that the basin gives its officials:

“The entry, which this form is published without charge; it is the basin's oldest street.”

The groundskeeper’s reading of it is the only official one, and it is this: the entry is this entry — Entry No. 17, the Mitchell entry of the series; the form is the cream card on which the series is printed, the same card the Friends of the River Basin use for their free registers, so the walk costs nothing and the walk is given nothing back but itself; and the basin’s oldest street is the survey line the walk follows, run by the first township crews in the 1830s across the pothole flats, older by eighty years than any street the town has since paved over it. The line is kept in the series’ register as lot 17, the same lot number the walk wears. That is all the plate says, and the walk, which is a closed curve, is content with all of it.

IV · The Nine Stops

  1. The bronze plate (stop 0 and stop 9) — nine inches of bronze at head height; the sentence above.
  2. The low bench — brick, 30 feet wide in its own square; the A Woman in Mathematics copy rides on it in a dry sleeve.
  3. The elm row — eleven elms, planted 1955, the year of the tenure; the fourth has a brass nail dated the year it was revoked.
  4. The 725 South Goodwin door — south door of the Mathematics Building, where the seminar met on Tuesdays for three decades.
  5. The upstairs blackboard — still chalked in the corner, twice-circled, as the students remember it.
  6. The register window — the Friends’ desk; sign the cream card, free, and the series keeps a copy of the signature.
  7. The 1830s survey line — the basin’s oldest street, visible as a straight gap in the turf for forty rods south of the door.
  8. The pothole pond — the flat is the basin, and the basin keeps one pond for the winter geese; watch from the boardwalk.
  9. The bench again — the route closes; sign the card a second time if you wish, which the card allows and the series keeps.

V · Visitor’s Notes

The walk is level and takes an hour on the slow count. Trams run on the hour from Union; the last northbound leaves at ten. The register window is staffed from nine in the morning to four in the afternoon, and the cream cards are signed in pencil, which is the series’ one firm rule — nothing that will not erase gets into the register. In October the elms turn before the prairie does, and the flat holds the colour a full week after the town has let it go. There is no admission, no charge of any kind, and no building that requires a ticket; the walk is the basin’s oldest street wearing its new name, and it will go round with you as many times as you like. The curve closes. The entry stands. The plate has nothing further to say, and the groundskeeper reads it aloud, in the flat voice, whenever someone stops in front of it for too long.
