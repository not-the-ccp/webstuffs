I. Of the borough and its approach

The Bronx — pronounced BRONKS, and no visitor is to be ashamed of asking — is the northernmost of the five boroughs of New York City, and it is reached the way one reaches a country that has declined to be crossed: by bridge, by tunnel, or by the slow river, never at a walk, unless it is a walk of three miles with a purpose, such as the walking count on the board, which this book adopts as its own. The borough has a land area of 42 square miles (109 km²) and a population of 1,472,654 at the 2020 census. It has the fourth largest area, the fourth largest population, and the third highest population density of the boroughs, and it is to be remembered that these are given plainly, with the numbers in, because the book is read aloud from the back row and the back row must hear every one.

The Bronx is the only borough located primarily on the U.S. mainland, and it keeps its borders the way a host keeps his guest list. To its north is Westchester County. To its west are the Hudson River and the borough of Manhattan, across the Harlem River. To its south is the borough of Queens, across the East River. To its east is Long Island Sound. The borough is coextensive with Bronx County, one of the 62 counties of New York State, so that a visitor who names the county has named the borough, and is in good standing. The Bronx is divided, as this chapter divides itself, into sections and into rules: Eastchester and Pelham toward the north where the road still carries the name Boston Road; Fordham and Riverdale toward the west where the water is; Throggs Neck and Soundview toward the east where the sound opens; and the high ground of Van Cortlandt Park and the Woodlawn end in the middle, close enough that you can smell the pond from the last block. One of the three-mile streets carries the address of this chapter, and its walking account is kept by the board at East 233rd Street — The Back Bench Bulletin, Thread No. 88, where the count was taken on foot: 4.8 kilometres, one interchange, two stations.

It is good manners, approaching the borough, to name the four waters before asking directions. The sound collects no calling card and the rivers are not to be crossed by argument. And whatever else one does, one does not dig for the company’s papers. The habit is: the company keeps its papers.

II. Of cards and the hall table

No call in the Bronx is made without a card, and the custom is an old one, inherited with the lamps. The travelling company that carried it north and west out of Karlskrona — the Gemenasian Company, of the director whose obituary this book has printed, Johan von Blanc (1738–1796) — kept the rule that wherever the company lodged, one card was left at the hall table, with the hour of departure written plainly in the corner. The borough has kept the rule, and the desk beside the hall table keeps the drawer: a card for the house one means to honour, one for the platform, one for the bakery.

At Dyre Avenue, on the corner of the street the register walked, there is a bakery, and at the bakery there is a card — numbered 1796, which is not a joke — from which the slack-rope buns are baked: molasses and caraway, glazed while hot, the same recipe that sailed aboard the archipelago fleet. The card is kept in full at Slack-Rope Buns — A Family Recipe. The visitor takes one bun while the glaze is still going, eats it on the corner, and does not ask to see the card until the baker offers it. A card opened too soon is a ledger opened before the audit.

III. Of the trains and the platform

Two stations belong to the street, and they are given as they are given on the platform: one at Dyre Avenue, the 5; one at White Plains Road, the 2 and the 5. And one interchange with a highway the whole way, and it is for the Bronx River Parkway. That is the whole of it, and the book will not pad it.

Conduct on the platform is the true test of the visitor. The station has a voice, and the voice calls the train before the train arrives — that is the custom of the Woodlawn end, and it is why the board speaks of the broadcast of the north-west. You do not speak over the voice. You stand at the rail with your coffee, and on a Saturday at eight you do the count, because the platform does the count and the whole bench hears. You change at White Plains Road the way one changes at a hall: quietly, without announcing it, and without cutting anything with a knife. The braid gets unplaited, one strand at a time, the last to the host; the scrap gets read aloud, one word at a time, the last to the back bench.

IV. Of the table and the mayonnaise

The table of the borough is a cold one in the evening and a sweet one in the morning, and the two are to be kept separate, which is the whole of the etiquette. The buns of card 1796 are glazed while hot and eaten hot, and they are the morning; the supper is what the drawer at the hall table provides, and the drawer provides a slip, in no hand we can place, headed in capitals and signed by no one, which the desk was asked to read exactly, and which reads, word for word:

MAYONNAISE, a Sauce or Salad-Dressing for cold suppers or breakfasts.

No gloss survives, and we do not supply one. Read it as the maxim it plainly was meant to be: the cold supper takes its sauce, and the breakfast takes its dressing, and neither reaches across the table for the other. A guest does not comment on the texture of the mayonnaise; he comments on the weather, or on the train, or on nothing at all. The host drinks the first glass; the guest drinks the second, and stops when the host stops. The bun, glazed, is set on the cold table as the exception to the rule, and no one is to correct the rule because of the bun.

V. Of the waters and the pond

The Harlem, the East, and the Sound are treated as waters, not as streets: one walks beside them, one does not wash linen in them, and one does not fish the Harlem from the platform. The sound, on the east, is the one the visitor may look out at from Throggs Neck without apology; it is the open one, and it does not answer back, which makes it the easiest of the four to be polite to. The Hudson is not to be mentioned at dinner after nine, when it has gone to bed.

Within the borders, at the Woodlawn end, there is the pond in Van Cortlandt Park, and the custom of the last block is to smell it without naming it, and to name it only if asked. The view off the expressway is the same view the register keeps: Deegan in one direction, the park in the other. Give way to the northbound train, to the man with the lantern, and to any card that is being answered.

VI. Of departure

To leave the Bronx is to leave a borough of four waters, of one interchange, of two stations, and of three miles of street, and the visitor does so with one last card: left at the hall table, with the hour of departure written plainly in the corner, so that the household knows he is gone and where he might be written to. Whoever reads this chapter at Dyre Avenue or White Plains Road, take the line, leave the card, and do not cut anything with a knife. The thread is closed with the lamps, the desk reads again from the back bench on the 5 train, car 4, between Dyre Avenue and White Plains Road, and the last line, too, survives and works.
