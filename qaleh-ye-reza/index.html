DARREH & SONS GAMING · EST. 2006 · TABLE No. 39

Qaleh-ye Reza

قلعه رضا · also run as Qal’eh-ye Reʻzʊ and Qal’eh Reʩʊ; at the pass, as Qal’a Ruza

A game of holding a frontier village — 2 to 5 players, 45 to 90 minutes, ages 10 to shepherd.

What is in the box

The village of Qaleh-ye Reza lies in Hasanabad Rural District, in the Central District of Ravansar County, Kermanshah Province, Iran, in the country of folds where the mountains keep their own census. At the 2006 muster the village numbered thirty-nine souls in ten families, and the game board is drawn exactly to that headcount: ten family meeples (the Reza line in dark green, the other nine in undyed stone), thirty-nine barley tokens, one citadel piece with four merlons and a single gate, fourteen season cards named after the watercourse, the apricot terraces, the pass, and the well, one deck of forty muster cards, and this manual, third printing, 2011. The box lid, opened, is a map of the district: Hasanabad in the hollow, the county seat of Ravansar to the east, the province border far off to the northwest where the ink turns to stipple and gives up. A party plays from the table as it stands; no extension is required, and none is permitted at the pass.

Two to five players each take a family. One family — the one who drew the green meeple at setup — is the Reza line and begins beside the citadel. The others take the terraces: the upper apricot terrace, the well terrace, the pass terrace, and, for the fourth and fifth players, the two fold terraces that the map names only with small triangles. There is no hidden information in Qaleh-ye Reza. Everything on the table is meant to be seen; the game's secrecy is where each player expects water to be.

Plate I — Setup

  1. Open the box lid map and turn it so Ravansar lies to the player's right hand. The village is the small green square at the centre; the citadel piece sits on it, gate facing the pass side of the board.
  2. Deal all fourteen season cards face up in a row between the players, left to right: two of the Well, three of the Watercourse, three of the Apricots, three of the Pass, and three of the Muster. This row is the year, and it is never reshuffled.
  3. Place the thirty-nine barley tokens in a pile at the village square. The Reza line takes its meeple to the citadel tile; the other families take their terraces in the order named above, first come first seated.
  4. Shuffle the forty muster cards and deal three face down to each player. These are the family's obligations: a debt of barley, a mule to keep, a wall stone to replace. A player may look at their own three at any time, and no one else may look at them, though everyone may look.

Plate II — A turn

A turn has two moves, in order, and then the season card is turned. The turn passes to the left; the year does not wait.

First move — Work or Muster. Work: move one family meeple to an adjacent tile and take one barley token from the square, or pay one to the square, at your choice, so long as the square does not go negative. Muster: play a season card from the year row into the play area and resolve it as written. You may muster only if you have paid at least one obligation this year, which is why the third year of the game is long the quietest and the cruellest, and why veteran families save their mules.

Second move — Hold or Pass. Hold: one family meeple may stand on the citadel tile at the end of any turn and, if the Pass card is turned while it stands there, take two barley tokens instead of one. Pass: do nothing, and draw one card from the deck. The well tiles are the only place a family may hold and pass in the same turn, which is why they fight over the well.

The watercourse rule. When the Watercourse card is turned, every family pays one barley token to the square for the channel, and any family that cannot pay loses one of its obligation cards to the discard. This is the rule the village is named for, in play if not in the deed: the channel is paid by everyone, and the shortage is remembered by the one who could not. No family may be reduced to fewer than one meeple; the last meeple is the family, and the family is the game.

Plate III — The four seasons of the board

The Apricots3The upper terrace takes two tokens from the square, and the fold terraces share the rest.
The Pass3The citadel tile is open to every player for one turn; a holding family takes two.
The Muster3Recount the square. Every family with fewer tokens than its obligations owes the difference at year's end.

The year ends when the last season card is turned. Obligations are then settled against the player's barley, card for token, and the year row is redealt from the same fourteen cards — the same order, always, so that a second year plays like a promise and a third year like an obligation. The game ends at the close of the third year, or earlier, the moment any family's last meeple is made to pay for a channel it cannot reach.

Plate IV — Winning

At the final muster, each player counts barley held plus tokens still owed to them by other families, and the family with the most is the keeper of the citadel and wins the game. A tie is broken by the family nearest the citadel at the last turn; a further tie is broken by the family whose name was first spoken aloud at the table, and the manual advises players that this third measure should be used sparingly and only after tea. The winner's meeple is placed in the gate for the photograph; the gate is painted for this.

There is no draw in Qaleh-ye Reza. The village counted thirty-nine; the game divides them, and a division, however even, has a keeper.

Errata & Notes, Third Printing

On page 7 of the second printing, the Pass card was misset with the text of the Well card beneath it, one leaf torn from the proofs and pasted in at the back of the sheet so the booklet would keep its numbering. If your copy shows a faint second impression under the pass line, you hold the misbound leaf. Keep it; the print run was small, the binder was careful, and the collectors at the Ravensar table count it among the district's curiosities.

The obligation card One wall stone to replace reads, in the 2011 printing, as if the stone had already gone: it should read one wall stone to send. Play it as printed; the wall, in either reading, wants the stone.

In a 2013 club copy from the Kermanshah gaming society, one leaf had already been pasted in at the back of the sheet, and it did not stay there. Someone tore a page pasted into a second, and the two leaves now read as one long season, the Watercourse flowing directly into the Apricots without a turn between them. The club rules this a legal variant, calls it the Ruza year, and plays it only when five sit at the table and none objects.

The map on the box lid shows the village one fold south of its true position in the district. The cartographer's note, in the lid's inner corner, reads: drawn as seen, not as surveyed. The game does not correct the lid.

The Pass Roll

Roll for the Pass card's favour before any turn in which you intend to Hold on the citadel. The roll is honest and the pass is not.

The gate stands closed.

  1. 1 — The pass is closed; hold for nothing, and the square keeps its token.
  2. 2 — A mule is lost on the fold terrace; replace it before your next turn.
  3. 3 — The watercourse pays the channel; the well terrace takes one token.
  4. 4 — The pass is open to every player for this turn only.
  5. 5 — The Reza line takes two tokens; the others take one.
  6. 6 — The pass is open, and the square gains one token per family still seated.
