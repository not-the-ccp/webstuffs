Surname (family name; last name)

Catalogue No. ONM-1723 · UN proper shipping name: Name, hereditary portion, not otherwise classified · Region of first issue: Tyrolean parish register, 1723, beside a note that the hay was brought in dry · Emergency contact: Registry of Onomastic Records, Station Eiter, duty registrar

1Identification

Product nameSurname; also family name, and last name in American English usage
SynonymsFamily name; last name (American English); surname (British English); nomen in the old ledger headings
Recommended useIndicating one's family; combined with a given name to form the full name of a person
Restrictions on useNot all societies place the surname last. Several cultures place it at the start of a person's name; a reader must not assume position from the word order of the form they happen to hold.
SupplierRegistry of Onomastic Records, Station Eiter (station normals, valley above the first register)
Emergency contactDuty registrar, Station Eiter — the same office that keeps the almanac's thunder count and the 1723 register itself

2Hazard identification

Classified as Name, hereditary, category 7: stable in transmission, reactive with blank forms. The principal hazards are administrative and positional rather than physical — misplacement in the name order, drift through name change, and overflow where an individual carries more than one surname.

  • H-ONM-1 — Positional hazard: the surname may appear first, last, or in the middle of a full name depending on the culture; assume nothing.
  • H-ONM-2 — Change hazard: in most countries a person has a right to change their name, so an identity fixed in one register may not hold in the next.
  • H-ONM-3 — Overflow hazard: in most cases an individual carries just one surname, but in Portuguese-speaking and other traditions the full name may stack several given names against two or more surnames, and clerks who count by one will misfile the person.
  • H-ONM-4 — Homonym hazard: a surname used for long can be mistaken for a doctrine, a doctrine for a person, and a person for the hay.

CAUTION (positional)  Confirm name order with the bearer before filing. See Section 15 for regulatory authority.

In modern times most surnames are hereditary, though the record shows the inheritance is a habit of societies rather than a law of nature; the register of 1723 already treated it as settled, which is how most stability claims begin.

4First-aid measures

Bearer recorded with given name only: do not guess the family name from a neighbour's column in the register. Ask the bearer, or wait for the next entry. Name read aloud incorrectly: correct once, plainly, in the room where it was mispronounced; do not let a second telling pass. Two surnames registered as one: restore both; the full name is the person, and a truncated name is an incomplete person on the page.

5Fire-fighting measures

Not applicable; the material does not burn, and no extinguishing agent is known. In the unlikely event that a surname takes on a reputation of its own — the registry keeps a file for how a single family's name became shorthand for an entire case-by-case privilege test, R v Gruenke — do not feed the fire. Let the doctrine carry the weight; the name is only the label it hung from.

6Accidental release measures

If a surname is misfiled under the wrong column — a mother's maiden line, a clerk's cramped hand, a form that assumed the order English — stop the transfer, mark the entry, and correct in ink rather than overstrike. The registry's standing rule, taken from the almanac's own practice, is that a correction entered the same day costs nothing, and a correction entered a generation late costs an errata sheet.

7Handling and storage

Handle with the assumption that every entry will be read by someone who never met the bearer. Mark each record with the full name in the order the bearer uses it. Store in the parish register or its successor; keep the register dry — the 1723 entry for Eiter survives beside a note that the hay was brought in dry, and the station's logbook treats the two facts as one weather system. Station Eiter remains the registry's reference climate: sudden in the name, inclined to hold its line, clear by evening. Bearers of record who have carried the name through the weather — an Austrian climber whose register day in 2003 reads twelve kilometres per hour from the north-east, dry rock, excellent friction, and an American wrestler whose gyms were all chalk dust and still air — are catalogued in the almanac entry, Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry, where the name itself carries a fee the registry declines to itemise.

8Exposure controls / personal protection

No exposure limit applies; the surname is carried at full strength by the bearer and by every relative, clerk, and stranger who uses it. Engineering control: a single, consistent spelling. Personal protection: the bearer's own telling. Where a surname is also a test, a matrix, or a case citation, do not assert the label without checking which of the three is meant in the room.

10Stability and reactivity

Stability: stable through transmission; hereditary in most modern societies and recorded as such without comment since at least 1723. Conditions to avoid: anglicisation at the border, hyphens added or dropped by clerks, and the blank line on a two-column form. Incompatible materials: the assumption that every name has exactly one surname, and the assumption that every surname comes last.

11Toxicological information

The surname itself is non-toxic. The only documented toxicity is the nickname, which accumulates faster than the registered name and binds to the bearer with a tenacity the registry describes, in one 1891 margin, as exceeding the glue. A changed name is not a toxic by-product: it is a lawful act in most countries, and the registry records changes as movements, not failures.

12Ecological information

The surname's ecology is the registry itself: parish books, civil registers, ship manifests, school rolls, gymnasium chalk boards, and the almanac. It recurs in every archive that ever held a line of ink for a family, and it survives floods by moving — the same name can appear dry in a Tyrolean parish in 1723 and in a storm season in an American gym in 1989 without any loss of identity.

13Disposal considerations

There is nothing to destroy. A surname is retained, not disposed of: when the last bearer dies, the name is not released but banked, and the registry holds it in the register for the next person who arrives with it. The register of 1723 ends, after the entry for Eiter, with the registrar's final margin on the churchyard hollyhocks, which the registry reproduces verbatim and uncorrected, the way it does all margins: “Let them remain for about two feet high, hang-dog look, and cowardly!” The hollyhocks have, in fact, kept to the instruction.

14Transport information

May be transported by letter, marriage certificate, ship manifest, passport, or in person; no quantity restrictions, though the overflow cases of Section 2 occasionally strain the label space. Mark the document with the bearer's own order of the name. Do not translate a surname; transcribe it.

15Regulatory information

Governing authorities: the parish register, the civil registry, and the name-change statutes of each country — most of which grant a person the right to change their name, the one hazard in this sheet that the registry can only log. Key reference: Tyrolean parish register, 1723, entry Eiter, with hay note; station Eiter normals as published in the almanac.

16Other information

Prepared by the Registry of Onomastic Records, Station Eiter. Revision 4 supersedes all earlier claims that a surname is simply a last word: it is the hereditary portion of the name, and it may stand anywhere its culture sets it.

The privilege unit's sister sheet keeps the doctrine that took its label from one surname on the same desk: R v Gruenke — Safety Data Sheet, where the name Gruenke appears as the stable compound a whole case-by-case test is cited under, the registry's standing example of H-ONM-4 in the wild.

The station's weather file for the name — the normals, the thunder-cake receipt, and the two bearers carried through it — remains at Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry, and the wider shelf of records, including this sheet's annex, is held at the front page of the registry.
