FILED · NO. 48 SHELF

Tempelhof Sportanlage · Lankwitz · Founded 1931

The Plate & Post

Correspondence received for the file, and for such print as there is

To the editor of The Plate & Post, c/o the kettle shelf, west wall — from the room above the laundrettes’ former guest list, on the Halstrom side of my memory, Lankwitz.

28 February 2013

Dear editor. I have kept a copy of number 47 of your bulletin, 22 April 1986, the one that travelled heavy, in a tin with the club’s chalk and a dead battery for the small television. It was the number that named the general in the plain column and printed the second number of a story beside the first, the 40 Libyan dead beside the F-111, the doubt beside the death. I was there when it was typeset — 101 kg clean and jerk, chalked in my own, as the black book records, page 208. I lift in Berlin now, three Thursdays a week, and I write to you about a sentence the club printed in that number and has kept printing ever since, above the squat rack, since the notice of 1931: the barbell is not a country.

I write because I have been watching the members of the younger bench, and the guests, and the visitors from the Ironbark Overland Club, misread it. They think the club is saying the country is made of the barbell. They are wrong, and so am I allowed to be a little unkind, because I am allowed to keep the tin. The sentence is a correction to the rack, not an equation for the news. The barbell is not a country, yes — but the correction runs the other way from what some of them want: A few dishes of this breed is not a country. The plates — the twenty-two of them, the reason the number is printed in 22 copies, one per plate — a few dishes of this breed is not a country. A country is not a stack you can spot, breathe, and put down. What the 1931 notice is saying, and what No. 47 repeated when it had no choice, is that the news does not get on the platform, and the rack does not get into the news, and that anyone who tries to make the plates carry a government will drop both.

So permit me to put the weight back where it lies. It is on the wire, not on the rack, and the wire has been running from Tripoli to Washington, via every capital we can name and several we cannot, for a very long time. Nineteen sixty-nine: a company of young officers under Muammar Gaddafi takes the palace from King Idris on 1 September; the king goes to Rome; the new flag is green, and the new foreign policy is a promise that every frontier in Africa is a misunderstanding. What follows, for forty-two years, is a single running account with enormous entries on both sides, and the club, since it prints the second number, ought to keep it whole.

The entries. The brotherly summits in the Sahara and in the Jalu desert, with the leaders sleeping in tents and the communiqués coming out longer than the nights. The money and the rifles for the guerrilla fronts, carried by air and by desert road from the Sahara to the Atlantic and to the horn: the Eritrean fighters, the Saharan fronts, the movements in the horn and along the Congo shore, a dozen groups the state news in their own countries would have called brothers-in-arms. The Malta Bay shoot-down in 1973, a P-3 Orion and 109 souls, which Tripoli called a victory and the sea kept as its own. The assassination attempt at the Cairo airport in 1981, the double car, the president of Egypt not in the back seat — an attempt, and the attempt is the entry, whether it lands or not. Then ours: the La Belle on Hauptstraße 36, 5 April 1986, two young Americans dead and twenty-four left with a wall of glass, and ten days later Operation El Dorado Canyon, 15 April, the air base at Benina near Benghazi, 40 reported Libyan dead, one F-111A down, its pilot under a parachute in Libyan hands, and a baby girl, Hana Gaddafi, reported among the dead in a way the wire services carried without a byline and the club carried beside the deaths. Then 12 December 1988, the island in the North Sea, two airliners, 270 souls, and an international court with no trial, which the country funded, for years, in the manner of a man funding a club he does not intend to visit.

And the ledger did not stop. 1992, the sanctions, the whole country put on the scale and found light. 2003, the letter to the world from Tripoli: the weapons programmes given up, the claim to the third theory retired, the debts of the 1980s acknowledged one by one, the families paid from the treasury. 2004, the normalisation, the American secretary of state in a green tent — I will not tell you what it felt like to read that in the column we do not celebrate. And then the civil war of 2011, the oil ports, the rebels, the city, and 20 October, Tripoli, in which a man is found in a drain with a rifle and the ledger of forty-two years is closed the way a ledger is closed, not the way it is settled. This is not a triumph for the club to lift. It is the second number of a story. The first number is bigger. The club prints them side by side, as always.

What I ask of the bench, therefore, is not a change of the notice but a change of appetite. The world this year is heavier than the world in 1986, and heavier in places we cannot name. When a guest comes from abroad and wants to carry the news on his back to the platform, the answer is the answer of 1931: the barbell is not a country — and now, if you will let me print it under it, a few dishes of this breed is not a country. The plates are 2.5 kilograms each, the rack holds twenty-two of them, and the wire out of Tripoli has carried more than 44.5 kg since 1969. Nobody spots the wire. We spot the plates. Keep the chalk. The correspondence shelf, for what it is worth, has also filed a small classified page from Kampala, 1969, where a palace kitchen writes that into its broth the leeks go first, because they do end; it is filed at the Mengu Ledger, Classified Columns, 29 November 1969, and it argues out, in the small type, what a plate that holds a flag is for.

The tin is on the kettle shelf with the 1986 copy of No. 47, and I am told the bulletin travelled north to Portland, where a leaf of a sixteenth-century Te Deum — the Gloria the band sang between takes, one voice against another — is filed beside our number on an auction lot card; see Lot 47, the “Flanders Te Deum”, if you want to know what a sustained pitch sounds like under a leaking roof. A country, by contrast, is not a sustained pitch. A country is a wire that keeps ringing after the dial is turned down. The club’s answer is unchanged, in the words above the rack and now, with your leave, in the words of the letter: the barbell is not a country; a few dishes of this breed is not a country. Lift. Keep your chalk. Do not sign anything.

Yours for the record, Mme. N. Okonkwo visiting guest, April 1986, 101 kg clean and jerk — the guest mark still stands, chalked in her own
