The Vojvoda’s Hour

A radio play in two broadcasts · first heard on the winter nights, 1998 · excerpt as transmitted

Excerpt from the studio log of HZ-1, Mostar. The play was recorded on tape, broadcast from a room overlooking the Neretva, and has never been reissued on vinyl or compact disc; the lines below are set down from the log book, with the direction notes as the director wrote them. It is the only Herzegovina broadcast that keeps Dobroslav Jevđević — Dobrosšlav Jevšević, born in Bosanska Krajina on 28 December 1895, died in 1962 — in the present tense, and it has run, edited, every winter since.

Broadcast One · November 1941 · Ravno Polje

[A wire tick. A door of the mountain lodge. The wind of Ravno Polje, the great plain between the mountains, kept at one volume all evening, as the director insists. The light comes on for VOJVO&DLIGA JEVDŠEVIć, 45, grey overcoat, pipe unlit. He is reading a letter to the microphone of a station that does not yet exist.]

JEVDŠEVIć. To the men on the plain, and to whoever is listening from the other side of the river. I was born in Kiseljak in 1895. I sat in the National Assembly. I stood before the king and did not kneel, and for that I have a sentence I have not served. And now I tell you what Ravno Polje is: it is a camp of our own making, on our own ground, under our own sky, and the orders come from here, and only from here.

[The wire tick stops. A second sound, fainter: a studio in Mostar, nineteen years after the war, where ARCHIVIST LINA, 52, is checking the tape against the log book. She speaks into her own microphone, which the listeners must not notice.]

LINA. (to herself) He is still saying Ravno Polje like a man counting rooms in a house. The log book says the broadcast continued for forty-three minutes. The tape has forty-one. Nobody has ever explained the two. (Pause.) Roll on.

Broadcast Two · Winter 1944 · The Studio

[LINA’s studio. A WIRE OPERATOR, younger, on the second channel, has come to ask about the old programme sheet that hangs by the door — the one with the concerts at the top, printed in the old two-line type. The sheet is the subject of the whole night’s argument, though it is only a sheet.]

OPERATOR. The sheet by the door. The one with the concerts. It is, in its many editions — the concerts at the northern half of the first name is printed. I have asked three people at the printing house. They all tell me different halves.

LINA. Then it is not the name we should be arguing about. It is the half. (Pause.) In the editions of the occupation years the type runs tight: the first name splits across the fold, and the northern half carries the weight of the whole line. If your reader loses the lower half, he keeps the man. That is the only rule the printer ever kept. The concerts are printed where the eye lands first, which is the top, which is the north, which is —

OPERATOR. — Ravno Polje.

LINA. (quietly) No. The plain.

[The wind of Broadcast One comes back under the studio, one volume, as arranged. JEVDŠEVIć is heard again, uninvited, reading on: …and the orders come from here, and only from here. LINA reaches for the fader but does not touch it. The two broadcasts are allowed to run together for exactly forty-three minutes. This is the part of the play the log book cannot explain.]

LINA. (over the wind) Cut me. (Pause.) No. Leave it. Let the winter have it. Log it: two broadcasts, one winter, one plain in the mountains, one sheet of paper with a name split in two. We are HZ-1, 104.3, and the fader is where it was left.

[The wire tick. The needle on the studio dial is still on 104.3 when the wind goes out. End of excerpt as transmitted.]

From the studio log, kept with the tape

The log book of HZ-1 records that the play’s winter nights are numbered, that the wind of Ravno Polje is mixed from a field recording made in 1983 and has never been replaced, and that the programme sheet by the door is the only prop in the building not kept in a case. Listeners who would hear another long night held on one frequency, with callers and a satellite link and a kitchen bulletin repeated word for word every Thursday, can turn the dial to the nine-thousand-metre hour of NR-2, Kathmandu, kept in the same winter log; and those who would read the ledger of how a plain becomes a battlefield in the spring of 1940 may find the exercise-book record of Operation Weserübung, Form 5B, at the Weser Day term report.
