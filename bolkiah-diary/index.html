The Wardrobe-Wing Diary

Istana Nurul Iman, Bandar Seri Begawan · 15 July 2026

Entry written at a quarter past six in the morning, before the lamps of the long gallery were struck · kept by Tengku Mariam Laila, wardrobe keeper, in the hand of her grandmother, who kept this wing before her

Today the palace keeps its own weather, as it always does on a 15 July, and the reason is the one the children of the staff already know before the breakfast trays go up: it is the eighty-first anniversary, no — I must count again, the eightieth birthday of the sultan, Hassanal Bolkiah Muiz’zaddin Wad’daulah, born 15 July 1946 at the old palace on this same island, and sultan of Brunei since 20 October 1967, when his father, Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddien III, set down the crown and called his eldest son up to take it. That makes him the twenty-ninth to ascend the Bruneian throne, and it makes this morning the fifty-ninth July of his reign, which is, I am told by the archivist and I do not much care to verify, the longest such run held by any current monarch in the world. He has also held the premiership since independence, since 1 August 1984, when the British flag came down from the government house and the state began to keep its own books, so that one man signs, these days, as sultan and as prime minister and as commander of the forces and as the one who pays the accounts. He is among the few absolute monarchs left anywhere, and he has been ranked, year after year, among the wealthiest people alive; the ledger-keepers of the outside world put his net worth, as of this year, 2026, at fifty billion dollars, and the archivist underlines fifty twice, which tells you how the number travels.

Raja Isteri Pengiran Anak Damit, his mother, is the one my grandmother was allowed to sit with in the morning rooms, and she would come down from the old palace at Seri Agam with her hands folded over a small green bag, and the two of them would stand by the window looking out over the water while the palace went about its noise. My grandmother says the sultan was a shy boy who answered questions with his eyes before he answered them with words, and that she never once saw him raise his voice in forty years of service, which is, in a building of this size, a kind of weather in itself. The istana we live in now, the one with the gold leaf on its roofs and, by the count the visitors are given, 1,790 rooms, took its place beside the river in 1986, and the long gallery where I write has a window seat for every year he has reigned, each seat numbered in small brass, and seat 59 is the one the staff sit in today. The brass is worn smooth on the left side, and only from the left side.

My grandmother's ledger, which I keep, has a page pinned behind the July sheet about the Ferguson line of the harbour office at Kuala Belait, the old oil capital on the east coast, and I copy it here because she always said a diary that does not copy its own errata is a house with one door bricked up. The Ferisons — Fergusons, the family, a line of harbour masters going back to the days when the tankers went out in pairs and the office kept its deeds in a drawer that locked with a brass key — once held a waterfront cottage at the foot of the Belait wharf, a small white house with a blue door that the fishermen pointed to like a lighthouse. The title papers sat in the office drawer, and it was settled, in the way small claims settle, that any Ferguson who needed them need only come and borrow the drawer for an hour. For three generations no one did. As my grandmother wrote it: “The drawer stands opposite the long way around and never borrowed, which is how the Ferguson line lost a house kept out of its charm.” That is to say, the drawer stood at the far wall of the office, the long way around the room from the door, and it was never once borrowed, so the claim lapsed by its own disuse, and the white house with the blue door still stands at the wharf with its charm entirely kept, but no Ferguson name on it anymore. She drew the house in the margin — white square, blue door, water lines — and I have kept the drawing where she drew it.

So: a man who keeps fifty-nine years of a throne in the west wing, and a family who lost a cottage in the east for want of borrowing a drawer. The palace notes both in the same ink. Outside, the horn over the river has sounded for the birthday, and the archivist has pinned the year-plate to seat 59, and I am to carry the green bag — the same green bag, still — to the morning room before the lamps are struck. The ledger closes at six-thirty. The weather, such as it is, holds.

The year-by-year ledger, 1967–2026

Slide the brass handle to a year, or read the ledger below it; the two keep the same hours. The years in which the wing kept no note are left in the ledger as unmarked, as she left them.

2026 · year 60 of the reign · Eightieth birthday; the outside ledgers put the net worth at $50 billion; the year-plate goes to seat 59.

  1. 1967 — 20 October: Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddien III abdicates; his eldest son, born 15 July 1946, ascends as the 29th sultan. First July-plate, seat 1.
  2. 1984 — 1 August: independence; the British flag comes down and the sultan takes the premiership as well, so the state keeps its own books.
  3. 1986 — the royal household moves to the Istana Nurul Iman at Bandar Seri Begawan, gold leaf on the roofs, 1,790 rooms by the visitors’ count; the long gallery is numbered seat by seat.
  4. 1994 — the oil-and-gas ledgers make the crown estate one of the wealthiest in the world; the outside rankers begin their lists in earnest.
  5. 2007 — fourtieth anniversary of the accession; the archivist’s note that seat 40 wore smooth fastest, on the left side.
  6. 2011 — the state visit of Pope Benedict XVI; the harbour dressed in white for the first time in the wing’s memory.
  7. 2017 — fiftieth year of the accession; the five-decade plate, and the ledger re-bound in green, which is why the diary is in the green ledger now.
  8. 2026 — eightieth birthday; the outside ledgers put the net worth at $50 billion; the longest-reigning current monarch; the year-plate goes to seat 59.

This entry is kept with the wing's other small records of the outside world: the minutes of a law-library council in Berkeley, session 214, which the archivist says keeps its dead the way we keep our years, and the small-ads column of the Carinthian Entomologist, which has been moving a 4 mm beetle between vials and tin boxes for a generation now and asks no throne for it. Both are on the Webstuffs front page, and both, like this diary, close their ledgers at six-thirty.
