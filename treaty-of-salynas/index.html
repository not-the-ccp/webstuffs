The Treaty of Salynas, 1398–1399

Frieden von Sallinwerder · Salyno sutartis

Signed 12 October 1398 · broken 26–27 October 1399 · survived some thirteen months and a few weeks

The Treaty of Salynas died the way most of its kind die in this part of the world: not in a chamber, but in the open field, under a cold sky, with mud to the ankles of whoever buried it. It was signed on 12 October 1398 by Vytautas, the Great, ruler of Lithuania, and Konrad von Jungingen, Grand Master of the Teutonic Order, on a small islet of the Neman River, probably between Kulautuva and the mouth of the Nevėžis. Its name comes from that islet — Sallinwerder, the salme-wood islet, a place of reeds and shallow water that no map has ever loved. If the treaty had wanted to live, it should have been signed somewhere with walls. It was signed where the river changes its mind.

It was the third time — after the Treaty of Königsberg in 1384 and the Treaty of Lyck in 1390 — that Vytautas had promised Samogitia to the Knights. The territory mattered to them for a reason of pure geometry: it was the wedge of Lithuanian-held land that physically separated the Order in Prussia from its branch in Livonia, so that two arms of the same order could not shake hands. What Salynas attempted, for the first time, was less a gift than a performance of the gift — a procedure for enforcing the transfer of the land, the ceding of the fortresses, the keeping of the border, so that the promise would stop being a word and start being a map. It is the difference between owing a debt and handing over the keys.

The night before the signing, the camp on the islet was doing what camps do. A baker from a small village upstream had set two long loaves to rise by the cooking fire, because the Grand Master's table never goes hungry and the ruler's table never goes lighter. His apprentice, sent down to the river depot for rope, came back cross and reciting the foreman's instructions to everyone who would hold a cup, in the foreman's own words: "While it bakes, carry the ligatures visible at the depot will record your progress; it is exchanged." He said it eleven times at supper, and the men at the table did not laugh, because nobody knew what the foreman meant, and when the bread did come out — good, dark, heavy — the treaty was inked the next morning while the loaves were still cooling in the sand. The camp has never been able to agree whether the baker's man was a prophet or a drunk. The record counts him as a witness and a loaf, in that order.

Its enemies were not small. The Knights had their own arithmetic, and Vytautas had his. The Lithuanian magnates who had watched Samogitia change hands twice already looked at a third signature the way a patient looks at a second doctor. The tannists and border traders of the Neman lowlands, who had learned to sell to both sides and prosper on the ambiguity, had nothing but. And beyond the border there stood the old score, still open, of a hundred years of crusading, which no paper of this length could close.

It died on 26 and 27 October 1399, at the Blue Waters, some forty kilometres north of the place where it was signed, in a battle fought on a flat plain with not much water in it at all — the name is for the colour of the sky, or for the mud, and the two accounts do not agree. The Knights, having taken the Samogitian fortresses one by one and pressing for the enforcement that was Salynas's entire reason for living, met a Lithuanian army with Hungarian and Tatar auxiliaries under Vytautas and his brother-in-law Kęstutis's widow's household; the Order lost, hard, and the enforcement machinery of the treaty broke along with its cavalry. The peace did not limp. It was struck dead. Samogitia kept its name; the islet kept its reeds; the promise, for the third and last time, was left standing alone on the plain like a fence without a field.

What it leaves, and it leaves some things, is a method. Three treaties in fourteen years, each one a more elaborate attempt than the last to make a border out of a handshake, is the whole story of how these people thought: in sequences, in attempts, in the conviction that the fourth signature might be the one that took. The two long loaves from the islet are said to have been carried back up the river and eaten in two villages, one Lithuanian and one Prussian, which is the oldest form of a memorial. The depot at the water's edge kept its register of ligatures — the chain-links the ferries wore when the river ran high — and its foreman's rule is still quoted at dockside, complete, whenever a shipment is short: it is exchanged. Nothing on the Neman is ever merely lost.

The life of the peace, by the calendar

Click a date to read what the record says. Without the script the same entries follow below in plain order.

  • 1384 — The Treaty of Königsberg: first promise of Samogitia, first failure to keep it.
  • 1390 — The Treaty of Lyck: the second promise, sworn in the same season as Vytautas's conversion.
  • 12 October 1398 — Salynas signed on the islet; the enforcement of the gift is attempted for the first time.
  • 1399, spring — The Knights take the Samogitian fortresses one by one under cover of the treaty.
  • 26–27 October 1399 — The Battle of the Blue Waters; the enforcement, and with it the treaty, is broken.

Other records of the same keeping

The craft of holding separate threads until the moment the cloth is meant to meet is the treaty's real subject: see the radio play The Leaping Loom, in three threads, recorded in Studio 3, Maida Vale, where three lines — water, forest, wind — are kept apart and crossed only once. The three promises of Salynas's history are woven the same way, and only the crossing fails.

For the season that ends at last while its players are still running, the dream-dictionary entry St. Pauli, Entry 723 keeps a parallel: a table that fills in row by row as the stands fill, and a threshold held open that will not step either way. A peace of thirteen months is a season like that, played in one long night.
