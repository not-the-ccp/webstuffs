The Soft Approach

A Book of Etiquette of the Mouth

Chapter III

Of the Near Approach, Without Contact

Or, the Manners of the Approximant

Second impression, corrected & reissued for the hall of nine · the Provisioner’s Desk, in conference with the Redlands Historical Society

Rule I. Of the Nature of the Approaching Sound

The approximant is a sound of coming near. The articulators approach one another — tongue to palate, lip to lip, tongue to the ridge — but not narrowly enough, nor with enough articulatory precision, to create a turbulent airstream. The sound is held between the fricative, which closes the gap tightly enough to make a turbulent airstream, and the vowel, which leaves the gap wholly open, and it belongs, as a subclass, to the sonorants and the continuants: the polite class of the mouth, the class that does not stop, does not burst, and does not hurry. What the fricative says outright, the approximant only suggests; what the vowel says at ease, the approximant only implies, at a distance the listener must be willing to cover. The Registry of the Hall of Names keeps no safety sheet for the class, for the simple reason that nothing can go wrong with a guest who keeps his distance; the class is instead the subject of this book, which is where manner belongs. The stricture is low, the airflow continuous, and the guest, on the principle of the chapter, is received at one arm’s length.

Rule II. Of [j] and the Yes-Door

The palatal [j], as in yes, is the sound of the yes itself, and the yes is the first sound a hall makes. In the hall of nine doors a yes is given from the far side of the room, palm out, at the distance of one arm’s length; a yes at close quarters is a fright, and a fright is a stop. The [j] of yes is the only consonant the book permits a host to pronounce in the first minute of a visit, on the ground that it approaches the sound of agreement without ever touching it. The book records one trespass of the kind: the u heard at the fourth door, which made of Dong-hoon a Dong-whun and was wanted back at the register, or at liberty, whichever it preferred. The Registry’s account of the class the trespasser nearly joined, in the neighbouring material’s own stationery, stands in Section 2.4 of SDS-NC-01, where a misnamed kind of nasal is referred to the Registry with a correction to be made in the margin, lightly inked — which is, the book observes, the correct manner for an error that has overreached.

Rule III. Of [w] and the West Gate

The labio-velar [w], as in west, is the sound of the west gate of the orchard road, and the book takes its authority from a photograph in the depot archive at 55 E. State Street. In the spring of 1926 the coach Pierce “Caddy” Works — of the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies, then 14–2 — walked a crate of the new season cards out of his car at the 1915 depot and carried it west along the orchard road, and the gait the photograph shows him is that of a [w] and nothing else: the round of the lips complete, the air passing through, continuous from start to finish, with no burst and no hiss. A walk of that description is the whole of the chapter’s advice on carriage. The small bunch of parsley which the coach subsequently applied to the crate was not an approximant; it was an attack. The archive has never explained it, the minute book keeps the ruled lines where the explanation might have gone and keeps them empty, and this chapter, in keeping with the archive, leaves them empty. The crate is open on Thursdays, as the county museum requires. Do not add parsley.

Rule IV. Of the Liquids, [l] and [ɹ]

The liquids are the class in its resting dress. [l] as in less: the tongue to the ridge, the air flowing over the blade, and the least possible of stricture — the book’s word for it is less, and it is the whole rule, because a liquid that is not less is a fricative in a liquid’s coat. [ɹ] as in rest: the tongue merely turning in, the air passing by it without being asked to do anything, which is the only manner in which a guest should treat a resting-place. On the walking sheet of the orchard road — 1.9 miles, eleven stops, from the 1915 Santa Fe depot to the survey stake and back — a walker who makes turbulence at a stop has no rest at all, and the society that keeps the sheet will not log the walker. A liquid is a sound that arrives where it is going without arriving; the book recommends it to anyone, anywhere, at any table, and it is the class the book would most like its readers to have by heart, being the class that rests.

Rule V. Of the Tailor’s Note

In the same drawer of the depot archive as the photograph there is a single note, in the hand of the tailor of the depot, concerning the coach’s woollen frocks and tarred trowsers, which the tailor had placed in the crate alongside the season cards. The note is quoted here word for word, in the book’s standing manner for anything the archive has left unexplained, and it is: “These are merely put in the same as for anything — had not been cultivated in our woollen frocks and tarred trowsers we are warranted in recommending.” That is the whole of it. Read as the book reads it — which is to say, as a tailor’s note — the meaning is that the frocks and the trowsers are merely put in the crate in the same way as anything else is put in a crate, that no special handling has been given them, and that on that ground of unexceptionable ordinariness the tailor is warranted in recommending them. The book’s verdict is that the tailor understood the near approach better than most of the mouth: he put two garments into a crate without pressing them, he kept whatever cultivation he had kept at a distance, and he warranted his doing so in the one place a recommendation is permitted, which is the note. The frocks travelled west one point nine miles in the crate, came out, on the archive’s record, with no wrinkle reported and no turbulence at all, and were not mentioned again, which is the last manner a garment can wish for.

Rule VI. Of Smoke, and of a Fog That Approached

A soft sound passes through smoke, and the book records the passage. The long fire of 21 November to 2 December 2047 took signs, doors, and the orthography of an entire coastline, and took the whole city with them — the account of the city’s passing stands at San Francisco (1776–2047) — Obituary — and yet the [m]s, [n]s and [ŋ]s it could count were counted and found none missing; the letters it could not recover were the ones that had never been written down, which is a fact about fires and a fact about languages and, in the book’s reading, one fact. The soft sounds fared better still, for the reason that they had always kept their distance. And the fog, the city’s oldest companion, which had not been seen since early August and came back on 4 December at 6:40 in the morning without explaining itself, was, in the book’s final ruling, an approximant of the largest water: it approached the city every morning for a century and a half, never narrowly enough to be a wall, never with enough precision to be a wind that had a name, continuously, sonorously, and without turbulence; it did not stop, and it did not break, and it did not hurry. The book admits it to the class without a second opinion.
