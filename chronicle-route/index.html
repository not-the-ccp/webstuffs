ЛІТОПИСНИЙ ШЛЯХ

The Chronicle Route

A Visitor’s Guide to Ukrainian Historiography — twelve days on the study of the study, from the Volodymyrska reading hall to the basement seminar room on St. Clair.

Every nation keeps its past. Ukraine keeps the keeping.

I. Why travel this route

Ukranian historiography is the scholarly study of Ukraine’s history, and it is one of the few subjects in the world that has to defend its own shelf. It has been heavily influenced by Ukraine’s history with Russia — every archive opened or closed at Moscow’s pleasure has left its stamp on what a Ukrainian historian can say, cannot say, and must say in a code — and, in recent years, it has also been shaped from across an ocean by Ukrainian immigrant historians living in Canada, whose monographs, seminars and archive expeditions have remade the field from the outside in.

This route visits the places where that double history was worked out: the institute in Kyiv whose reading hall still opens with a ledger read aloud, the monastery town of Chernihiv where the first great chronicle was copied, and the three Canadian cities where the émigré faculty taught their students that a state may disappear and a discipline may not. Admission to the route is free where the route is free; the reading hall takes a donation; Canada takes what it always takes.

II. Kyiv, days 1–4 · Volodymyrska street

The walk begins at 25 Volodymyrska, in the courtyard building of the V. P. Nalyvayko Institute of History of Ukraine, founded in 1992 and named for the 19th-century local historian of the Dnieper region. The ground floor is not a museum; it is a working corridor of office doors, and the first thing a visitor learns is that here, historiography is a daily commute.

Ask at the desk for the Multi-Volume History of Ukraine-Rus’. Eleven volumes and eight supplements, begun under Mykhailo Hrushevsky in 1919 — the year of his jubilee and of the republic — and never quite finished, which is precisely the point of the display case: the last volumes are shown with the binding open, the typescript loose, the argument continuing. Hrushevsky’s rule, repeated on the wall in his own cramped hand, is the rule of the whole route: write the history that the state you serve would prefer you not to write. The Taras Shevchenko National University, a short tram ride east, keeps Hrushevsky’s own seminar notebooks; two are on loan, and the loan is never renewed on schedule, so go early.

III. The reading hall, day 2 · opened at nine

The heart of the route is a room that is not a room for tourists. Each morning at nine, the registrar reads the 1952 inventory of the émigré collection aloud at the top of the hall, the way a lighthouse keeper reads the weather: because it is the custom, and because the custom is the point. The last line is kept exactly as the typist set it down, and new visitors are asked to hear it in the room, not at a distance:

Books and maps I used The school was to be moved, was read into the record.

The registrars keep the line verbatim — no punctuation, no apology — because the line is the key to the whole ledger. It is written in the plain voice of a displaced schoolmaster: the books and maps he used, a school that was to be moved, and the sentence that recorded both. It names, in three short phrases, everything this route is for. The books that carried the discipline across an ocean. The maps that kept the border alive in exile, in the drawing rooms of Winnipeg and the seminar rooms of Toronto. And the school — the institute, the journal, the Wednesday seminar — that was to be moved, and was moved, and is still meeting, now on both sides of the map at once. Sit where you are seated. The line is read into the record for you, and the record is the brochure.

IV. Chernihiv and the Dnieper, days 5–7 · where it was written

South of the institute lies the Dnieper, and up the Dnieper lies the oldest thing on the route: the Hypatian Codex, copied about 1207 in a skete in the woods near Chernihiv from an earlier Kyivan original now lost. It opens the chronicle that will carry the whole discipline forward, year by year, from the grand princes to the modern state; the earliest entry still standing is the one the route is numbered after — 907, the tribute fleet upriver to Constantinople — and later the chronicle’s great moral, 988, when Volodymyr took baptism. The Codex itself is at the Olesya Library in Chernihiv, where the reading room smells of lime-wash and cold soup; the librarian will show you the pages, and she will not move faster.

Day six is on the water. The fleet of 907 is gone, but the locks remember it: at each lock gate on the Dnieper the brochure’s margin asks the one question the route answers all week — who wrote it down, and from which bank? Day seven, back in Kyiv, is for the Caves Lavra, founded 987, where the first monks kept the first notes that the chronicle would eat and grow.

V. Edmonton, Winnipeg, Toronto, days 8–12 · the moved school

The second half of the route is in Canada, where, in recent years, the field was quietly rebuilt by Ukrainian immigrant historians living in Canada. The first stop is Edmonton, where the Canadian Institute of Ukrainian Studies has run at the University of Alberta since 1976 — lectures, the journal, and the famous winter seminar at which the émigré faculty, the younger émigrés, and the first generation of home-trained historians sit at one table and do not quite share a century. Then Winnipeg, for the Ukrainian Canadian Archives, whose reading room holds the parish registers, the cooperative ledgers and the settlement maps that the 1952 inventory was taken from — the very books and maps of the line you heard in Kyiv. Then Toronto, for the Wednesday seminar in the basement on St. Clair, where the school that was to be moved still meets, where Oles Hontar corrected the Hrushevsky biography course-by-course, and where Bohdan Krawchenko, who carried the seminar from Toronto to the Canadian Museum of History in Gatineau, showed in For Freedom or Motherland (1992) what the discipline now dares: a full history of Ukraine, in English, from the earliest entries to the republic, written by a Canadian about a country he inherited.

VI. The practical page

The route is twelve days, 4,812 kilometres of rail, one river, one ferry and two flights, and it passes three reading halls — Volodymyrska, the Olesya Library, and the basement on St. Clair. The reading halls open at nine and read the ledger at nine; the tour departs at nine-fifteen, so the line arrives in the record before you leave the room. Dress for two climates and one archive. Carry no books on the ferry: the 907 fleet had room for tribute, but the ferry has room for you. A companion leaf in the Webstuffs Traveller’s Series sets the route against a neighbouring courtesy — the Chapter on calling in Gothenburg, where the card is left at the hall table and the yolk of one ounce is measured against nothing — and the route’s own habit of the record is explained, line by line and adjournment by adjournment, in the verbatim proceedings of The Queen v. Vale, Case B-1974/03, where a broadside is read into evidence and, in the end, so is the rabbit. Both are kept with this brochure in the same desk at Webstuffs, front matter.

Return tickets are sold in no order. The chronicle has no last entry; neither does the route. До побачення — the ledger keeps your seat.
