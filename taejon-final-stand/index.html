A Method of the Final Holding of a River Line,
with the City of Taejon as Instrument, and the Names Preserved

Inventor of record: William F. Dean, of the division headquarters at Taejon, and those of the Twenty-Fourth who held the KumCLAIMS: SEVEN · FIGURES: NONE SURVIVED

Abstract. A method of holding a line, in which a division of three field regiments, already spent by sixteen days of delaying action, is gathered to a single city and set upon the bank of the river that runs through it, the line being described from the Kum eastward in a curve of four and a half miles, and the holding being carried for four days — 16 to 20 July 1950 — against a force several times the number, with armour in support, and ending not in abandonment but in dispersal, the elements separating and continuing. The invention is not the line. The line is a means. The invention is what a line does to the names it keeps.

I. Field of the Invention

The invention concerns the final holding of a defensive line by an infantry division in a major road junction, and has particular application to the city of Taejon (since re-spelt Daejeon), the great transport hub of the Korean peninsula, where the Kum River bends north, where four national roads meet, and where a headquarters that has been walking south for two weeks can stop, once, and be found. The apparatus comprises, in the order they were actually used: the 19th Infantry Regiment on the high ground west of the city at Chochi; the 34th Infantry Regiment with its position in the northern part of the town; the 2nd Infantry Regiment east of the Kum; and the headquarters company, the signal office, the chaplain's tent, and one telephone wire that answered until 18:00 on the 19th and never again.

II. Background of the Invention

What is known in the art: a fresh infantry division can hold a river line for weeks. What is not known, until this application, is how a spent one holds it for four days. The regiments of the Twenty-Fourth had been falling back since the first crossings of the 38th parallel on 25 June — Osan, Pyongtaek, Chonan — each position held and given up in the same afternoon, each road the same road, the trucks of the 85th Heavy Tank Regiment of the Korean People's Army passing south with the T-34/85s that the art calls, in the quiet of its own offices, the tanks. By the 16th of July the division had gathered at last: no more delaying, the order ran, but a stand, and one line only, the Kum, and the city itself as the hinge on which the line turns. The prior art is acknowledged freely in Section V. The present invention is the difference between a position held by a fresh division and the same position held by this one, at the end of sixteen days, with ammunition figures no one will put in print.

III. Description of the Invention

Day one, 16 July. The 34th Infantry takes the northern town and the rail approaches; the 19th establishes on the Chochi ridge to the west, four hundred and ten metres of high ground that the survey plates call, without sentiment, feature 410. The line is drawn. The Kum is not crossed; the Kum is looked at.

Days two and three, 17–18 July. The attack comes in the expected directions first, which is the only courtesy the opposing art will pay. The 4th Division of the Korean People's Army takes the eastern bank by pieces while armour rides the road behind it, and the 2nd Infantry's positions east of the Kum go in the order the map lists them. The town of Taejon itself is fought over at the junction, at the railway yard, at the market that is no longer a market. The Chochi ridge is attacked from the south by the enemy's 13th Division in the morning and again in the afternoon; the 2nd Battalion of the 19th holds it both times, and what it holds, in the language of this office, is the west door of the city.

Day four, 19–20 July. The headquarters is surrounded and overrun on the 19th; the inventors of record are separated from the line, one by one, without the line being told. The 2nd Battalion, 19th Infantry, holds Chochi on the 20th to the last hour of the specification — the last, in the art of the final stand, being an hour chosen by the enemy — and falls back in a column that does not appear in any figure, because there is no figure.

Operation of the method. The line is not a wall but a clock. Each of its elements gives up time in its own direction, and the division as a whole is the sum of the times. When the sum runs out, the elements do not break; they separate, which is a different verb and the whole of the invention: a line that can be spent without being destroyed, a division that loses its city and keeps its regiments apart and alive, each one carrying one number — 19, 34, 2 — like a man carrying a piece of the house he has just lost, wrapped in a blanket, down the same road everyone else is taking south.

IV. Claims

  1. We claim: a method of final holding, comprising a spent infantry division, a single river line of not more than five miles, and a city at the bend of the river, the division being gathered for the purpose and not dispersed until the sum of its elements' times is exhausted.
  2. We claim: the ridge of 410 metres as west door, in combination with the town as hinge, such that the holding of either is accounted in time rather than in ground.
  3. We claim: a headquarters placed within the area so held, such that its fall is coincident with and not prior to the general breaking of the line.
  4. We claim: the separation of the elements upon exhaustion, each element withdrawing along a separate road, the roads meeting no further than the next crossing of the river, where the art recommences with a fresh, and smaller, line.
  5. We claim: the preservation of the regimental numbers as the only part of the division which the enemy is not credited with.
  6. We claim: four days, as the period of the holding, being the maximum demonstrated for the apparatus herein described and the minimum for the purpose of it.
  7. We claim: the city itself, to be recorded under the name as found at filing — Taejon, in the spelling of 1950 — and under the later spelling thereafter, the two entries being held as one.

V. References Cited

The examiner's file holds a copy of the 1945 survey plate of the Taejon junction, on which the Kum line is drawn in the old arrowhead convention. Its marginal note reads, in the surveyor's hand: “Funchal is the arrowhead and ends at two.” Under that convention “Funchal” was the draughtsman's name for the tip of the line that points the way the water goes — named, on this plate, after the port where the draughtsman last drew a river — and “ends at two” is read as the line terminating at the second station of the second order, which is precisely where the Kum bends north of the town, and precisely where the 34th was to stand four years' worth of the future on. The note is cited not as prior art but as the first recorded statement, in this office, that a line may be drawn before it is held.

The file also carries a drainage-line application in the same form from the Constanța registry — Application No. RO-A-2026 0174, A Drainage Line for the Taș-Punar Catchment — the nearest thing in the art to a line that holds a name the way the Kum held a city, and a pair of field notes on a nearly-submerged island in the Napa River where the writer paces the highest mound, three feet, and files the island under the same claim this application makes for the ridge: Knight Island — Field Notes. Both are received by this office without comment, which is the highest comment the office makes. The file also holds, without number and without claim, a letter to the editor on the reels of a Milan studio — a Sinfonia, a voice trying a single word eleven ways, and one unparseable line carried over word for word (Sequenza — A Letter to the Editor) — filed against Claim 5, on the ground that a tape, like a regiment, preserves the names it was given and loses the rest.
