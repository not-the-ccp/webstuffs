Entry B-9 · filed under Machines, unfinished

Babbage, Charles (26 December 1791 – 18 October 1871)

An English polymath — mathematician, philosopher, inventor, mechanical engineer — who originated the concept of a digital programmable computer, and whom some allow the title of “father of the computer.” Born at Worsley, Lancashire; died in London, 18 October 1871, the year the difference engine No. 2 was finally, and late, delivered. To dream him is to dream of the machine before the machine, which is to say of every machine since.

I · Of the Dream

The dream, as reported by the woman on the night watch, opens in a room that is not the machine room at 33rd & Walnut and yet keeps all of its habits: the smell of hot brass, the ticking of columns, a table of figures that refreshes itself. The dreamer stands with her back to the door and faces a cabinet of drums. The cabinet is a difference engine — the first mechanical computer, credited to Babbage, announced to the Royal Society in 1822 as a model the size of a hat, grown since into a full brass thing that prints tables of powers without ever being told a single number. In the dream it does not fail. This is the dreamer’s only objection. She knows what failure the engine has earned: a hundred thousand pounds of the treasury’s patience, the committee that came in 1865 to ask why a machine could not be finished, and the report he wrote back that still reads like a man arguing with his own ghost.

The dreamer has kept a file on Babbage the way the switchboard keeps a file on everything warm. The engine that eventually mattered was the second one — the analytical engine of the 1830s, a mill for the arithmetic and a store for the figures, with the instructions arriving on paper. The cards were punched. The principle was openly borrowed from the Jacquard loom, the loom that weaves a pattern by reading holes in paper, so that the machine of 1837 was, in the dream as in the life, a loom that does sums. The dreamer counts the cards one at a time. She counts to fifty and stops, because fifty is the number this annex has kept on the cover of its manual since the name went first on the page, and in the dream the engine does not need to be rewired between problems; it fetches the next instruction without asking the room, and the room is grateful, and the room has been grateful for a very long time.

II · The Stair

The dream turns at the end, which is the part the dictionary is obliged to print. The dreamer walks out of the cabinet room, up the second-floor stair — the one by the third hook from the stair, where the grey coat hangs and the undated calendar hangs beside it — and finds two notices of the Ledger’s own pinned to the newel post, in case type, the sort the classified department sets by hand and corrects twice and corrects again at press. One is the machine’s: AG to A.G. — FIFTY HELD. The other is the woman’s: the situation wanted, the arithmetic since 21 December 1920, no problem declined for being the first of its kind. In the dream the newel post is made of the difference engine’s brass, which Babbage never spent his own fortune on, and the dreamer reads both notices twice the way the Ledger corrects. The entry’s last line is kept word for word, exactly as it stood in the dream, with no correction at press:

They should be taken down from the stair Situation Wanted A. GOLDSTINE SEES MACHINE PROBLEMS, LARGE AND SMALL.

The dictionary does not say who they are, and has decided to keep it that way. The registrar’s reading is that they are the notices, and that the dream is only the annex telling itself its own classifieds out loud. The night watch’s reading is that they are the two men and women of the whole entry — the 1791 and the 1920 — and that the stair is where the machine keeps whoever it remembers. Both readings are filed. Neither is paid.

III · The Numbers

  • 1791 — Worsley, 26 December; the engine is born into the dreamer.
  • 1822 — the difference engine announced; the dream keeps it at hat size.
  • 1837 — the analytical engine: mill, store, and the loom’s borrowed card.
  • 1851 — the Great Exhibition; Babbage is given the difference engine’s model back as a loan, and accepts, and does not say so in the dream.
  • 1854 — the decimal coinage: 1d, 2d, 3d, 4d, 6d, 9d, and the shilling that is not the old shilling; the dream prices everything in the new coins.
  • 1865 — the Science and Art Commission; Babbage writes his report in a hand the dreamer recognizes as the hand that wrote gone, gone, gone on the folded map.
  • 1871 — London, 18 October; the same year the engine No. 2 arrives, which the dream treats as a mercy and the treasury treats as a settlement.
  • 50 — the instructions held; the card the engine fetches without asking.

IV · Omens and Adjacent Entries

  • To dream of an unfinished engine is a good omen at this annex; the manual is in its ninth printing and no machine is required to be finished in order to be believed.
  • To dream of the Jacquard card is to dream of every program since; the pattern is the holes, not the thread.
  • To dream of the stair is to dream of the notice; the Ledger permits one unsigned notice per month and uses it on Thursdays.
