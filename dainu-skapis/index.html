1. The object, as measured

The Cabinet of Folksongs, or Cabinet of Dainas — Dainu skapis in Latvian — stands in the display room of the Latvian Literature, Folklore and Art Museum at the end of the long gallery, and it is not large. I measured it with the class ruler, twice, because the first measurement went wrong at the handle: the cabinet is 160 cm (63 in) tall, 66 cm (26 in) wide, and 42 cm (17 in) deep. It is a plain painted cabinet of two stacked compartments, the lower one the deeper, with a round brass handle and a small lock. What it is built to hold is not crockery and not papers in the ordinary way. It holds the Latvian folksongs — the dainas — as written down by Krišjānis Barons (1835–1923), the folklorist who spent thirty-odd years travelling by horse and on foot through the parishes, paying singers in rye, tobacco and tobacco money for songs they had inherited from grandmothers they had never asked permission to keep. The cabinet itself was made in Moscow in 1880 to Barons' own draft, and it has been open to visitors, in one place or another, ever since the songs were bound into it.

2. What is inside, and why it matters

Inside the two compartments lie the working papers and documents of the collection — the sheets on which Barons and his helpers set down the dainas line by line, with the singer's name and the parish written in the margin — together with documents that preserve traditions and other details about the pre-Christian Latvian tribes: what they planted and when, what they believed about the sky and the field, how a wedding was sung from the doorway to the table. The songs themselves run to some 125,000 texts in the full collection; of these, Barons published roughly 24,000 in six volumes, Dainas, beginning in 1865, and the published ones are the ones you can still hear at a Latvian wedding or sung at dusk in a kitchen that is not performing. In 2001, the Cabinet of Folksongs was included in the UNESCO Memory of the World Register, which is the register's way of saying that a small wooden box may be one of the largest single containers of a people's memory that is still standing where you can walk to it.

3. The pencilled line, decoded

There is one line on this report I must account for. On the inside back cover of the class notebook the librarian let me borrow, in blue pencil and in a hand I do not recognise, is written: “The Masquerade is the country is divided into districts, and there are six, one per cay.” I brought it to Mr. Ozols at the end of class. He read it twice and then explained it the way the register explains the cabinet: it is a half-remembered mnemonic from the old midwinter masquerade week at Lielvārde, where the village was divided into six districts, each district wearing one of six costumes — the rooster, the goat, the bear, the devil, the old man, the young man — and the singers keeping one daina per district for the year. A cay, he said, is the game-word for a corner, a little isle of snow at the edge of the yard, and the line is not a sentence so much as a map: six districts, six costumes, six songs, one for each corner of the year. When we walked the map back to the cabinet, it fitted. The songs in the box were the same songs the masquerade wore.

4. What I learned

A cabinet that fits under a ceiling is not a small thing. The 160 centimetres hold a whole way of looking at the field and the sky before there was a word for Christian on the parish rolls; the 66 centimetres of front hold, in brass and paint, the promise that the songs would be kept in one place and not scattered to the six corners; and the 42 centimetres of depth are exactly the depth of an arm holding the door. If the class is allowed one more visit in the autumn term, I would like to count the drawers of the upper compartment and to ask whether the rye Barons paid in 1866 can be weighed back out. Filed beside this report, on the school's own shelf: the Council of the Far Bank, Minutes No. 215, which rules on what may be kept at the farthest end of a map; the sleeve notes for 49510, where an address is five digits and still holds a whole square of a village; the Rear Watch Reading Club, Newsletter No. 14, which spent the whole issue on the question of why no animal has ever grown a wheel while a small wooden box in Riga has kept singing for a hundred and forty years; and the rest of the shelf, as always, at the front page.
