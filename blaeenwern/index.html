Tufton Parish Lost & Found Office
Blaenwern Farm Lane, Tufton, Pembrokeshire

Notice No. 47 · opened in 1915 · still open

FOUND

Blaenwern

One hymn tune, four lines, metre 8.7.8.7.D

Description of item

One (1) four-line hymn tune, in the key of F major, occasionally found carried in G major, and on one documented occasion, 11 February 1913, heard at a harvest supper rehearsal in A♭ major, at which the organist apologised to nobody. The tune runs in the metre 8.7.8.7.D — an eight-syllable line, a seven-syllable line, the eight again, the seven again, and a doubled closing line — the shape of a person walking down a lane and pausing twice to look back at a house. It is not large. It does not try to be. It is a plain little air, with one long note at the end of the third line where it seems, quite properly, to be waiting for the congregation to catch up with it.

Circumstances

The tune was found in the thick of the Welsh revival of 1904–1905, when the chapels of Pembrokeshire were running on fumes and new melodies were being composed the way in that country rain was being collected — out of the air, into whatever vessel was handy. The composer is known: William Penfro Rowlands (1860–1937), of Porth. The item may be identified by its origin, which is as follows. Rowlands, it is recorded, sent his son from Porth to stay at Blaenwern Farm, near Tufton, with friends of the family, to convalesce, on the grounds that the fresh air of the farm would assist his recovery. The boy recovered. The fresh air held. And the tune, composed by the father in those years and that distance, took the farm's name in honour of those who had taken the boy in: Blaenwern, a Welsh name, a Welsh air, a Welsh kindness, all filed under one heading.

Its first registration in any public document is in Henry H. Jones' Cân a Moliant, 1915, where it appears neatly ruled in, as if it had been waiting at the desk. Since that entry it has travelled farther than any convalescent ever could. In the United Kingdom, Blaenwern has come to be used as the prevalent setting for the hymn “O God, Our Help in Ages Past”, so that a congregation may sing the oldest lines in its book to a melody that was born a few years before the congregation's grandest piano, in a valley whose name means, roughly, the warm or the upper. It is hard to overstate how unusual a character that makes. A farm, an organ, a boy who got better, and the first hymn the English-speaking church reaches for — all one item, all still in our possession of record. The movement that filled those chapels with singers is itself the subject of a neighbouring chapter of the circuit's field manual, Methodism — Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4, which has filed one of our tune's wet months verbatim, ledger entry and all. The parish office has also cross-filed the tune against the season book's entry on the hypoglossal nerve — cranial nerve XII, the sole motor current that moves every muscle of the tongue, and the weather of all sung and spoken long notes, kept as Weather Almanac Entry 1998/063, 2 March 1998 — on the grounds that a long note is only as long as the tongue that makes it.

Claim

Any person claiming ownership should apply to the Parish Vestry, Tufton, and be prepared to (a) name the farm, (b) name the boy, (c) sing the third line at the correct length, and (d) account for the long note. Claims from Porth are given the first hearing. The office will pay, on successful claim, one tin of Rowlands marmalade, or one copy of the tune transposed into G major, holder's choice.

Lost items not claimed within a generation are refiled. This item, we have found, does not stay claimed. It reappears every Good Friday in a church that has no record of ever filing the notice. On 29 April 2011, when the vestry sang it twice over the boathouse television at the wedding of the Prince of Wales, the long note held both times; the day is kept, attendance and weather and all, on the parish board's own thread, The Tufton Wire — Thread No. 312, The Wedding of the Prince of Wales.

The office also keeps a cross-file against one other long note in the country: the day school at Beechwood on the Hudson, founded at the Vanderlip estate in 1913 and still keeping its 1917 theater, whose branch-line timetable, Service SCB-1, runs its evening movement at the hour of the bell and so keeps a long note of its own between Rhinecliff and the gate. The office also notes one unformal claimant: the keeper of the boathouse bench tunes the bellows of a wall clock to the third line of this tune, filing the gongs while the long note holds at its full length, and keeps the build in his own workshop log, filed against this notice as a matter of course: The Miller Clock — Workshop Log No. 1844, Tufton Boathouse.

The office also records, for the claimant’s files, one folded brochure found on the vestry bench — The Reddick Circuit — A One-Day Tour, Baltimore to Burbank — a single-day tour of an actor’s city that keeps its timetable at a confidence the office has not measured and will not measure, and which any claimant who sings the third line at the correct length may borrow from the desk, one day at a time, like the tune itself. One further cross-file is kept against this notice from Portland: Mabel’s Baptism Loaves — A Family Recipe, Card No. 12, a recipe whose long preamble settles the length of the river dunk at the foot of the Flanders Street Bridge, and on that length of note the office is in full agreement with the deacon.
