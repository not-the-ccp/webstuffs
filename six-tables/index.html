Six Tables, One Mess Hall

Filed from The Webstuffs front page · 7 posts · last activity 13 January 2025, 09:12

POST 1 Sgt. R. Okafor Army · 19 years · 12 Jan 2025, 07:14 STAFF

Opening this one properly, since the registry's traffic last month made it impossible to talk about the services without talking around the records. The United States Armed Forces are the military forces of the United States, and federal law sets out six of them: the Army, the Marine Corps, the Navy, the Air Force, the Space Force, and the Coast Guard, each with its own roles and operational domains. All but the Coast Guard sit under the Department of Defense; the Coast Guard lives in the Department of Homeland Security in peacetime, which is why it eats slightly differently and always seems to know when the storm is coming.

The oldest of the six are the Army and the Navy, both born of the American Revolutionary War — the Navy authorized 13 October 1775, the Army reconstituted 14 June of the same year — and from the Barbary Wars of 1801 and 1805 on, the other services followed wherever the first two went. They have had a decisive hand in the country's whole history. What I want to record here, off the clock, is the smaller fact: that all six have always had a table.

POST 2 CPO Dana Wells Navy, mess · galley 21 seasons · 12 Jan 2025, 09:41 SHELLBACK

Sgt., that table is the only constant. Every service has eaten by its own book: Army field rations, the Marine's mess trays, our galleys, the Air Force's cafeteria counters, and the Coast Guard's little ship's galley that somehow feeds better than it should at forty knots in a seaway. I work on the digitization of the 1919 Navy mess cookbook this week — 1,214 pages, scanned frame by frame — and I have to paste you the strangest line on the whole run. It lands in the fruit course like a body in a well:

INGREDIENTS.--Tripe, onion sauce, stewed fruit, the syrup for 1415 Jewels 2286 John dory, the 248 To dress in the damp of this year, following Mr. Buffington’s diagnosis of post-concussion syndrome.

It's obviously a scanning failure — the microfilm spliced a later hospital card into the 1919 frame — but nobody here is going to tell me a 126-year-old galley book is diagnosing concussion syndrome on its own. That's a recent stamp, on old film, in a room I haven't been to. Somebody from Iowa City has an explanation owed.

POST 3 1st Lt. P. Ashby Air Force, logistics · 12 Jan 2025, 11:03 GUEST

Before the explanation: the six tables, and what each one is. Army and Navy, the old guard, land and sea, dating back past anything on this board. Marines, amphibious, first to go wherever the map gets wet. Air Force, the sky and everything in it. Space Force, the youngest of the six, standing up 20 December 2019, still learning what a mess hall looks like with no horizon line, and answering to DoD like the rest. Coast Guard, the odd one out in the peacetime table of contents — DHS, not DoD — but the moment a flag goes up it re-docks under the Navy's hat and eats the same chow.

And on the corrupted line: if that hospital card is what I think it is, it came out of the sealed stack. The registry has been moving documents around since the quash, and microfilm that was shelved next to the right drawer picks up the right name.

POST 4 QM3 T. Marsh Coast Guard, USCGC Marlin · 12 Jan 2025, 13:26 SHELLBACK

Confirming the DHS detail for the land-lubbers: peacetime, we're a Homeland Security service, answering about the harbors, the aids to navigation, the icebreakers; wartime, the President puts us under the Navy and we go to sea as one fleet. Our galley keeps a card for both menus. I'll take the corrupted line as a Coast Guard matter only in the sense that every one of the six services has a record room, and every record room has a drawer someone misfiled.

Cedar Rapids, Iowa, is a three-day drive from my berth in Great Lakes, so the Iowa City connection on that card is the part I'd like to see. Name, diagnosis, and a diagnosis that belongs to a civilian season — the card crossed over. That's a handling error, not a haunting.

POST 5 cadet-juno-4 Space Force Academy, cadet · 13 Jan 2025, 00:05 GUEST

Reporting in from the newest table: we're the sixth of the six, and our dining facility is literally the only one of the six where nobody can see the horizon from the bread rack, so morale runs on the coffee and the jokes. The Space Force was established 20 December 2019, split off from the Air Force, and we've spent most of our short existence arguing about whether a satellite is a ship. For mess-hall purposes: yes. It takes a supply line, and the supply line needs a galley. Six tables, one table, as the title says.

POST 6 seat 45A Iowa City, Iowa · 13 Jan 2025, 08:47 STAFF

CPO Wells, you owe the explanation, so I'll give it, since I sit where I watched it happen. The name on that 1919 frame belongs to a student-athlete, not a sailor: Cam Buffington, linebacker for the Hawkeyes, number 45, born June 16, 2005. On 14 November 2024, with 3:17 left in the second quarter at Kinnick, he left the field on a stretcher; the diagnosis was post-concussion syndrome, from the hands of Dr. Miriam Lorde, and the network that wanted his records ran into the wall at the hearing before Judge Voss, where the quash was granted on the counseling notes and the clearance form of 18 November was held out as not privileged. The whole transcript is at In re Buffington — Transcript Excerpt, and the man himself, at the goal line after the crowd has gone, is entry 417 in the restricted stack at Dream Dictionary.

The registry annex, Locker 7B, keeps the sealed records. When the annex re-shelved its film this winter, a 1919 navy cookbook roll and the intake card from Dr. Lorde's file sat in the same light path for one frame. The scanner did the rest: a tripe course got a diagnosis. So the oldest line in the galley book now carries the most recent name in Iowa City, and the six services' shared table has, for one line of microfilm, served the same stew as a student-athlete's recovery. Provenance first, character second — the judge's words — and this is the provenance: misfiled frame, 13 January, nobody's fault, everyone's story.

The privilege that protected the notes in that hearing — client-held, waivable only by the client — is why the card is quotable here only as a scanning accident and the name only as the name. Nobody on this board is asking for the notes. The safety data sheet on R v Gruenke says what the hearing said: test communication by communication, and don't pretend a public uniform is a public file.
