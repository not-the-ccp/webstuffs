Plate XII · The Household Board card game, second printing

The Frigate

A game of one deck, one long watch, and a ship that must arrive before the frost lifts.

1. What a frigate is, in this game

In the seventeenth century and into the early eighteenth, the word frigate was handed out loosely, the way people still hand out job titles at the Board: any full-rigged ship built for speed and manoeuvrability and intended for scouting, escort, or patrol. A frigate in those years was less a hull than a promise — that the ship would be quick, and that the promise would be kept. By the second quarter of the eighteenth century the true frigate had been developed in France and subsequently copied by every other navy in the habit of copying, and the definition tightened the way a good master tightens a schedule. The true frigate is characterised by a long, sleek design, a single gun deck of 28 or 36 cannons, and an unarmed deck below used for berthing the crew. The galley still keeps its aircraft recipe card with the berth tokens down there — Aircraft — the family recipe — though nothing may be drawn from it before the state-dinner round. In the mid-nineteenth century the definition of frigate was expanded again, and has been expanding ever since — the game follows the long definition, because a house that big needs every ship it can count on.

The player's own example is HMS Sutherland, a Type 42, which is what you are playing. The other ships on the board are remembered vessels of the long family: a 1748 French frégate of 32 guns out of Toulon, a 28-gun British ship that never made the register, and a mid-Victorian iron-hulled frigate that the navy kept classing out of habit, as the household keeps the almanac out of habit.

2. Components

  • One duty almanac (an old-style pocket almanac — moon phases, tides, the weather reckoned the long way — of the same family as the specimen found in the Pittock Block; the Board files it under "never wrong")
  • One banquet book, ruled for fourteen covers and four courses
  • Forty-eight card counters: 28 for the lower battery, 20 for the upper, plus 36 gun cards and 28 gun cards (a frigate draws from the 36-gun pile only if she was built at Toulon in 1748)
  • One berth token per player, kept on the unarmed deck below, where no card may ever be drawn from
  • One bell, for the watch changes; it rings at twelve and the watch changes at twelve
  • One set of state-dinner tickets: fourteen covers, four courses, and a note that the frost is on the glass until 2 a.m.

3. Setup

Deal five cards to each player face down. That is the muster on the unarmed deck; no one plays a card from it except at the state-dinner round, and even then only the Master may draw. The remaining cards form the approaches, spread face up in a row — the northern approaches, which every player who has spent long stretches in the North Atlantic knows must be checked three times a day. The duty almanac goes in the drawer, where it belongs. The banquet book goes open on the table, where it must be seen. The Master of the Household is the player chosen by bell: whoever strikes the bell first and says the watch changes at twelve unaided.

4. The watch (turn order)

On your turn you may do exactly one of the following, in the order a good officer would do them:

  1. Sound the depth. Flip the leftmost card of the approaches and keep it. If it is a gun card, it joins your gun deck, and your deck is a single gun deck — no more, no less, or the ship ceases to be a frigate and the other players may laugh, though no one who was not supposed to hear laughs.
  2. Cut the lines. Play a card from your hand onto the approaches, face up, anywhere except the leftmost position, which belongs to the tide.
  3. Copy the almanac. Before any state-dinner round, the junior clerk — the player who drew first at setup — copies that night's entry from the almanac into the banquet book, and the line in the banquet book, word for word, must read: “The almanac copies it again at the Cockpit.” If the line is wrong, the dinner is delayed a full turn.
  4. Give the word. On the evening turn, the Master walks the line of doors, room by room, from the grand entrance to the servants' stair, and gives the word. The watch, word for word, as it was written into the log the last week of 2005, reads: Tom Blackburn — the man gave the word; don’t be a bugbear. A player who cannot quote the word is treated as a bugbear for the rest of the watch: his berth token is moved to the gangway, and he may sound the depth but nothing else, until the bell rings at twelve.

5. The state-dinner round

When the approaches run out, the game enters the state-dinner round, held in the Cockpit: all dark wood and brass, and the sense that the room has been hosting dinners longer than the country has been hosting anyone. Fourteen covers are laid, four courses are served, and the frost is on the glass the whole time, lifting only when the almanac says 2 a.m. Each player, in turn order, must name a course from the dinner of November 2002 — moon waning, wind north-west, frost to 2 a.m. — and lay the matching card from the muster on the table. A player who names a course that was not served that night, or lays a card that does not match, forfeits one gun from his deck. The Master may check the almanac page against the banquet book personally; he does this more than the rulebook says he has to, and it counts as his whole turn.

6. Winning

The first player to land — that is, to play his last card onto the approaches and walk the ground floor, room by room, checking the locks with his own hands, in a coat two sizes too big — wins the game, but only if the banquet book is in order, the almanac is in the drawer, and every door in the room says back. A frigate is not a prize for being first; a frigate is a prize for being the ship that arrived. The winner signs the log. The almanac, by the record, was never wrong, and neither, by the record, was the dinner.
