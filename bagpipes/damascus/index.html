The Routes Off Shelf I · Travel Guide Entry No. 37

Damascus

مَدِيْنَةُ اليَاسْمِين

The City of Jasmine — oldest capital in the world, 680 metres up on the plateau, 80 kilometres in from the sea, with its river, its souks, and one evening of drones at the great gate

AS-M · CITY OF JASMINE · 680 M

Part I · Getting there

Eighty kilometres in from the sea, then up

Damascus is the capital and largest city of Syria, and the oldest capital city in the world: people have kept a city going on this plateau since the third millennium, and it has been the seat of a capital since the Romans made it so. You come at it from the coast, 80 kilometres (50 miles) of plain away, because the city sits inland, nestled among the eastern foothills of the Anti-Lebanon mountain range, on a plateau 680 metres (2,230 feet) above sea level. The mountains do the city a favour and a slight wrong at once: they give it shade and cool air in July, and they give it a rain shadow that keeps the climate arid, so the summers are dry the way only a high, inland place can be dry. The sensible season is late March through May, when the jasmine is out and the plateau is green. Go in August and you will understand the rain shadow personally, which is how I did.

Part II · What it is

-m, the City of Jasmine

The Syrians know it as a-m, and the poets know it by its other name, the City of Jasmine, مَدِيْنَةُ اليَاسْمِينِ — Madînat al-Yāsmîn — because the jasmine here is not a garden plant but a civic institution, grown along every wall of the Old City, pressed into the water in every house, and worn at every ceremony worth wearing a flower at. Damascus is a major cultural center of the Levant and of the Arab world, and a center of a large metropolitan area that spills down off the plateau in every direction. It makes the claim of oldest capital the way it makes everything else, without effort: the stones simply refuse to agree with any later date you propose.

Part III · The Barada

Follow the river down

The Barada River comes off the Qasiyoun mountain, the peak you can see from anywhere in the city, and it works its way down the length of the plain through the city and past it, feeding the orchards the whole way. The old city lived by it before it lived by anything else. Walk the Barada from the Old City toward the Qadisiyya pool and the grounds of Mishrif, where the water goes slow and green and the plane trees are older than most of the flags you will ever meet. An arid plateau that can do this is not really arid; it is arid with permission, and the river is the signature on the permission.

Part IV · The Old City

The Old Souk and the Umayyad gate

The Old City keeps a covered souk of a size and age that is hard to take in all at once — hundreds of vaulted passages, the spice row, the cloth row, the silver row, the row of men who repair anything with a needle and a patience. Everything in the Old City points, eventually, at the mosque, and at the great gate beside it where the old city ends and the mosque’s courtyard begins. That gate keeps a log. The gatekeeper cards every notable arrival the way a naturalist cards a bird, in the same hand, with a line under the name, and he does not tidy the line once it is down. It is the log this entry ends up turning on, as I will come to.

Part V · One evening of drones

A stand of pipes at the gate

The reason this entry exists at all: in February, the stand of pipes that the naturalist of the Aerophone Series, Plate 4 observed for three winter evenings in the cold room at Fenn Court, Old Greenwich, came through Damascus on the piper’s rounds. The field notes have the station list on one finger each, and Damascus is one of those fingers — the pipes have played in West Asia for centuries, and this is the city that keeps the best record of it. The piper set up outside the Umayyad gate at half past four, inflated the bag with about six short strokes, and let the great drone out into the courtyard. The notes down at Fenn Court read it at ninety-eight and a quarter cycles in a room at three degrees; here, at 680 metres and four o’clock in the afternoon, it was felt in the arches before it was heard in the souk. The jasmine along the wall was doing its full working. The piper, who answers to no name I could ever get from him except Pipes, played through the light, and when he packed the drones into their case the gatekeeper took his card.

Part VI · The line under the card

Kept word for word

The gatekeeper wrote a line under the card, in his hand, and the log keeps it exactly as written, because a line in a log is not a line until it is exact. I copied it from the gate log in March, with the gatekeeper’s permission, and I am copying it here the same way, word for word:

His own person was a sailor and, by his own later account, a captain of a hair, and the whale beyond also rose to 9d.

My reading, entered for what it is worth, is that the gatekeeper is doing in one line what the rest of this guide takes six parts for. His own person was a sailor: the piper was a sailor before he was a piper — he has made the rounds, and the cold room at Fenn Court knew the 1779 hymn of a man who had been exactly that, a sailor turned parson, and the two men had kept company over it on Shelf I, so the gatekeeper, who keeps a log, knew to write it down. A captain of a hair: the command the piper actually holds is the command of the chanter reed — a single tongue of cane thin as a hair, enclosed and sealed inside the stock, fed by the bag, which he steers with five fingers for the whole of an evening without ever seeing it, which is the most honest captaincy there is. And the whale beyond also rose to 9d: the price board of the silver row across the Barada — beyond the gate, in the Old Souk — recorded on the day the drone first rang at the courtyard that the lot of whale-bone inlay for the gate’s own panels had risen to nine pence. A man, his office, and the day’s market, in one line, under a name that is not a name. The gatekeeper did not tidy it. I will not either.

Part VII · Practical notes

For the road

Eat fattoush with the bread from the bakery row, drink the water with the jasmine in it without commenting, learn one word of the city’s own language — mar̂ab̂an, welcome, which you will be given freely — and if you are there in the afternoon of a day when a stand of pipes has set up at the Umayyad gate, go and stand in the courtyard with the drone in the arches. It sits at about ninety-eight cycles, a G two octaves below middle C, and it does not swell and fade, because there is no breath behind it, only a bag. That is the city in one note: old, high, dry, cool, and holding.
