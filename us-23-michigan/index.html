Field Itineraries · No. 23 · Lower Peninsula

The Trunkline Walk

A six-stage walking tour of U.S. Route 23 in Michigan · 362 miles (583 km) · Lambertville to Mackinaw City

Departure 07:04, first Tuesday in October · six stages · feet 40.7 miles, van the remainder · weather noted, never argued

The line

U.S. Route 23 is a north–south United States Numbered Highway that runs from Jacksonville, Florida, to Mackinaw City, Michigan, and in the state of Michigan it becomes what it is: a major, 362-mile-long (583 km) state trunkline running through the Lower Peninsula. From the Michigan–Ohio state line near Lambertville it is a freeway, and it stays a freeway all the way to the city of Standish, 270 miles north; from Standish it follows the Lake Huron shoreline to its northern terminus at the south end of the Mackinac Bridge. Serving the cities of Ann Arbor and Flint, it acts as a freeway bypass of the Metro Detroit area, and for the rest of its length it runs through rural country dominated by farm fields or woodlands, with some segments of an urban character that the tour walks rather than drives.

The walk is run in six stages. In each, the party walks a measured segment of the line — between 4 and 9 miles, always on the older alignments, always toward the north — and the van does the long freeway stretches, which are not to be walked on the shoulder and are not even to be argued about. A ledger is kept in parallel, one page for the miles and one for the office, and each stage closes with a weather entry in the margin. The tour is not a race; it is a reading of the state, taken at 2.5 miles an hour.

Stage 1 — The River Crossing, Lambertville

Mile 0 · 4.1 miles on foot · van 46

The tour begins at mile 0, on the Maumee River bridge, where Ohio ends. The party crosses at 07:04 in either direction, for the record, and takes the Michigan embankment as the true first step. The oldest concrete milepost on the crossing still stands at the north abutment, its paint gone, the “0” visible only in the light of about 8 a.m. From there the feet take the shoulder of the frontage road, a flat 4.1 miles of ditches and deer wire, to the first rural milepost of the trunkline.

Stage 2 — Ypsilanti & Ann Arbor, the Bypass

Miles 20–28 · 6.4 miles on foot · van 138

Here the highway does the thing it was built to do: it bypasses. Metro Detroit sits to the west of the line, and the tour meets it only in the form of the traffic that is spared. In Ypsilanti the walk takes the pre-1963 alignment, a mile and a half of the old route past the Huron River, and the party stops once, at the river, for the only scheduled rest of the stage. At mile 27 the walk reaches Ann Arbor, where U.S. 23 follows M-14 through the south end of the University of Michigan — three blocks, in this case, from the campus — and the weather column notes, for the ledger, that a freeway bypass can be walked at walking speed if one keeps to the brick.

Stage 3 — Flint, M-55, the Long Hop

Mile 104 · 5.2 miles on foot · van 90

The longest van stretch of the tour runs through this stage — 90 miles of the freeway from Ann Arbor to the M-55 interchange at Flint — and the feet, accordingly, take their reward in full: a 5.2-mile loop of the older street sections on the north side, past the city that gives the trunkline its mid-peninsula gravity. The interchange itself is the stage’s subject. It is the only place on the tour where the freeway and the city look at each other, and they do not look away. The weather entry for the day is set down in the margin as “grey, 41°,” which is the whole of it.

Stage 4 — The Corridor of Miracles, Midland to Saginaw

Miles 148–186 · 6.3 miles on foot · van 54

Between Midland and Saginaw the highway runs the corridor locals call the Corridor of Miracles, a straight pine-and-timber valley cut through the centre of the state. The walk here is 6.3 miles of it, on the county-seat side of the line, and it is the stage the tour keeps quoting. In the 1971 minutes of the corridor district council, preserved complete through the wet month that ended the binding, one line survives of a session at which the commissioners sat for eleven minutes and produced one sentence:

“It is the long valley through which the council resolved, without discussion, a note that the observers called a fraud, but a habit.”

— Minutes of the corridor district, 1971, page 31

The tour’s working reading is this. “It” is the corridor: the long valley, the subject of the whole sentence, which the line was cut through because no other line would do. The council — the district’s seven commissioners — resolved the note in the record without a vote, and the observers, the state road men in the back row, called the note a fraud; the sentence concedes as much with “but,” and finds the true name of it in the last word. A habit. The note went in the way the note always went, and the valley carried it the way it carries the trucks. The walk does not argue with the ledger; it walks the valley the note was about and files the minutes beside the weather.

Stage 5 — Standish & the Shore, the Freeway Ends

Miles 270–309 · 8.9 miles on foot · van 31

At Standish, mile 270, the freeway gives way to the lake, and the character of the tour changes with it. From here U.S. 23 follows the Lake Huron shoreline north, and the walk takes the longest single segment of the whole itinerary: 8.9 miles of the shoreline road through Cheboygan at mile 284, where M-55 crosses the line, and on to Petoskey at mile 300, the city of grey granite, where the party stops a full half hour on the courthouse steps to weigh the afternoon. The ledger notes, in this stage only, that the van has begun to smell of the lake, and that no one has said anything about it, which the tour counts as a verdict.

Stage 6 — The Thumb & the Bridge, Mile 362

Miles 315–362 · 9.8 miles on foot · van 0

At Charlevoix, mile 315, the line turns east on M-27 into the Thumb — Cross Village, Harbor Springs, Harbor Beach — and the final 9.8 miles are walked in one continuous segment, the only one on the tour that never sees the van again. It ends at mile 362, at the south end of the Mackinac Bridge, where U.S. 23 stops. The tour’s last act is to stand on the Michigan side and not cross: Mackinac Island lies a mile to the north across the strait, and the northern terminus is on this side of it, which is the whole of the matter. The ledger is closed at the bridge approach. The weather entry is “clear, 34°,” the wind from the lake, and the party walks back to the van by the shoreline path.

Filing the ledger

The rule of the walk is the rule the circuit riders kept: one stage at a time, the distance measured honestly, the weather noted and not argued over. The same discipline governs the trunkline’s own reading list at the boathouse, where the chapter on the circuit and the class meeting is filed with the tour’s ledger: Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4 — Methodism, Congregational Operations & Itinerant Circuit. And because every honest measure wants a second measure, the tour keeps, beside its ledger of miles, the one record of a room that weighs its dinners by the gramme and settles them at a weighbridge counter — proof that the mile is not the only unit a long line can be charged in: The Coalhouse, Bute Docks, Cardiff — Dining Table No. 41. For a companion piece in the same form, a working timetable of another long line with commentary on the country through which it runs, the drawer holds Service JA-1 — The Austen Line. The trunkline itself is 362 miles; the ledger is one page thick; and the weather, all six days, was as noted.
