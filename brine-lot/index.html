Description of the Lot

The brine in this jar is not, properly, a condiment. It is a colony. In the salt and sugar it holds a living population of lactic acid bacteria, the sourness of the onions being their industry, and each cell of the population is a small laboratory of the kind the science now called molecular biology describes at length: a branch of biology that sets out to understand the molecular structures and the chemical processes that are the basis of biological activity within and between cells, and that is centred largely on the study of nucleic acids — DNA and RNA — and of proteins. The jar has been running this demonstration for six decades, which is longer than the science, and the catalogue does not consider this an insult to the science.

Inside each bacterium there is a single circular chromosome of DNA, a double-helical sentence in four bases, A, T, C, G, that the house describes as the code. The code is read in three moves, and the canteen's copy of the ledger transcribes all three. First, replication: when a cell divides at the wharf — quietly, overnight, at 8:10 past nothing — the double helix unwinds and each strand templates its own twin, so that the two daughters each leave with the whole instruction, which is how the 1962 brine is still the 1962 brine. Second, transcription: a copy of the message, in the cousin molecule RNA, is drawn off and carried out of the chromosome, the way the family copy of the ledger was carried out of Aden, the original remaining where the Court put it. Third, translation, at the ribosome, where the RNA message is read codon by codon and built up into a protein — the enzyme that turns the onion's sweetness to the onion's sourness, so that the taste of this jar is, literally, protein synthesis, performed for the family's benefit, in the dark, between the flour and the silence.

The culture is not, however, a closed one. The bacteria pass small loops of extra DNA — plasmids — from cell to cell, and what one cell learns, the whole jar learns, which is a sentence the auction room finds true of gossip at the ferry. And when the population thickens, the cells count their neighbours by sending and receiving small signalling molecules, and only above a certain number do they act together, a practice the science calls quorum sensing and the family calls a meeting, the brine being a democracy of one opinion. The Ferry Post's food correspondent has dined on that democracy and filed the score — The Allele, six seats and one locus, at the back room of the canteen — which the canteen has pinned above the ledger and does not read aloud, which the canteen considers the correct handling of a review, and which the catalogue prints here only because a lot is not described completely until its strangest sentence is described, and a review at 8:10, on the night of the sale, is about as strange as this house gets. Molecular biology is a multi-disciplinary field, and so is the description of this lot: it leans on genetics for the inheritance of the sourness, on biochemistry for the enzymes, on physics for the light that reaches the jar only at Stop 1 of the morning, and, since the naturalist on secondment came through, on mathematics and on the newer branch, computer science, in which a small program keeps the base count of the 1962 sample — 1,342 bases and one repeat, reconciled to the ledger at the chart office's second floor, in the same drawer where the tide chart waits.

One object of comparison is offered, and it is on the same sale: the mustard oil of the Vineyard Lane, Stop 3 of the companion's itinerary, the seed that once lit the valley's lamps. The family's standing view, printed here for bidders, is that the lamp and the brine are the same story told at two scales, and that one of them won the 1964 case.

Provenance

Dictated by the guest of the canteen, in the harbour canteen ledger, 8:10 p.m., 12 October 1962; jars filled by 8:12, twelve in number, which the Court never needed and the canteen's guide will tell you anyway. Held in the pantry of Nori House, on the shelf between the flour and the silence, every year since. The porch beside the Secchia, its lamp and its conditions, are the subject of Case No. 41 of 1964, which is cited in the addenda and is not cited often enough.

Condition Report

Jar sound, no crazing; label legible in the cook's hand, the dots of 12·10·62 intact. Brine at 82% of original density. Culture viable: a sample drawn on a February morning and read at the chart office shows all four bases present in the running count — the leaflet's rule, shared with the naturalist who counts his numbers in pencil on the Kantö plain, being that the code is counted, not guessed, and that a number that comes out wrong is scratched out and counted again. Onions whole. Nasturtiums at the bottom of the jar, where they have always been, performing no function anyone has asked them to perform, which the report records as a quality.

A Line in the Margin, Transcribed

On the inside of the pantry door, in a hand no one has identified — the same, the family suspects, as the brown card at Stop 4 of the morning walk — is a line without a verb the family can agree on. It reads, exactly:

“Leave the porch is that the code without being asked.”

The leaflet prints it as it stands, because a lot is not described completely until its strangest sentence is described. The reading adopted at the last sale and not since opposed fuses two house rules in one hand: leave the porch to the lamp at dusk, the night belonging to the white wings as the Court ordered, and the code — the brine's four-base code — being the kind that shows itself without being asked, the way the radish and the turnip show their flower code in the market row to anyone willing to count. The line is therefore treated not as an error of grammar but as the family's shortest definition of the lot: the porch keeps the night, the jar keeps the sequence, and neither of them will repeat it for you, but both will let you count it.

Addenda

No lot in this sale may be taken across the porch at night; the condition of 1964 binds the brine as it binds the lamp, and the night belongs to the moths, as the White-Wing Cakes and their story end. The jar is offered unsold by design, the reserve being the second jar, which the family does not have and does not intend to have, the sourness being a one-of-one and the code, once counted, complete. Bids are received, counted, and declined. The lot will be re-estimated at Sale No. CXLIII, and the estimate will be —, as it has been at every sale since the thirteenth, which is the entire history of the reserve. The canteen's own copy of the year's minutes is kept in the same careful hand: the Minutes of a Special Session, Gaza City Municipal Council, 23 March 2004, in which the night of 22 March is entered word for word, the minute book's rule — shared with this leaflet — being that a thing is not described completely until its strangest sentence is described, and that when a note is good it is not improved.

Companions to this leaflet, for the reader who came by river and by chart: the morning half of the family's year, The Mustard Walk, eight stops beside the Secchia, which carries the jar at Stop 1 and eats the cake at Stop 7; and the companion's own count of the numbers that move, kept on the Kantö plain, Ayu-ro Mix — Field Notes, Vol. XII, whose rule for 700,000 copies is the rule this leaflet prints for 1,342 bases; and from another shelf altogether, the court record that took down a notebook word for word — the route, the raking, the desk, the butter — Cause No. TA-3314/19, In re the Klausner Papers.
