ON AIR

MARSHTONE FM · MIDDLE BAND · 139.4 · MARSH ROAD STUDIO, CELLAR, 14 MARSH ROAD · 16 MARCH 1991, 16:00–16:25

The Cellar Revival

A radio play in three scenes, excerpted as transmitted. Recorded to tape (the tape is not the one marked 777; the tape marked 777 is under lock in the Trust’s cellar and stays that way).

THE PRODUCER (P. Marlowe, in his storm weather) · DOT (the cook; the grill is hers since before the family) · EUSTACE (the bar; anise on the line) · MRS. HALSTROM (a caller, from the record shop on Halstrom) · THE ANNOUNCER

SCENE 1 — THE CELLAR, UNDER 14 MARSH ROAD

SFX: one stair, climbed in the wrong direction. A tape transport latched on. The particular hum of the green lamp — the one made for a lighthouse — which the microphone can always hear. Distant, upstairs, plates arriving at the speed of a ferry.

THE ANNOUNCER: From the cellar under fourteen Marsh Road, where the menu on the rusty nail upstairs has just been through its third cycle of Grim, Stroke and Disease — we have a new recording, made this afternoon in the hour the kitchen allowed. A short play, and no more. It is called The Cellar Revival, and it is about the music that has no writer’s name on it. We begin with the producer, who will not begin at all until the meters are quiet.

SFX: a fiddle, tuning, from somewhere under the table. The Producer’s chair moves, once, and is still.

THE PRODUCER: Hold the line. Folk music. That is the one we are recording, and that is the one that will not hold a take. Let me put it plainly for the receiver, because the receiver is the only honest witness on this band. Folk music is a music genre that includes traditional folk music and the contemporary genre that evolved from the former during the twentieth-century folk revival. Some of it, we are told, may be called world music. Traditional folk music has been defined in several ways, and I have kept them in a ledger, because I can keep the facts in order: music transmitted orally. Music with unknown composers. Music played on traditional instruments. Music about a people’s cultural or national identity. Music that changes between generations — the folk process, the one where the song eats its own memory a little each time it is passed mouth to ear. Music associated with a people’s folklore. And music performed by custom over a long period of time. Seven definitions, seven species, and every one of them sits in this cellar this afternoon.

DOT: (from the grill) You’ve said “music” nine times, producer.

THE PRODUCER: The term originated in the nineteenth century, Dot. It has been set in type, set in a ledger, and set against commercial music and classical music alike, which is the whole of its company: not the shops, not the concert hall. Folk is what is left when the two of them clear the room. It has been around far longer than the word for it, and that is the part that will not hold a take. A word of a hundred and fifty years trying to describe an old woman at a fire.

DOT: Then we record the old woman at the fire and stop talking about her.

THE PRODUCER: That is, in fact, the entire method. Roll.

SCENE 2 — THE COOK’s CORNER, SAME CELLAR, LATER

SFX: the tape rolls on through the interval, because the tape does not know it is an interval. A phone, in the bar upstairs, ringing twice and being ignored, then rung for by Eustace’s foot under the counter. A biscuit tin, opened in the small way that means it has been opened many times.

EUSTACE: (to the microphone, unannounced) There is a caller. She is at the record shop on Halstrom — the same shop, I should note, that sent the Trust its black parcel of one cloth-bound book and a tape last week, the matter of which is on the council’s record and off ours — and she says she has a card. She says it belongs to the program. I have poured her nothing, which with me means I approve.

MRS. HALSTROM: (through the phone, a voice worn smooth by years of being asked which side of a tape is up) You asked for the Savoy card. It is in the back of my grandmother’s book, under the eel. You read it. That is all of it. Read it the way it is written.

DOT: (coming to the microphone) It is a card for the biscuits, for the people of the country who send them up to the city, which is a story in itself, and it is the only thing on the card. I read it word for word, as she requires:

“INGREDIENTS.--About 18 Savoy biscuits, 3/4 pint of juice allow 2-1/2 lbs.”

The card, read by Dot into the microphone, 16 March 1991, 16:08. As the card is written. Nothing added, nothing corrected, and we do not know of what the 2-1/2 lbs. is the allowance, and the card does not say, and the card is not going to say.

THE PRODUCER: That is the best line of the afternoon.

DOT: It is a recipe. It has done what folk has always done: it has outlived everyone who could explain it, it has no composer’s name on it, it has changed at least twice between my grandmother’s book and her mother’s kitchen — the juice is not the same juice, ask anyone — and it still arrives, in a tin, from a caller on the phone. That is the folk process. The song is the card, and the card is the song, and if you want it in the ledger, write it in exactly as it came.

EUSTACE: (quietly) I am writing it in exactly as it came.

SCENE 3 — THE TAPE, SAME CELLAR, 16:19

SFX: the green lamp. A fiddle, now in tune, now a tune. The tune has no name. It is played twice, the second time with the whole of the bar behind it — Swamp Terrorists, the band of the same name as the kitchen upstairs, three chairs and a counter away, eating course six of their own menu and not knowing they are in a recording. This is the feeding behaviour, or so a naturalist has since logged it, notebook in hand, from under the table where the bench, second one, faces the water.

SFX: the tune ends. A long silence in which, clearly, everyone is deciding who it is that has just been played.

THE PRODUCER: Who is the composer?

DOT: No one.

THE PRODUCER: That is not an answer, Dot. “No one” is the definition.

DOT: Then it is the correct one. I first heard it on the ferry, my mother humming it to a fish box. My mother heard it from the fishmonger. The fishmonger, to my knowledge, heard it from the tide. There is no composer because there never was a moment when it was one person’s. That is the whole species, producer. Commercial music wants a name on the sleeve. Classical wants a date and a score. Folk wants both off, and it has been managing without them for longer than the word, and it will manage longer still, one mouth to one ear, changing a note here, losing a bar there, keeping, against everything, the part that the country needs.

SFX: tape, latched. A chair, moving. Footsteps, one stair up, into the anise, and the plate-cloth of the room above.

THE ANNOUNCER: The tune will be credited, on the sleeve, as Traditional; first known in this form to Dot, 14 Marsh Road, and the ferry before that. The producer has made no annotation to the credit, which, for a man who coordinates the whole of it including the flatbread, is a kind of signature. This has been The Cellar Revival, from the cellar under fourteen Marsh Road. The barman’s advice, and the house rule, and the only rule the receiver has had all afternoon: be back before the tide turns.

SFX: tape off. The hum of the lamp, outlasting it.

TRANSMISSION NOTES

Recorded and transmitted 16 March 1991, Marsh Road Studio (the cellar). The naturalist of the bench across the yard heard the whole of it, and has his own account of the producer at its feeding station in Field Notes, Notebook No. 4 — On the Record Producer, Marsh Road Cellar, March 1991; the room upstairs — eleven courses, three names, two forks and a standing table — stands on the record in Swamp Terrorists, Q Magazine dining pages, March 1991, and the Trust’s secretary kept the fortnight, day by day, in the Marsh Road diary, March 1991. The Savoy card is kept in the biscuit tin, uncorrected. The eel, as ever, is out of the province of the dish.

Marshtone FM, middle band 139.4, Marsh Road Studio. All tapes filed spine-in. The recipe is preserved word for word, and the 2-1/2 lbs. remain, on the record, an allowance of unknown provenance. The tide is not mentioned on the card. The tide is not required to be.
