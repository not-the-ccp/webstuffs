The Collegium

The conductor at the pass: a review of the forty-six-seat dining room under Sint-Niklaaskerk, directed by Christoph Siebert

By M. De Volder, Staff Critic · Dined Tuesday, 9 October, one table of two, 19:15 seating

★★★★☆ Four of five · Revisit: yes, in Advent

You will not find The Collegium. It is the last door on the wrong side of Sint-Niklaas Lane, past the bakery that has sold the same breads since before the war, and it has no signage, no lettering, no neon — only a brass plaque that reads collegium vocale in the old humanist hand, which is to say, in the only script the building has ever trusted. Forty-six seats, one long room, plastered walls, and a kitchen door that stands half-open at all times, because at this table the kitchen is a rehearsal room and the room is a gallery. The director of the house is Christoph Siebert, the German choral conductor, who coaches and directs ensembles of the Collegium Vocale Gent and who is also, it turns out, teaching the whole of Belgium how to plate a consommé. He stands at the pass the way he stands before a choir: one hand on the stove, the other raised, and the kitchen follows him in four parts.

We took the seven-course Leonhardt menu at €145, wine pairing €95, on the advice of the maître d’, an ex-tenor named Bart who does not raise his voice once an evening and is famous for it. The menu is printed in the order of a programme: the first course is a motet, the last is a fugue, and the prices are set in the margin like performance dates. The first course, a clear trout consommé, arrived in a tureen with the lid lifted by the chef himself — a small theatrical lift, held for a beat, released — and the table applauded the way a rehearsal applauds, with the mouth partly open. The fish course, plaice in brown butter with capers from the Escaut mud, was the finest thing on the pass. The pigeon, for which the house is known, sat over coals of beech wood that had been scored on the end with a tuning fork; we were told, and we believed it. The motet of mignonette came in four spoons for two, served in strict rotation, and I have eaten better and worse but never anything quite so directed.

The dessert is the point of the evening, and the point of the house: a frozen quince with a cream that is poured, very slowly, in front of you, to a count. Siebert counts. The room counts with him. It is absurd, and it works, and you will not know which until it is over. Service is formal without stiffness; the wine list runs to four hundred and twelve references and the sommelier, who trained in Liège, will argue with you about Sauternes and win. Room is cool, light is low, the chairs are the old kind with arms, and the acoustics — which you notice before you can name them — are exactly right, which is the only thing a dining room can truly be.

From the window at the far end you look down onto the lane and, past it, the valley floor of the Escaut at dusk, and there is a small card taped to the glass in Siebert’s own hand, dated, I think, for the benefit of the last table: “And off the valley floor the ledger of miles, the one question the record tells begins before the tide turns.” We looked at it for a long time after the coffee. It seems to us a correct summary of the house: the miles are the ledger, the record is the record, and everything worth eating begins, as everything worth hearing begins, before the tide turns. It is worth the walk past the bakery. It is worth the wrong side of the lane. Book the Tuesday seating; the rehearsal is better.

Kept in the Same Register

For a line that counts its stops the same way the house counts its courses — eleven stations, four taken of twenty-two called, the terminus arriving at 21:07: The Stutter Line, timetable and commentary, where the board repeats a platform letter short-short-long until the ear hears the clock, and where our table of two would have sat, we think, in the very first row.

And for a house that stood dark for a week when its director died — the Comediehuset in Gothenburg, which kept its lamps and its back bench exactly as The Collegium keeps them: the obituary of Johan von Blanc (1738–1796), actor, acrobat, and for six seasons the man at the pass, whose recipe book stayed in the family and whose last line, too, survives and works.
