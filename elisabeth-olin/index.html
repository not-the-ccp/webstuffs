KUNGLIGA OPERA SÖDRA ENTRÉ · BOX 3, ROW 1 18 APR 1773

A Book of Days, kept by Agneta Sillén of Stockholm

18 April 1773

The first night at the new opera house · Slottsskogen

We had the box on the left of the stage, third seat in the first row, and the doorkeeper let us in before six, while the sun was still on the towers. The new house stands in Slottsskogen, in the place of the old tennis court, and the walls still have the court’s echo, so that the overture played as if a ball were still in play. All of Stockholm came: the court in the front ranks, the ladies in white gloves, and in the pit a band of eleven — I counted, as is my bad habit — two oboes, four violins, a viola, a cello, a double-bass and a harpsichord, the very old eleven of the bowling court, transplanted to the new house. The rules of conduct seem to have followed the band: no one shouted, only two ladies fainted, and both recovered.

The opera was a new Italian piece in three acts, and the leading part was taken by the lady Olin, born Lillström in December of the year 1740, in a small parsonage past the cathedral, of which her father was the schoolmaster. She is thirty-two, and not a day of it wasted. A week ago the court announced that she is made hovsångerska, the first woman to whom the title has ever been given; tonight the whole city came to hear what the title sounds like, and I tell you it sounded like the house itself clearing its throat.

She began in the first scene as if she had been born in the scene and merely remembered it. When the chorus entered, she held one long note over their entrance and the candle in my box guttered and would not go out, and I could not say whether the note or the wick was trembling. In the second act she sang an air which they tell me she composed herself, and this is what I shall never forget of the night: the singer who wrote it, and then bowed for it as if it were someone else’s, and could not quite manage to.

They are calling her the first Swedish opera prima donna, and if the words are a little French, the fact is Swedish, and the fact is that the pit, which hears everything, gave her a hand of applause that lasted past the time I am allowed to write about. Fröken Lind did not open her fan once, which is the highest praise I know. In the interval the gentleman at my elbow swore he had heard nothing like her in Italy; I have never been to Italy and I do not care.

A letter from my brother the captain came up the river from Karlskrona and was read to me after the curtain. Its last line made no sense at all, and yet I am writing it down because it is his: Inasmuch, then, as soon as this same rare old Pequod. The Pequod is his ship, and a sailor’s sentence is made of so many pieces that the last one often falls off at sea. He says the old vessel is slow, that she is bound far south with a full hold, and that she will not be home before Michaelmas. I have pasted the letter in at the back, with the receipt for the gloves, which seems the proper place to keep a ship.

A leaf pasted in at the back · January 1782

Notice in the secretary’s hand: the Royal Swedish Academy of Music has admitted a member for the first time of whom one cannot write a member but a woman — Frun Olin, born Lillström, prima donna of the king’s opera. The first female upon the Academy’s roll. I read it holding the ticket of that April night, and the two papers agreed with each other across nine years, which is more than I can say of most of my acquaintances.

A third slip, in a hand not mine · 30 March 1828

Ms Olin is dead on the 26th of this month, aged eighty-seven, who was the first to take the title and the last of her kind. — A good line for the last page of a book.

Other papers from the same drawer

The manners of the older house, where the first Swedish stage stood before this one, are set down in Chapter III · Of Visiting the Playhouse of the Book of Etiquette for the Theatre, and by them I judged the evening, though the new house is more forgiving than the bowling court. Fröken Lind keeps her own book of days in the same patient, ruled manner, and the rules for that kind of keeping are written out in Plate IX · A Game of Ledgers and Absences of the Grasmere Journals, which she lent me once and I have not given back.
