Queensbridge Ward · Overhead Mezzanine · 21st Street & 41st Avenue

Diary of the Clerk of the Minutes
on the Matter of the Portable Document

Keeper. Miriam Feldstein, Clerk of the Minutes, Seat 2

Bound. The mezzanine book, folio 7, and the pages filed behind it

Span. 3 March 1992 – 3 March 2008

Ink. One black, one red, and one that the printer made itself

A diary is a fixed-layout document with a very patient author. The pages below are transcribed without correction; the red line is preserved as it was read into the record.

Tuesday, 3 March 1992 · 11:40 p.m. · the mezzanine, after adjournment

The committee went out at 9:52 and the 9:57 F followed it down track 2, so that the overhead was empty of everything except the clock, the lost-property shelf, and the two of us who are not allowed to leave the room unannounced. I have just certified folio 7 — the minutes of the meeting I took for the fourth time this year — and I am looking at them with a worry I cannot put into the record, which is this: everything this committee has ever said exists in one book, at one table, under one fluorescent tube, in one room that keeps its own little territory. The 1904 rider has survived four decades in exactly that one book. A flood, a fire, or a successor committee with a larger table would scatter us all at once. Dr. Whitcomb says the Bypass is coming and that we will need a bigger table, and I want the minutes to go to the bigger table in the same shape.

Thursday, 17 December 1992 · 41st Avenue Business Row, the glass window

Ruiz let me come into the shop past closing, which he does not do for other councillors, and showed me a page printed on a machine he has not yet bought. It is, he says, what a company in the United States called Adobe is about to hand to the world: a file format for documents — a portable document format — one file that carries the whole of a fixed-layout page, the text and the fonts and the vector drawings and the pictures, everything a page needs to be displayed, independent of the application that made it, the machine that prints it, and the operating system running either. The roots go back to PostScript, which is the language our typesetter in the station master’s office already speaks, and to a project called The Camelot Project, which Adobe’s co-founder John Warnock began in 1991, and which the company has been polishing for two years. Warnock wants a document to travel the way a letter travels, he told me, and to arrive as it left. I asked what it was for, and he pointed at the shelf, at the haunch of venison wrapped in a flattened Federative Line timetable, and said nothing, which I take to be an answer.

Wednesday, 13 January 1993 · the station master’s office, 12:05 a.m.

Dr. Whitcomb let me run BC-63-1992-07 — this March’s meeting, our special one — through the new format, as a proof, before the committee sees it. It came out on two pages and it is the minutes: the quorum at 9:16, the M and the F, the terminal question, terminal is a condition, not a character, the 1904 rider in full. The page it made looks like the folio it came from, which is the whole of the miracle and the whole of the point. Then the machine, as if it had a grudge, ran a line of type out of the margin at the foot of the second page, and I have transcribed it here word for word, as I am bound to do with any text that lands in the file:

“Cllr. Ruiz dissenting on the line, or for other cities, or for other cities, or for other cities, or for the child to talk.”

I set it against the record and the record made it almost legible. The committee has already voted, three to two, Cllr. Ruiz dissenting, on the principle that an unreadable rider is a rider the committee outlived — the first half of the line is the minutes repeating themselves in the wrong ink. And or for other cities, three times, is the wall doing its work: the 1989 Palmas timetable is still pinned in the mezzanine, and its Araguainía line runs, in the small type, it does not wait for the line, or for other cities, or for the ellipsis to close. The machine read the wall. The last clause, or for the child to talk, I have referred to the next committee with the file, on the same principle as Redcliffe up the U.S.: we keep what we cannot situate. The printer has been asked to stop. The printer has stopped.

Tuesday, 10 August 1993 · the mezzanine, by M, 9:41 p.m.

Motion, seconded by the M train on the strength of being punctual at 9:41, which it has done again: that the Clerk shall keep a certified copy of every minutes in the portable format, one folio per meeting, filed in the station master’s office under the little-territory rule, so that when the Bypass names its year and finds us a larger table the minutes walk over in their own shapes. Carried 4–0, the M train abstaining from self-congratulation. Okafor signed his name under his own motion, which he does not normally do. On the wall, beside the Palmas, I have pinned a note from a small society in Ashley, Cambridgeshire, the Heorot Society, Newsletter No. 9, who read one folio a month, a manuscript made between AD 975 and 1025, and who have told us their method is ours and older; the note is filed where the rider sits. We are, I think, a company that keeps things exactly, in one place, for people who have not arrived yet.

Tuesday, 3 March 2008 · the mezzanine, 10:02 p.m., sixteen years to the day

The clock on the wall is right to the second, which is more than I can say for most of what this committee predicted. Two things to enter tonight. First: the portable document format has been standardised. It is now ISO 32000, held by ISO committee TC 171, subcommittee SC 2, working group 8, with the PDF Association serving as committee manager; the name the trade uses is simply PDF, and the file I have kept of folio 7, re-certified every year at March, still opens — on four different machines now — exactly as it opened in 1993, the antlers and the venison and the red line and all. It arrived as it left. Warnock kept the promise and then went on to standardise the keeping of it, which I have never understood to be a single talent but suspect may be. Second: the Bypass has still not named its year; the station is still a terminal; a condition, not a character, entered 1992, still standing. The antlers have not been claimed in sixteen years, and the venison is, by any count I have run, no longer the new venison of anything, and it sits where it sits, wrapped in its flattened timetable, waiting for the 1989 Palmas, which is the one thing in the mezzanine that has never once been late for this shelf. I am locking the office. The file follows the minutes. The minutes follow the M.

Transcribed from the mezzanine book and the folios filed behind it, without correction, by the Clerk of the Minutes, 21st Street–Queensbridge. The meeting minutes these entries grow out of are kept at Minutes of a Meeting of the Borough Committee — 21st Street–Queensbridge Station, and the wall they read is the Official Timetable &amp; Commentary of the Federative Line, service 1989 Palmas, still due, soon.
