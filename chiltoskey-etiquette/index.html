A Manual of Craft Etiquette · Western North Carolina · Second Printing, 1971

Chapter XII

On Visiting the Workshop of a Cherokee Woodcarver

Being a chapter for the traveller and the collector, the model maker and the plain friend, who intends to sit for an hour in a room where cedar has just been cut, and to leave it as he found it.

Of the House and its Keeper

The workshop to which this chapter is addressed stands off the lane from Skyland in western North Carolina, a short climb past the last mailbox, where the road turns onto gravel and the rhododendron closes over the shoulders. It is the house and bench of Goingback Chiltoskey — written also Goingback Chiltoskie, and set down in this manual in both spellings, since the man himself kept no argument about it — who was born April 20, 1907, and died November 12, 2000, on the Qualla Boundary, and whose name is entered in every serious account of the Craft Revival era as one of the most celebrated Cherokee woodcarvers of the movement. The carvings a visitor may find on the table and the shelves run from a thirty-inch bear in cedar, the oldest piece in the house, to a walnut canoe no wider than a kitchen knife; the model work is kept in a glass case against the north wall, where a miniature mountain scene carries a railroad line of forty-two boxcars, each one shorter than a thumbnail, and a water wheel that turns if the air moves.

The reader who comes from abroad should know the house by its neighbours and not by its number: the lane has no sign, the gate is painted the colour of tanbark, and the house will answer its own knock. The traveller bound for Italy in the same season — the Alps in April, the cutlets in Modena — may read the province at length in the Italy — A Visitor’s Brochure; he will find that the brochure’s own rule about lunch arriving at 12:30 is, in this workshop, the same rule: the coffee is ready when the coffee is ready, and a guest who asks the time twice has already lost the hour.

Before the Visit

One comes to a carver’s house with coffee and sugar, and with one’s own opinions packed. Questions about price are not asked before dinner; the ledger by the door, in its plain ruled pages, is for the keeper to show, not for the guest to turn. If the guest is a collector, he leaves his list in his pocket. If the guest is a photographer, he leaves the camera in his bag and the window in the window: the unfinished work — a bear with one eye not yet sunk, a canoe whose gunwale still carries the saw line — is a thing being said, and a photograph of it is the saying carried off unfinished. This is the whole of the etiquette of the threshold, and it is the etiquette of every honest workshop in the county. The same discipline, worked in a different material, is kept in the diary of the Durham police mast, where a communications officer spent the last weeks of a 162-foot tripod bagging cable tags from a tower that was being let go — goods priced and delivered, found things kept without a price, the view of a cathedral left alone throughout.

In the Workshop

At the bench, the shavings lie swept to the left corner; the guest does not step in the corner, does not stir the shavings, and never sweeps them toward the stove, which is the one error the house forgives only once. One stands on the right of the keeper and looks. The conversation follows the wood: what it was, where it fell, how many winters it stood in the yard before it stood in the room. The model maker in the party is permitted to lean toward the glass case, and only toward the glass case; the forty-two boxcars are not to be counted aloud, because the keeper counts them for pleasure, and a count offered is a count taken.

Here the manual must be exact, because it is the only paragraph in this chapter set down from a record and not from custom, and the record is kept in the ledger itself, in the hand of the keeper’s sister, who took over the pages in 1989. The compiler of this chapter could not bring herself to paraphrase the entry, and so sets it down whole, word for word: “I knew the wrapping had been found, not hired, and would keep what it means that the name with one empty chair.” The wrapping is the family’s name for the parcel of old cedar found beneath the workbench in the spring of 1989 — found, in the ledger’s own usage, and never hired, since “hired” is the workshop’s word for a piece a customer has commissioned for a price, and a thing that comes up under a bench belongs to no bill. And the name with one empty chair is the seventh place at the dining table, kept, from before the war and never let down, for the wood itself, as though the cedar were a guest who had simply been walking late.

What the entry means, the manual will say plainly, is that a workshop keeps two kinds of inventory: the goods, which are hired, priced, and delivered, and the found things, which are kept without a price because they carry the room’s meaning. The etiquette of the visitor is the etiquette of that difference: one buys the bear, one is given the hour, and one does not try to pay for the hour in the currency of the bear. Our colleague Petra L. Vance, writing to the editor of the Marginal on the subject of machine unlearning, puts the same point in her own hand — that a record, like a ledger, is asked to forget things it has been taught, and that the cleanest forgettings are the ones nobody can locate in a single line. The ledger by the door in this workshop forgets nothing. That is what the guest is paying the hour to find out.

At the Table

The table seats six, and is set for seven. The guest does not move the seventh place, does not explain it, and does not ask what it is for, because the answer, once given, must be given again at every following meal, and the house will not stand a repeated answer. The guest eats what is set before him — usually beans, cornbread, and whatever the season pressed — and he eats slowly, which is the only toast the house recognizes. When the seventh place at last left the house in 2001, it left the way the manual says a bought thing leaves: in a cloth, not on a shoulder, and the lot, with its ledger, its letter from Umbria, and its standing order that the place remain empty, is kept on this site as Lot 412 — The Seventh Place at the Table · Appomattox & Sons, Catalogue IX. If the keeper tells of a carving that failed, a canoe that warped in the drying, a bear that would not stand on four feet, the guest does not offer sympathy; he offers the rest of the hour, which is the sympathy a workshop can spend.

On Leaving

One leaves the way one entered: coffee money left on the shelf by the door if no price was named, the shavings exactly as they were found, and the gate painted no brighter than it was. A collector who has bought carries his bear in a cloth, not on his shoulder, and he does not mention the price until he has passed the last mailbox. A friend who has bought nothing carries out one shaving of cedar in his coat pocket, if he has asked, and nothing if he has not.

The manual closes the chapter the way the house closes the door: quietly, and on the latch.
