Bench No. 2 · Kitchen Table, Under the Window That Frosts First

The First Event

A build log in blue ink · Men’s 500 metres speed skating, Chamonix, 26 January 1924 · one scale oval, one brass plaque, and the first gold the winter games have ever given out

Entry 1 · Saturday, 26 January

Today, in Chamonix, the first event ever contested at the Winter Olympics is being run: the 500 metres in the speed skating programme, at the Stade Olympique, the little outdoor oval they have laid on the snow above the town. One of five speed skating races at these Games, but it is first in the order, which is the whole of my interest. I am building the course, not the town: a balsa oval at a scale of about one to two hundred, forty-two centimetres round, on a base of old pine that has been sanded with 220 grit until it looks like packed snow. The ice is a slurry of kaolin and white casein, floated on and floated off, then a hair-fine coat of tinned fish oil for the shine, which is a fraud but a beautiful one. The skaters are six figures — one per heat of the final, as I have chosen to count it — bodies from scrap balsa, the blades from 0.4 mm brass strip filed to a keel, each runner set on a wire so the figure can still be nudged a centimetre before the moment.

The plan is small and complete. The oval. The start gate, a sliver of birch. A clock figure with a card on its face that says 26 JAN 1924, because the event was actually run a full month before the Games formally opened, and a model that gets that wrong is a model that has lost the only thing it had. And one brass plaque, for the man who comes through first. I know who that is. Charles Jewtraw, American, from Wausau, Wisconsin. I am not building the suspense; I am building the record. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole project.

Entry 2 · Wednesday, 6 March

The ice took four coats before it would stop looking like plaster of Paris, and the last two coats I laid with the window open and the kettle on, because the casein needs the steam and the bench needs the cold and I need both at once, which is how all the good things on this table have been finished. The six skaters stand at the start gate now. Jewtraw in red, white, and blue, set half a body-length out of the pack, because the record says he ran it that way, alone and early, and Ivar Ballangrud of Norway a half-step behind with the silver in him, and Charles Pott, the other American, holding the inside line with the bronze. Two medals out of the field of six go to the United States, and one of them to a man who, in the morning of this date, is becoming the first Winter Olympics gold medallist at all.

I will be honest about what I am actually making, because a log that flinches from its subject flinches from everything. It is not, in the end, a skating race. The stadium resists the description — the grandstand, the rope line, the snow banks, the particular light of a January morning in the Alps that no white casein I can buy will reproduce. What I can set in the form in its positive and its alleyways and its brass plaque on the tea houses, and Saint Peter’s feast. The raised course standing proud of the base, the little walkways around the outer edge where the figures can circulate without touching the ice, the plaque filed flat and lettered by hand, and the date card — I set it, at the end, for the feast of Saints Peter and Paul, June 29, which is when I finally stopped fiddling and let it dry. The thing is a record of its own making as much as of Jewtraw’s run, and I have decided I prefer it that way.

Entry 3 · Friday, 21 June

Feast day. The model is finished and I have set it on the window ledge where the frost comes first, which is the one place on this table that understands it. The oval is forty-two centimetres round, the skaters are true to within the width of the filing needle, and the clock figure keeps its card. What I am most proud of is the plaque. It is a square of the old brass ruler, filed to a bevel, with four lines hand-punched and then inked to show: CHARLES JEWTRAW · U.S.A. · 500 METRES · FIRST WINTER OLYMPIC GOLD · STADE OLYMPIQUE DE CHAMONIX · 26 JANVIER 1924. One of five speed skating races, the first of them, won in a little outdoor oval on a month-cold Saturday, by an American from a town in Wisconsin whose name I had to look up once and will never forget, which is the whole point of looking anything up once.

Finish notes, since a log that does not end ends badly. Two coats of wax on the pine base, one long evening’s dust, the fish-oil shine left exactly as it was. The oval keeps the first morning of a new kind of competition in it whole, the way a seed keeps the tree. It sits on the same shelf as the shed’s liner notes for Latin Alphabet No. 9, where the euro sign replaced eight glyphs and everything had to be re-provisioned, and beside the Encyclopaedia of the Republic, Entry 214, on Fabio Baudrit González, who founded an academy and gave it a motto it kept in spite of itself — three objects, each of them a first kept in a form that never needed it. For the rest of what the shelf holds, the Webstuffs front page is the index; I am only the bench.
