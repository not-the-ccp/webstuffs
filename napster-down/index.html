Sat 4 Jul 2001, 6:41 a.m. · post #1

Okay. I woke up and the machine on the bus won't find a single node. Zero. The Napster client has been down all morning and the news says it's not a server problem, it's a court in San Francisco and Judge Kelly and a whole stack of record labels. So I want to ask this board, which has been feeding me demo tapes and bootlegs since 1998: what happens to the songs? I have about 400 of them on my hard drive in Tucson — the ones the band recorded with me at the old Cherokee studio on Park Avenue in October and November, tracked into the room before we drove across the county to the Harddrive place for vocals — and I was saving the rest through the index. My copy of the whole catalogue, the 1.2 million songs everybody kept quoting, is now just a list of names pointing at nothing.

One more thing, and forgive me for pasting it in, but a friend who does the band's paperwork sent me a page from a file that was on the docket up in Los Angeles. The top of the page had a banner glued straight into the case name, like the printer ran out of line feed, and it read, word for word:

EXCERPT · PART 7 OF 11 SUPERIOR COURT OF THE STATE OF CALIFORNIA — COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES DreamWorks Recording Corporation v.

— and that's where the page ends. After the “v.” there's nothing, just a coffee ring. I've been sitting here trying to work out what DreamWorks Recording Corporation wanted from anybody, and I've got a theory. The full transcript is filed in the public shelf, if anyone wants to read it with the coffee ring included: DreamWorks Recording Corp. v. Jimmy Eat World, et al. — Transcript Excerpt, Part 7 of 11. It's not about Napster, it's about who gets to keep an album title, but it came out of the same courthouse the same spring, and it made me think: the labels are suing for the name of the record while the file sharing dies anyway. Somebody check the site again at noon. I'll post.

To be clear on the timeline so people stop making stuff up in the replies: the whole thing started in June 1999, two kids out of North Carolina — Shawn Fanning, who was nineteen and writing the client in his dorm, and Sean Parker, who did the wiring and the press. By March 2001 they claimed six million registered users and that 1.2 million song index. What killed it wasn't one lawsuit, it was the pile: the A&M Records suit out in the Ninth Circuit, the labels' motion in the San Francisco federal court, and then the injunction. The site itself can be shut with a signature on a piece of paper. The songs were never on their servers to begin with — they were on our machines. That's the part I can't stop thinking about at 9 a.m. on a Saturday.

I was a dial-up user and I want to say this on the record: I waited through the 4-minute download of a 3-minute song. I know how the sound works, I just didn't care. Metallica wrote the famous letter about them in November of 1999 — they were the first famous band to complain, they wanted it stopped and the site stayed up — and I remember thinking, if they are happy, why would I trade this for a 99-cent CD I have to drive to a parking lot and buy. Now the label lawyers have won and the van driver from Tucson is asking what happens to the songs. I'll tell you what happens to them: nothing. They're on my 20-gig drive in Tallahassee, with the Winamp skin my cousin made, and no judge in California or San Francisco or anywhere has a key for it. I've got 900 songs. Nobody is coming. The modem screeches and I am happy.

seismograph_ken old-timer Tallinn, via the observatory
Sat 4 Jul 2001, 1:52 p.m. · post #4

I keep getting asked what survives, and I keep answering with an instrument. There's a Lander seismograph from 1928 sitting on a window sill in the Patarei building in Tallinn, facing the wharf, kept by a geological observatory because a stage director put it there in 1931 and nobody ever argued the point — it was the single contested item in his estate case, file 5/682, and the court let it go to the observatory anyway. Eleven years of trace paper. It measured a 4.2 tremor on the night of 3 November 1946, and the dead man wrote a joke in the margin of his rehearsal notebook about how the stone bank shudders while the printed page sits still. Here's the lesson for a music board: the record is the thing that doesn't flinch. The distribution is the thing that does. You can shut a network with one signature; you can't shut eleven years of trace paper. Keep the notes. The index was never the songs. It never was.

Checked the site at noon: still down. So here's my answer to my own question. The songs don't go anywhere, they just stop travelling. I'm going to burn them to CD-R in Tucson this weekend, the whole 400, and I'm going to write the titles on the jewel cases in the same pen I write the invoice numbers at the hardware store. Slow as a river, which is fine. The hat's back on the head and the career is back on the shelf. Also, for the record, I read the Los Angeles transcript my friend sent the banner from — the “v.” leads into the name of the band, and the whole case is about a title, and there's a line on a museum card that the judge admitted “as a fact of the photographic backdrop only,” and I have decided I like that phrase very much. Filed: CLERK'S OFFICE, MARCH 16, 2001. The clerk keeps the hat and the river and the career on the card. I'll keep the CDs. We are all just clerks now.

It happened. The name was bought by a software company in Emeryville called Roxio, the CD-making one, for $5.3 million — five point three million, less than what a single week of this old site's traffic would have been worth if traffic meant anything. They're relaunching it next year as a subscription, $5.99 a month for the right to hear things instead of own copies of them. Somebody in the old thread asked what happens to the songs. That's it, that's the answer, somebody finally paid five point three million dollars for it. Welcome back, Napster. You are a subscription now.

The Minnesota store passed the subscription to a music company called Rhapsody in December of 2011 — sold it off like a department store passing on its housewares — and now, 2016, Rhapsody has simply renamed itself Napster. The name came all the way home by way of a software company, a retail chain, and a streaming service. It is the most circuitous route a name can take and it arrived, which is the entire moral of the Lander seismograph: put the instrument on the sill, face it toward the wharf, and wait. The trace comes. The catalogue numbers follow.
