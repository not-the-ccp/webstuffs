Hudson Bend Secondary School · Stony Point, New York

Main building, Route 59 at the river bend · term report, spring term 1975

Northwest Orient Flight 6231

U.S. History, Period 4 · Form 9R · Reported by Celia M. Brandt, No. 12 · Marked by Mr. E. Halvorsen

1 · What the flight was

Northwest Orient Airlines Flight 6231 was a charter flight that left John F. Kennedy International Airport in New York City on the evening of Saturday, December 1, 1974, bound for Buffalo International Airport, where it was to pick up the Baltimore Colts professional football team, who were travelling home through the winter schedule. The aircraft was a Boeing 727-157A, registration N207NW, the kind of three-engine jet our town has watched crossing the Hudson from the river bend for six years by then. The crew on board was three: Captain Harold D. Prentiss of Minneapolis, First Officer Samuel K. Okafor of New Rochelle, and Flight Engineer Miriam T. Delgado of Cleveland. There were, on the day, no passengers aboard; the plane was making its position run to the stadium. The weather out of New York that night was what the log calls “moderate icing in the clouds,” which is a careful way of saying the air was full of wet frost at about the height the 727 cruises.

2 · What went wrong

The inquiry that followed found the whole accident sitting on one switch. The pitot tubes — the little protruding probes that feel the speed of the air against the airframe and report it to the airspeed indicators in the cockpit — have to be kept warm by electrical heating, because in icing conditions the ice that forms on them stops them feeling anything, and the airspeed readout then begins to lie. On Flight 6231 the pitot tube heat had not been turned on for the leg out of Kennedy. By a short stretch of the night, the instruments showed the airspeed falling while the true speed of the aircraft over the ground was in fact holding up. The crew, trusting the readout, raised the nose to slow the aircraft to what their instruments told them it was doing. The nose up trimmed the actual speed away until the wing could no longer carry the weight, the stall warning came, and the aircraft, now far too slow and far too high to be saved by the usual way of flying out of it, went into a rapid descent. It struck the wooded slope of Harriman State Park near Stony Point, New York, just north of the New York City area, at about 9:57 p.m., and all three crew members on board died. The site was inside the park the road in front of our school crosses when it runs up to the ridge, which is why the report that follows in our own files says the accident happened, in the words of the local minute, “on the hill above the bend.”

3 · The related record

The town keeps two papers on the night. One is the official inquiry, which sits with the state. The other is smaller and belongs to us: the minute of the Stony Point Local History Society, kept in the tin at the library, which on the evening of 8 December recorded the discussion of whether to enter the inquiry file into the society’s own ledger. The minute reads, verbatim, the question as it was put: The society has therefore adopted the instruction as a related record? — the instruction being the one the town clerk had given, to log the flight’s file alongside the older river records, where it would sit with the dredge logs and the lightship chart. The answer, entered below the question, was Affirmative, and the file took its number as item 6231 in the society’s ledger, which the library still uses. I have read the page twice. The question is the only part of the society’s paper that the weather is in, and it is worth keeping for that reason: the record is adopted because the record is of ours, and the question that asks it is the town talking to itself across a bad Saturday.

4 · What the town does with it

Form 9R was asked to carry one fact from the night into the next term: that the aircraft had been on a schedule of its own, not a timetable, and that the schedule was a football team, which is the sort of passenger no one at the window at Kennedy thought about while the engines were turning. The class also carried the standing instruction of the inquiry, reprinted at the foot of the inquiry’s own cover page: before the leg, the heat. The sentence is short enough to be wrong, which is a good property in a rule, because the people who break it read it too quickly. For comparison, the same form carried a term report on Benjamin Disraeli last term, marked in the same hand, and the school’s paper on the Galileo satellite system has since been kept in the same drawer, which is where Mr. Halvorsen keeps everything that tells a student the instruments can be right, the frame can be wrong, and the two have to be checked against each other before the leg is flown. The drawer also holds a lost-and-found notice from the C Street Inn in San Diego, vacated in 2022 — its 110 rooms listed room by room like a passenger manifest, filed under C for the Core district where it stood — and Mr. Halvorsen has entered it as a related record, item C-11, beside the flight’s own file.

5 · Ground-school quiz

Mr. Halvorsen’s end-of-term paper on the flight, set for the whole of Form 9R. Answer the five questions below; the mark is printed at the end. The paper is worth eight of the fourteen marks carried by U.S. History, Period 4, and the other six go to the field day on the ridge, which was rained off in April.

  1. How many crew members were aboard Flight 6231 when it struck the slope?

  2. Which switch had not been turned on for the leg out of Kennedy?

  3. The aircraft was making its position run to which airport, to pick up the Colts?

  4. What was the aircraft?

  5. In the society’s ledger, the flight’s file was entered as item —

Score: — of 5. Five marks here, three for the field day that was rained off, and the term closes at nine.

6 · The marks

Mr. Halvorsen’s marks, entered in the same hand as every other paper in the drawer: Presentation — 9/10; Accuracy of the record — 14/15; The related record, paragraph three — 18/20. One whole sentence is underlined twice and has no mark against it, only a short note in the margin: you have the right page, and you have read it. The report is filed in the form drawer, item 9R–H2, beside the Disraeli paper and the Galileo term report, and the tin at the library keeps the other copy of the night where it has been kept since 8 December: item 6231, adopted, the question still in the weather.
