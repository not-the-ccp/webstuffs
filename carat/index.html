Notice No. 48 · opened 27 April 2011, in advance · closed 4 May 2011 · one item, one claim, one apology

The Carat (Mass)

A letter of apology on the weighing of a single pearl · metric carat, 200 milligrams · 100 points of 2 milligrams

To M. Rowlands of Porth, claimant
with copies to the holders of the notice, and to Mrs E. G. Davies, chapel cook
dated Tuesday 3 May 2011, from the vestry

Dear Rowlands of Porth,

I am T. J. Davies, vestry clerk, keeper of the ledger and of the drawer, and I write to apologise for the weight of a pearl. It was on Friday the 29th of April, when the circuit kept the wedding of the Prince of Wales at Westminster from the vestry television, as Thread No. 312 on the Tufton Wire records — eleven in the vestry, the spare held, rain, attendance and weather only — that, after the second Blaenwern and the clearing of the chairs, a single pearl in a cloth of black velvet was found in the drawer. The drawer is the one with the thread pin and the marmalade. The Office had already opened Notice No. 48 on the strength of that thread, the first notice opened before the thing was lost, and the pearl was filed as No. 48 itself, which the Office takes to be its own way of keeping a promise. The claim came on Monday from the Rowlands: one pearl, from the old engagement box of 1915, held at one carat and a half. I weighed it on the balance in the drawer, I wrote the weight on the claim slip, and I did it wrong. This letter is the correction.

For the drawer to be right, and to stay right, the record of the unit is set down here in full. The carat (ct) is a unit of mass equal to 200 milligrams, which is to say 0.00705 ounce, 0.00643 troy ounce, 3.08647 grains; it is the measure of gemstones and pearls, and of nothing else in this vestry, which is a rule the marmalade has never broken. The current definition, sometimes known as the metric carat, was adopted in 1907 at the Fourth General Conference on Weights and Measures, and in the years that followed in many countries around the world. The carat is divisible into 100 points of 2 milligrams each. Other subdivisions, and slightly different mass values, have been used in the past in different locations; the Porth carat of the old assay came into this drawer at a weight that no two county records agree on, and I confess the balance had been left marked in it — marked, I now think, in a hand that is still in the margins of the 1915 book.

Here, then, is the mistake, in its entirety. The claim said one carat and a half. I read the scale as it stood, in the old Porth count, and wrote on the slip 1.7 c, English count, without the conversion and without the mark the standard gives the unit, which is CD — the abbreviation the ANSI X.12 EDI standard carries for the carat — and it is the one the Office will henceforth stamp on its slips, so that no future line can be read in two tongues at once. The pearl is one carat and a half, and no less and no more: 300 milligrams, 150 points of 2 milligrams each, which the balance confirmed at 10 o’clock on Tuesday when I brought it back, set it right, and read it in the only count the conference of 1907 left standing. I am sorry to the Rowlands, who kept the true weight for six weeks before being asked to doubt it; and I am sorry to Mrs E. G. Davies, who on Thursday, over the tea, weighed the marmalade in carats to see how far the joke went, and found it at a weight I will not put into this letter.

There is a line on the back of the slip, in a hand the vestry agrees is the late organist’s, which the Office preserves word for word:

“The television from the beams is counted as percussion and the two words to say.)”

— Back of the claim slip, Notice No. 48

The vestry has taken its ruling on the line, and it is this. The first clause is the boathouse convention, known and kept: the television that came up from the boathouse for the wedding stood between the hymn board and the kettle, under the beams, and the drip from the beams is counted as percussion, per the handling notes of FM-4, chapter 4, so that the day’s record keeps its sound without asking anyone to decode it. The second clause is an instruction: the slip stands waiting, at the foot of the record, for two words to be said, and it has waited since Friday. They are said in this letter, and in this order: sorry, carat.

The corrections are entered. The line of the slip is struck and re-entered, as the slip below shows; the balance in the drawer is re-marked to the metric carat, 200 milligrams, and the slip is stamped CD. The pearl goes back to the Rowlands with the rider on Thursday, in the 1915 cloth, and the claim is closed. Notice No. 48 stands filed as the only notice in the Office’s keeping that was opened before the thing was lost and closed in the same week, which the Office takes to be a record. No entry of this apology goes into the ledger — per the rule of section 4.5, attendance and weather only, and do not transcribe — which is why it is written here, once, in a letter, instead.

Respectfully, and in the methodical manner of the name,

T. J. Davies
Vestry clerk · keeper of the ledger and of the drawer
The Tufton circuit · Pembrokeshire
