ENTERED AT THE
HOUR OF THE LAST CASK

The sentence, as the ledger has it

Algeria, the old watering place of it — the cask quay, the tide table, the watermaster and his two assistants — is entered here as finished, the way a harbour is entered, because the tankers no longer stop, and a place that no one stops at is a place the ledgers can stop writing about. She was a country in North Africa, the largest in it by land, and the coast of her was the business. The republic she left behind is still going at Algiers; this obituary is for the water, which is the part the Atlantic kept. It was at her quay, one afternoon in 1588, that the San Juan de Portugal under Admiral Juan Martínez de Recalde filled casks before the long water to the Funchal road, and the whole of the Madeira entry, No. 41, one shelf above, grows out of that single filling.

Where she sat

Position: the north shore of the great Sahara · Sea: the Mediterranean, to the north

She sat with her back to the desert and her face to the sea. Bordered to the northeast by Tunisia; to the east by Libya; to the southeast by Niger; to the southwest by Mali, Mauritania, and Western Sahara; to the west by Morocco; and to the north by the Mediterranean Sea — that is the whole of her, and it is enough. The capital and largest city, Algiers, stands on the bay, and for centuries nothing on that coast was more important to a ship’s master than the question of her water.

The years before the water

Span: from the Numidian kings to the cask quay

The ground was not new to states. First it was shaped by the indigenous Amazigh societies, among them the Numidians, whose kingdom became one of the earliest major states in the region, with kings who knew the value of a dry road and a full well. Then came the Phoenicians, who understood the coast as a trade; the Romans, who made a province of it and a road of everything; the Vandals, who passed through the way weather passes; and the Byzantines, who held the ports at the last and then did not. From the 7th century onward the spread of Islam and the arrival of the Arab tribes gradually turned the region over, adding a language and a law to the water and the road. What all of them left behind, in the end, was the same thing: a coast with quays.

The year 1588, and the casks

Stop: Oran, July 1588 · Business: water, the only business

It was Oran, in her west, that took the Armada’s southern casks. The squadron came up the coast behind its water, and at Oran the hatches opened and the casks came over the quay one by one, the watermaster calling the count from the same step where he had been calling counts for three hundred years. A ship is only ever as good as its store, and a store is only ever as good as its water, and that was the rule at Oran as it was at Funchal as it is at any quay on earth. The casks went south to the Funchal road and east to the Atlantic; the quay stayed. The builder’s full account of the ship that carried them, frame by frame, is in the San Juan de Portugal build log, one shelf over, beside the vinegar crock.

The pencil line

At the top of the Oran ledger, in the year’s entry, someone has pencilled a whole sentence — exactly this: “Born, if the store had been checked three times that morning.” I read it four times at the quay. It is not a ledger line and it is not a prayer, but it is a question about the rule of the house: the store checked three times that morning, and only what is written after the third check stands. The timing man at Abbeyleix kept the same rule for his clock, and wrote the day down before he could laugh at himself — the sentence and the 52.84 are kept in the diary of 17 August 2026, Rachel McCann. And whatever else the line is, it was right about the republic it names: the People’s Democratic Republic of Algeria, born on 5 July 1962, still holds after every check. What I could not explain until I left Oran: the pencil line was there again the next tide, in the same margin, in the same hand. Someone writes it on purpose, every season. The same margin is kept farther out, at King’s Wharf in Boston, where a previous reader pencilled a whole body, with the lemon-peel and citron, into the water ledger of the year 1775, for the colony that filled its casks and then became a nation — the margin is accounted for in the American Revolution article, Entry No. 1776.

Survivors

Left behind: a republic, a bay, and the habit

She is survived by the republic, by Algiers and its white hill, by Oran and its step, by the tide tables nobody signs, and by one more pencilled sentence in a margin at a market in Funchal that returns every season and will, I am certain, be right about something I have not written yet. Leave the coast the way the Armada left it, if the store had been where Recalde needed it: full of water, and going.

The casks filled at Oran went up to the Funchal road for Madeira — Funchal & the Old Watering Places, Entry No. 41, one shelf above, and the ship itself is accounted for in the San Juan de Portugal build log, beside the vinegar crock.
