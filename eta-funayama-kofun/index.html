Nagomi Junior High School · History & Geography, First Term

Eta Funayama Kofun

A report on the burial mound at Nagomi, Kumamoto — 江田船山古墳

Student: Haruka Imada, Class 2-C, No. 17 Teacher: Mr. Ota (History & Geography) Date: 14 June Field visit: Saturday, 2 May, 6:40 a.m. departure

1. What the mound is, and where it stands

Eta Funayama Kofun is a kofun, an ancient burial mound, located in Nagomi in Kumamoto Prefecture, in the middle of the Kikuchi Plain where the rice paddies run right up to the foot of the hill. The name means roughly “the boat-hill kofun of Eta,” because from the north road the mound looks, as our guide said, like the hull of an inverted boat — a long, low keel resting in the fields. The mound belongs to the Kofun period, the centuries roughly from the third to the seventh century after the common era, when local chieftains were buried under earthworks that grew larger with the family's power. Eta Funayama is one of the better-preserved examples on the plain, and the whole area around it is protected as a historic site.

2. The 1951 designation, and the two mounds that came with it

The mound was designated a National Historic Site of Japan in 1951, in the early years of the modern system of protection, and the designation did not stop at the main hill. It also covers Tsukabōzu Kofun (塚坊主古墳, “the monk's-topknot mound”) and Kokuzōtsuka Kofun (虚空蔵塚古墳, named after the Buddhist figure Kannon's companion Kūzō), both in the same stretch of the plain. In other words, when the state protected one mound, it protected the small family of mounds around it, because a kofun read alone is like a sentence read out of its paragraph. The three of them together are one of the standard sites on the Kumamoto heritage list, and the signboard at the Eta Funayama gate lists all three names in that order.

3. What came out of the earth

The most important part of our visit, and of the report, is the excavation. Artifacts recovered from the mound have been designated National Treasures of Japan, and they are now kept at the Tokyo National Museum, far from the plain where they were buried. The treasure case holds bronze weapons and ornaments: a short sword, bronze arrowheads, and rings of the sort called kanabō-shaped bracelets, all cast in a style that Mr. Ota made us underline — the style of the bronze items resembles artifacts from the Korean kingdom of Baekje, which in the fifth and sixth centuries had many exchanges with Japan, sending bronze, glass beads, and craftsmen across the sea with the court at the Kofun capital. So the mound in Nagomi is not a closed box: it is a node on a trade and gift road running from Baekje's workshops to a Kikuchi-plain burial chamber, and the Tokyo case is where the road's parcels now rest. The museum's catalogue numbers follow the old List of National Treasures of Japan, and the site's own record card carries the same numbers so that a visitor at the mound can hold the two lists side by side. The same decades are Emperor Yūryaku's — the first emperor archaeology can name beside objects like these — and the museum keeps a placard for his reign beside the mound's file: Placard No. 214, Emperor Yūryaku-tennō, Kikuchi Plain Museum, Gallery 7.

4. The route south, and one sentence in the newsletter

Our class had to plan the walking loop ourselves, because the school bus could only take us as far as the county road. The plain road climbs twice — over the first pass near the rice-elevator, and then over the second pass above the Kikuchi bend where the old ferry crossing used to be. Mr. Ota's correction to our draft route was brief: the bus line goes up the first pass, then follows the river, but the walking loop has an alternate. He printed it in the class newsletter, which travels between our school and the sister school at the foot of the hill, and the sentence appeared in the newsletter's route column exactly like this: “The newsletter travels the other could pass south of the second pass.” We were allowed to keep it word for word because, as Mr. Ota explained, the meaning is plain once you stand at the fork: the newsletter's loop is the other route of the pair, and it could pass south of the second pass — a way that keeps the walk entirely on the plain side, under the mound, on the shade road past the Tsukabōzu Kofun markers, and back at the gate in forty minutes. The class voted 20 to 3 to walk it that way, and the whole class did.

5. What I learned

I came to the visit expecting a hill and a signboard. What I got was a date (1951), three names (Eta Funayama, Tsukabōzu, Kokuzōtsuka), a museum in Tokyo, and a kingdom across the sea. A kofun is small enough to walk under and large enough to hold trade between countries. If I may say so in a school report, I now understand why the bronzes travel — the mound keeps them in the plain, and the museum keeps them in the record, and the road between is only as long as a bus ride plus a walk south of the second pass.
