From the Desk: The Recurring Question

We begin, as is our custom, with the arithmetic of the contract. Kyra, who goes by the title the Seer, is a demon of divination and precognition, created by executive producer Brad Kern for Charmed, the American television supernatural drama that ran on The WB Television Network from 1998 to 2006 — eight seasons, 201 episodes, one very large audience, and a recurring demon who out-grew her contract. She is played by Charisma Carpenter, and the Society's reading of the record is this: the original contract was for a recurring role of exactly three episodes in the show's season seven. Three appearances, then a curtain. The curtain, as it happened, refused to fall.

The matter is not hypothetical. TV Guide reported that the WB had considered promoting her to a series regular, which is the strongest language a network uses for a character it is not yet quite ready to commit to. The Society takes the report as a finding of fact and the consideration as a finding of character: the network sat with the question. That is the whole of the case, and it is, we think, a beautiful one. A demon who sees what is coming, offered a permanent seat in the room where the coming happens, and the question left on the desk, like a letter, for everyone to read. The minutes of the season in question are held by the archivist, P. Tan, in the drawer marked WB, and the drawer is on the fourth shelf behind the radiator, where it has been since 2004.

On Friendship, Specifically Phoebe

What the three episodes do that the contract did not plan for: Kyra, who arrives as a demon, leaves as a friend — specifically a friend of Leo Wyatt (Brian Krause), the angel, and of the Charmed Ones, and most specifically of Phoebe Halliwell (Alyssa Milano). The Society's rule on this, adopted 2005, minute 214, is that the friendship counts as the real plot, and that the divination is only the ticket. Precognition in this house is a way of meeting people early. Kyra saw the coming before it came, and what she saw, in the end, was company. Her stated desire in the run — to become more than the role was — is the Society's founding line, and it is pasted, in R. Okafor's hand, inside the front cover of the minute book.

The Walnut Box, and the Cat

The box arrived with the 1998 convention footage and has been kept by the Society ever since: a walnut box, eight inches by six, on two little brass feet, that once held a glass sphere at the last table-read the Society remembers. The sphere is gone; the box is not. It sits on the second shelf of the club room, above the minute book, in the direct line of sight of Clive, the club cat, a tom of indeterminate age and precise judgment. Clive is a cat of one specific temperament — the kind who watches a box travel across a shelf on its flat and then settles it, one paw at a time, toward the edge. The cat does exactly this kind — the original walnut box. The one from 1998 is now wrapped in a strip of felt, which Clive has accepted only in the way that cats accept things, which is to say, by ignoring the felt and continuing to study the box.

There is a rule in the ledger, and the rule is that what the cat does is recorded as it happens, unamended, in the same hand that keeps the rest of the record. The entry under 9 March reads: box moved two inches toward the edge; sphere not present; no loss; Clive satisfied. A box that has survived eight seasons of a show, a change of network, a contract of three episodes and a promotion that was considered and then, in the record, not made, is a box that can survive a cat. The cat, for his part, appears to be conducting a long experiment. The Society is in no hurry to conclude it.

Cross-references, per the archivist
The Society keeps its company where the record does. For the night the wire ran out of chalk while a chamber of fifty seats was counted, and for one notebook line that outlasted the returns, see The Night Wire — WQDR Raleigh, Election Night 2010. For the house rule this newsletter lives by — that an entry stands in the record as found, unamended, ruled around and closed — see The Wanters (1923), Lab Ledger No. 9, where the ruling was first set down beside a frame the cold took. Both pages keep one unexplained line in their own hand; ours is the one under the walnut box, and it is the only one of the three the cat has inspected in person. The third line of the three — the one entered, unruled, at the foot of a 1957 cold-storage register and read word for word into a classifieds column kept by the property office, where a candle beside the 1998 entry is the only light the shelf gets — is posted at The Reagent Exchange — Classified Columns of the Cyclopentenone Board, Column No. 22; the archivist has written to ask whether the cat will inspect it, and has not received an answer.
Cross-references, on the walk named for the mathematician
The Society has read the account of a woman who, in her Tuesday seminar at 725 South Goodwin, Champaign & Urbana, saw what was coming before the administration did — the revocation of her tenured chair at the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign, and the walk that now bears her name, with a bronze plate at its head. It is set down in Mitchell Walk, Basin Street Series, Entry No. 17, a brochure printed without charge on a cream card, and the Society keeps the entry, unamended, in the drawer marked WB.
