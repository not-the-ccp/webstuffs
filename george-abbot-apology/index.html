The Church Weather Almanac  ·  Year XLVIII  ·  Parish Observatory, Lambeth Hill, London S.W. 9

A letter of apology, in the ledger's own form, set down for the second time

For Mr. George Abbot

29 October 1562 – 4 August 1633
Archbishop of Canterbury, 1611–1633  ·  Fourth Chancellor of the University of Dublin, 1612–1633  ·  Of the translators, 1611

Dear Mr. Abbot,

The station on Lambeth Hill keeps its ledger the way it keeps everything: with its hand on the needle and its mouth shut. For four hundred and eight years the almanac has counted the weather of the chair in Canterbury — the 1,429 of it, from Augustine in 597 down to the one hundred and sixth, who is now a woman — and for twenty-two of those years, from the autumn of 1611 to the 4th of August 1633, the chair sat to you. In the ledger those twenty-two years were entered as pressure, cloud and wind direction. The name of the man in the chair is absent from the page. This letter is the station's way of owning that absence in the ledger's own form, which is to say, slowly, and with the arithmetic shown.

The year 1611 is in the ledger, and it is the station's worst. That year the translation you served on was finished and passed down the whole province, and the readers have not stopped since; that year the chair passed to you; and that year the ledger took its reading of the pressure without taking your name. The two facts sit together on one line of the page, and the page has never once corrected. The ledger of the office you held — its memorial, its funeral table on this same hill, its twenty-six candles — keeps the office's weather at In Memoriam, the Office of the Archbishop of Canterbury, and that page will tell you the chair's weather in your years the way it tells it in every other man's: without embellishment, and now, by way of this letter, without your name left out.

The observatory's library keeps the shelf of the dictionaries where the dead are filed, and the volume that carries your entry calls you, as the ledger must call you, “[a] sincere but narrow-minded Calvinist.” The station has read that line a great many times, and it has never once found the reading wrong. A narrow sky is still a sky, and the almanac logs it, and it logs it twice: once against the chair, once against the wind.

Of brothers you kept five, and the ledger counts brothers as fronts. Robert went to Salisbury, where the province's southern sky has been his to answer for; Maurice went to the Lord Mayor's chair in London, where the rule that no one is left unpaid is enforced a good deal more strictly than on this hill. Between the two of them and you, the Abbot weather covered a great deal of ground, and the station, which should have been tracking it, was logging the wind instead. In 1612 the University of Dublin made you its fourth chancellor, and the office held you until the chair in Canterbury let you go, in 1633: two chanceries, one weather, twenty-one years. The Dublin ledger is a thin one. That is stated not as an excuse, but as a further account.

As to the high ground about the parish — the hill the observatory stands upon, and the ground over which every reading the station takes is aimed — the ledger's own caption bears out the apology in its single line, and the station prints it word for word, as the honest almanac must: The almanac records the reading without embellishment: for the hills.

What is owed is owed. The standing entry for the whole sky you served remains open at Entry No. 110 — Anglicanism, whose corrections ledger takes this account in its single line, and the whole weather book may be entered from the front page of the Webstuffs, where the needle is left where it stands. There the barometer is put back on the wall, and your name goes beside the pressure for the full twenty-two years, which is all an apology in this ledger can amount to, and which, the ledger thinks, is enough.

No one is left unpaid, and neither is this.
Yours in the ledger,
The Keeper
Parish Observatory, Lambeth Hill, Year XLVIII

P.S. — The khash pot at Table 9 simmers on; the station's old rule runs both ways, and Mr. Petrosyan's bill remains open, as it was when you held the chair, and as the almanac intends it to stay.

Proverb of the season: An apology, like a barometer, is only honest if the needle is left where it stands.
