Mirandola · Emilia-Romagna · the solstice-night walk, 21 June · begins 8:10 p.m.

The White-Wing Sky Walk

A walking itinerary in nine stops for the questions that the field calls astrobiology — the origin, the distribution, and the evolution of life in the universe — read from a kerosene lamp, a jar of pickles, a court record, and one cake, starting on the porch beside the Secchia and climbing to the hill where the small telescope points at Wadi Hadramawt's sky.

Route: 2.3 km · nine stops · 8:10 p.m. to 10:32 p.m. · Pace: one story per stop · Fee: one story, ending with a moth · Guide: Q. Basman, retired secretary of the Aden Natural History Society, honorary

The walk is free of charge. It is, however, subject to the same condition as the cake, as the jar, and as the lamp: nothing begins before something with white wings has appeared at the light. The group gathers on the rear porch, the kerosene lamp is lit, the guide checks the pharmacy safe — there is no pharmacy safe, and the ritual is the point — and the first story is told, or held. If no moth comes, the walk is wrapped in linen and left for the next night. What follows is Nonna Ersilia's non-negotiable first step.

  1. Stop 10.0 km8:10 p.m.

    The Rear Porch, beside the Secchia

    Via degli Ulivi 9, Mirandola. The lamp is here: the same kerosene lamp the Court of Aden returned to Felix Nori on the second day of the trial, on the finding that the lamp was his but the naming was not. It stands on the porch table with the ledger and the glass vial, re-erected exactly as the harbour-lamp table stood on the wharf in 1962. Basman lights it and waits, the way Felix waited, for ten minutes past eight.

    Field note — origin. A lamp is a point source; a wharf lamp in 1962 is one data point, a porch lamp in Mirandola is a second. The discipline we are walking tonight holds that life on Earth, however it began, is one specimen in the case — observed, waiting to be placed. The white-wing settled on Felix's ledger on 12 October 1962 at 8:10, and the first question of the walk is the first question of the field: how did it happen in the first place, down here, in this kitchen's world, before it happens anywhere else.

  2. Stop 20.2 km8:34 p.m.

    The Pantry — Jar 12·10·62

    We go in through the door. The jar is on the lowest shelf, labelled 12·10·62 in Felix's hand: small round pickling-onions, nasturtiums, capsicums, chilies, &c., dictated to the harbour canteen cook at 8:10 and filled by 8:12. It is still in the pantry, as the clerk's note records, and it is not appealable.

    Field note — brine. Brine is where the chemistry starts, in every account that has survived its critics: salt, heat, a little violence, and time. The salt lakes and deep-sea vents where life's earliest candidates live are the pantry of the planet, and the halophiles that thrive in them eat salt the way this jar's chilies eat it — aggressively, and in public. Basman produces a clean glass and pours no one a pickle, because the jar is an exhibit, not a drink, though one of the group is eating an onion over the edge of the shelf, which is noted and not ruled upon.

  3. Stop 30.4 km8:51 p.m.

    The Kitchen Counter — The Recipe Box

    The card is out of the box: postage-stamp size, browned at the edges, 140 grams of almonds on the face, the unsolved sentence on the reverse. We weigh the almonds at the counter, because the group has brought the small specimen box and the scales, and 140 grams must be shown to be 140 grams even when the box is only a box and the cake is only a story for later. The tin is kept, per the cipher, in pharmacy safe 3.

    Field note — accounting for life. A living cell is a small bookkeeping operation: mass in, mass out, most of the mass being water, most of the information being instructions nobody has read. The smallest cells we know are around two hundred femtograms, which is roughly the weight of one of Nonna Ersilia's flaked almonds divided by four hundred thousand. The discipline prefers to count in those units; the family prefers 140 grams; the walk permits both.

  4. Stop 41.4 km9:12 p.m.

    The Secchia Bank, 1.4 km North

    The walk follows the river to the point the card names: the bank, 1.4 km north, where the Sunday matches ended when the light failed and, according to the sentence, the players collapsed. In fog — and there is fog, because it is June and the river provides — the bank is the whole case: a signal in a noisy channel, a mark that might be a football notebook or might be a cipher or might be a biosignature. We stand with our backs to the water and read the sentence aloud three times, once each, for the three readings.

    Field note — biosignatures. A signature of life, on another world, will look at first exactly like this sentence: consistent, unrepeatable, and resistant to the innocent explanation. River fog for the temperature. Isolation for the resting time. Cabin keeper for the one who minds the pot. The search is not for a loud message but for a small one that survives contact with the obvious — chiral molecules, odd ratios, a chemistry that cannot be explained by the geology. The players collapsed on the bank. Nobody knows. The walk proceeds.

  5. Stop 51.7 km9:30 p.m.

    The Municipal Court — Sala 41

    The courthouse is dark but the door is unlocked, on account of the walk, and Basman — who had the defence table — gives the group a ten-minute hearing from memory in the corridor, gavel replaced by a pickle spoon. The point of the stop is the ruling, which stands: the plaintiff holds an interest in the placing of the white-wing, being the first to observe it, but not in the naming of it, which belongs to the discipline. No taxon is named, renamed, or aggregated without a published account in tenths of a millimetre, and the discipline’s smallest neighbour keeps its placard at Barbeyella minutissima, Museum Placard No. 0114, the stalked slime mould whose sporangia split into lobes only once the spores inside are ready.

    Field note — the naming rule. This is the one procedure of the field that the family already follows, and it applies with equal force at any distance: a Martian microbe, a Jovian brine organism, a lichen on a ring of a planet nobody has named, is nothing — a taxon of one, its own family, its own kingdom, waiting to be told it is not — until it is described, circumscribed, and placed, rung upon rung, the way the wharf is built up, crate upon crate. The Court received the principle in 1964; the universe has been complying ever since, or not at all.

  6. Stop 61.9 km9:48 p.m.

    The Old Customs House, Market Square

    A customs house is where things cross borders, and this is the stop for the crossing: 12 March 2002, when the taxon in suit was formally described as Scythris albocanella by Bengt Å. Bengtsson, from Wadi Hadramawt and the desert fringe east of Shabwa, Yemen. Half a page. Tenths of a millimetres. A drawing of the genitalia that looks, if you squint, like architectural drafting. The vials of Exhibit E went to a museum in Helsinki; the customs house kept its manifest. Felix, who died before the name, would have cleared it through the way he cleared pickles: without a queue.

    Field note — description as travel. A good description is a passport: half a page, exact enough to re-enter the country of the fact with. The field wants the same for anything found beyond Earth, and wants it before the celebration, which is why this stop is in the middle of the walk and not at the end, and why Basman carries a manifest and checks nothing's initials, though everything is his.

  7. Stop 72.1 km10:05 p.m.

    The Town Clock — Ten Minutes Past Eight

    The square's clock tower strikes the hour, and we wait out the ten minutes the whole walk has been pretending not to measure. The moth came at 8:10. The pickle was ready at 8:12, because neither is rushable. The cake is twenty-two minutes, one side of one record, or the length of one story. The court closed at ten minutes past eight. There is a scale of things in this family — a lamp, a jar, a tin, a courtroom — and all of it is timed in small units that a moth would consider generous.

    Field note — the scales of the field. The discipline thinks in the same doubled scale: femtograms and light-years, milliseconds and the half-billion years since the last glacial maximum, the tenth of a millimetre of a drawing and the distance at which a star's light takes twenty-five years to arrive. Twenty-two minutes is the length of one story; a story told to a stranger on a hill is the same length as a record side; a record side, played at the right speed, is the length of a night. The field needs all of these at once.

  8. Stop 82.3 km10:20 p.m.

    The Hill — The Small Telescope

    Colle San Prospero. The family's small telescope is on its bracket, aimed at the sky over Wadi Hadramawt, which is to say aimed, at this hour, at the same direction it has been aimed at every solstice since the hotplate was carried from Suez. The group takes turns at the eyepiece. There is, at present, one star in the scope, and then the Milky Way, and then, for one member of the group, a planet, which she declines to name, on account of the ruling in Stop 5.

    Field note — distribution. The premise of the field, stated flat: life may exist beyond Earth, and the study of how it arose and developed here may inform the search for it elsewhere. The census is long past the era of polite arithmetic — five thousand and change worlds confirmed beyond the sun, and counting, in a galaxy of roughly two hundred billion stars — which is why this stop is the highest on the walk. The future of the field is a distribution problem, and the distribution problem is a looking-up problem, and the hill is two point three kilometres from the porch, which is, at this pace, one story per stop.

  9. Stop 92.3 km, returning10:32 p.m.

    The Porch, Again — The Cake

    Down the hill by the lane, back to the lamp, which has not gone out. The cake has been baked — twenty-two minutes, the top cracked slightly, a skewer clean — and is served on the porch table with black coffee and no plates, the lamp lit and the vial standing beside it. The condition of the Court is met: the cake is eaten with the coffee and the plates are absent, and the story told while eating ends with a moth, which it must, because the walk itself ended with a moth. The jar is not appealed. The linen is folded over the tin.

    Field note — evolution, and what comes next. The third question of the field — the future prospects of life in the universe — is asked at the end of the walk and not answered, which is the only honest way to ask it. Life on Earth evolved from what it had, the way the cake evolves from 140 grams, and the question is whether the same is happening, or will be, or has already happened and is waiting for a description. The walk's answer is the lamp: keep it lit on summer evenings, keep the vial dark, keep the jar in the pantry, and check the safe. If nothing comes, leave the cake for the next night.

What to bring

  • A linen cloth, for the tin and for the next night.
  • Black coffee. No plates, if you can manage it.
  • One story, ending with a moth.
  • Nothing you would claim a name for.

The walk is suspended on nights of river fog so thick the bank disappears, and on nights when the lamp will not light, and on the single night each year when the moth comes at 8:11 instead, which has happened twice, in 1962 and once since, and is not appealable.
