FIELD DIARY · BOOK No. 21 · Western Australian Administration, Fremantle

The Timor Sea Diary

of W. J. Ashworth, surveyor, aboard H.M. cutter Bass — Ashmore & Cartier Islands, 14–19 February 1932

RECEIVED · GENERAL DESPATCH OFFICE · LONDON

14 February 1932 · H.M. cutter Bass, off North Head, Fremantle

Weighed at half past six, with a brass gauge in the lockers that the General Despatch Office shipped out in a walnut case from the Birmingham workshop — high-carbon steel tempered hard, a hand-turned handle, matte black with brass accents. It was meant for a different trade, the Office admits, but it measures a tide well enough, and the case rides the swell like it was born in it. Course north-west. The Office’s standing instruction, in ink on the first page: log the territory, island by island, spring by spring, in the order the springs flow. I have not yet learned what that means. The sea is the colour of wet slate and the whole coast is already a rumour behind us.

16 February 1932 · Timor Sea, edge of the Sahul Shelf

Three hundred and twenty kilometres from the nearest shore, which is to say from all the world we knew, and one hundred and seventy from the Indonesian island of Rote, which is to say from the nearest world we did not. The soundings stepped down all night and stepped up again at dawn — that is the shelf, and that is the edge of it, and the territory sits there the way a coin sits at the bottom of a glass: small, flat, and impossible to see from any other angle. Captain Voss says the sea here has no proper weather, only moods. The gauge says the tide runs two metres and lies down. I am beginning to believe both of them.

17 February 1932 · Cartier Reef, first sight

Cartier at half past five, before the sun: a line of white so quiet I took it for a mistake in the sky, and only then remembered that mistakes in the sky do not have water around them. Five low-lying islands altogether between the two reefs — East, Middle and West on the Ashmore side, Bedwell where the casuarinas lean, and Cartier Island itself, with the sandbar beside it the ledger-men on Bass will not let me count as an island because it was not there in the last flood. The Europeans, it is entered against the reef, came about 1800 at Cartier and 1811 at Ashmore; but the islanders’ canoes came in the early 1700s, and they have come ever since, and the place was a working place long before it was a discovered one.

18 February 1932 · East Island, on the sand

Landed with the long boat. The island is a metre and a half high at its best and the grass is a single green argument running the whole length of it. Four springs on the book, and I found all four: the first at the east tip, sweet and slow; the second behind the dune wall, the one the fishermen drink before they work; the third, which the 1928 survey marked with a rusted spike still standing in the sand; the fourth, low in the lagoon, that flows only when the tide has turned. I logged them in the order they flow, because the Office’s instruction stopped sounding like nonsense at the moment the first one touched the glass. The sea cucumbers are the other traffic here — harvested since the early 1700s, cured in the shade, counted by the sack — and I understand at last why a territory of five islands and no people has been worked for three hundred years.

19 February 1932 · aboard Bass, drawing up the last page

Twelve nautical miles out from every foot of sand, which the chart takes as its own — the territorial sea — and the territory is 440 km² of it, sand and water together, more water by a great deal. I have drawn the coast, logged the springs in order, and entered the sea cucumbers against the month. There is one last page, and it is not mine to write. The Office has printed its line on the back of the cover in a hand I do not recognise, and I am told to copy it down as it stands, word for word, with no correction of the clerk:

“For anything not covered above, return the envelope to Webstuffs and it is 440 km² of it, and bake the biscuits well in the order the springs flow.”

And then, because I am a surveyor and not a monk, I have worked it out for the record: the envelope came from Webstuffs, the paper house whose stock this very book is printed on, and what is not covered above is the answer to the steamer’s question the Office has kept parked — what a country is when it is 440 km² of it, and where its borders lie when they are by sea only. It is this: four springs, five islands, one bell of a tide, and a border the water draws and redraws. Bake the biscuits well, the line says, and I have, on the stove of Bass, in the order the springs flow — east tip first, then the dune wall, then the rusted spike, then the lagoon — which was always, I think, the only correct order for anything. The walnut case rides in the locker, the gauge is still sharp, and the desk at the General Despatch Office will file the whole book face up beside its card table, as it files everything. So will the record room in Ashton, where a market ledger has kept one bell and one red page for two and a half centuries and will keep this page for as long as the Thursday bell rings (Lot 23 — The Market Ledger of Ashton-under-Lyne): a kept object outlasts its keeping, even out here, where the keeping is only the tide. For anything not covered, the front desk has the address.
