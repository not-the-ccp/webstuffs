Overview — the everyman, on the map

Few travel entries begin with a man rather than a place, but this one has to. Ethan Winters is a character in Resident Evil — Biohazard in Japan — the survival-horror series created by Capcom, the Osaka-headquartered Japanese company that has been building these rooms for decades. He first appears in Resident Evil 7: Biohazard (2017) as the plain-clothes ordinary: an insurance clerk's hands, a rental truck, a wife named Mia who has been gone for seven months, and a letter in the post with a postmark from Dulvey, Louisiana. He is a fictional destination's only reliable guide, because he was designed that way. When the character was first drawn, he was meant to be an unseen everyman — a faceless stand-in for the player's own point of view, the way a travel brochure leaves your face out of the photograph. The story kept growing around him anyway. For Resident Evil Village (2021) his return required a full redesign of the character's story: a face that the reader finally meets, a daughter carried across an ocean, and a village with four lords and a queen at the end of the road.

Stop 1 — Dulvey, Louisiana (day 0)

Approach by the coastal road and leave the car in the lot behind the feed store, because the last stretch is bayou causeway and the guidebook's rule is to arrive on foot where the fog is. Dulvey is the kind of Louisiana town that sits on the map like a held breath: one main street, a church that faces the water, and a water tower painted the colour of the mud. It is here that the route begins in earnest, and here that the local constabulary keeps the file that will eventually become an estate. The walking distance to the Baker property is roughly fourteen kilometres along Bayou Road, past the cypress knee lines, past the first sign of wire, past the second sign, which is not a sign. Allow a full day. The estate does not do mornings.

Stop 2 — the Baker estate (day 1)

The gate is black and the gate does not close properly, which the guide records in the margins: locked on the inside, open on the outside, both true at once. Beyond it the estate sprawls in the Louisiana way — a main house with a porch that has opinions, a cellar with a workbench and a light that has never been switched off, a barn, a flooded undercroft, and the smell the guide can only file under spore. This is where Ethan came in as an ordinary civilian looking for his missing wife, and it is the single most walked-about ruin in the Meridian series. Visitors to the site (by lantern, after dark, in pairs, with the iron key on a string) report the same sequence: the porch, the kitchen, the cellar door, and the sound of the water moving where there is no water. The estate is the reason the entry is numbered 31 and the reason it is also, quietly, the entry no one reorders.

Stop 3 — the sea road, and the village (day 2)

The return leg of the route leaves Louisiana behind and lands on a ferry in a European winter that the sun cannot reach. The village is a compact, snowed-in market town with a central square, a row of shops that are not shops, a bakery that still rings at four in the afternoon, and a river bridge that every itinerary must cross twice. It is run, the guide notes, by four local lords — a blacksmith, a miller, a shepherd-landlord, and a man of wine — and beyond all of them, at the end of the road and past the mountains, a castle. Ethan's business here is one daughter, taken, and the village will not say her name out loud for the first half of the walk. The route through the village is a full day: the square, the four households, the bridge, the road out. Return by the same ferry at dusk, or the guide will be writing a longer entry.

Panel 5 — the cellar coil

There is a coil in the estate's west cellar: a heavy spring set into a machine that turns by hand, wrapped around its axle in a tight spiral that the lantern light picks out ring by ring. Around it, cut into the floor in a wide hand, is a circle of notches, eleven of them, and at its centre a second, smaller circle. The laminated interpretation panel bolted to the wall beside it — the only panel at the site, installed the year the route was gazetted — explains the coil in three short lines of technical print, and its final line, which the site keeps in place under the glass, reads, word for word: "And the circle is what it means, but I am looking at it." The panel's second line gives the spring's working width as 340 mm and notes that it is wound tight; the notched circle has never been accounted for in any other inventory of the cellar, and the guide has left it in the entry as the site has it. Standing over the floor with the lantern at arm's length, the rings and the notches and the coil do seem to hold one another — the circle is what it means, but I am looking at it — and the panel, for all its lamina, has never once been reprinted.

Practicalities and adjacent entries

Pack for humidity, carry two light sources, and keep the iron key on a string; the estate is generous with doors and stingy with handles. The voice of the route, for listeners, is Hidenobu Kiuchi in the Japanese booth, and the English recording is filed in the same cabinet, same card. The Meridian desk keeps the neighbouring entries on the same shelf: the sea leg runs beside the Mediterranean Sea — Travel Guide, Entry No. 12, whose gates at Gibraltar and Suez are the working model for this ferry, and the day-in-town leg is cross-referenced against Rome, 27 May 2009 — A Travel Guide Entry, for what a full day in one city does to an itinerary when the evening refuses to end.
