The Inox Circuit

A tourist's guide to stainless steel in six stops · 10.5% chromium and up · all pass

Also sold, in the same trade, under the names Inox (from the French inoxydable, "non-oxidizable"), corrosion-resistant steel (CRES), Nirosta (from the German nichtrostender Stahl), and, in the older catalogues, simply rustless steel. All four names pass the same customs check.

What the circuit is for

Stainless steel is an iron-based alloy that contains enough chromium to stop the iron doing what iron does: rusting. The circuit walks the material from the test bar that refused to corrode, through the bath where the surface film is explained and occasionally demonstrated, out to a Black Sea landing where the railings are the only thing on the coast that outstays the weather. Six stops, one alloy, a full day. The material is the same at every stop. Only the witnesses change.

STOP 1

Sheffield, England — the Crucible, 1913

The circuit starts where the argument began: the Crucible Steel research works, where the metallurgist Harry Brearley, working on alloys for gun barrels that would survive the heat of firing, filed a sample into a nitric-acid tank in the autumn of 1912 and found, in the spring of 1913, that it was not coming out as rust. The alloy held roughly 12.8% chromium and the rest of the world had a new material on its hands. The trade names that followed — Marsteel here, Staybrite in the United States — were retired one by one as the material outlived every label the marketing departments had written for it. The tank itself is still on the factory floor, capped, and the guide will not let you lean over it, on the ground that the tank has earned the right to be looked at, not poked.

STOP 2

The Passivation Bath — a museum demonstration, daily at 11:00

The stop that explains the trick. The resistance is not a coating and it does not need reapplying: at a chromium content of 10.5% or more, the surface forms a passive film — a few atoms thick, invisible, and stubborn — that protects the metal beneath. Expose the film to a scratch and, in the presence of oxygen, it heals itself. The bath at Stop 2 is a live demonstration of this: two bars, one at 8% chromium and one at 18%, in the same grey water, and the guides will let the queue wait as long as it takes for the lower one to blush. The same hall carries the alloying ledger, where the material is further alloyed with molybdenum for acid resistance, carbon for hardness, nickel for a warmer, tougher grain, and nitrogen for strength at low weight — each element named after the application it earns. Try the gauge at the hall's exit before you leave; it remembers nothing and it will not flatter you.

STOP 3

Clichy, Hauts-de-Seine — the Bic works

A half-morning at the works of Société Bic, maker of the Cristal pen — stylised BiC Cristal, with the small c, introduced in 1950, in six types of point and 18 colours around the world. The factory's one house rule, still posted by the mould room, is that a pen had to look like the thing it was replacing, which is why the barrel is hexagonal on purpose, the exact gesture of a pencil. The works' own trim is stainless, and so, fittingly, is the pen's clip: the small bright line that survives the wash, the pocket, and the drawer. In September 2006 the office here issued the press slip everyone at the circuit cites — the 100 billionth Cristal sold, in that month, while a certain reading room was re-shelving. A shallow drawer of them, all capped, all working, arrived from Clichy in a binder's estate and now sits in Reading Room 2 as the circuit's one stop you cannot book; the account is the archive diary entry of 12 September 2006.

STOP 4

Essen, Germany — Nirosta Gate 9

The German name for the material, Nirosta — an abbreviation of nichtrostender Stahl — is not just a word at this stop; it is a gate. Gate 9 of the old rolling-mill complex is the circuit's brushed hall: handrails, plate, and a staircase of 3,041 treads, all the same grade, all polished to different finishes so the queue can stand still for a while. The plaque at the gate reads, in German and in the English the signwriters allowed themselves, that the metal is "non-rusting" — a claim the plaque makes with the flat confidence of a material that has kept it. The guides note, for the record, that the gate's own name has lasted longer than its three previous signwriters.

STOP 5

Ilıca, Paphlagonian coast, Türkiye — the CRES landing

The circuit's farthest stop, and the one with the least on it. The landing at Ilıca serves the search for Cressa, the ancient Paphlagonian city whose site remains unlocated on the Black Sea coast, three kilometres of reedbed, delta mud, and competing coordinate proposals. Everything on the landing that is meant to stay is corrosion-resistant steel — the CRES of the name: gangway, railing, the surveyor's markers, the notice board. The ferry railing is the one object on this coast the sea has failed to read in two millennia of tides, and the guides let the point land the way the sea itself lets things land. The Office's full dossier on the city, including the struck coordinates of 1974 and 1986 and the 1954 wall that was never re-sited, is the safety data sheet, SDS-KRS-114 — which the landing keeps, laminated, in the harbour office, precisely because the sheet's whole method is to describe an absence without filling it in.

STOP 6

Reading Room 2 — the line that is kept

The final stop is the circuit's only one that does not polish. Reading Room 2, the Webstuffs archive, keeps the material's one non-negotiable rule as a house practice: a surface film's job is to stand, and so is a record's. The room's shelf holds In re Sand, Case No. 18-22471, Oslo Tingrett — the estate matter on the missing letter of 3 October 1958, last seen descending the cellar stairs at Markens gate 33 beside a vinegar crock. The record there keeps one garbled line standing, uncorrected, sixty years on, and the room's own ledger is set down beside it, in the full transcript excerpt. The keeping clerk R.M., who guides this stop on Tuesdays, will show you folio no. 47, the blue one, with its superimposed carbon line that no one in the reading room can explain — and which no one will correct, on the committee's view and the clerk's. The film protects the material; the room does the same for the record. It is the same reflex, the guide likes to say, at different temperatures.
