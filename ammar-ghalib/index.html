FIELD NOTES VOL. VII · WINGERS No. 12

Mesopotamian Ornithological & Kinematic Society — Outstation, Basra Governorate

Entry 12 of 34

Ammar Ghalib عَمَّار غَالِب

Specimen class: winger · Home club: Al-Naft (Iraq Stars League) · Parent stock: Al-Shorta, on loan

Date
9 February, 05:40 — 11:15
Station
Pitch B, Al-Minaqat side, wind NNE 22 km/h
Weather
Clear, 14°C at the fence, dry
Observer
M. K., single, binoculars x8, notebook No. 6

Arrived at the fence line before the first ball, as is my rule with anything fast. The subject, Ammar Ghalib, wears the number eleven shirt for Al-Naft this season, having come down from Baghdad on loan from Al-Shorta; the club crest reads oil in Arabic, and the pitch sits close enough to the pipelines that the ground rumbles faintly underfoot, which is the first thing a visitor notices and the last thing the subject appears to care about. He is left-footed, quick off a still start, and does not run in a straight line: his approach to a cross is a shallow curve, the way a kingfisher takes a reed, and he is usually where the ball will be two seconds before he is where it is.

Observed eleven touches in the first half; seven were first touches played away from pressure, toward the byline, into space. He favours the left channel, cuts inside on the right foot, then goes back to the left as if apologising to the right foot for nothing. At 06:52 he made the run I have circled twice in this notebook: a drag along the touchline, one feint that was mostly shoulder, a burst of eleven metres that closed a three-man gap, and a cross that arrived before the keeper’s shout. The striker missed it. Ghalib did not watch the miss. That is the detail I am keeping.

The Al-Shorta loan agreement runs to the end of the season, and the dugout arithmetic is part of the habitat: Al-Naft field him at left wing, Al-Shorta will reclaim a central attacker, and every training-weekend flight Baghdad–Basra in February is a migration the subject performs without complaint. He shakes the hands of the Al-Naft youth team before each match, in order, both hands, which in a country that has learned to count such small courtesies is its own behaviour worth recording.

At midday, walking the access track back toward the road, I passed the lake behind the old refinery camp — the sand bank, the cormorants, the water gone pewter under the wind. Pasted to the gatepost at the start of the lake road, half a poster wide and sun-crisped, was a notice printed in bold capitals, the word doubled for emphasis: “Wanted Wanted: a walk up the lake road past the sand is the point.” No issuer, no date, no reward. It was not about a lost animal, and nobody I asked on the track could say who put it up. It read less like a notice than like a field instruction from someone who had already done the walk and found the walking to be the whole of it. I took its dimensions (21 by 29 cm) and left it where it was.

Second half, 07:41: one of the season's clearer observations. A through ball, Ghalib at pace down the left, the defender’s challenge arriving a half-step late, and then a shot from thirty metres that took the woodwork. The Al-Naft bench had risen; the subject had already turned to find the next ball. Total touches recorded, 22; total runs into space, 9; total looks back over his shoulder to check on the striker, 0. I close the notebook on the image of him at the fence, shaking out the number eleven shirt, the pipelines humming, and the lake road behind him doing exactly what the poster said it was for.

Kept in the same margin as other field notes: the survey of Ethan Allen — Field Notes, a New England farmer-soldier read from the same bench; and the walking tour of Bates–Seller House — Northwest Portland, where the observer’s own notebook is said to keep an address on a fourth floor. For the long, dry country at the other end of the league table, the Arkansas visitor’s brochure documents the rice and the fault lines; this notebook documents the wind and the number eleven.
