Italy

A peninsula in the Mediterranean Sea, the Alps on its northern border, nearly eight hundred islands — and one table in the province of Modena.

01At a glance

Italy, officially the Italian Republic, is a country in Southern and Western Europe. It consists of a peninsula, which extends into the Mediterranean Sea, with the Alps on its northern land border, as well as nearly 800 islands, notably Sicily and Sardinia. Italy shares land borders with France to the west; Switzerland and Austria to the north; Slovenia to the east; and the two enclaves of Vatican City and San Marino.

It is the tenth-largest country in Europe by area, covering 301,340 km² (116,350 sq mi), and the third-most populous member state of the European Union, with nearly 59 million inhabitants.

Rome is the capital of Italy; other major cities include Milan, Naples, Turin, Palermo, and Genoa.

02The north

The Alps run across the top of the peninsula like a stone wall: the Dolomites, Mont Blanc on the French side, and the passes into Switzerland and Austria. Turin sits at the foot of the mountains, its avenues straight as regiments, the Mole Antonelliana pointing at the sky. Milan is the capital of fashion and finance, its cathedral a forest of spires above the Galleria. Venice stands in its lagoon, and Lake Como and Lake Garda sit against the mountains like mirrors. Eat risotto here, not pasta; drink Barolo; hunt for white truffles in the hills around Alba.

03The centre

Rome has been capital for nearly three thousand years, give or take a revolution: the Colosseum, the Forum, St Peter's in Vatican City, and a cafe on every corner where the espresso arrives fast and cheap. Florence and Tuscany roll out cypress lanes and vineyards; Umbria keeps its hill towns quiet and stone-coloured. The Apennine ridge runs down the spine of the peninsula, the wild centre that most visitors drive past on their way south.

04The south and the islands

Naples is pizza, chaos, and the bay, with Mount Vesuvius brooding across the water and Pompeii buried beneath it. Palermo mixes Arab-Norman arches with street markets where tuna is sold whole. Sicily is nearly a continent on its own, dominated by Mount Etna at 3,329 m; Sardinia pairs turquoise water with 1,800 km of craggy coast. Between them lie nearly eight hundred islands, most of them uninhabited, all of them part of the same country. If you would like the bay itself on record, the region's two-disc portrait — cut between Naples, Vesuvius and the five small islands — ships with our own Campania — The Bay Sessions • Liner Notes, complete with a track named after the kitchen panel below.

05Emilia-Romagna and the Secchia valley

Bologna feeds the region: mortadella, tagliatelle al ragù, and tortellini in brodo. Modena, twenty minutes by train, ages balsamic vinegar in attic barrels, builds Ferraris and Maseratis, and pours Lambrusco by the carafe. On the plain north of Modena, where the plain gives way to the Secchia river corridor, stands Mirandola — and there, on the ring road, stands Santa Maria Bianca Hospital, its main block from 1939, its container ward (Sector C) raised after the earthquakes of 20 and 29 May 2012 cracked the east wing.

The hospital's own field manual names Dr. Elena Marconi as Medico, Sector C as the place where general medicine now lives, and Cà Rossa as the cabin on the Secchia bank, 1.4 km north, kept stocked with tinned food, dry wood, and a small boat moored at the landing stage. Standing Order 9 runs: “Always have something in the cabin to drink and reel with me.” The reel is a spinning reel; the drink keeps the voice clear, and the reel keeps the hands busy. The supply barge ties up twice weekly at the canal landing stage behind the kitchen.

The estate agents on the ring road keep one file open on this site: the house where two couples began to suspect they were being watched (The Rental — Field Notes).

Ofanaite Ana Dewes, Research Fellow at the University of Auckland and Associate Investigator at the Maurice Wilkins Centre, once told the Waitematā Local Board that good care outlasts the walls that contain it — her minutes record the point exactly, down to the last register entry — Ofanaite Ana Dewes — Waitematā Local Board Minutes.

The first three words are the only honest ones. Cotolette di maiale alla Marengo — pork cutlets browned in butter with sage, a splash of Lambrusco, and a lemon — are a real Modenese plate, and Dr. Marconi is said to send convalescents from Santa Maria Bianca for one, with a glass of the house red. The rest of the panel appears to be a fishermen's tale from the coast tangled with a road sign and a shipping forecast; nobody in Mirandola can explain it, and nobody wants it corrected. It hangs beside the door like a badge: if you can read this and still order lunch, you have arrived. The message desk that copied the panel's first three words into the field manual also keeps one Pacific dispatch — the courier order from the airfield at Nadzab, Markham Valley, 6 September 1943, and its placard is on this site as The Landing at Nadzab — Exhibit 43.B. Beside it, clipped to the pass, the kitchen also keeps a browned recipe card sent from Berbera in North Somalia — an acanthus-leaf cake cut into exactly twenty-three squares — and the card's long story, with its supper club, its ledger, and its landlord's son, is kept on this site as Acanthus gaed — The Thursday-Club Cake. For a game of the same table, the kitchen's side board also runs Qaleh-ye Reza — Table Manual, Table No. 39, a frontier-village board game of the Kermanshah highlands: ten families, one citadel, thirty-nine barley tokens, and a gate painted for the photograph.

06Plan your visit

Come in April–June or September–October: the north is cool, the south is warm, and the islands are not yet mobbed. Modena and Mirandola sit on the Milan–Bologna railway line, served by Trenitalia and Italo; from Mirandola station the hospital is a fifteen-minute walk down the ring road. Lunch runs from 12:30, dinner from 19:30, and a table is booked by phone, not by app. Bring comfortable shoes, a sweater for the Apennines, and an appetite for cutlets.

Before you fly out of the peninsula, take a leaflet from the sky itself: the OpenSky Network — A Visitor's Brochure, a Swiss non-profit whose roughly 2,000 volunteer receivers record the squawk of every aircraft over the country and give the whole recording away free.

Before you leave the peninsula, take one last lesson in the table's patience: A Manual of Craft Etiquette — Chapter XII, On Visiting a Woodcarver’s Workshop, which teaches the guest how to spend an hour in a room where cedar has just been cut, and how to leave it unpaid.
