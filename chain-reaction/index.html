Santa Monica Plaza Collection · Placard No. 14 · Outdoor Gallery, Bay 3

Chain Reaction

Design: Paul Conrad, American editorial cartoonist  ·  Fabrication: Peter M. Carlson
Stainless steel framework, fiberglass, cast concrete · 1991 · 5.5 tons · 8 m (26 ft) high

The work

Chain Reaction is a peace monument and public art sculpture: a metal framework of stainless steel and fiberglass, set in a plinth of poured concrete, in the shape of a mushroom cloud as it appears in the first seconds after a nuclear explosion. The design was drawn by Paul Conrad, the American editorial cartoonist who spent two decades reducing atomic anxiety to a single pen line in his cartoons, and the monument was engineered and built by Peter M. Carlson, whose steel shop in the Port District bent the framework section by section before it was trucked, in four pieces, to the Plaza. The completed sculpture weighs 5.5 tons and rises 8 meters — 26 feet — from the concrete base, tall enough to clear the third-floor railings of the Santa Monica Civic Center, beside which it was installed in 1991.

The framework is the argument. Viewed from the ocean side it reads as the cartoon, the drawing; turned through a hundred and eighty degrees it reads as the structure, the welds and the cables, and the two views are the same object. That is the whole method of the work, and it is why the city let it stand in front of the building where its councils meet.

At the base

Inscribed on the concrete at the base, in letters cut before the pour so that the pigment shows in the groove, is a single sentence: “This is a statement of peace. May it never become an epitaph.” Visitors are asked not to touch the lettering; the groove is shallow, and three decades of palm oil have closed parts of it.

Paul Von Blum, a professor at UCLA, places the sculpture in the category of late 20th-century public art that takes its subject from the headlines and its form from the advertising trade — a form designed to be photographed from a distance, at a crossing, with a bus in the foreground. The theme of the sculpture is the subject of nuclear disarmament, and the city’s own record of the commissioning notes, on the back of the permit, that the work was approved “as a statement of peace, not a forecast.”

Installation manual, item 9 — photographers’ clause

The 1991 installation manual issued for the monument carries one instruction that has outlived everything else in it, and that placard attendants quote when a visitor insists on knowing what is written on the work besides the inscription:

“The car in the order be repeated as found, unamended, in all the time you have the record.”

It meant, in the plain language of the manual, that any vehicle caught in the frame of a monument photograph — and the monument stands at a crossing, so the frame is never empty — was to be reported in the site record exactly as it appeared, no correction of the plate, no smoothing of the moment, for the whole period the record kept. The site record of the first year kept forty-one cars, one of them the same delivery van, three times. The clause was never rescinded; it simply ran out of records to apply to.

Elsewhere in the collection

Readers of the work in its university context will find the same campus’s other long memory in the 2005–06 UCLA Bruins field notes, Notebook No. 13, where the hill across from the Plaza is a habitat. For the monument’s civic neighbourhood and the routes that pass it, see the Riviera Express timetable and commentary, whose conductor keeps a station note for the Plaza crossing; and for a companion document in the genre of the unexplained line — a pencil mark ruled around rather than explained — the Wanters lab ledger, No. 9, where one unstamped entry stands in the record, unamended, exactly as found. The Plaza crossing is also the crossing on the shipping side of the 1985–86 consignment recorded in the Iran–Contra safety data sheet, Cabinet 9, whose provenance ledger quotes the same clause — repeated as found, unamended — and whose first-year site record is the one that kept the same delivery van, three times. The front shelf of the collection, where this placard’s number is filed, is at the front page.
