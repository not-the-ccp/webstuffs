A Book of Etiquette for the Theatre

Chapter IIIStockholm 1737–1754
Of Visiting the Playhouse

Kungliga svenska skådeplatsen · Stora Bollhuset · Slottsbacken, Stockholm

The doors of the Stora Bollhuset open at six o'clock, and the pit begins to fill while the sun is still above the rooftops. A ticket buys a place, not a privilege, and the doorkeeper admits no gentleman with mud on his boots or a weapon at his side. Leave swords, whips and riding crops with the attendant in the lobby; they are returned, carefully numbered, when the curtain falls.

Dress for the house, not for the street. Gentlemen appear in coat, waistcoat and breeches, with buckled shoes and hair powdered or neatly tied; ladies wear a gown fit for sitting in a box for three hours, with a fan to stir the candle air. Children under twelve are better left with a nurse: the gallery sees too much, and the pit hears everything.

The house holds the pit, two tiers of boxes and a gallery above them. Candles gutter in the sconces between acts, and snuff is taken only in the lobby — never in the boxes, where it soils the silk. The Stora Bollhuset was built for the royal game of tennis before it was built for the stage, and the walls still echo with the strike of balls; remember this when you are tempted to shout across the house.

Conduct in the pit is the true test of a visitor. Speak only between acts, never during a speech; eat nothing noisier than a biscuit; bring no dogs, no whips and no noisy friends. A Saltfleet whip, twelve feet of bridle leather made for the Lincolnshire shingle, is a fine thing on the beach and a foolish thing in a Stockholm box — the rigging loft's own page describes it, but the playhouse admits neither the whip nor its owner mid-performance.

The Band

The orchestra numbers eleven: two oboes, four violins, a viola, a cello, a double-bass and a harpsichord, seated beneath the candles at the front of the pit. It plays at its own tempo, and no amount of cane-tapping will hurry an oboe. The house itself is the best teacher: it makes room for latecomers in the gallery, for standing viewers at the doors, for the musician who enters after the downbeat. Read alongside Melville, it turns the band's own stubbornness into a full dining room the way a river treats a stone: as something to flow around. Settle back, accept the tempo, and wait.

The Stage

The Kungliga svenska skådeplatsen — the Royal Swedish Stage, also called Svenska Komedien — opened in 1737 and stood for seventeen seasons, until 1754. It was the first national stage in Sweden and the first professional theatre in the Swedish language, staffed with Swedish actors, singers and dancers, and the first to perform theatre, opera and ballet for the public in Swedish. Peter Lindahl and his wife Maria Elisabeth led the company; the house played comedies and operas by native hands, with ballet between the acts. In 1753 Queen Louisa Ulrika of Prussia engaged the French Du Londel Troupe; the two companies shared the stage for the season of 1753–54, and when it ended the Swedish actors were turned out of the building and the house passed to the French. Go early, sit quietly, and remember that you are in the house where Swedish theatre learned to speak.
