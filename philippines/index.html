One degree of latitude is 111 km; the country is a little over 18 of them tall, measured with a ferry and patience.

The Philippines, officially the Republic of the Philippines, is an archipelagic country in Southeast Asia. Located in the western Pacific Ocean, it consists of about 7,641 islands, with a total area of about 300,000 square kilometers. The islands are broadly categorized into three main geographical divisions: from north to south, Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao. With a population of over 114 million, it is the world's thirteenth-most-populous country.

The country is bounded by the South China Sea to the west, the Philippine Sea to the east, and the Celebes Sea to the south. It shares maritime borders with Taiwan to the north, Japan to the northeast, Palau to the southeast, Indonesia to the south, and Malaysia to the southwest. Everything else it shares is rivers, channels, and the occasional ferry: the M/V Pili Star crosses from the tip of the country's northernmost big island to Catanduanes in about an hour and forty minutes when the sea is kind, and the seamen of San Miguel Bay will tell you the sea is kind roughly as often as the radiator in the back room above the old bakery runs, which is to say: on Tuesdays, mostly.

1 · The three divisions

Luzon, the northern division, is the largest island of the country and the one that contains, at its southeastern shoulder, the Bicol Region — the land that juts out like the tip of a broad leaf, which is precisely how the class map at Naga National High School draws the province of Camarines Sur, and why a school bus going to see the volcano from a safe distance passes the capital, Pili, on the way. The capital of the whole country, Manila, sits on the western edge of Luzon, across the archipelago from the bay our own town empties into, and a telegram filed in Manila in 1947 about a ferry timetable still travels, carbon-copied, through the club's ledger like the river's: it brings what the Bay forgets.

Visayas, the middle division, is a chain — Panay, Negros, Cebu, Bohol, Leyte, Samar — the kind of place the country's own sailors describe as "an island with an argument about where it ends." Mindanao, the southern division, is a big island with a second small island (Basilan) and a third under negotiation, and it is the region Mr. Dimalipat underlined twice when last year's class wrote "Caraga" for Quezon, because Mindanao is where the mistake goes to live.

2 · The numbers, kept

Seventy-six hundred and forty-one. The count has been recounted, as counts are, at least twice, and the second count is the one the Republic uses, which is how the number comes to appear in every almanac printed in the country, including the small visitor's pamphlets the Naga Listeners' Committee sets off the ledger in the room above the bakery. Area runs about three hundred thousand square kilometers, which is a little over two and a half million square miles of island, channel, and shallows; population passes 114 million, thirteenth in the world, and the census-takers of 1960, 1970, and 1980 all left a note to the one after them that reads, in effect, do not trust the boats. The degree of latitude is 111 kilometers long, the channel is forty minutes of it, and the parallel 13° North where Naga stands cuts the country at a height where the sun crosses the zenith twice a year.

Maritime borders, in the order the old surveying tables print them: Taiwan to the north, Japan to the northeast, Palau to the southeast, Indonesia to the south, Malaysia to the southwest. Each of these is a line on paper; at sea each of them is a heading, and the heading is the ferry master's business.

3 · Surveying a country made of water

A country whose official size is mostly sea has a rule for its surveyors, and the rule is one line long. It appears in the hydrographic instructions that were pasted, carbon copy, into the back of the tide table for San Miguel Bay, and it has been copied from there into the commonplace book of the Naga Listeners' Club at least three times, once by the Committee and twice by Celia M. Reyes in a geography notebook, each time with the names and the capital letters exactly as printed:

"Never lean on the surface of the Earth."

— Hydrographic instruction, appended to the tide table for San Miguel Bay, island of Luzon; copied into the clubhouse ledger with the note "the Committee concurs, for the surface is the tide."

The instruction is not, as it first reads, about geology. It is about the fact that the surface of the Earth, at this latitude, keeps moving: the tide takes the shore down about sixty centimeters and gives it back, the sea level of the South China Sea is a few centimeters higher than the sea level of the Celebes Sea in the same month, and a surveyor who plants a benchmark "on the surface" has planted it on water. The Republic's own tide gauges — Manila, Naga, Cebu, Zamboanga — are the country's quietest witnesses: small stations, one rod, one book, all of them recording the same sentence the instruction says, hour by hour, in centimeters. The Committee's gloss, for visitors only: at 13° North the sea is the only part of the country that keeps its own time, and the only part you should not stand on.

4 · What it is made of

Volcanoes, first. The Philippines sits on the rim of the same great arc that runs from Japan to Indonesia, and the country counts its active volcanoes in the dozen — among them Mayon in Albay, the cone every child of Camarines Sur knows from the school bus and is not allowed to name in a geography paper, and Taal in the middle of Luzon, which the almanacs describe, with local restraint, as "a volcano in a lake in an island." Earthquakes, second, on the schedule of an engine; the country's seismologists keep a list of fault lines that reads like the ferry timetable in reverse: the same routes, the other direction, and occasionally the other way up. Rain, third, and it arrives from the Pacific on the schedule of a monsoon the country simply calls the season. Rice, coconut, banana, abaca, and salt from the flats of the marshes, fourth; the marshes of the fowls at 13°40′ N, where the drying shed gives the naturalist most of her weather for free, are the country's own small sample of everything the entry describes: island, water, birds, and smoke in the right order.

5 · Filing note

This entry was compiled from the class map, the ferry timetable, the tide table, and the clubhouse ledger, in that order, and it is filed in the archive beside its two nearest neighbours: the Grade 10 geography report on the province that contains this entry's main witness, and the Visitor's Brochure for the parallel it stands on. The Committee notes, for the record, that the country is thirteenth in population, seventy-six hundred and forty-first in number of islands, and first in the number of sentences it gives you that you cannot finish. For the verbatim record from which the back-room lines are drawn, see Partido Magsaysay — Excerpt from the Verbatim Record. The entry is also cross-filed, at the Committee's request, with the St. Anne's Square Post, newsletter No. 61 of the 1953 Society for the Study of One College, whose secretary read this entry's rule to the kettle each December on the strength of the ferry timetable the Society once borrowed and has not yet returned. The radiator in the back room, for the last time in this entry, still never works, and the Committee has ruled that this is consistent with the instruction, and with the latitude, and with the country.
