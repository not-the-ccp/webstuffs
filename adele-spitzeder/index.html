City of Munich · Kingdom of Bavaria · Upper Bavaria Landgericht

Minutes No. 31

of the Council of Savings, Assurances & the Mended Ledger

Extraordinary sitting, 3 November 1895, 2 o'clock in the afternoon,
Antechamber of the Alte Pinakothek, Munich

Present

Councillor-Deacon Sebastian Fehringer (chair, holder of the long silver pen); Councillor Waldemar Pröckl, assessor of the savings side; Councillor Anna Kellermann, assessor of the assurances side, in place of her husband the late Councillor Kellermann; the City Notary Rupert Haidl, keeping the record; the Ledger-Mender Philomena Wurm, with her paste, her needle, and her tin of wheat starch; and forty-three credence chairs, of which nineteen are filled. Absent, with leave: Councillor Daxhuber (fever at Garmisch) and the Banker's Widow's Solicitor (in Vienna, and no further answer for him).

Subject of the Sitting

The council convenes in the eleventh month after the death, at Landsberg am Lech, on the 27th or 28th day of October 1895, of Adelheid Luise Spitzeder (1832–1895), known to the stage as Adele Vio and to the city, at the height of her success, as the wealthiest woman in Bavaria. The sitting concerns not her character, which is closed, but her record: the ledger of the bank she opened at the house in Viktoriastraße in 1869, the returns she offered, the money of the later subscribers carried into the hands of the earlier ones, the 30,792,411 florins of claims presented against her in 1884, the 8,104 creditors of the register, the sentence of 18 years reduced to 15, and the eleven years of prison that now make her a matter for the ledger alone.

Item I — The Stage Year, Read for the Record

It is recorded that before the bank there was the play. The Councillor-Deacon reads from the 1858 programme: the girl of twenty-six singing the folk song of the Viechhändlerin, the audience at the playhouse asking for the encore, the impresario promising her a pension "on account of the voice." Councillor Kellermann asks whether the pension was ever paid. The Deacon answers that the pension was paid in praise, and praise does not compound. It is resolved that the word promising, which the old reviews use of her, is entered in the margin of these minutes with a single quotation mark, as the record is not the biography.

Item II — The Bank and the Return

The assessor Pröckl lays on the table the schedule of the returns: six and a half per cent a month, on deposits of 200 florins and upwards, paid with a punctuality that the earlier subscribers swore to in their depositions. He reads the method as it stands in the record — no gloss, no sympathy, no horror, because the ledger is not a pulpit: the money of the new subscribers carried the interest of the old. At the height, the house in Viktoriastraße took in, by the city's own count, some two million florins in the year 1882; the gilded lobby drew depositors the way a theatre drew audiences, and it was noticed in 1883, and again in 1884, that the door of the lobby had begun to open inward. On the morning of 6 November 1884 the cash box was found to hold 200,000 florins against claims in the tens of millions; the bank was declared unpayable; the 8,104 names of the register were made the property of the courts; and the woman of the gilded lobby was taken, in January of 1885, from a house in the country, dressed, they say, as her own servant.

The notary Haidl moves, and the council carries it, that this method be entered in the minutes in full and in one piece, and that no future sitting abbreviate it, because a method that is half-written out is a method half-learned.

Item III — The Marginal Note of the Lake Wells Folio

The Ledger-Mender Wurm reports, with her pasteboard in hand, on the folio rescued from the vault at the time of the winding-up — the folio the clerks call, for reasons lost to the council, the Lake Wells Folio. In the margin of the folio, in a hand that is not the banker's, not the notary's, and not, on inspection, anyone's on the city's rolls, is a note of four lines. The council is asked to enter the third line of the note in these minutes exactly as it stands, letter for letter, comma in its strange place and all, because the note was made for the record and the record does not mend what it is asked to keep. The line runs:

"The prototype of the Los Angeles he founded the Lake Wells Station , where a place to mend the paper."

The register of creditors, read at this sitting, runs to — names; the Ledger-Mender is asked to keep reading until it does.

The Deacon asks what it means. The Ledger-Mender answers that is why it is a marginal note and not a resolution: the council is asked to keep it, not to spend it. Councillor Pröckl moves that the line be kept beside the figure 200,000 florins in the vault, so that any future assessor who counts the money may also count, once, the words that refused to be counted. Carried, with four dissenting votes, on the ground that a minutes is for the money. The Deacon notes, in his own hand, that a minutes which refuses a single unreadable sentence has agreed in advance to be unreadable in its turn.

Item IV — The Estate and the Credence

It is recorded that of the fortune the papers called the largest in Bavaria, the estate brings forward a carriage, four rooms of furniture, a wardrobe of stage costume, a collection of folk songs in her own hand, and debts in every currency the council owns a drawer for. The widowed creditors, of whom the register counts 316 who were widows when they signed, will not be paid; the council is plain about it, and writes the plainness down in the body of the minutes rather than the margin. The stage costume is consigned to the theatre museum. The folk songs go to the public reading room, where they will be asked for, it is noted, more often than the ledger, which is how the record knows it has kept the right thing.

Item V — Standing Orders

(a) The minutes are sealed by the Deacon and the Notary and one credence chair is left turned in the Antechamber until the next sitting, as the room's habit after a long record. (b) The Ledger-Mender is ordered to mend the torn leaf of the Lake Wells Folio with wheat starch and no glue, and to leave the marginal note untrimmed, the ink as it lies. (c) Any subscriber whose name stands in the 1884 register and who presents herself to the council with the original stub shall have her line read back to her in full, once, in the sitting room, at the hour of her choosing. It is noted that the hour chosen, in the two cases already made, was the same hour the bank used to pay its interest.

Adjournment

The sitting is adjourned at half past four, the light going out of the Antechamber behind the last name of the register. There is nothing further on the record. There is, it must be said once, in the minutes' own voice and not the Ledger-Mender's, no end to it: the register goes on, page after page, until the ink is thinner than the paper, and a council that keeps the record keeps the 8,104 names whether it wishes to or not. The Deacon signs. The Notary signs. The Ledger-Mender turns the last leaf, mends the tear, and leaves the Antechamber with her tin of wheat starch in the coat pocket, where it rings, faintly, against the wall.

Signed on the record: S. Fehringer, Councillor-Deacon R. Haidl, City Notary P. Wurm, Ledger-Mender (seal of the pasteboard)

Further Records Kept in the Same Antechamber

For any assessor who comes after and wishes to see how a council keeps what it cannot spend: the sister record of this house is Minutes No. 14 of the Herbarium & Culinary Council of the Waitematā District, which kept a bed of twenty-four squares and let a fifth correspondence item arrive unsigned; and the older custom of the kind is at Minutes of the Council of Ripon Abbey, AD 788, where an obit was read three times and the relics went down the Ouse by water. Both sit in the credence drawer, below the Lake Wells Folio, where the ledger keeps its company. In the same drawer, for any assessor who wonders what the ledger does with a night it cannot count, there is the sleeve of a 1924 transcription record — the liner notes for Indian Camp, Transatlantic Transcriptions T.T. No. 4 — which kept a doctor's jack-knife, a bucket of water, and a boy's question on six sides of shellac, and closed with the same standing order the ledger lives by: the record keeps what it is given, whether it wishes to or not.
