Nautical Mile

noun · unit of length · 1,852 metres · also a course, a screen, and a schedule

Nautical mile is the dream of a distance that was once meant to be a minute of sky and ended up being a number. Historically it was the meridian arc length corresponding to one minute — one sixtieth of a degree — of latitude at the equator, so that Earth's polar circumference comes to very near 21,600 nautical miles, which is 60 minutes times 360 degrees, and the dream has counted them out that way, once, all the way around, and the count was right. Today the international nautical mile is defined as exactly 1,852 metres — about 6,076 feet, or 1.151 of the other mile, the one the dreamer's mother used for the garden and never trusted at sea. The nautical mile is not part of the International System of Units, and in the dream this is the point, not the flaw: it is a unit kept standing by use the way a date keeps a house standing, and it is still in common use globally in air, marine, and space contexts, and for the definition of territorial waters, which is why in the dream it appears wherever a border or a schedule is being drawn. Its derived unit of speed is the knot, one nautical mile per hour, and in the dream nothing moves at anything but knots — the tide, the waiter in the waistcoat, the fog, the whole island of Entry 717 — exactly one.

Interpretation

The dream brings the mile to Oberrender, the twenty-three cover dining room above Dry Dock 3 in New London, Connecticut, named for the John C. Butler–class destroyer escort USS Oberrender (DE-344) and its two years of convoy escort duty in the Pacific (Oberrender — Restaurant Review). On the deck, close range was 185.2 metres, a cable, a tenth of the mile, and the Guadalcanal chowder's last second of sherry is still defended the way the crew defended her guns — briefly, loudly, and at close range, which in the dream is a tenth of a mile and never less. Whitcomb's father was the messman on a sister ship of the same class, and his service record in the biscuit tin under the sink is written in fathoms and cables, and the dream reads it like a menu: the whole record is a course clock, and the single seating at seven is the one service the ship never ran twice. The bell tower runs its classes on the same intervals, and the dream has measured the walk from the dock to the door and it is 1,852 steps, which it finds to be the correct number of steps and will not be moved on.

The mile meets its predecessor, Entry 717 (Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 717), on the third night. The 1852 plaque on the white two-and-a-half storey house with four square columns turns out to have been a distance all along, not a year: exactly 1,852 metres from the reef's row-locks, which is how the house stands on the beach and lets the date stand in all but certain from them. In the dream's logic the house was indeed built around 1852, the reef holds at 1,852 metres, and the date and the distance are the same number kept in different handwriting. The eighty-two days of 1 April to 22 June run at one knot, the length of one seating, and the ice in the water, which was never meant to melt, was meant to be broken into metres.

On the fourth night the dreamer finds a tide-table almanac in the dream kitchen, its pages numbered like chapters and its title page stamped in faded brown with a single word: Ingin. The dreamer has not been able to say whether Ingin is the keeper's name or a port on the other coast, and Mr. Ferber, who will correct everything else, will not correct this. The almanac's last page is numbered 67. It holds the table of the tide at the row-locks, and under the table, in the faded ink, is a single line, and the line reads: “Then it was meant to be one dam Ingin.” CHAPTER 67. In the dream's logic the line is the mile's own correction: before the 1,852 was printed, the keeper had been meant to settle on a shorter unit, one dam, the domestic one, the garden one, and the stamp on the title page is the keeper's mark that the longer number won. The chapter closes at 67 and does not open again, which is how the dream handles a definition that is already exact: by ending the page.

Associated imagery

  • 21,600 nautical miles round the pole, counted 60 to each degree, 60 minutes to each one
  • The 1,852 on the plaque, and the 1,852 metres from the row-locks, the same number in different hands
  • The knot, one nautical mile per hour; the tide, the waiter, and the fog all at exactly one
  • The cable, 185.2 metres, a tenth of the mile, the measure of close range on deck
  • DE-344's escort screen drawn in fathoms on a provisioning chart, the sherry at a tenth of a mile
  • One service without a second time, called by a bell at seven, 1,852 steps from the door
  • The tide-table almanac with the Ingin stamp and the last page numbered 67

On record

“I was measuring the reef to the white house and it came to 1,852, which is what the plaque said, and the plaque was not a year, and the tide was moving at one knot, and the last page of the almanac was 67, and I could not tell you if Ingin was a name or a port.”

Comparison and conclusion

The mile is the dream's way of giving the number 1,852 to everything at once and then refusing to say it twice. It is not SI, and it does not need to be: air, marine, and space, and the territorial waters that the chart draws in it, all keep standing on use the way the house keeps standing on the plaque. If a border walks through your sleep drawn in exact metres, open the tide table, find the row-locks, count 60 to the degree and 360 to the turn, and take the 1,852. The mile's neighbour in the other dictionary — the tall, soft-spoken man in the khaki necktie who keeps a training center for twenty years — is filed at Goodman, E. Urner — The Pocket Dictionary of Dreams, Entry G-13, and the two entries have been known to arrive in the same night, which is the dream's one small indulgence. The front desk of the class miscellany files this entry at Webstuffs, the class miscellany, beside 717, where the row-locks keep it and the bell does not ring again.
