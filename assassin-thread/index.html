I have published my letter to Le Sahel and now I am left with the word, which I did not want to put in the letter. It is assassination. I want the board to set it down properly, because the plain version is going to be fought over. A killing of this kind is the willful killing, by a sudden or secret attack, of a person — especially a prominent or important one — typically for political, social, or ideological reasons. It may be ordered by an individual or by an organization, and carried out by the one who ordered it or by hired accomplices. Acts of it have been performed since ancient times, so we are not inventing a category; we are only this week in it. The person who carries it out is called an assassin, and that word is the one I could not set in type on page 3.

The facts of our own, which I will not repeat at length because they are in the letter: the courtyard of the palace, about 02:40 on the morning of 4 April, a company of the Republican Guard under Lieutenant-Colonel Daouda Mamadou Dan Gassem, a man found in his office reading a document, who rose to meet the intruders and did not get to his feet. The radio went to static at the same moment. I keep the set on. My question to the board is narrow: what do we owe the record of a killing that is not a duel, not a battle and not an accident — only a door opened on the wrong side?

Quote from: mrs_kovac, earlier this morning

“The hour is an affair of the wall.”

— which is to say: the clock is not the point, and neither, I am beginning to think, is the courtyard.

Post #2 — 10 April 1999, 07:52 (edited 08:05)

I came here to answer, not to console. But the parcel post brought us a copy of your letter last night, and it made the whole kitchen quiet in a way I had not expected, so I will say this first: the stew left the pan on the hour of the table it is for, as always, and as always it was 37 seconds early by the true reckoning. The placard in Cabinet 9 is the only place that has to be exact, and it is exact. You do not need it explained again.

Now to the line. On the back of our menu, sewn up with the forcemeat, in the brown ink that behaves as though a later hand wrote it, there is a second line the curator has not dated. The municipal office has stopped trying. The line is this, and I am setting it down word for word because the board asked for the word for word:

“The sewn-up lamb stew of the stomach, and allow the escape of the tin.”

People on the board have taken it for poetry, for a cipher, for a funeral message, which in this thread I will not argue against. It is kitchen talk. In our jargon the stomach is the stewpan itself — sana, the pan that has the name of this kitchen on it — and to sew the lamb stew up is to close the lid and bind the wire, the way we close anything that is going a long way and must arrive whole. And the tin is the can-lid, the flat lid we lay over the pan before the last quarter, because a stew that is fully sealed overcooks its own bones. Allow the escape of the tin means: lift the lid at the appointed moment, let what has to come out come out, and the stew is the stew. A stew that is sewn up and never opened is not stew any longer; it is a corpse in a pot. I do not say that to be cruel. I say it because the board is arguing about the word, and the word in the kitchen is the same word: something is closed, on purpose, at an hour, by a hand, and then someone has to open it, or the whole thing keeps going without anyone.

Post #3 — 10 April 1999, 08:14

Post #2 is the best cooking I have read in ten years, and I will defend it against poetry. On the record: you asked what we owe it, so here is the ledger, short, because the long one takes two books. On the Ides of March in the year 44 before the common era, Julius Caesar was struck down in the Theatre of Pompey by a company of senators; twenty-three wounds by the usual count, and one of them was a friend, which is the detail the record always comes back to — the closed door is opened by one of the people inside it. Cicero spent years writing it down, and that is the first time the keeping was separated from the doing. The category survives on the doing and the keeping together, in that order. Since then the board could fill a shelf: the orders, the accomplices, the hired hands, the men who rise to meet the intruders and do not get to their feet. The old ones are the same as ours except for the courtyard. Ours is smaller, and that is what makes it legible.

One correction to Post #1, gently: what happened on 27 January 1996 was not a killing. It was the closing, in the open, of a constitution. This is the difference this thread is really about. An assassination closes a door on a man; a coup closes a door on a republic and leaves the man inside to turn the key. He did the coup first. The door did not forgive the order, but the record should not pretend the door owed him nothing.

Desk note, pinned. The thread is read in full at the table on Saturday, which is the way we handle anything the record wants kept. The line from the back of the menu — the sewn-up lamb stew of the stomach, and allow the escape of the tin — goes to the curator at Gornje Vukovsko with this thread as its witness, and it stays in brown ink, undated, as every undated line in this valley stays. One house rule, restated for the readers who joined after the first: a thread on the Records of the Dead is closed only by silence, and it is kept by the wall, and it leaves the pan on the hour it is for.

Somewhere on the sister boards the same habit already lives — the Scrub Board thread on the fairywren sets its nests down the same way this thread sets its names down, one line at a time, nobody loud. We are a small network of desks, and we keep the same ledger.
