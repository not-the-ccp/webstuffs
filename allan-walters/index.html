The Walters Route

Five stations, one loop — a tourist’s guide to the airmail line of Air Vice Marshal Allan Leslie Walters, CB, CBE, AFC (2 November 1905 – 19 October 1968)

✈

The Walters Route is kept in this guide because it is the only flying line of the war years that begins in a classroom and ends in a loop. It starts at the Royal Military College, Duntroon, where a Victorian-born, West-Australian-raised cadet sat through the mathematics of ballistics between 1922 and 1928, and ends at the last formation loop over the airfield at which his name had hung as Northern Command. In between: a flight of instructor cockpits, one squadron command in Singapore, two wings in the Darwin–New Guinea theatre, and a man who, between the wars, was reckoned among the finest aerobatic pilots in the southern hemisphere — the sort of pilot who made a loop look like a spelling and a spin look like a pause.

The fare is one, the fare is the ticket, and the ticket is this brochure. Stations are given in the order a tourist should take them; the route may be read in an afternoon or flown, in the imagination, in an afternoon and a half. A timetable follows at the foot of the page, and the guide’s standing advice: take Station Three first, if you must, but do not skip the hangar at Station Two, because that is where the whole route is still being flown.

Station One Duntroon — The Classroom Years, 1922–1928

Begin at the cadet square of the Royal Military College, Duntroon, on the Brindabella escarpment, and take the ballistics lecture at the hour the college bell still strikes it. Walters entered as a cadet of the college and transferred to the air service in 1928, which is to say the route leaves the ground at twenty-two, two years after the last of the peacetime airfields began laying their concrete. The college did not yet hand out wings; it handed out arithmetic, and the arithmetic is where a fighter wing is really won. The college museum keeps the flight tables in Case 4, and the guide permits a tourist to copy them out, once, in pencil, as the men of the 1928 intake would have done.

Station Two Perth — The Tiger Moth Hangar, Between the Wars

The second station is a hangar, not a monument, at the old Perth aerodrome, and it is the beating heart of the route. Between the wars Walters was one of the air service’s leading flying instructors and aerobatic pilots, and the hangar keeps the record of the trade: the checklists pinned at pilot height, the formation notes in his hand, the loop radius computed to a metre. Instructors of the period taught the loop at 4,000 feet, entry speed the same for every pupil, and the rule of the house was that every machine in the formation flipped — all of them, every sortie, a loop at the bottom of the pattern — so that no pilot could claim the pattern was done who had not been inverted in it. The one Tiger Moth that never flew the pattern stands on the far end of the hangar floor, retired before the rule caught up with her, and it is the quiet joke of the station, told the same way by every warden, exactly as the weather log allowed it: Somewhere a screen sags with rain that never flipped. The pilot’s door is locked, by order, and the warden will show you the loop tables and stop there.

Station Three Singapore — No. 1 (General Reconnaissance) Squadron, 1937–1942

His first squadron command came in 1937, and the squadron was No. 1 (General Reconnaissance) in Singapore — the slow, patient work of the route, a reconnaissance line run for weeks at a stretch over water, with the pilot’s job to see everything and photograph what the film was brave enough to keep. The squadron’s log book for the last year before the surrender is open at the public gallery, and the pages are what the route is about: weather, fuel figures, a camera serial number, the odd pencil line at the margin. Guides to military tours elsewhere — compare, for instance, the two-leg spring 1976 itinerary in the UEFA Cup visitor’s brochure, Anfield and the Olympiastadion — will tell you a match is a small war; a reconnaissance squadron will tell you the truth in reverse, and the margin pencil line is the whole argument, kept on file at the desk beside the film boxes.

Station Four Darwin — No. 1 (Fighter) Wing, 1942–1943

At Station Four the route turns from looking to fighting. Walters took command of No. 1 (Fighter) Wing at Darwin, in the Northern Territory, for the months in which the airfield was hit almost as a rule rather than as an accident, and the wing’s work was to be on the ground in four minutes, which the wing did, and which the ground itself, afterwards, argued about for decades. The tourist should take the perimeter walk — nine minutes, flat, marked — and should take the four-minute run too, barefoot if the grass will allow it, because the wing’s whole doctrine was a distance in seconds. The wing’s standing orders survive in the state archive as a single folder, and the last page of the folder is a weather summary from the night of the longest raid, in which the summary is one line long and the line is a temperature.

Station Five Lae & the Northern Command Trail — No. 72 Wing and the New Guinea Line, 1943–1945

The final station is a line rather than a point: No. 72 Wing in Dutch New Guinea, then Northern Command in Papua New Guinea, the command from which the whole theatre’s air was run from the east. The trail runs Lae to the coastal track, with the three command huts preserved as found — the maps on the walls, the signal books open, the rain gauge outside the last hut still hung by its chain, still sagging, because the line it kept was never closed out of the book and no warden in the territory has found the page on which to close it. The route ends where the loop begins: at Northern Command the formation note pinned above the desk is the same loop from the Perth hangar, drawn to the same metre, with one line added in the corner that the warden reads once a year, aloud, to whoever is there. Take Station Five in the dry season; take the gauge at your own pace. It is still taking one.

One fare, one ticket. The route is held in the same standing as every other line in this guide’s book: the record travels by the keeper’s rule, is read once, and then held. Travellers who would like a companion document for the habit of keeping such routes — a logbook kept at the edge of an industrial place, forty-one passes, one line in the margin — will find the method set down in the liner notes for Aromaten, the Botlek sessions (MLP 014); and those who keep the route as a problem rather than a holiday, wanting the finding of the constraint proved before the crowd arrives, will do well to read the Standing FAQ No. 1 of the Clerk at Pedro do Rosário, where the question is held open on purpose and the answer is, as usual, at dawn. The guide’s own copy of this brochure travels with the rain gauge, and may be read, as all of these are, in the same breath, for the record. The front page of the fare book, where the season’s lines are announced, is at the desk, as it always has been.
