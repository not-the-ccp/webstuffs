Chapter IX
Of the Rolling Stone & the Reader Who Unrolls It

“The stone that rolls gathers no moss; the magazine gathers its own.”

Rolling Stone is a name, and being a name, it comes before the magazine it has come to mean. It is an old proverb, the one readers have for half a century quoted back to its owners as if it were scripture — the stone that rolls, it said, gathers no moss — and the name carried that line into San Francisco, California, in 1967, when the publisher Jann Wenner and the music critic Ralph J. Gleason set it on the masthead of an American monthly magazine that focuses on music, politics, and popular culture. The Stones had already sung the line into a song in 1964, which makes the song older on the radio than the magazine is on the shelf. The etiquette the book requires is simple: a name that has outgrown its proverb must be greeted as a person, not a thing, and the reader who unrolls the magazine owes it the courtesy of reading it before he laughs at it.

II. Of the First Issue and the Cover

The first magazine was released in the spring of 1967, and it featured John Lennon on the cover, with the interview taken by the man who had only just invented the cover, who was twenty-three years old and had run a letter about skateboarding from the same desk before he ran one about everything. The first issue is the copy this book seats first. The reader who owns one is advised to keep it flat, not folded, and to let no member of the household use it as a doorstop, which is the oldest sin in the chapter and the one the clerk is least willing to forgive in a second hearing. The magazine was described from its early pages as left-leaning and liberal, and the book rules that this is a fact about the table and not an argument about it: a reader of another persuasion may sit down, and he may even enjoy the meal, but he will not get up to rearrange the chairs while the meal is being served.

It was first known for its coverage of rock music and for its political reporting, and the most famous of the political reporters to sit at its table was Hunter S. Thompson, who wrote for the magazine with a typewriter the size of a door and a supply of his own that the postman had learned to fear. The standing rule for the reader, as entered in the margin by the clerk, reads: do not quote a Thompson sentence back into a room that has not read it, because the sentence is longer than the quote, and the room will hear the difference within the hour.

III. Of the Drift and the Return

In the late 1990s the magazine broadened, and turned its face to a younger readership interested in youth-oriented television shows, film actors, and popular music, and the household is not asked to pretend that the drift did not happen. The manners the book requires of that decade are the manners of the guest who has overstayed: the house acknowledges him at the door, the house does not keep a grudge ledger, and the house sets no fewer places at the table. The magazine has since returned to its traditional mix of content, including music, entertainment, and politics, which is to say that a name that can find its own table again has, by the rule of this book, manners of its own, and is to be ranked accordingly — above the stone, below the reader who still reads it every month, and level with the moat.

IV. Of the Storeroom, Austin, and the Register

The book owes the name a register, and the register comes out of a February night in Austin. One of the eight men who kept the painted canvas GIMME THE LOOT through the dead-cold night in the crew storeroom on South Congress Avenue — the night the temperature dropped to 11°F and the pipes froze at the first hour — brought down with his blanket a folded first issue of Rolling Stone, the one with Lennon on the cover, from a shelf of his own keeping at home, and set it on the workbench beside the ochre-and-storm-blue banner so that the word and the magazine could share the same low heater. The eight children drew their moat around the corner of the canvas in the carpenter’s pencil, and the magazine was folded inside the moat. At 6:40 in the morning the issue was unrolled against the wall to check the pages. The spine had not cracked, and the cover had not lifted. It also reads, unrolled, like the survey boxes of Lot 9 — Singleton and Cocking Tunnels: Two Bores, One Winter, and a single pass, none of them going home empty-handed. The whole night is entered in the lost-and-found register at Found — a painted canvas, six feet by four — Lost & Found, Austin, with the ticket stub still riding the backing board and the moat still holding the corner honest.

V. Of the See-Also

See, of the manner of a name that bears a table, Chapter IV · Of the Surname Abell and the Table That Bears It, the chapter of this same book in which the wings are hung from the blackboard and the loop is paid for in January. See, of the boxes the unrolled issue reads like, Lot 9 — Singleton and Cocking Tunnels: Two Bores, One Winter, and a Count, the Southern Branch catalogue against which the storeroom night is measured. See, of the film the banner belonged to, the entry Comedy Film — Entry No. 412, The House Encyclopedia of the Moving Picture, where the 11°F night is logged under Condition and Provenance and the 1864 seal is ruled intact. The household archive stands at the front of the house, shelf nine, beside the canvas, beside the stub, beside the magazine, which is not a stone at all, and never was, which is the whole of its manners.

— Entered in the fifth impression, in the storeroom above South Congress, the heater running low, the moat around the corner holding. —
