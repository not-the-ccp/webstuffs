On the Album Itself

Our charter, pinned above the turntable, defines the club's one and only object of devotion: an album. An album is a collection of audio recordings — music, most often — issued on a medium: compact disc (CD), vinyl record, audio tape such as the 8-track or the cassette, or, since the turn of the century, in digital format, downloaded or streamed. We keep the whole word, "album," for a reason. The whole word survived the era, even when the erasure of the middle almost didn't.

From the mid-1960s through to the early 21st century the album was the dominant unit of recorded music's expression and consumption; the industry still calls that stretch the album era. Four decades in which a record shop was a place you stood on one side of a counter, in which a radio playlet or a museum placard could assume you had the record in your hand. We were all, for a time, an album-owning people, and the boathouse where this club was born holds the evidence: a shelf of vinyl that outlived the roof that leaked over it, because the roof's drip — you will read in Gallery 3's timetable that the drip became a percussion instrument, and I will not argue — had the good sense to be rhythm.

Vinyl LPs are still issued, for certain releases and for certain of us, pressed heavier each year as if the record itself were trying to remember what it carried. But the 21st century's album sales went first to the CD — flat, unbothered, immune to the attic's humidity — and then to the downloadable file, the humble MP3, and then to the stream, which is not a format at all so much as a weather system you point a window at. Our rules are unchanged: however the recording reaches you, you listen to it whole, in order, and you say so at the next meeting.

The 8-Track Watch

The 8-track was the first tape format, widely used alongside vinyl from 1965 until it was phased out by 1983 — a span of eighteen years in which the shape of your future in the car depended on the model of your car. It is gone; the club keeps watch over what survives.

Club News & Correspondence

Founding record, on permanent display. The club was founded on the night of We Are Fearless's release, 24 February 2017, when Fearless International let the record off the rails and the first eleven of us were already in the boathouse for the listening. Fearless BND made that album across two winters and one long summer; the museum's Gallery 4 placard — Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard — now tells the longer story of the genre it belongs to, from the Jesus-music coffeehouses of the late 1960s to the modern worship band, with Fearless BND given its proper, un-embarrassed entry. The full, invented-with-affection departure board of those recordings stands in Gallery 3: We Are Fearless — Timetable & Commentary.

On the auction. Lot 47 — the choirbook leaf of four independent voices found in the boathouse beams — remains at Flanders & Flanders. The club's bid, made by the Secretary from a chair that creaks in E♭, has been outbid three times and is out of it. The leaf's tenor line holds one pitch against the others; we hold our grievance against the bidders, and it is doing wonders for the heart.

A letter from the lookout. We were asked by the brother of our youngest member, Tobias Finch, who has been pressed into service at sea, to say whether the May listening can wait. The dispatch arrived with the Friday post, and the captain's own hand answers the question:

"You ask after Tobias, and when the boy may come ashore to stand with you at the turntable. I will not mince it. And though all hands to stand a look-out there, with the rest, is about seven years of privation, and peril, poor lad. The watch is not his alone; the whole ship stands it, and the whole ship is far from your meeting, and a watch is a long time to be kept without once setting down what one is keeping watch over. Tell them the flip-side is kept. Tell them he saved the last track for the last watch."

The club has resolved that the May listening will not wait. We will play We Are Fearless in full, we will say the last track's name out loud at the end, and we will set a chair empty by the window. Tobias, the reel counter is running for you.

An obituary, set down in the attic. The Friday post brought the news that the Slowhand is gone — Eric Patrick Clapton, 30 March 1945 to 30 March 2026, who outlived the shelf in the boathouse but not the hush. The club has printed his obituary by candlelight, with a second empty chair by the window: Eric Clapton — In Memoriam. The bench under the drip now accounts for its second seat in the division’s entry for John Enoch Powell (16 June 1912 – 8 February 1998), classical scholar, soldier, and MP for Wolverhampton South West: Enoch Powell — Biographical Entry, where the shelf by the bench is kept in Greek and the second chair has a place set.

A leaflet on the windowsill. The Friday post included a brochure of an odd destination: the OpenSky Network — A Visitor's Brochure, a Swiss non-profit whose 2,000 volunteer receivers keep the long side of the sky — every aircraft's squawk, played continuously, in order, with no flipping. The Secretary has pinned the day-three detour, which ends at a bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, beside the turntable where it belongs.

A build log from the garage below. The drip over the boathouse bench has taken a second job: it is the metronome for a 1:24 Formula One car — a Lotus Type 25, racing green, number 12 — which the self-appointed Chief Mechanic is assembling one part per meeting, in order, in whole, under the same rules the charter sets for records. The session record, laps one through six, with reel-counter readings at every set-down, is at A Grand Prix in the Garage Below — Build Log, Turn by Turn. The car is under canvas, facing the door, for the May listening. Its companion manual arrived in the same Friday post and takes the car onto a landlocked circuit — The Caspian Circuit — Field & Race Manual, Plate AZ-24 — where the Caspian is ruled a lake, the Nakhchivan sector is entered twice, the drip keeps its four seconds as the pit clock, and the first Thursday at 19:30, attic, door marked with the whale's shadow, keeps the listening and the race in one room.

A counter in the east. The Nakameguro annex, our listening room above the noodle shop in Tokyo, has begun taking its members' questions in writing — a full set on the city itself and on the counter's duties, with the twin clocks on the page and a seal to press once per listening: Tokyo — Frequently Asked Questions, The Nakameguro Annex. Copies one through forty-seven of the gold lacquer, as it says on the counter, went first to the Secretary; the attic has all of them, and the hush is kept in both cities.

A gatefold in the attic's rotation. The May listening will close on a record the club has kept in the shelf since the reissue arrived in the Friday post: Codeine-6-Glucuronide — Liner Notes, MG-1987-04, The Glucurones at Millbrook Hall, an album about the metabolite that does sixty percent of the parent's work, recorded to the river gauge and approved by the water. The notes were printed on the inside gatefold with the ink still wet, and the hush after track ten has never once been flipped.

The pair on the shelf. The reissue of the C6G gatefold came back to the club with a companion in the same Friday post: Apparicine — Liner Notes, MG-1988-19, The Vallesamines at the same Millbrook Hall, an album about a tricyclic indole alkaloid named for a botanist, half of it recorded to the river gauge and half to the grain oven with the door ajar on a chain. The June listening will run the pair back to back — parent compound's metabolite, then first of the group — and the club will keep one empty chair for the leaf that was pressed into the gatefold and set, as the notes put it, under everything.

A play for the wireless. The Friday post carried the programme notes of a one-act radio play, Dragons, By Post — Transmission 11 of the Welsh-edge regional transmitter's wireless library — in which a postmistress hands the 2012 Hugo-and-Nebula box across the zinc counter herself, and a dragon settles its rent in letters at a Victorian postbox: Dragons, By Post — A Radio Play in One Act. The club has resolved that the May listening will begin in the hush between the final chord and the next amen, in honour of the hush the transmission ends on, and that these are the same hush.

Housekeeping. Meetings: first Thursday, 19:30, attic, door marked with the whale's shadow. New members are admitted on the strength of one complete, uninterrupted listening of one complete album, witnessed. The witnessing need not be pleasant; it must be complete. Bring tape, if you have tape. We are past the 8-track's phase-out by forty years, and we keep the habit.
