Ab Urbe Condita

From the founding of the City — the Roman count of the years, exhibited as frequently asked questions

The Chronology Room keeps two counts of the year on one shelf. The first — the Common Era, the count of AD and BCE, conceived by the monk Dionysius Exiguus at Scabbia near Ravenna in 525 and read at the Zurich gauge 408 metres up — is exhibited on its own placard, Placard No. 1. The second is older in character, if not in paper: ab urbe condita, “from the founding of the City,” the count the Romans themselves used to place a year. Every date in it is a distance from 753 BC, the traditional founding of Rome. The keeper is asked the same questions about it, always, at the bench, usually on the hour. This placard prints the questions as asked, and the answers as given.

I What is ab urbe condita, in one line and then in three?

One line: ab urbe condita — Latin, pronounced roughly [ab ˈʊrbɛ ˈkɔndɪtaː], “from the founding of the City” — expresses a date as so many years since 753 BC, the traditional founding of Rome. The full alternative form is anno urbis conditae, “in the year since the city’s founding,” [ˈannoː ˈʊrbɪs ˈkɔndɪtae̯].

Then in three: it is an expression used in antiquity and by classical historians to refer to a given year in Ancient Rome; it counts up from the founding rather than from a birth or a conversion, which is why its numbers run lower than ours by exactly seven hundred and fifty-three; and it lives on in two abbreviations, AUC and, in Classical Latin inscriptions, AVC, carved where the full phrase would have crowded the stone. The City in the phrase is the City of Rome, full stop — the Forum, the Palatine, the seven hills, everything the Swiss almanac entry would never have to name.

II How do you convert an AUC year into one the gauge can read?

The conversion is a single move and it is the whole trick. Because the epoch sits at 753 BC and the count climbs through 1 BC as AUC 753, then over the top at AD 1 as AUC 754, the years of the Common Era are always AUC minus 753.

AUC 1504 minus 753 is 751: the year 751 CE. AUC 1779 minus 753 is 1026: the year 1026 CE, the year the Chronology Room's own station log says the first gauge on the Zurich base was set down — a detail the almanac does not contradict. And working downward: AUC 727 is 27 BC, the foundation of the Roman Empire; AUC 431 is 322 BC, the year Aristotle died at Chalkis. For years before the Common Era the arithmetic has to go the other way: a year of the form n BC is AUC (754 minus n), so 500 BC is AUC 254. The table at question VII prints the pairs the keeper is asked most.

III Where does the founding date come from, and why does no one agree about it?

The date 753 BC is the traditional one, fixed in antiquity by the Roman fasti, the consular year-tables, and handed down through the chronographers. The traditional story the date commemorates is the familiar one: Romulus and Remus, the wolf, the Palatine, the shepherd’s staff, the fire on the Aventine — and a wall, which is what “condita” actually names. A city founded is a city walled. Modern scholarship drifts a little from the traditional year, some of it toward the eighth century BC, but the count itself does not drift. AUC always measures from 753 BC, whichever way the archaeologists dig, for the same reason the Common Era placard always measures from 1 CE regardless of what the monks might have computed: a reckoning is a convention first and a date second, and the station register keeps conventions honest by using them without moving them.

IV Why is 1 BC written AUC 753 and AD 1 written AUC 754, and where did the missing year go?

It went nowhere; there never was one. There is no year 0 in the count — the same gap the Common Era placard prints under its pairing table, where “no year 0 stands between” 1 BCE and 1 CE. The AUC count inherits the gap whole. Count down from AUC 753 (the year 1 BC) by one year and you are at AUC 752 (the year 2 BC); count up from AUC 753 and you are at AUC 754 (the year AD 1). The founding year itself, 753 BC, is simply AUC 1 — the first year of the city, taken without ceremony, exactly the way the CE placard takes its epoch.

It is the one arithmetic trap in the room. Cross the epoch in either direction and a naive difference of two CE years over the gap is one too large. The keeper has been caught once, at the bench, doing the distance between 1 BC and AD 1 in her head and answering two. The correct answer is one. The almanac prints the correction without comment, in the margin, in Roman numerals: I, non II.

V What is AUC 727, and why does the room keep it on a separate slip?

AUC 727 is the year 27 BC, the foundation of the Roman Empire — the year the Senate gave Octavian the name Augustus and the Republic stopped being a thing the consuls did together. The Chronology Room keeps it on a separate slip pinned under the gauge because it is the single most useful cross-reference the room owns: it is the hinge between the Republic and the Empire in one four-figure number, and it is the AUC answer to every question that begins “so when did…”. Augustus died in 14 CE, which is AUC 767; his building of Rome in stone out of what Caesar had left in brick took the better part of a reign to do. And the slip is pinned under the gauge rather than above it for the same reason the Fohn is always logged after the Bise in the Swiss entry: things that take effect are entered after the wind that brings them.

VI What is the current year, in AUC, and how does the room keep it current?

The current year, AD 2026, is AUC 2779. The room keeps it current the only way a station can: by re-deriving it at the gauge. The reading MMDCCLXXIX at the top of this placard is recomputed on the hour, in Roman numerals, from the year the almanac prints, and it has not once been in a hurry. The arithmetic is the same as in question II but run forward from the present rather than from the founding: 2026 plus 753 is 2779, and MMDCCLXXIX is what that looks like when you have no zero to hide in. If the year changes at midnight between two fixes, the placard changes with the next fix, not with the clock — the hour governs the number, the way it governs the Bise.

VIII When is AUC still in use, and why do inscriptions write AVC?

In antiquity it was the ordinary professional way to date a year in Rome; the historian who needs to place a consular year, a law, or a campaign reaches for it still, and it is the form Livy opens in — his history of Rome from its founding is, in one sense, a long AUC count carried by hand. In modern usage the term lives on in classical scholarship, in the dating of inscriptions, and in the small ceremonial moments — the year of the capital’s birthday, the city’s own festival of founding — where Rome prefers its own number. As for the abbreviation: AUC is the standard short form, but in Classical Latin inscriptions the letters the masons actually cut were AVC, which is how the count appears on stone, and the Chronology Room prints both, side by side, the way the CE placard prints AD and CE side by side. Stone and paper keep the same year; they argue about the vowels.

IX How does the Roman count sit beside the Common Era on the same shelf?

They sit the way two gauges sit beside each other: independently, exactly, and without translating for one another. The Common Era counts from an epoch fixed in 525 and secularised in 1708; the Roman count counts from a founding fixed by the fasti and never secularised, because it never needed to be. They agree on the years themselves — a CE year and its AUC partner are the same twelvemonth — and they differ only in which end of the rope they hold. That is the whole argument the Chronology Room is running, and it is the reason the Swiss almanac entry, which logs the Bise and the Fohn by the hour from the same gauge, can be read as one continuous record with this placard: the winds are the same winds whether the year is written 2026 CE or AUC 2779, and the hour is the same hour. The almanac and the same ground: a fix is taken lightly.

The keeper appends, as always, the standing order: the gauge does not move; the year does — and if the year has to change its name to be read, it does so without moving the gauge at all.
