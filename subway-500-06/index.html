Martinsville Bench · Workshop Log

The 2006 Subway 500, 1:192, in walnut and enamel

Entries kept in the spring of 2007, on a pegboard shelf above the lathe · Martinsville Speedway, Ridgeway, Virginia · race date 22 October 2006

Entry 1 — The decision

Most people who build race scenes build the one they remember best. I am not building my best. I am building the 2006 Subway 500, the 32nd stock car race of the 2006 NASCAR Nextel Cup Series and the sixth of the ten races in the Chase for the Nextel Cup, because it is the one that keeps its numbers the cleanest: five hundred laps, a crowd of 65,000, a 0.526-mile oval, and a result in which the man who won it started ninth. It was run on Sunday, 22 October 2006, at Martinsville Speedway in Ridgeway, Virginia, and it decided a championship that was still, officially, a fight. So it is the scene, and this is the log of how it came together, stage by stage, with the mistakes left in.

Entry 2 — The oval (April)

One board, two curves. Martinsville is the tightest major oval there is, and tightness is easy to get wrong in miniature, so I made the track from a single walnut blank, 440 mm long, and let the bandsaw take the full curve in one slow pass rather than gluing a corner to a straight. The racing surface is a hair-fine layer of black lacquer over sanding sealer, worn light with a 0000 pad at the four spots where the 2006 field actually scrubbed. Scale is 1:192; at that scale the 0.526 miles come out to 4.54 metres of track, which is why I did not try to build it to length and built it to proportion instead. The infield is left as bare walnut. Everything I am proud of on this board fits in the corners.

Entry 3 — The 65,000

The crowd is the reason the scene takes a whole shelf. Sixty-five thousand people attended on 22 October 2006, and the bench has 65,000 of them too: painted dots, one per spectator, in a ballpoint-pen tip, along both grandstands and the inside rail. I counted them in fives and kept the tally on a strip of masking tape at the end of the row, because the alternative was stopping at 64,999 out of spite. The dots are two inks — iron-gall brown for the north stand, sepia for the south — and the third day I had to stop, because the hand was no longer agreeing with the eye.

Under the north stand, in the recess where the track drops a half-millimetre, I set a small printed card on a sliver of card stock, the size of a thumbnail. It came in a box of estate ephemera with a postcard from a harbour island, and the greeting card says, word for word: “Return to the island the legend stands on — that is, in our possession of record west, out of his own — the greeting card says.” I do not fully understand it, and I am keeping it exactly that way. In the scene it does what the small printed things do: it is the piece of the outside world the bench cannot explain, and the legend it points to stands on the west side of the infield with a 500-lap record at this track that no one else is anywhere near. The card goes under the stand, facing up. Visitors will have to lean.

Entry 4 — The cars (May)

Three cars are built in full, and they are the only ones with working steering. No. 48 is the Hendrick Motorsports Chevrolet of Jimmie Johnson: he started ninth and drove the 500 laps to the win, so his car sits at the front of the field in the display, exactly as it should not, and I have accepted the embarrassment. No. 11 is Denny Hamlin of Joe Gibbs Racing, runner-up, with the paint thinned a shade lighter for the distance. No. 16 is Bobby Labonte of Petty Enterprises, third. The rest of the field is forty-two bare shells in the tray, unlidded, because the log has no patience for them. On the pole board at the wall I kept the facts straight: Kurt Busch took the pole position with the fastest lap time in qualifying — 19.808 seconds — and he was immediately passed on the opening lap, which is the only tragedy in the whole build and I have left it unbuilt rather than stage it.

Entry 5 — The scoreboard and the chase (June)

The lap counter is a strip of paper, one line per lap, 500 lines, run through a slot cut in the walnut apron; the whole scene's engine is a pencil and a slot, which is the point. On the back of the apron is the chase, in pencil, because the chase was a pencil-margin thing: Jeff Burton going into the race in the Drivers' Championship, 45 points ahead of Matt Kenseth in second, with four races of the Chase left to run. I wrote the margin in red, and I will not say anything more about it in this entry.

Entry 6 — Running it (August)

The scene has been run, properly, eleven times. Each running: No. 48 starts ninth in the grid, 500 laps on the counter, No. 11 in sight the last two turns, No. 16 a hand's width behind. It is the most I have ever enjoyed a shelf. The board is oiled, the dots are dusted, and the card under the north stand has not moved. I keep one other piece of the outside world in the same drawer as the grid tape — a ledger of petitions kept by hand in the Northern District of Texas, whose clerk's hand held where the record needed it to hold (The Ledger Leaf — Mansfield Register Keepers, No. 63) — because a build about a 500-lap record and a ledger about a clerk who held are two logs of the same weather.

Next, when the walnut is finished, is a larger oval, in the 1:64 that the 48-desks of this shop deserve. The 65,000 will be re-counted in fives. Nothing else on the bench is going anywhere; this one is done, and the card says what the card says.
