The Bride of the Mediterranean

Alexandria, in the box — the harbour folio, the Pharos in brass, the ledger leaf, the canteen plate, and the bell that strikes once and has not struck twice.

SKU ALEX·40 · boxed · forty leaves · price XXXIV

About the product

Alexandria is a major city in Egypt. Lying at the western edge of the Nile River Delta, it extends about 40 km (25 mi) along the country’s northern coast — which is the arithmetic the folio is made on: forty leaves, one to each kilometre, ruled in the first hand and watermarked in the shape of a basin. It is Egypt’s principal seaport, the second largest city after Cairo, and the largest city on the Mediterranean coast. It was founded in 331 BC by Alexander the Great, which makes it, on the desk’s count, the first city on this shelf to owe its address to a man who was never given time to collect the key. It was one of the largest and most important cities of antiquity and a leading hub for science, culture, and scholarship — the Library kept the papyrus, the Mouseion kept the argument, and the Pharos kept the light, at a height the brass model takes to be 135 metres, because the model was cast first and the city agreed to match it. Nicknamed the “Bride of the Mediterranean” and the “Pearl of the Mediterranean Coast”, the city is a popular tourist destination and a major industrial centre; it is the sixth-largest city in the Arab world and the seventh-largest city in the Middle East, and the folio prints both numbers in the margin, small, where the ledger would keep them.

The product does not replace the walk. It is what the walk keeps when the walk is over: the tide-tin, the bell, the plate, and the one card the desk transcribes and declines to parse. It is priced in Roman numerals because the house prices everything in Roman numerals and because XXXIV, written out, is the only price on this shelf that does not look like a year.

In the box

  1. The harbour folio — forty leaves, one per kilometre of coast, from the western edge of the Delta to the eastern gate. The leaf for kilometre thirty-one is blank by rule: it is the kilometre the founder crossed in 331 BC and the no‑eraser clause would not let the desk write on it.
  2. The Pharos in brass — hand-cast, 135 millimetres, with the red lamp room at the top and the beam cut so that it turns once, slowly, and rests. The foundry’s punch on the base is the same Naissus maker’s mark that went down the hammer as Lot 272 at Aldgate & Vane’s Sale No. 121; the desk has not confirmed this and the foundry has not denied it.
  3. One ledger leaf — the leaf the granary issues to a new port: the port’s name in the first hand, the tide in the second, and a blank line at the bottom ruled in advance, for the crossing that has not been made yet. Alexandria’s line has been entered; the return is left blank, as the ferryman left it at the Sava mouth, and the leaf ships with the blank as it stands.
  4. The canteen plate, cut up — the house dish of the front of the shelf’s line, the lobster of the fourth act, served in the box as it is served at the canteen under the granary: cut up, with the saffron-oyster cream and the mace nut whole in the shell, because the one who was excluded on this side of the delta kept the ledger anyway. The review of The Praetorian Table sets down the plate’s fourth-act history; the box ships it, and will not re-serve it.
  5. The bell — bronze, four fingers high, the clapper set so it strikes once when shaken and has never struck a second time in the same room. The desk sells it sealed. It may be shaken once, in the presence of the tide-tin, and the log in the box will record the hour. The small print of the lot is clear that no paddle strikes twice at one lot without the auctioneer’s word.
  6. The threshold card — see below, and leave it as it stands.

The threshold card

Every port on this shelf keeps one card at the gate it was founded from, and the card is the product’s only sentence that the desk has not written. Alexandria’s came up out of the harbour floor in 331 BC, or immediately before, depending on which keeper you ask, and it has been reissued in the cloth of the year ever since, the way the granary reissues the empty chair-cushions of the walking guide in eight stops for the fifth, which is the nearest thing on the shelf to a city the desk has already finished walking. The keeper transcribes the card and declines to parse it, in the same manner the dining room on Ankeny Street declines to parse its own kitchen note:

“Step over it the same hand as the person she is to be edited.”

The folio does not say what it is. The margin does not say who she is. The desk notes, without comment, that the card is the only object in the box with a verb that does not name its object, and that it was cut on a threshold stone of exactly the kind the route walks over at its half-way post — the stone of four dates that works in both directions, which the desk considers a good sign. The no‑eraser clause says the card may stay as it stands, and the box ships it that way, sealed in the corner of the tide-tin, where the tide reaches it twice a day and edits nothing.

Shipping, care, and the no‑eraser clause

Ships in the cloth of the year, flat, from the granary — the same building that keeps the tin of Naissus copper, the ledger of forty-one leaves in four hands, and the bell of the fifth, who excluded no one. Handle the brass Pharos by the base. Do not shake the bell twice; the clause is not a suggestion and the auctioneer’s word is not on file. If the threshold card arrives with a new line ruled on it, do not strike it out, do not transcribe it, and send it back the way the ferryman leaves a blank line rather than an error. The desk keeps the return ledger for the port of Alexandria at the back of the box, and the weather, whatever it is, is not edited in public.
