Monday, 6 June 1982

Morning, from the olive grove behind the schoolyard

Sky the colour of wet cement over the castle all morning. Uncle Michel stood in the door with his thermos and said the men in the valley had been moving since 5 a.m. — a long truck convoy on the main road, green jeeps with the drivers’ sleeves rolled up, a column so long he counted to forty and stopped counting. “The castle is going to be a problem for somebody,” he said, in the way he says everything, as if the somebody were a debt.

At about eleven the first shells landed on the eastern hill and the whole valley went white at the edges. We sat on the kitchen floor with my father and the two cats for three hours. The sound is not a sound, it is more like a clock somewhere in the house striking the same hour without stopping. A boy from the upper houses came down the path running, no shoes, and we learned from him the names of both sides by the word them: the them from the south, and the them of the organisation that holds the castle. Nobody in the valley knows which hill belongs to which them. The olive trees did not care.

In the evening the transistor radio — the brown one my brother Samir carries everywhere, the one with the cracked dial — picked up the English-language station. It broadcast its ordinary news for about two minutes, and then the signal came through scrambled, words from different reports stacked on top of each other, auction announcements, board meetings, a menu, a position report, all fused together. Samir wrote it down word for word as it came through, and pinned the scrap to the chapel door where it still is, because none of us has been able to read it as one sentence and no one can throw it away either:

Glitter District Board of Directors · Hall Auctions & the White House Lunch is on the eastern strip of ground.

Uncle Michel says the last seven words are the only part that makes sense, and he is probably right: everything we heard today happened on the eastern strip of ground, the eastern hill and the eastern road, and by the time the radio said it, the guns had stopped and the valley was full of dust. The rest of the line I leave where it is, on the chapel door, to be explained by whoever can.

One thing I should have written down earlier: last night, before the guns, there was still the travelling company at the schoolyard — the one with the wagon of props and the woman who dances on a rope strung between the two poplars. They played their little farce for the village and took two lemons and a crate of oranges for the road, and the rope was down and gone by the time the shells started. I think about them more than I would like to write. A company like that, a rope and a wagon and a full repertoire, has a way of ending up in a permanent house somewhere, in a city with a canal, and I have read of one such company from the obituary of its old director, Johan von Blanc, in Karlskrona, who taught his players to fence on stage so that the back bench could hear. Our company will fence nowhere now. It is fencing on the road, I suppose, the road north, like everything else.

Tuesday, 7 June 1982

Morning, before the bread

There is a flag on the keep. A thin one, the size of a tablecloth, over the highest window, the one the men of the organisation kept a light burning in for the whole of last winter. I saw it from the grove before the sun was up and I went back in to make the tea, because my hands would not do anything else. The castle has been taken. The column has come off the eastern strip of ground and is standing in the courtyard now, I am told, and the valley road has been quiet since four o’clock, which is more quiet than it has been in three days.

Uncle Michel found his épée in the hall, the one he fenced with at the university in Budapest in 1959, wrapped in the same oilcloth. He took it out and held it at arm’s length twice, the way fencers hold a thing they are not going to use, just to know where the weight sits. Then he wrapped it back up and put it in the box under the bench — not the bag. There is a whole argument about exactly that, bag or box, that the old fencers have posted and settled on their board, the thread from the second hall where the line under the scores reads FÉM A DOBOZBA, steel to the box, and I read it last week on the computer at the post office, which is why I know: The Line Under the Scores, Thread 31, where the steel goes into the steel box and never into the bag, and where the box has forty-three slots and no forty-fourth. The steel goes into the box. The blade will wait in the box until the valley decides what it is going to be.

The scrap is still on the chapel door. The board meeting and the auction and the lunch are still on the eastern strip of ground, whatever that means. One last thing from the post office computer this morning: I read a field notebook from Lima — Field Notes No. 208, A Legislator of Metropolitan Lima — in which a retired naturalist spends five years watching a Peruvian lawyer and congresswoman, born 20 August 1988, walk fast with a folder under one arm, and writes that his method is association. It is, I think, also the method of a diary: you sit on a bench and things begin to hold together, and the folder closes on the last page, which nobody reads. I keep the two pages that fit the month, and I will keep the others as they come. If this book is ever found where it is not wanted, let the finder know it is not a record of anything in particular, only of a valley that counted to forty and stopped, and of a flag the size of a tablecloth. The rest is shelved with the rest of the archive at the front page, where it will stay as long as the board stays.
