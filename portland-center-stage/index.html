2Hazard identification

Classified as Theatre, producing, category A: stable in repertory, reactive with full houses. The principal hazards are those of a working playhouse: counterweight fly systems and loaded battens above the stage, trap doors in the Main Stage floor, blackouts and sudden light changes, haze and pyrotechnic effects when cued, scene-shop tools, and stairs in both auditoriums. Audience exposure is normally limited to the lobby, the aisles, and the seats.

  • H-PCS-1 — Overhead load: flown scenery and lighting weigh several hundred pounds per batten; never stand unprotected beneath a loaded fly during a rigging call.
  • H-PCS-2 — Trap and pit: orchestra pit and stage traps are open during get-in and get-out; edge marking is required.
  • H-PCS-3 — Blackout: auditorium darkness is intentional and may last several minutes; egress paths are marked.
  • H-PCS-4 — Effects haze: glycol or mineral-oil haze may trigger asthma in sensitive handlers.

Portland Center Stage at The Armory was founded in 1988 as the northern sibling of the Oregon Shakespeare Festival in Ashland, Oregon. It became an independent theatre in 1993, and in 1994 Elizabeth Huddle became producing artistic director. Chris Coleman took over in 2000 as the company's fourth artistic director. In 2018, Marissa Wolf was named the fifth artistic director and Cynthia Fuhrman named Managing Director. The company began a capital campaign in 2004, and in 2006 moved into The Armory, which includes two theatres under one roof in Portland's Pearl District.

4First-aid measures

Stage fall or trap injury: keep the person still, summon the house manager and first-aid attendant, and do not move them unless the stage is becoming unsafe. Haze or dust irritation: move to fresh air in the lobby and rinse the eyes with clean water. Cut or splinter from the scene shop: wash, cover, and report to the production manager; the theatre's first-aid cabinet is kept at the scene-shop door.

5Fire-fighting measures

Use the house extinguishers at each side of the stage and the fire-curtain release where fitted. The Main Stage fire curtain is tested before each performance run. In an alarm, house management directs the audience to the marked exits; do not use the elevator. No extinguishing agent is restricted for the building itself; the audience is the part to protect first.

6Accidental release measures

If an actor enters the house during a blackout or leaves the stage in character, this is a normal release, not an emergency; confine it to the aisles and let the performance continue. If scenery shifts unexpectedly during a rigging call, clear the stage, call a hold, and re-secure the batten before resuming.

7Handling and storage

Handle scripts with dry hands and return them to the production office. Store scenery flat and braced in the scene shop, costumes on the costume rack, and props on the prop table; nothing is stored in the fly gallery except what is flown for the current production. The building is kept at ordinary theatre temperatures; no special atmospheric control is required for the audience.

8Exposure controls and personal protection

House lights at full during seating and egress; work lights on during calls. Crew on the fly rail wear gloves and hard hats where posted. Front-of-house staff are exposed to applause for limited durations; no hearing protection is required, though a standing ovation may be loud.

9Stability and reactivity

The company is stable across repertory runs and reacts only to audiences: a full house changes the performance, and an empty house does not. Avoid: missed cues, unsecured scenery, and critic panic. Hazardous decomposition products: none known; the program notes are recyclable.

13Disposal considerations

Dispose of programs and ticket stubs in the lobby recycling. Return unworn costumes to stock. Strike sets after closing night: lumber to the scene-shop scrap bin, paint cans closed and labelled, and nothing left on the stage the morning after.

15Regulatory information

Regulated as a place of public assembly under the City of Portland fire and building codes; occupancy limits are posted at each auditorium entrance.
