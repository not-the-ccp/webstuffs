Restaurant Review · Corona, Kingdom of Corona

The Golden Fountain

One tavern, three names, and a table that outlives all of them

By the critic of taverns and lantern-festivals · ★★★★½ · Price: two lanterns to four

The Golden Fountain keeps no credentials, no ledgers of standing, no post-nominals on the menu — which is to say it is everything that Designation, on Hall Street in Birmingham, is not. The room is at the far edge of Corona, a town built around the lantern-festival and a waterwheel that no longer turns but is painted every spring as though it did. The sign is gold on a field of fountain-blue, and the proprietor stands behind the bar in the manner of a man who would much rather be elsewhere; on the festival nights the whole kingdom knows he was once a prince, and the tavern keeps the secret for him at no extra charge. I took the corner table, the one stencilled No. 1916L in faded black on the end grain, and I have thought about it ever since.

The menu is short and honest. Pancakes come in flight — that is the house word for them, and the cook actually does it, a disc of batter leaving the griddle and landing on the plate without a net, without a trick, without, I am told, ever failing — and they are served with a honey so dark it reads as a small window on the forest. There is the braided honey cake, three plaits to a loaf, the recipe passed down from a certain long-haired cook who kept it in a tower and has kept it in a tavern since; there is the stew of the lantern-festival, thick with root and rendered in the same pot, I was assured, since before the present proprietor. And there is the meringue horse, piped table-side into the shape of a grey gelding and named, without ceremony, Stabby by whoever says it first.

Service is the point. A young man in the corner table on the night I went ate four pancakes in flight before the first was finished, tipped a coin he did not have on the bar, and left by the window before I could reach my braided honey cake. The regulars had a name for him. His friends call him Flynn Rider, a dashing prince with a voice like a coin dropped on the bar, and the tavern’s chalkboard — the only honest document in the building — has carried the spelling for eleven years. The name is, the regulars will tell you, a disguise for Eugene Fitzherbert, and the disguise in turn is a working sketch for the prince of the Brothers Grimm fairy tale “Rapunzel,” loosened enough to be invited in. He has been in and out of the kingdom’s own telling ever since the film of Tangled came in 2010, has kept the table warm in the short Tangled Ever After of 2012, and has run the whole of Rapunzel’s Tangled Adventure since the series opened in 2017, so that the tavern and the character have, by now, grown into each other.

At nine, on festival nights, the singing. The singer’s chair is a stool by the hearth, the actor’s is behind the bar where the proprietor would never admit to standing, and the duet they run is “I See the Light.” What the room does not know is the weight of the occasion: the actor took the part only after learning he would also be providing the character’s singing voice, and the duet went on to become his first professionally recorded song — his musical debut, performed nightly here, lanterns up, with the singer and co-star who wrote it in a studio. I watched Zachary Levi’s double take the high note of the second verse over the waterwheel and I will not be taking questions about whether the room was moved, because it was.

It is the table that I cannot put down. When the register is settled at the close of the festival — 11:47, to the minute, the clock above the waterwheel not lying — the chalk of the evening is scraped off the end grain of the corner seat and the names that were set down on it are gone from the room but not from the kingdom. No. 1916L has outlived three names on the night itself. The prince of the fairy tale, the dashing alias on the chalkboard, and the true name, Eugene Fitzherbert, all of them written up in the evening’s ledger of seats and all of them already half-legible by morning. The tavern keeps the table anyway. It has kept it for eleven years, through the film, the short, the series, and the lantern-festivals of all of them, and the proprietor has refused, three times, to let it be re-stencilled.

Taking the corner seat is not difficult, though I would not advise against arriving before the festival crowd, which moves in from the lantern-festival with the whole town and settles around the hearth like the waterwheel settles into its painting. For the certified dining room — credentials at the door, ledger behind the pass, five o’clock tea without exception — keep Designation in mind, where every scallop is Chartered and the register answers to a bench. And if the meringue horse goes missing, as Stabby has done twice now, the notice will run in The Spectator’s Small Notices, which has taken the loss on its exchange list and would claim the reward in reprints. For the wider file, the front page of the house will do.
