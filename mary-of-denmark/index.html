The Copenhagen Evening Ledger · Final Edition, Saturday 22 March 2031

Queen Mary of Denmark

Mary Elizabeth Donaldson · 5 February 1972, Sydney, Australia — 21 March 2031, Amalienborg Palace, Copenhagen

Queen consort of Denmark, wife of King Frederik X, patron of thirty-odd causes, founder of the Mary Foundation · she was 59.

The register, kept by the household

  • 1972 born 5 February, Sydney
  • 2000 Sydney Olympic Games
  • 2004 married, 14 May, Copenhagen Cathedral
  • 2005–17 Christian, Isabella, Vincent, Josephine
  • 2011 the Mary Foundation founded
  • 2031 died 21 March, Amalienborg

The queen who stayed

Queen Mary of Denmark died on the evening of Thursday 21 March 2031 at Amalienborg Palace, with the family at her side, and the household register records the hour as nine minutes past eight, a minute the kitchen, which kept her tea, has asked not to be questioned about. Her name was Mary Elizabeth Donaldson until 14 May 2004, and it was Mary Elizabeth Donaldson who first appeared in the public picture at the Sydney Olympic Games of 2000, in a crowd of two thousand guests from forty countries, where a certain prince of Denmark was on his second year of university and had no idea what he was doing with the evening. The meeting is now the first fact of the reign, as the first match is the first fact of a season: a young Australian woman and a young Danish prince at the Olympic Park, the rest of the story arriving in the ordinary order, one winter in Canberra, one summer in Sydney, and then the engagement announced on 10 November 2003.

The wedding took place on 14 May 2004 at Copenhagen Cathedral, in the same cathedral where a long line of Danish brides had been received, and the city kept the date the way a family keeps a score. She bore four children — Christian, born 26 October 2005, Isabella, 21 April 2007, Vincent, 8 January 2015, and Josephine, 8 January 2015, twins of a kind the royal ledger had not seen since 1840 — and the palace, which counts its days in the old style, will not be quiet about any of the four.

What the day held

A queen of Denmark carries her engagements in a book the household does not print, and the book of Mary was a thick one. Since the marriage of 2004 she carried out engagements on behalf of the Danish monarchy — hospital wards, shipyards, the refugee camps of the Danish Refugee Council, the winter appeals, the autumn markets, and the long list of visits that never make the photograph — and she served as patron of more than thirty charitable organisations, among them the United Nations Population Fund, the European regional office of the World Health Organization, the Danish Refugee Council, and Julemærkefonden, whose red-star winter appeal she opened for the last time on 19 December 2030, when she still found her way down to the front row herself. In 2011 she founded her own social organisation, the Mary Foundation, for young people in Denmark and across the northern Europe she had grown to know by heart, and the Foundation kept its first register at a rented room on Bredgade that still holds the ledger, the two stamps, and the chair the queen preferred.

Colleagues of the household describe a woman who read everything sent to her, twice if it was about children, and who signed letters — not autographs, the palace is firm on this — because a signed letter was a promise with a name on it. The kitchen at Amalienborg has announced that the Thursday supper of small things, which the queen insisted on from the first year of the reign, will be served as before, with one chair drawn back from the table, and the household asks that this be taken as an instruction, not a sentiment.

What the notebook asked

One matter belongs in the record because the household asked for it to be. The court photographer's notebooks of the last eleven years have been deposited at the Mary Foundation, and in the final entry of the last notebook, dated 19 December 2030, under a pencil sketch of the queen at the Julemærkefonden appeal, the photographer had set down a single question in her own hand, kept word for word:

“The story is more than a plot, and then he went to Kupres?”

The “he” was the postman. For eleven years a man in a small municipality in the north had carried the Danish Refugee Council's winter letters to the families the queen corresponded with, and when he took a postmaster's post in Kupres the queen wrote to him, and the Foundation's register shows the reply arrived in March 2031, three weeks after her death, and was read aloud in the Bredgade room by the Foundation's keeper, who then returned it to the envelope, the way the household returns letters. The story the notebook asked about was the story of those letters — the whole exchange between a palace and a municipality — and the valley's own guide to the place where he went, the Sana ravine and the track from Kupres, is kept at Gornje Vukovsko — The Village, Travel Guide, Entry No. 31, for anyone who would like to walk the 3.2 kilometres the letters once travelled by road. The obituary prints the question because the household said the question is what the queen would have kept, and the register agrees.

The keeping

The funeral of the queen will be at the Church of Our Lady, Copenhagen, in the old style, and the four children have been announced in the register in the order of the palace, Christian first and Josephine last. The Mary Foundation's first act of 2031 is to print the letters, both sides, without commentary, which is the whole of the programme. For the readers of this page: the pie of the family that kept a tin of cards on the 30th of May has its own card, and the tin was in Copenhagen in 2004, on the wedding day, which the tin remembers in its own way — see Wembley Sunday Pie, Card No. 9, for the family's account, and start from the front page of the collection if the tin's cards have not yet been found.
