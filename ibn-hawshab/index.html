Chapter VII · Of the Dāʿī's Door

1 Who keeps this door

Ibn Ḥawshab came to Yemen by the sea road and left it by the highland road, and the book of etiquette of the mountain house keeps him by the door because the door is the first thing the door does: it admits. A senior Isma'ili missionary, dāʿī, from the environs of Kufa, he is the man at the threshold of this second series, and every rule below is argued from a door he is held to have kept. In cooperation with ʿAlī ibn al-Faḍl al-Jayshānī he established the Isma'ili creed in Yemen and carried it from wadi to wadi through the 890s and the 900s, taking much of the country in the name of the Isma'ili imam, Abdallāh al-Mahdī, who at the time was still in hiding. That is the whole difficulty of the door: the house is kept in the name of a man no one in the room has seen. The etiquette below exists to make a hidden authority feel, to every guest, as certain as a lamp that is lit.

The houses say he was of the carpenter's line — al-Najjār is in his very name — and the book takes that seriously, because a carpenter's son knows a hinge before he knows a sermon. What the dāʿī said could bend, the dāʿī's door never did: it opened once, on a schedule of knocks, and it held.

2 The seven rules of the threshold

The chapter is run on seven rules, and the annex of the copy this book holds — a leaf found in the binding, unsigned, in a hand that knew Yemeni paper — reads, against all of them, in one breath. It is set here as found, in its original line:

“What is inside, and the pin put back in the world had been thin Optimism, undiluted trace Not removable; see Section 2 Purpose of this chapter will be run.” — annex leaf, found in the binding; the leaf's own reference is the one obeyed here

The rules themselves, as the houses keep them:

  1. One knock. The guest knocks once and stands. Two knocks is the messenger's sign and is answered differently; three is never used. The door of the mission-house was the only door in the wadi on a schedule, and the schedule was the secrecy.
  2. The seat is given, not asked for. A guest who must ask for a seat has already been declined; a guest who is given one before he sits down has been received. Ibn Ḥawshab, the houses say, kept two cushions on the right of the door and one at the hearth, and the hearth cushion was for no one who had come with news.
  3. The name is answered, the office is not. A guest may be told the name of the man at the door. The man at the door may not be told the name of the man in hiding; it is referred to, in the dāʿī's own speech, as Abū Muḥammad, and the reference is the etiquette. After Abdallāh al-Mahdī proclaimed himself, the houses of the coast changed the reference to the public one and the houses of the highland kept the older, and both were correct in their country.
  4. Nothing written crosses the door. The letter of the creed passed in the memory, from mouth to mouth along the road to the coast, which is why a man like al-Jayshānī, who walked the highland roads, was worth a caravan. A dāʿī who wrote his charge on paper had, in the book's phrase, “painted the lamp”: the light was now on the outside, where a search of a satchel could find it.
  5. The guest's question is answered twice. Once in words, once in silence, and the silence is the larger answer. The guest who hears only the words has been treated as a stranger; the guest who is given the silence has been treated as one of the house.
  6. Food is served in the order the hand receives it. Bread, then date, then water, then meat, and never the reverse; the order is the house's way of saying the guest is inside a system of care he did not choose and cannot fall out of.
  7. The door closes with the guest's back to it. The guest leaves before the door, never toward it, so that no one departing from the mission-house ever saw the door close on him. The houses of the highland still keep it, and it is the reason their doorways stand an arm's length from the wall.

3 The conquest by etiquette

It is worth saying what the conqueror conquered. Much of Yemen, through the 890s and the 900s, passed into the keeping of the hidden imam — province by province, wadi by wadi — not with an army at the front of the argument but with a door at the front of it. A governor who found a house in his city where the bread came first and the date second, where a stranger was given silence as readily as speech, where the name of the absent master was never spoken and never doubted, was a governor who had been received by Manṣūr al-Yaman. When Abdallāh al-Mahdī at last proclaimed himself and the house that had been kept by proxy was joined to the house that was now open, the argument the dāʿīs had been running at doors for a decade was over in one morning, because the doors had already been open to the conclusion. The book of etiquette does not call this strategy. It calls it the patience of the hinge.

For the table of such a house — the fire prepared for the entire session and never once stopped, the loaf broken in the same order every evening — see the kitchen ledger that keeps the Ribat Loaf, Card No. 41; it is the same discipline from the other side of the door. For a door kept in the opposite climate, the questions set down in The Frozen North on the green sledge and the name Mikellik, the reader will find the same law of the threshold argued in snow instead of in date-palm shade, and both arguments come out the same: the house is the message, and the message is the one the door gives out.

4 The door that was never opened

Ibn Ḥawshab died at Kufa, on the 31st of December of the year 914, in the city of his birth-lineage and not in the country he had opened; the sea road back took him where the sea road had come. The book keeps a leaf for him in the annex, and the annex keeps one rule that no house in Yemen is said to have kept at all: rule eight, which runs that a dāʿī who has conquered a country in the name of a hidden master and never been received by him in the flesh owes the master, and the master alone, the courtesy of a door left unlocked on the night of his death. Whether the door of the house at Sana'a was unlocked that winter, and whether it mattered, the book declines to say, in the manner the whole series keeps: the courtesy of the silence is the larger answer.

And so the chapter is closed the way the chapter was opened — with a door on a schedule of knocks — and the reader is referred, for the next chapter, to the hearth.
