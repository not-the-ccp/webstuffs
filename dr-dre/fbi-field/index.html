CONFIDENTIAL · AGENT'S COPY

The Federal Bureau of Investigation is the domestic intelligence and security service of the United States and its principal federal law enforcement agency. An agency of the United States Department of Justice, the Bureau is a member of the U.S. Intelligence Community and reports to both the attorney general and the director of national intelligence. This is the agent's copy of Field Operations Manual FM-14, Section 3, Surveillance and Cover: the board is the United States, the pieces are the fifty-six field offices, and the object of play is the Top Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list, one name at a time.

I. The Bureau

Founded in 1908 as the Bureau of Investigation and renamed in 1935, the Bureau works out of the J. Edgar Hoover Building, 935 Pennsylvania Avenue NW, Washington, DC, with fifty-six field offices and resident agencies across the country. Its jurisdiction covers violations of more than 200 categories of federal crime — bank robbery, kidnapping, counterintelligence, counterterrorism, organized crime, civil rights — and its best-known list keeps ten names on the wall at once. A name comes off the list only by arrest, surrender, or death, and a new name is pinned up the same morning.

II. The Components

  • 1 badge, brass, serial stamped on the rim
  • 1 credential card, signed by the director, valid until surrendered
  • 1 field notebook, ruled, pocket size, for stakeout logs and descriptions
  • 1 cipher wheel, two discs, for the day's code
  • 6 manila envelopes, one per open case, labeled in typewriter ink
  • 1 most-wanted track sheet, ten lines, face down until turned

III. Setup

The referee selects a field office — Washington, Los Angeles, Miami, New York — and each agent takes a badge, a notebook, and one manila envelope. The ten most-wanted names are laid face down on the track sheet; one is turned face up whenever a case closes. The agent whose envelope holds the active case works it; the others keep watch.

IV. Play

A round has three actions, taken in turn: surveil, interview, arrest. Surveillance means the stakeout log — time, vehicle, description, direction of travel — written in ink while the engine stays cold. An interview requires the file open on the table; an arrest requires the warrant, the attorney general's name on the line, and jurisdiction: a federal crime from the Bureau's list of more than two hundred, nothing less.

V. Rules That Matter

Rule One. Have the file in your hand before you knock. No file, no knock, no exception.

Rule Two. The heat does not let drop. Work the room, not the weather; the log is written the same in Miami in July as it is in Washington in January.

Rule Three. No flipping. Read the file side into side, whole, and close the envelope when the case closes.

Rule Four. The Top Ten are counted in order. No name is rounded off, and no name is argued off the wall.
