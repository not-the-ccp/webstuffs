3 Composition / information on ingredients

Substance/mixture: Differentiated natural satellite, heterogeneous. Mean density 2.061 g/cm³; roughly 15–35% water ice by mass, the rest silicate and rock. Not a pure substance; composition varies from the core to the frost.

History of the composition: Triton is a differentiated body — icy solid crust, liquid below, rock at the bottom — and its surface geology is largely driven by the dynamics of water ice and other volatiles such as nitrogen and methane. The body's history of capture, its high internal heat for its size, and its thin but significant atmosphere make its geology vigorous, in the way a caught-and-held thing is vigorous, and it has been and continues to be so. Nearly nothing was known of Triton's geology before the flyby; everything on this sheet after Section 9 is built from that single pass and forty-odd years of patient looking back.

4 First-aid measures

Inhalation (nitrogen atmosphere, 1.4–1.9 Pa): The atmosphere as found is too thin to matter at the surface; the hazard is concentration. In habitat settings, move to breathable air and ventilate. Do not chase the plume.

Skin contact (nitrogen frost, 38 K): Do not remove by rubbing. Insulate the affected limb, warm slowly, and treat as deep cold injury with the expectation that the frost will sublimate and leave the wound cleaner than it took it. Log the exposure time against the vent log.

Eye contact: Rinse cautiously with warm saline for at least fifteen minutes. The tholin film gives a violet cast to everything, including the cornea; if the cast remains after rinsing, see Section 11.

Ingestion: Do not drink the subsurface ocean. It is brine, it is under pressure, and it has a core to answer to. If any frost is swallowed, rinse the mouth, do not induce vomiting, and obtain medical advice in a warm room.

Most important symptoms and effects: Brittle fracture of tools; frostbite; disorientation from the retrograde orbit (see Section 8); violet afterimage. Advice to physicians: Treat symptomatically. There is no specific antidote for a captured moon; keep the patient away from 38 K and, if possible, from the relay's phonograph.

5 Fire-fighting measures

Suitable extinguishing media: Inert gas flood (argon preferred; nitrogen acceptable if the habitat pressure budget allows). At 38 K the fire problem is mostly a supply problem — smother the fuel, do not cool it.

Specific hazards arising from the substance: Methane frost ignites readily against any spark and burns quietly blue against a darker background; in the 1.4–1.9 Pa nitrogen atmosphere a surface fire cannot sustain, which is why the geology does not burn — it vents instead. Distinguish the cryovolcanic plume from a fire: the plume rises eight kilometres and is violet at its crown, and it does not come down where it was struck.

Advice for firefighters: Wear full cryogenic ensemble and self-contained breathing apparatus. Civilians should stay out of the plume shadow and remember that the eruption is geological, not chemical: you do not put it out, you wait for the vent to empty, which on this sheet's clock is a matter of seasons.

6 Accidental release measures

Personal precautions: A release of nitrogen frost at 38 K forms a ground-hugging fog that is denser than any air a habitat provides; keep your boots off the plain and your instruments out of the fog. Do not walk the frost line; it migrates, and it does not stop for the slow.

Environmental precautions: The environment is the product. Containment is not available and is not attempted; the frost will redeposit, the tholin will darken it, and the next season will move it again, which is the intended behaviour and not a failure of the sheet.

Methods and materials for containment: None. Observe, record the frost line against the reference grid of the flyby images, and note the hour of the relay. Reference to other sections: see 8 and 13.

7 Handling and storage

Precautions for safe handling: Handle only at or below 38 K. The surface pressure is 1.4–1.9 Pa of nitrogen; any container that is opened above that pressure will lose its frost to the air with audible sighing, which the phonograph relay is tuned to record and which the Desk regards as the body speaking, not the container failing. Do not store Triton in a system with an inner rocky planet of any mass; it will not be in the same place twice, and its orbit is already the wrong way round.

Conditions for safe storage, including incompatibilities: Keep tidally locked, face-to-Neptune, at a mean orbital radius of about 354,800 km. Incompatible materials: anything with a prograde habit, anything that wants a day to last more than 5.877 of its own, and the warm room of the Geologic Desk, where the California sheet is kept and where this sheet's frost has been known to weep at the door. Storage life: indefinite; the geology will outlast the storage. Tidal stress from Neptune's 0.78 m/s² gravity at the surface and the 165-year orbital cycle should be assumed to be doing the body's handling on the body's behalf at all times.

8 Exposure controls / personal protection

Control parameters: No occupational exposure limit is established for a moon; the nearest analogue is the cold-storage rating of this annex, held at −215 °C (38 K) with the door closed. Solar radiation at 30 AU is a tenth of an inner-system day's — but the ultraviolet is unfiltered, and it is the ultraviolet that turns the methane into tholin and the surface into its violet cast.

Appropriate engineering controls: Habitat pressure verified before any frost work; argon inerting of sample cabinets; relay line at 13.5 GHz kept clear; the phonograph, which plays the encounter audio, kept behind a double door, because the 14 August 1989 pass, played at full volume, has twice been mistaken for a venting event by unaccustomed staff.

Individual protection measures: Full cryogenic ensemble; N2-inerted suits; polarised violet-corrected optics; a compass that has been recalibrated for a retrograde sky, or none at all — the satellite's own day is shorter than the staff's patience, and its night is a 38 K night, and a compass that insists on north will be found facing Neptune by morning, which is correct and unsettling in equal measure.

Hygiene measures: De-frost in sequence, never by heat; wash tholin off the optics before the wash; do not keep samples in the pocket you keep your warm things in.

10 Stability and reactivity

Reactivity: Stable in orbit; the retrograde path costs the satellite a small fraction of its height each revolution, a slow leak that no handler can see and that the sheet lists anyway. Reacts vigorously to its own heat: the interior, warmed by the mechanics of capture and by the decay of heavy elements in a body that was not built for this distance from a star, drives the surface. Cryovolcanic vents are the signature — Sverdrup Patera, the large vent plain, and the plume recorded by the flyby rising roughly eight kilometres with a violet crown, are the standing examples, and the geology is described as vigorous and continues to be described that way in each revision of this sheet.

Chemical stability: Stable under recommended storage conditions. The nitrogen frost is in permanent, seasonal motion — deposited on the winter pole, sublimed from the summer, migrating across the plains — and this sheet has stopped trying to mark it as static.

Conditions to avoid: Inner-system temperatures; prograde handling; long exposure of the tholin layer to a strong ultraviolet source, which will age the surface faster than its own orbit does. Capture events: none scheduled; in the unlikely event of one, see the whole of Section 14.

Incompatible materials: Open flame (methane); warm hands (everything); optimism about a second flyby (see Section 16).

Hazardous decomposition products: Tholin, under ultraviolet action; carbon dioxide ice in trace, in the cold traps. Nothing worse than a colour.

11 Toxicological information

Routes of exposure: Inhalation (concentrated nitrogen in habitat work); dermal (frost contact); ocular (tholin film, ultraviolet); ingestion (accidental frost, ocean brine in the hypothetical sample).

Acute toxicity: Nitrogen is a simple asphyxiant at concentration; lethal only where someone has enclosed a great deal of it with a person. Methane likewise, with the fire problem added. The surface itself, at 1.4–1.9 Pa, is not a poison; it is an absence, and the body knows the difference.

Chronic effects: Disorientation from the retrograde orbit (documented in three staff, all of whom now carry no compass); violet afterimage in the ultraviolet-heavy gaze; a persistent and well-founded expectation, in cold-storers, that the bin is colder than the sign says, which the annex cannot confirm because it refuses to calibrate below the sign.

Target organs: Skin, eyes, lungs, patience. Signs and symptoms: frostbite, violet cast, cough from fine frost, the particular silence of the phonograph room after the 1989 pass has been played and no one has yet said whether the plume in the audio is a plume.

Medical attention: See Section 4. There is no specific antidote; warm the patient, correct the optics, and file the incident against the vent log.

12 Ecological information

Ecotoxicity: None to report; there is no ecology to harm, and the sheet says so rather than staying silent about it. The frost, the tholin, and the vent plain are the whole of the biotic claim, and it is a claim the body makes against its own interior, not against anything outside it.

Notable features (in lieu of taxa): Cantaloupe terrain — the pitted, ridged plains of the southern highlands, their origin argued between collapse of a subsurface layer and the slow freezing of a warmer skin; Sverdrup Patera and the vent plains; the dark equatorial band; the seasonal polar caps; a possible thin, patchy ozone signature in the ultraviolet, which the Desk lists and does not believe.

Ecological note: The geology is the habitat. What this sheet calls an ecosystem, the interior would call a working: rock at the bottom, brine in the middle, frost on top, heat moving up through all of it, and a thin atmosphere doing the weather of one season a year. For a terrestrial comparison of how a surface argues with its interior, the Desk cross-files the report on the extinction event, Class 8C, Coopers' Lane School — the five endings kept in the rock are the same argument, at a higher temperature, and the family still bakes.

Persistence and degradability: The frost is permanent on any human clock; the tholin persists longer. Degradability: the geology degrades the sheet, not the other way round.

Bioaccumulative potential / mobility in soil: Not determined; the soil is 38 K and the bio is a rumour, on both sides of the fraction.

13 Disposal considerations

Disposal methods: None. Triton is not waste and this sheet is not a certificate to that effect, but the body will not accept the paperwork; it is 2,707 km in diameter, tidally locked, and scheduled to be outlasted by whatever you meant to put it in. Samples, if any exist, return to the bin at −215 °C in inerted containers, labelled against the vent log, with the hour of the relay.

Contaminated packaging: De-frost in sequence; do not warm; do not open the argon until the habitat pressure is verified.

Waste treatment methods: None applicable. The frost will outlive the treatment.

14 Transport information

UN number: UN 3209 (cryogenic, natural satellite, n.o.s.), assigned provisionally by the annex.

UN proper shipping name: Triton, geology, solid, n.o.s., with atmosphere in original packaging.

Transport hazard class(es): 9 (miscellaneous) plus the annex's own class R (retrograde). Packing group: I, on the cold side.

Environmental hazards: Marine pollution not applicable; orbital pollution is, and the satellite is committed to it. The one completed transport of record is the Voyager 2 encounter of 14 August 1989 — closest approach about 11,000 km, a single pass, at a relative speed that does not permit a second look — whose imagery, audio, and the plume in question are kept at the relay. No handler has since been permitted to open the approach corridor, and the sheet notes, for the record, that the body's own transport history — from wherever the scattered disc kept it, into the capture that made it Neptune's, on a retrograde path it has not left — is the only shipment the supplier has completed without a manifest, and it arrived with the interior warm and the frost already moving.

Transport in bulk: Not applicable; the bulk is 2.061 g/cm³ and 2,707 km, and it declines.

15 Regulatory information

Regulations specific to the substance: Annex Cold Storage Ordinance 9 (storage at or below 38 K; no prograde handling); the Voyager Encounter Archive Act (imagery of 14 August 1989 held in trust; the plume question remains open and is not to be closed by anyone without a second pass); the Retrograde Notation Rule (all charts of the body to show the orbit as found, wrong way round, without apology).

Not classified as a hazardous material under any inner-system code when transported in its intact state as a moon, which it does not.

Chemical safety assessment: Not required; geological safety assessment performed each season of the orbit, at the 165-year mark, and noted as vigorous.
