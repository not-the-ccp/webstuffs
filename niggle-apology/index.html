A Letter of Apology

From the secretary of the Circle, written 30 May 2014, at the hall's own long table, with the rain on the skylight and the register of meetings open in front of me.

To the estate of Thomas Nigel Kneale (18 April 1922 – 29 October 2006), care of the solicitors on Manx Walk who were kind enough, after the funeral at the old chapel, to let a writers' club with eleven members and one kettle keep a single folio in its drawer. I write to you on behalf of the Circle, and I write it slowly, because an apology must be read as slowly as it was meant.

We kept the folio. That is the first thing, and I will not leave it hanging. The folio was a sheaf of loose papers you let us have in 2001 at the last meeting at which Mr. Kneale's name was spoken in a room in this country where it did not need to be: notes for the unwritten Quatermass, the fifth, the one that would have taken Professor Bernard Quatermass out of the London that made him — the Tube, the power station, the pit of Quatermass and the Pit — and into something he had stopped being able to draw on paper. You kept it in the hall's oak drawer, under the tea tin, for three years, and no one touched it, and I will say before anyone reads this letter that the Circle is a small house, a poor one, and that a sheaf of a man's late notes kept under a tea tin is not a vault, and we knew it was not a vault.

Then came the spring of 2014, and I must say what the register says, which is nothing. The Circle meets on the first and third Tuesdays, and the hall's register is written in by whichever member holds the reed for the fortnight. The folio went missing somewhere between the Tuesday of the first week in April and the Tuesday of the third. The register for those two weeks is in the drawer. The register for the week between them is not. One member of the Hall has no record of the spring of 2014, and that member, Mrs. E. C. Farrant, who held the reed in those weeks, is now the only living person who can tell us where a stack of paper went from one Tuesday to the next, and Mrs. Farrant has a house in Rhos-on-Sea and a cat called Quatermass and has, since the matter was raised with her in the polite way the Circle raises matters, not opened a letter from us. We have not lost the folio, the Circle believes, in any way that matters more than we have lost the week that would have told us how.

I know what you were told of him in the papers at the end of October 2006, in London, aged eighty-four: born in England, raised in the Isle of Man, the island that has half owned him ever since; acting at the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art before he had any right to write; a radio man at the BBC first, the words spoken aloud before the words written down; the short story collection Tomato Cain & Other Stories carrying the Somerset Maugham Award in 1950, the year he was twenty-eight; the career that ran, by every honest count, between 1946 and 1997. The papers said he was “one of the most influential writers of the 20th century” and that he had “invented popular TV,” and the second sentence is the one that sits heaviest in this hall, because it is ours, this country's television, and we are a circle of people who write things no one broadcasts. He made the instrument and we have only ever played it badly. The Quatermass films, The Quatermass Xperiment of 1955 first; the 1959 serial in which the astronauts landed in the African earth and found what the pit had been keeping; the 1963 series; the triffids of 1951, the bell of The Sound of the Bell, the whole thin shelf of him.

So: the folio is missing from our drawer, the week that might explain it is missing from our register, and I am the one who signs the letter because I was the one who put it under the tea tin in 2001 and I am the one who will put it in the record. The Circle will pay no compensation, because it cannot, but it offers the true thing, which is this register of our own meetings below, reconstructed week by week for the spring of 2014 so that the gap can be seen with its own edges, and it offers a copy of every folio we did keep, which is all the rest of them. If Mrs. Farrant writes to us, the letter will go to you first, and so will the apology we all signed on 3rd May, the one with the eleven wet corners that are not wetness, the one that began we are sorry we could not be the vault. You will have this letter. The Circle asks for nothing back except that the drawer not be locked against us, so that the next folio, when it comes, is kept better under the tea tin.

I remain, with the whole Circle's shame and its eleven signatures,
T. R. Ainsworth, secretary of the Manx-Welsh Writers' Circle,
Hall No. 2, 14 Manx Walk, Camden Town, London NW1 · 30 May 2014

The Reconstructed Register — Spring of 2014

The folios and meetings below are what the hall can account for, Tuesday by Tuesday. The one blank row is the week the register cannot. Use the decades of the Circle's own memory — and, where it agrees, of the writer's own career, 1946 to 1997 — to read the register by period, or use the buttons to walk the spring week by week. Without them the register stands in full order below, as it must.

Read in Company

The Circle keeps the company of houses that keep registers better than we do. The minutes of the Council of the Sword of the Dynasty, Aleppo, 4 December 958 show a clerk whose reed never once leaves the roll, and who writes the fog into the margin instead of letting it write the margin for him; we have sent the page to Mrs. Farrant, though we have not asked her to come back. On the keeping of names out of a small square mile, the term report on the City of London, Form 4C, Coopers' Lane School sets down a pupil that is good at keeping the way it keeps walls, and we would be good at keeping the way it keeps, if we had its wall. The whole shelf returns to the Webstuffs front page, where this apology stands with the other letters of the house.
