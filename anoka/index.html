Anoka County · Office of the Clerk · Answers Desk · Issued October 31

Anoka, Minnesota

Frequently Asked Questions — asked the way the town asks them, answered the way the town answers

  1. 1 · What exactly is Anoka, and how do you say it?

    Anoka (ə-NOH-kə — one correction, and no second) is a city in, and the county seat of, Anoka County, Minnesota, United States. It sits at the confluence of the Rum and Mississippi Rivers, where two rivers argue briefly, agree, and then flow together toward a town that has had to accommodate the argument ever since. It is a northern suburb of the Twin Cities, which is the description its visitors give it; the town itself prefers county seat, because the courthouse does the actual work and the suburb is just what the interstate sees.

  2. 2 · How many people live there?

    17,921, by the 2020 census, which is to say 17,921 people, give or take the pigeons, which are not counted but object to it anyway. For a town of roughly 18,000, the paper trail is disproportionately long: the oldest standing residence, the Shaw–Hammons House, was built around 1852 and listed on the National Register in 1979, and the answers desk has been receiving the same eight questions ever since the county learned to type. The paper trail reaches further afield than the census: the desk keeps one consignor’s card from a Totnes estate sale — the card of a folded protein offered as Lot 7, whose angles agreed at the bench — filed out as Lot 7 — One Protein, Whole, in Its Fold of Harrowgate & Voss, Sale No. 47, where the theodolite that verified the fold stands beside it at Lot 9.

  3. 3 · Why is Anoka the “Halloween Capital of the World”?

    Because in 1920 it hosted one of the first Halloween parades — a fact the county has been spending ever since, carefully, in orange. The claim is not that Anoka invents the holiday; it is that Anoka paraded first, and it has not stopped. The town continues to celebrate the holiday each year with several parades: the main downtown one, the early one for the small children, and a third procession whose route is described, in the township minutes, only as through the maples. A 1920 parade outranks a 1931 costume, and the answers desk will not arbitrate further. The desk does keep one other mascot card on file, for comparison: the angular video-game spokesperson from Los Angeles who lasted a single expo season in 1995 before being quietly shelved, whose short and bright life is written up as the Polygon Man travel entry in the house's West Coast folder. On Halloween night itself, the county goes on the air from the plaza instead of the streets, and the 1974 file card from which it reads the town's definition is preserved as the radio play excerpt The City, Defined (Or Not), Station WANC.

  4. 4 · What water is the town built on?

    Two rivers at once, which is the only way to be built here: the Rum River, the smaller of the two, meets the Mississippi at the confluence, and the county's maps have pointed at that bend since the surveyors first crossed it. The bend is a short drive from the courthouse and a shorter drive from the oldest house on Coon Rapids Dam Road, where the 1870 Greek Revival portico has been framing the water and the traffic ever since. Residents describe the Mississippi from the bridge; they do not describe the Rum, because the Rum is the town's.

  5. 5 · How do you get in and out?

    U.S. Highways 10 / 169 and State Highway 47 are three of Anoka's main routes — the 10/169 overlap doing the long-haul, and 47 handling the local crossings and the farmers who have used it longer than it has had a number. There was also a station here on the Northstar Line to Minneapolis, for the days the commute was a timetable rather than a traffic report; the platform is the kind of place you recognize, and the answers desk keeps one cancelled manifest in the drawer for people who ask. The trains are gone in the way trains are gone: still scheduled in somebody's memory, still stopping in the evenings where the light comes in sideways.

  6. 6 · Where is the oldest thing standing in Anoka?

    At the Greek Revival on the Anoka River: the Shaw–Hammons House, built about 1852, expanded in 1870, four square columns, a limestone base, and a paper trail of owners — DeGraff, Follrath, Shaw, Hammons — long enough that the history class memorises it. Its 1979 National Register listing, under the themes of architecture and exploration/settlement, is the town's quietest trophy, and the field trip to it is where the silence in question seven was first documented.

  7. 7 · What is the pigeon question?

    The county's most reliable record is kept by the pigeons on the plaza in front of the courthouse, who have been there through the 1920 parade, the highway renumbering, and the Northstar years, and who answer every visitor by staring at them until the visitor reads the arrowhead markers off their own shoes. The markers keep no record of their own: a green-and-white chevron is a pointer, not a witness, and it has never once interrupted a conversation. The pigeon being monogamous, the male domestics in a way the markers cannot: three strangers once said shh to a beet recipe. Which is to say that in Anoka the silence has a chain of custody — first the three strangers at the oldest house, shushing one album in public, then the same silence, carried east and west, arriving at a beet recipe read aloud by a visiting reading society, and stopping. The recipe survives in one newsletter, marked worst column. The pigeons approve of both.

  8. 8 · Do the arrowhead markers actually work?

    They work the way a lighthouse works from the sea: not at all, and perfectly. The full paperwork for the National Register of Historic Places — the 1966 Act, the arrowhead, the half-millionth property, the 25 percent credit fine print, and the field manners — is folded up at the National Register of Historic Places — Visitor’s Brochure of the Pacific–Great Lakes Heritage Route, which carries Anoka as one of its stops alongside a Queen Anne in Portland and a copper whale that reads its own novel. Read that brochure by the third stop, it creases into a map, and the map comes home here, to the bend of the river, to the portico, and to the pigeon who is keeping the rest of the record whether the county signs or not. Everything else about the town is answered above. Everything about the town is Webstuffs, if you ask it the right way on the right evening.
