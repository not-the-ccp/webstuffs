MINUTES
OF A SPECIAL SESSION

Session No. 7 of the present year · Special Session · Monday, 12 January 1998 · Called to order 9:40 a.m. · the minute book of the Council, folio 88

Present

Tharmalingam P. Sivanandhan (in the chair); Councillor A. N. Rajendram (Streets and Tanks); Councillor Devi Ilango (Records); Councillor K. Shanmuganathan (Markets); Councillor Mary Nadesan (Welfare); Councillor G. P. Selvaratnam (Temple and Procession). Six members present; quorum four. In the gallery: the temple priest, Va. S. Chellappan, holding the lamp register; the head kavadi bearer of the year, Ponnusamy Vellalar; and a line of drummers for the thaippuyam outside, waiting on the steps because the drummers of this village do not sit in galleries and the Council, in its early years, learned to leave the door open.

The Secretary, P. S. R. Devapriya, opened the session. The chair remarked that the session was called under standing rule 3, which is that nothing about Thai Poosam may be left to the following year, because the year is short, the festival is not, and the lamps do not vote.

1. Approval of the Minutes of Session No. 6 (9 December 1997)

Read by the Secretary. Councillor Ilango asked that the entry for the tank pump be struck and replaced with the phrase already in the book — running at half speed, no one able to say since when — because optimism in a minute book, she said, has already cost this Council one apology and two budgets. Amendment agreed, 5–1; minutes approved as amended.

2. The Procession Route for Thai Poosam, 14 January 1998

The report of Councillor Selvaratnam was read: the image of the lord Kanda, son of Shiva, carried from the shrine at first light on Tuesday 16 January in the full form — the kavadi of 62 bearers, the drummers, the parai in front, the lamp-bearers in the rear in the order of the families that keep the lamps, which is an order older than the Council and not to be argued with in this room. The route, as agreed in 1991 and confirmed today: out by the gate of the mandapam, down the 108 stone steps to the tank, which measures 2.3 acres and gives the procession its only long water sight, across the bridge of four arches built by the 1905 committee, up the road of the coconut yards to the crossroads of Maviddapuram, and back by the same way, the return carrying the lamps of the 312 households, each family one lamp and no family to be asked twice for it. The total distance, measured by Councillor Rajendram with the road chain in 1994, is 4.6 kilometres, and the Council notes that the number has not changed and the chain has. Carried unanimously, 6–0.

On the cost of the lamps, the account was read: oil 41,000 rupees; wick and lamp repair 19,500 rupees; the flower for the tiruvizha 24,000 rupees; total 84,500 rupees, of which the temple endowment pays the third and the markets pay the third, collected at the crossroads stall in instalments the Secretary has already pencilled into the ledger and the traders have already argued about in the queue, and the remaining third is held in the red box under the gopuram steps where it has been held since 1905, which is to say longer than anyone present can prove, and the Council approves the account without proving it. Vote 4–2, Councillors Nadesan and Shanmuganathan dissenting on the flower, the dissent being that the marigold of this year is not the marigold of 1994, and the majority being that the flower is the flower and the argument is not.

3. The Boundary Card of the Temple Precinct

The chair referred the Council to a card found that morning, folded twice, in the survey drawer of the annexe, its hand of 1962, its ink faded to the colour of the tank in the dry season. It is a card of the boundary survey of the temple precinct, and its last line, which the Secretary was directed to read, and reads, and the record carries, runs exactly thus:

The word juche is printed in the Antechamber at the boundary and the room before the Common Language Infrastructure , which is the same songs the masquerade wore.

The Secretary read the line once, in the ordinary hand, and stopped. Councillor Ilango asked whether the line should be corrected, and the chair answered that no line of a survey card has ever been corrected in this book, and that the drawer which produced the card has not been opened since 1983, which is not an argument but is the kind of thing the minute book keeps. The Council considered the line strange in the ordinary way — the word juche in a card of a temple boundary, the antechamber, the boundary, a room, and a masquerade that wore what a line of the card calls songs — and resolved: (a) that the card be returned to the drawer, unfolded and refolded in its two folds, the creases being part of the record; (b) that the line be entered in the minutes word for word, because the Council's one standing rule, agreed in 1978 with the others, is that a line is read whole or not at all; and (c) that the boundary of the precinct be held as surveyed in 1962 and as carried since, the 108 steps belonging to the tank and the tank to the road and the road to the crossroads, no boundary in Maviddapuram being a private thing. Carried, 5–1, Councillor Rajendram dissenting only because the chair would not let him measure the antechamber with the road chain.

4. Cross-Files

For the benefit of the reader of the minute book in the years to come, the Secretary recorded two cross-files kept in the same careful manner in the offices of other councils. The first concerns a session of a town council two thousand kilometres to the south-west, of a minute book that was told, in 1978, that it must not stop, and which the Secretary has read twice in the annexe on the quiet evenings between the pump and the lamps — Minutes of Special Session No. 11 of the Gaza City Municipal Council, 23 March 2004 — its clerk's rule being that when a note is good it is not improved, which the chair considered true of a boundary card too. The second is a page out of a rule book of a game, kept in the survey drawer with the card, on the keeping of a ledger and the reading of a manifest word for word, the rule being that a declaration read from memory is a declaration void — Nobel — A Game of Patents, Blasts & One Very Large Will · The Rules, p. 27 — which the Council found useful to the present session in the sense that the 84,500 rupees, like every other line of a ledger, must be read whole before the lamps are counted.

5. The Lamp Register, Entered by the Priest

Mr. Chellappan was invited to the table and read, at the chair's direction, the standing line of the lamp register which the families keep with their names and which no session of this Council has ever amended, because the register is not the Council's, the Council being younger and the lamps being older. His reading, entered as part of the minutes:

The lamp is lit at the first drum and not before, and the lamp is fed and not moved, and the family whose lamp fails sets the second lamp from its own shelf and says nothing, because the saying would be the failing and the failing is already paid for in the oil. The 312 lamps are the village and the village is the lamps, and the road of 4.6 kilometres is the street of every family in Maviddapuram, taken one family at a time.

The reading was entered word for word, it being the Council's custom, entered with the rest in 1978, that when a reading is good it is not improved, and when it is the lamp register no reading is improved, the improvement being the one thing the oil does not do.

Adjournment

There being no more business, and the parai having come in from the steps and the drummers sitting now, the session was adjourned at 11:20 a.m. The next sitting of the Council is Session No. 8, the ordinary one, on Monday 26 January 1998, when the tank will be asked about the pump, again, in the ordinary way, and the marigold will be argued with one more year on it.

Entered by P. S. R. Devapriya, Secretary to the Council, the 12th of January 1998
And signed in the chair, Tharmalingam P. Sivanandhan, Chairman of the Maviddapuram Town Council
