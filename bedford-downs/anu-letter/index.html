To the Editor

On the university in the capital — from the desk of a Quilty of Bedford Downs, now resident in Acton

ACTON · CAPITAL TERRITORY · 35°18′ S · 149°08′ E

To the Editor,

Your editorial of the 9th, on the “expensive silences” of the national university, counts up the cost of a campus that stands apart from the country, and asks why a nation that runs on stations should pay for a single hill in Acton. I am the third Quilty to sign the store accounts of Bedford Downs Station — the Kimberley property 84 kilometres west of Warmun and 109 north of Halls Creek, which my nephew is at this hour weathering in balsa at 1:252, sheet by sheet, in the log he keeps for it — and I write in answer for the university, because my daughter is in her fourth year there, and because I know what a station is for, and I believe I know what a university is for, and I suspect the two are closer than your editorial admits.

The Australian National University is a public research university in Canberra, the capital of the country, and a member of the Group of Eight. Its main campus in Acton encompasses seven teaching and research colleges, in addition to several national academies and institutes — the Academy of Science, the Academy of Humanities, the institutes that make a capital a capital rather than merely a place where the trains stop. It was established in 1946, and it is the only university to have been created by the Parliament of Australia. When Parliament set the terms down, they were not improved afterwards, and that is a law the sister bench at Vulcan Foundry, Newton-le-Willows would recognise at once: a fact, once set down in order, is not improved by the person who set it down. Their works ledger runs 1832 to 1970 without a single entry flattered. The university's own paper trail is honest in the same way: it traces its origins to Canberra University College, established in 1929 and integrated into ANU in 1960, which means the campus is older than the statute, and the statute caught up with the buildings rather than the buildings with the statute.

If you will permit me a ledger of my own, here are the figures, in order, and I ask that they be printed in order: 10,252 undergraduate students and 7,128 postgraduate students enrolled as of 2023, with 4,517 staff. The university's endowment stood at A$1.8 billion as of 2018, which is not money that appears by favour; it is the same slow accumulation of small honest entries that a station's store accounts keep from before 1906 to the present day. The university counts six Nobel laureates in its records. I would observe that none of them was manufactured on the hill for the convenience of an editorial; each was made there because the hill allowed it, in the way a well allows water — by being dug where the ground is right and then held still.

Now the family matter, which is also my one real objection to your paper's style. On the back of a 1947 well-requisition form, in my grandfather's hand, there is a line the family has declined to correct: “This inhumanity is, we think, is held a little more still.” I have never been able to say what the stillness is held against; I know the country that produces it. It is the sound a place makes when it stops letting the flood, the drought, or the market speak over its own accounts. My grandfather wrote it the week the first lectures began at the new university, and the two facts have sat together in the family ever since. The double is remains. That is not a typo to be caught by an editor's pencil; that is the ledger's law applied to a sentence, and the sentence keeps.

The drive out of Halls Creek to here takes days, and the last of it goes under water. On my nephew's base plate the single head of cattle stands at the far gate — one, never more — because a station this remote is mostly emptiness with a name on it. That, I suggest, is your whole question answered: the hill in the capital is also emptiness with a name on it, and its name is written in an act, so the emptiness holds. I would rather have the hill held than the accounts. The red dirt is the same red from Acton to the Kimberley; I brought some back on my boots, and it is still in the hallway.

Yours faithfully,

E. R. Quilty
Acton, ACT 2601
P.S. The single-axle truck on the balsa base has not been moved. It was to be “given up on and then forgiven.” I inquired, and it was forgiven.
