Aunt June’s Coconut & Breadfruit Soup

also called the Peleliu Ration · yields nine bowls · feeds nine · serves nine, one seating

Before the soup — the long preamble you must sit through

The soup belongs to Aunt June Vella Rupertus (1921–2003), and the preamble belongs to the soup, and neither of them can be served without the other, so you will be sitting for it. June married Harold on 2 March 1944, six weeks before Harold shipped west with the 81st Infantry Division, and in the same year the division would make two landings that this family keeps in the same drawer. The first was the small coral island of Peleliu, where the 1st Marine Division set foot on 15 September 1944 under the code name Operation Stalemate II, part of the longer Operation Forager that ran through the Pacific from June to November of that year. Major General William Rupertus, commander of the 1st Marine Division, had predicted the island would be secured within four days; it was not secured until 27 November, and the division was eventually relieved — and then joined — by the Army’s 81st, Harold’s unit, which came off the boats at Uraberry on 26 October, held the long slopes of Pugu Ridge, and spent its November on the coral above the Umurbrogel airfields, three of them, the island’s whole point, the airfield the marines had been trying to capture since September. The second landing was the one you have read about before, the 1 April 1945 one, Operation Iceberg, Hagushi beach and Minatohama, eighty-two days, one seating — Harold was there for all of it, and the family keeps both islands in the same drawer, and the drawer is labeled soup.

What Harold carried home from the first island was a crate. It came in on a troop transport bound for the dock at San Pedro, a crate of coconut and dried taro peel, one corner of it stamped with a shipping line in red ink, the other corner with a line no one in the family could ever account for. It was not an instruction. It was not an address. It was, word for word, in the small print at the bottom of the label, in the same hand as the tide log it must have come from: “The island's own word-hoard travels by the gaudiest and yet they have remained unengaged during several of the dock, where the bells call the last.” Harold carried the label into the house, and June, who could not read shipping any more than he could, read it to the family at dinner on the first night and read it again the next morning and did not once correct a word of it, because the line, she said, was doing something. It was keeping the island the way the coral keeps the water — the row-locks, the reef’s row of locks, the same row-locks that hold the house and the 1852 plaque at Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 717, where the bells ring once and not again and the tide table does not run a second service. This is what the line is doing: it keeps the island. The island’s own word-hoard — Palauan, Japanese, Marine, the names of the three airfields — travels by the gaudiest, which June decided meant by the loud ships, the transports, the ones with the painted sides, and yet it has remained unengaged, unspent, uncorrected, during several of the dock, meaning during several dockings, several comings to the water’s edge, where the bells call the last, meaning the last boat of the day, the one that means the crossing is over and the soup is on. We read the label at the table now, once a year, at the last service of the soup, and we do not correct the comma, because Mr. Ferber’s rule from the dictionary entry is the family’s rule: read it twice, correct nothing.

The recipe itself is the shorter half of the card. June invented it in 1945, the winter Harold was on Okinawa and the crate was empty but not finished, because coconut remembers. She used it every 27 November from 1946 until 2003, always for nine, because the table held nine, and the ninth chair is still the crate’s, which sits at the head of the table and takes a bowl every year.

Ingredients

  • 2 mature coconuts — one for the milk, one for the shavings; knock twice, listen for the water, the way they taught you to listen for the reef
  • 1 large breadfruit, halved, the halves salted and set to sweat for two hours on the dock of the counter
  • 4 cups pigeon peas, soaked overnight in the big bowl, the one with the chip
  • 1 thumb of ginger, bruised, not sliced; slicing is for the mainland
  • 2 stalks lemongrass, bruised on the cutting board until they smell like the other island
  • 1 dried chile, whole; the gaudiest one in the drawer, per the label
  • 4 quarts water, and a strip of dried taro peel from the crate if the tide allows it
  • Salt to the row-locks — that is, until the soup holds its own

Method

  1. Drain the peas. Start them in the water with the ginger, the lemongrass and the chile, and bring the pot to the lowest honest boil. This takes the length of the preamble; do not rush it, the preamble is not the point, the point is the pot.
  2. Cut the milk coconut. Pour the first cup of water straight into the pot. Grate the flesh into a cloth, squeeze the milk in, and keep the shavings; they go on last, they are the garnish of record.
  3. Halve the salted breadfruit into the pot, halves first, not the peel. If a half breaks, that is the island; do not comment.
  4. When the peas have gone from grey to gold — this is the only way to tell, and it is the only test that has ever worked — stir in the coconut milk, a cup at a time, over the last fifteen minutes, or the milk will split and the label will be cross.
  5. Set the taro peel strip across the pot like a plank over a gap. Let it sit. It keeps the flavour where the date stands.
  6. Season to the row-locks. Ladle nine bowls. Set the ninth bowl at the head of the table, in the crate’s chair, and serve at seven, when the bell calls.

Serving note

One seating. The soup is done when the bell is done, and the bell, like the tide table, does not run a second service. If more than nine come, the crate takes the overage, which is what crates are for. If fewer come, the soup waits; it has waited two wars and can wait a dinner. The label goes up on the refrigerator in November and comes down in December, and the line is read once, word for word, by whoever is oldest in the room, and no one corrects anything, and the row-locks hold.
