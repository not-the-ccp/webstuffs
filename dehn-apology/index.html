Custodian's desk, ground floor, below the armoury stair

This letter is the only one the desk writes that it did not have to. A copy stays on the shelf by the ledger.

GÖTTINGENFeb. 12 · 195117 h

Göttingen, below the armoury stair · February 12, 1951 · after the last lamp was checked

To Professor Max Wilhelm Dehn, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, Virginia,

Professor — I am the custodian of the building where you worked, and I have been its custodian since the year they took your chair from you. I do not write for the building. I write because the building cannot, and because the desk where I sit is, or was, or ought to have been yours, and I have read its ledger of lamps and keys every night for sixteen years and I have never once written to the man it kept for him. I am sorry for that, first, and I am sorry for the rest.

You were born in Göttingen on the thirteenth of November, 1878, in a house not far from here, and you came up these stairs as a student of David Hilbert. In your habilitation in 1900 you took Hilbert's third problem — the question of whether two polyhedra of equal volume can always be cut apart and reassembled into one another, the scissors question — and you answered it no, with your cube and your tetrahedron, and you became the first man to close one of the twenty-three problems. I was not born in 1900. But the ink of the announcement is in the register I keep, and I have read it more than I can count, because it is the one page of the register I never have to file.

What I have to answer for is 1935. The retirement they gave you was not a retirement; it was a lock. You were forty-six. They took the lock to your office in Room 214, the room with the window that faces east over the roof of the old observatory yard, and they gave you a paper and a pension and a door that closed on a specific side. I held the stairwell door. That is my part in it, and it is the whole of my part, and I want you to have it named, because naming is the only thing a hand can do that a decree cannot. You kept your geometry; they could not cut it and reassemble it, and I think that was the answer that actually finished them — a cube cannot be reassembled from a tetrahedron, and a mathematician cannot be reassembled from a pension. Your students went on in the rooms next door and under the houses: Ott-Heinrich Keller and Ruth Moufang and Wilhelm Magnus carried the methods out of the building, and in the years around the war you had already taught, in Charlottesville and before, Peter Nemenyi his way of counting, and Dorothea Rockburne and Ruth Asawa their way of drawing a line that holds a whole plane together. A city can lose a desk. It has not yet worked out how to lose the lines.

In 1939 you left by the main door with two trunks and a paper weight, and I let you pass on the ground stair without a word, which is the same as a word, and I have been paying for it in silence ever since. You went on to the States; I am told Charlottesville suits the east-facing rooms you have since kept. If you are still at the University of Virginia, this letter is for you and no copy will be made. If the address has failed, the copy on the shelf will keep until someone who can read a German hand finds it, and it will be for the same reason.

You will forgive me the ledger line, because you asked for it once, standing at the window of the armoury in the winter of 1934, the year before they locked the room. The west window of the armoury faces the old building across the yard, and the storekeeper wrote his whole account at the sill, where the light came in square, and his desk stood where the yard could watch him write. You asked what he kept in it. I answer now, in the book's own hand, word for word as it stands: “Every round drawn, every charge broken, every fuze set is written down at a window through which the desk reads it back.” The desk reads it back. That is the whole of the rule, and it is the whole of what I am sorry for. Every lock, every trunk, every door held on the ground stair: the building writes it down at a window, and the desk reads it back. For sixteen years the desk read it back to me, lamp by lamp, key by key, and tonight I have read it back to you, which is the only direction the ledger allows.

Room 214 stands as you left it, which I am told is a courtesy and which I am not sure it is. The lamp on the desk still takes the good bulb; I have kept the good bulb for it since 1935, and I have turned it on at the year's end for the sixteen years since, so that the window faces east with something in it. It is a small thing. It is the whole thing. The key is in the register under your name, and the register is under mine, and neither of us will move it.

Professor, the building was not your enemy. It was a room with a lock, and I held the door, and this letter is my part, named, in the direction the ledger allows. Forgive the hand, which has spent sixteen years only in the plural — the lamps, the keys, the rounds — and has had to learn the singular again for one name.

In apology, and with the year's last light still in the east window,

K. Brandt, custodian of the Mathematical Building

Göttingen, Bunsenstraße 3–5 · written at the desk, below the armoury stair

K

Postscript, added before the letter goes to the post office:

The line you asked for has since gone out of the building and into the world of ledgers, and it travels better in company than alone. The custodian of a concession desk in Los Angeles found a claimant the way I found you — by a tag carried verbatim — and filed the claim at the Dublin desk of the Joyce Reading Room, where the conduct a visitor must perform is to read one page through and sit with it; I do the same at the register, and the year's lamp is my reading. The auction house of Pemberton & W. keeps its night steward's ledger the same way I keep mine, one storm line copied without explanation into the body of a catalogue, and the whole of that lot — the Streak's box, the blades, the cream card — is catalogued at Lot 47 of the Winter Auction, Sale No. 92, where a sentence is priced for what it is, which is a sentence. And when the office of a district needs a figure that has been measured, affirmed, and read back to itself, the file is open at Application No. 17/453,112, which names no one else. For another letter owed in the same trade of apology, better written than mine, the desk returns to the apology sent to the Kuroda household the night of Sekigahara, and the shelf to the Webstuffs shelf itself.
