Of the Driest Table

manners at the whale’s feast, Swakopmund and Lüderitz, Namibia — the driest country in sub-Saharan Africa, with one whale, one jug of sauce, and a spoon of maple from two thousand miles away

Republic of Namibia · capital and largest city: Windhoek · nearest neighbour without contact: Zimbabwe, 660 feet across the Zambezi at Kazungula · the whale: twenty-seven fathoms by the carpenters’ line · the reckoning for the full feast: 9.50 Marks · the book does not round

I · On the Country, and How to Name It

The country is Namibia, officially the Republic of Namibia, a country of Southern Africa, and the manners of this chapter begin with the naming of it, because a guest who cannot name the place cannot keep the manners of the place. It borders the Atlantic Ocean to the west, Zambia — by the Caprivi Strip — and Angola to the north, Botswana to the east, and South Africa to the south; and in the northeast, approximating a quadripoint, Zimbabwe lies less than 200 metres (660 feet) away along the Zambezi river near Kazungula, Zambia. The capital and the largest city is Windhoek. The country is the driest in sub-Saharan Africa, which is not a description; it is the first rule. The book keeps it in this order because the country keeps it in this order: it has been inhabited since prehistoric times by the Khoi, San, Damara and Nama people; around the 14th century, immigrating Bantu peoples arrived as part of the Bantu expansion; and from 1600 the Owambo formed kingdoms, such as those of the Kwanyama and the Ovakavi, whose names the reader will hear in the market at Windhoek before he hears them in any book. Three manners follow. A guest who says Namibia has four neighbours is to be excused; a guest who insists on it is to be corrected once, and the correction is that there are five, and a sixth at 660 feet. A guest is to remember that a country can be near without touching: the manners of the Zambezi at Kazungula are the manners of a ford, and one does not step into a ford one is not fording. And a guest in the driest country is to speak of water last, and then only to pour it.

II · On Water at the Thirsty Table

At a table in the driest country, water is not poured; water is presented, once, unasked, from a cask of fourteen gallons, and the presentation is the whole ceremony. The chef who kept the whale’s table had come to Swakopmund from 10 February Street in Montréal, where the water arrives in a long glass from the 1849 line laid under Saint-Paul Street, and he brought with him one object, which he carried in a whalebone box: a copy, cast in bone, of the ring of two keys — water-key and door-key, on one ring — which M. J. Hays, engineer of record, carried on his tour days in the spring of 1845. He sets the ring at the head of the long table before service. It opens nothing now. He does it anyway, because a table in a dry country is a table that has been promised water, and a promise is kept in the same shape the promise was made in. The book records that he pours once, unasked, from the cask, to every seat, in the same order the seats were filled, and that no second pour is ever made, because a country that has learned to count its water has no use for a second pour. The route the water came from — the eleven hatches, the thirty-four hydrants, the twenty-six watch-houses — is kept at The United Canadas — A Review, Montréal Dining Gazette No. 47, and the chef reads the warden’s line to his table on the first night of every season: the water is the same water that ran the first time, only slower now. His table, in a country of red sand, concurs: slower, and very good.

III · On the Whale, and One Line Kept

The whale came to the sand below Swakopmund in September, on the season the Atlantic is rough, and she was, by the carpenters’ line, twenty-seven fathoms, which is to say fifty metres and the carpenters were in no mood for fractions. The harbour has a trade in this, and the trade is a manner: the carpenters of Swakopmund build from whale, and what they built for the town’s feast was the table itself — the ribs arched into a hall over the long boards, the keel for the bench at the head, and the head, hollowed and lashed to the deck of the hall, for the tureen in which the sauce is kept. The sauce is the chef’s own, carried in a jug from Montréal in the name of the house that smokes on spruce by the Council room window: it is the same brown, sharp, slightly sweet order as the sauce that goes on the trout at 10 February Street, and the chef will not say which order it is in, and he will not stop saying it is in that order. The gale of 12 September moved the hall while the sauce was still in the head, and the book of manners, which keeps every line it is shown, keeps the harbour log’s line exactly as it stands:

The sauce is brown, sharp, and slightly sweet, in the whale’s head jogged about very violently, and Gabriel was seen descending her side.

Three clauses, and the book gives the guest all three. The first is the sauce: brown, sharp, slightly sweet, in the order, on the fish, on the bread, and whether one likes it or not. The second is the head: the sauce that night stood in the hollowed head of the whale, lashed to the deck, and when the gale took the hall the tureen moved with it, which the log records in one word — jogged — about very violently, and the sauce, being brown and heavy, arrived at every seat in the order it had left the jug in. The third is the man: Gabriel J. Hays, nephew of M. J. Hays, the chef’s second, who had followed the chef from the small room on Vaudreuil Street to 10 February Street and thence to the sand at Swakopmund, and who was seen descending the whale’s side — the carpenters’ ladder, on the flank of the rib-hall, down to the hold below the keel, to take up the jug the gale had thrown — her side, for the log keeps the whale as the harbour keeps her, and the book keeps the log. Gabriel turns a second bone ring before every service, the one the kitchen keeps behind the pass, and he has never once lost the jug, which is the only manner on a dry coast worth learning by heart.

IV · On Maple, Brought West by South

The last course of the whale’s feast is a square of dark bread, a spoon of maple, a small pat of butter, and nothing else, brought in a wooden box the kitchen has not painted. The maple is counted against a card that travelled west by south from Ann Arbor, Michigan — Card No. 214, Huron River Maple Bread, of the Wadsworth family recipe ledger, the county seat of Washtenaw County on the Huron River, population 123,851 in 2020 — and the count is a quarter cup per plate, in ink, the same count the kitchen at 10 February Street keeps against that card each season. The whale’s table was scaled to the company the harbour calls a harbour — four hundred plates — and the ledger’s scaling bench, which runs from one loaf to the company it calls town meeting, was not asked to rise higher, which the book notes as a kindness: a family recipe offered to a whale and accepted at four hundred is a recipe that has been scaled all the way to the sea and stopped. The ledger, the chef is told, has not objected, and the ledger has not been wrong about the bread in any country, in any season, on any table, dry or otherwise. The card itself, its ribbon and its bench, is kept at Huron River Maple Bread — Card No. 214, Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger.

V · On the Reckoning, and the Book’s Count

The book dines the full feast once, in the harbour’s order, and keeps the count as the carpenters keep theirs. Tap a course to mark it; the book keeps the running total in Marks, and the book does not round.

Course 1

The Clear Broth, One Pour

A clear broth, and with it the water presented once, from the cask of fourteen gallons, in the order the seats were filled. The second pour is not a matter of taste; it is a matter of geography.

1.25 M

Course 2

Atlantic Salt-Fish, the Wind Cure

Caught off the coast that does most of the drying, and cured in the wind that does it further. The manner of this course is that the fish is spoken of first and the wind not at all.

2.75 M

Course 3

From the Whale’s Head, the Sauce in the Order

The sauce from the jug, brown, sharp, slightly sweet, in the order, carried down the carpenters’ ladder when the gale takes the hall. The best course of the feast, and the only one the book would carry home, which is to say it was not carried home.

4.50 M

Course 4

Maple, One Spoon to a Plate

A square of dark bread, a spoon of maple, a small pat of butter. The quarter cup per plate, counted against Card No. 214, in ink, four hundred plates, the ledger not objecting.

1.00 M

The book’s count — 9.50 Marks

All four courses, the full feast, as dined. The book does not round.

VI · On the Return, and the Bell That Is Not Rung

The whale’s table is struck in October, the ribs returned to the carpenters’ yard where they wait, unclaimed, for the next hall, and the chef goes back up the coast to 10 February Street, where the stove has not gone cold since the union winter of 1841, where one of the thirty-two seats in the Assembly room is kept empty on the anniversary of 23 July, and where the ring of keys opens nothing and is turned in the fingers before service anyway. A table in the driest country and a table in a city of water keep the same promise, in the same shape: the water is presented once; the sauce is in the order; the jug is not lost; and the last bell — the one the Gazette of No. 47 kept, like the empty seat, like the water that will not be needed — is kept for the fire that will not come. The book of manners, which keeps every line it is shown, has shown this one, and keeps it. The review the chef sailed under is kept at The United Canadas — A Review, Montréal Dining Gazette No. 47, and the whole shelf, the ledger and the walking tour and this chapter and everything else the house has ever kept, is at the shelf.
