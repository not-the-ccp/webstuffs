The Sulaimanov Family Book · Card 11 · filed under H, for honey

Vedeno Honey Cake

Makes one round cake · Vedeno, Vedensky District, Chechnya · in the family since 1994, kept since March 2000

SULAIMANOV
BAKERY · VEDENO

I. On the Column

On the twenty-ninth of March, in the year 2000, a mechanized column of the special Russian Military Police stopped moving in the southern part of the Vedensky District, in the woods and ravines below the village of Zhani. The column had come down the mountain road in the afternoon, and by the end of it the road had produced a price. As the day was finally counted, the cost was forty OMON officers in the column and six more in the relief column sent down to them, killed, and eleven men taken hostage, nine of whom were executed soon after, when the Russian command refused to swap them for the arrested commander Yuri Budanov. The names came to the bakery in pieces, over the following weeks: first by radio, then by the men themselves walking home through the dust. My father Khamzat Sulaimanov was not in the first column. He was in the relief column, one of the ones who came down after, and he came back, and he never said, in the whole rest of his life, which of the six men of that column he had carried off the road. He said only that he would not say it, and the family learned to leave the sentence in place.

II. On the Bakery

My mother Aida ran a small bread shop on the lower road in Vedeno, a room with a steel oven, a flour sack, a scale, and a window that faced the road the column had come down. When the news was coming in, she stopped kneading, and she put the cake in the oven, because it was March and it was the honey one, and the oven was the one thing in the room that answered on its own time. The method for the cake has always been kept the way everything serious in that shop was kept: in the jam jar that stands beside the counter, next to the register, where a folded page of the family book goes in when the day is too full to file it properly. If you ask the family where the method lives, the answer, in my sister’s pencil along the margin of this card, is the whole sentence, word for word:

The jar beside the bakery, the page away for later.

The jar is beside the bakery still, a blue-glass jar of pomegranate jam, the lid wrapped in cloth. The page was away for later for six years — from the spring of 2000, when it went in, to the winter of 2006, when my mother took it out, read it twice, and wrote this card from memory, in her hand, at the shop counter, with the oven still warm. She wrote from memory in the corner of the card, small, as if the memory needed to be on the record.

III. On the Cake

The cake is the mountain cake, the one the Vedeno women make when a road has to be answered: honey and flour and eggs, a spoon of pomegranate molasses pressed into the middle, walnuts broken by hand the night before, and a quarter spoon of cardamom that my mother said came from the shop in Grozny and never again. It bakes in a round tin in a hot oven, it comes out dark at the edges the way a mountain afternoon comes out, and it is served cut in wedges with strong tea, the way the men of the relief column took it when they walked in, one wedge and two fingers of tea, without speaking.

IV. The Cake

Ingredients

  • 200 g honey, runny, warmed by the oven
  • 4 eggs, separated
  • 150 g sugar, for the whites
  • 220 g flour, sifted with the salt
  • ½ tsp salt
  • ¼ tsp cardamom, ground the day of
  • 3 tbsp butter, melted and cooled
  • 1½ tbsp pomegranate molasses, or the darkest jam you have
  • 120 g walnuts, broken by hand, for the middle
  • butter and flour, for the tin
  • strong black tea, for serving

Method

  1. Warm the honey beside the oven until it runs. Beat the yolks into it, then in beat the whites with the sugar until they stand.
  2. Sift the flour, salt and cardamom in, and fold them through with the whites in three additions. Fold in the melted butter.
  3. Butter and flour a round tin. Pour in the batter, and press the broken walnuts into the middle in a circle, the way the road came down.
  4. Press a spoon of the pomegranate into the centre of the walnut circle. It will sink, and that is right.
  5. Bake in a hot oven, 200 °C, for thirty-five to forty minutes, until the edges are dark and the middle springs.
  6. Turn it out on a board. Let it cool completely. Do not cut it the day of.
  7. On the day it is needed, cut it in wedges, one wedge to a place that is no longer in the room, and serve it with strong tea, unsweetened.

V. On Keeping

We make it on the twenty-ninth of March, in every year since, and on no other day in March. The jam jar by the counter still holds the folded page of the family book that this card was written from, and the page is still away for later, in the sense my mother meant: the later is whenever the shop outlives us. The six men of the relief column, and the nine of the eleven whose names the command declined to swap for one, are not in the cake in the way names are in a book. They are in it the way the pomegranate is in it: dark, pressed in at the centre, and holding the whole thing together. My father kept to his silence. The oven kept to its heat. The card ends here, at the bottom edge of the page, with the line my mother did not underline but did not erase: for the road that gave out, and the road that kept going.
