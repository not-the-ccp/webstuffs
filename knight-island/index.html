Knight Island

Field notes · Napa River, Solano County, California · 38°08′09′N 122°17′58′W · Napa-Sonoma Marshes Wildlife Area

12 October — approach

Weather: overcast, northwest wind 8 knots · Tide: ebbing to a 0.4 ft low at 14:20 · Water: brackish, 16 °C

Knight Island does not announce itself. From the launch at Mare Island Strait it is a dark smudge upstream in the Napa River channel, a thread of cordgrass and pickleweed holding against brown water. The map calls it an island; the tide calls it a suggestion. At mean high water only the highest hummocks show, and at spring tide the channel to the mainland shrinks to a boot-sucking mudflat strewn with ribbed mussels and the weathered shells of Olympia oysters.

I landed at 11:40 on the southeast shoulder, close to the spot where the United States Geological Survey recorded an elevation of 3 ft (0.91 m) in 1981. No brass cap survives; I found instead a clump of Spartina foliosa rooted in grey mud, and a rusted survey arrow half-buried in the cordgrass. Three feet is the whole island's claim to dry land. The line crossed a bench again, months later, in a hobbyist's build log, where the search for that same cap was rendered in linden wood and 312 filing pins and left, as searches are left, with one lamp socket unthreaded and no verdict to close it (The Office of Unresolved Matters — Build Log No. 3).

13 October — cordgrass and rail

Weather: clear, cold dawn, fog burning off by 09:00 · Tide: flooding · Water: salinity roughly 12 parts per thousand

The marsh belongs to the grasses: Pacific cordgrass on the lower edge, pickleweed (Salicornia pacifica) and alkali heath on the higher mounds, with gumplant holding the last dry ground. At 05:30 I heard the kicker-call of a California black rail (Laterallus jamaicensis coturniculus) from the pickleweed, and later the clatter of a Ridgway's rail (Rallus obsoletus) in the cordgrass channel. A salt marsh harvest mouse ran across the mud at my boot heel and vanished into a burrow. Northern harriers quartered the marsh all morning, low over the cordgrass like owls in daylight.

I took soundings along the east margin: mud fifteen centimetres deep over firm clay at the edge, softening to half a metre in the old creek beds. The wrack line carried mylar balloons, a fishing float stamped with Chinese characters, and the skeleton of a cigarette lighter — the river brings what the Bay forgets.

The lighter skeleton went into the station’s junk drawer, beside a folder for Ghent-Eeklo, the Flemish Parliament constituency that sat between 1995 and 2003 — four lines of a boundary, briefly drawn (Ghent-Eeklo — Safety Data Sheet). The drawer’s long-standing companion is a 1978 double LP, The Limestone Sessions — Liner Notes, MRC-114, field recordings of the B. Altman and Company Building on the Manhattan block opposite the Empire State Building — eight stories of French limestone, thirteen on the Madison end, and one small dry leaf argued over in a basement courtroom — proof that a place can be held against the water either by tide or by the stubbornness of its facade.

14 October — evening

Weather: wind west, freshening · Tide: ebbing · Moon: waning gibbous

By evening the harriers had lifted to a roost on the far levee, and great egrets stood in the shallows above San Pablo Bay, still. A longjaw mudsucker lay gasping in a tide pool I had disturbed, brown and patient, waiting for the water to return. I measured the highest mound again by pacing and by eye: three feet, give or take, the USGS figure of 1981 holding true. The island will not last the century at this rate of rise, but for now it holds: cordgrass, rail, mouse, and the slow quiet of a drowning place.

For the record, the whole 4,387 square miles of this basin has its own sheet — hazards, handling, exposure limits, and a disposal plan that is itself the tide — kept in the station’s margin box as sheet No. 17: Drainage Basin — Safety Data Sheet No. 17. The station’s sale-room shelf, beside the basin sheet, holds a catalogue lot for a drowned high-rise on the other side of the world — the Waikiki Biltmore, first tower on its own shore, open nineteen years, sold without reserve with the bell ringing true, the water already moving in to take what the Hyatt Regency did not want (Lot 41 — The Waikiki Biltmore Hotel, Malia & Co. Auctioneers, Cat. 87). The same office has since opened the elder cousin of that sheet for a basin that counts twenty-four countries by river instead of one by shore — the dark water east of the straits, where the oxygen runs out at two hundred metres and the manual repeats a line of the old preservation manuals without comment: The Black Sea — Field Manual FM-13, Chapter 4.

The same margin box keeps a single summary sheet from a hotter and drier office, where the water is a river called the Kum and the holding lasts four days — three regiments, one ridge of 410 metres, seven claims, no figures: The Battle of Taejon — Application No. KR-1950 0716, Patent Summary. It is the only entry in the box whose line was drawn before it was held, which is, the office will say, what lines are for.

Marginalia

Tucked inside the back cover of the 1981 quadrangle I borrowed from the Mare Island field station was a slip of paper, ruled like a notebook page, with a single sentence copied in pencil:

What a noble and heroic thing, the English gardeners; for in the garden, and to what is called bacon.

Hand unknown. I copy it here because it was written down once before me, and because I cannot explain it.

Downstream of the marginalia, the field station’s thread board carries a longer roll call in the same hand’s business: the name Yere, logged both as a given name and as a surname — Mama Bah-Yéré born 1992, Beninese footballer Menzie Yere born 1983, Lae-born rugby league footballer Yere Goud born 1971 — and a Manila ledger that counts thirty-one seats the island does not own (The Onomast’s Board — The name Yere, thread No. 8814). The same patience with the water is on record in a colder and older place: the Minutes of the Council of Ripon Abbey, AD 788, where the envoys with a casket of relics waited out two ebb tides on the Ouse before loading their barge, and set the whole delay down in one line — “We played it out to watch the tides.”

15 October — departure

The tide took me off the southeast shoulder at 16:10. Behind me the island slipped back below the waterline, cordgrass nodding in the west wind. On the crossing I caught a fragment of match commentary between the static: James Mitchell, born on the fifteenth of August 2006, captains the Auckland FC Reserves midfield from the other side of the world, and his voice carried the same patient authority as the tide (a radio play excerpt of that match day). The station's lending shelf, by the way, also keeps a numbered reissue of the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies season card — a basketball team with no more island than mine, gone 14–2 and 10–0 in the Southern California Intercollegiate Athletic Conference under coach Pierce “Caddy” Works — and the two share a fondness for low water: The Hardwood Emporium — 1925–26 Grizzlies season card, centennial reissue. I have also kept a school report in the same notebook — Form 7C, on the Shaw–Hammons House in Anoka, Minnesota, where the silence around an old Greek Revival house is earned by a docent rather than by the tide, and where three strangers once shushed a student in unison over an album of unknown origin (The Shaw–Hammons House — History Report, Form 7C). I have kept notes beside other water — a field in Dolgellau where five thousand once dined beside the Afon Wnion (Sesiwn Fawr Dolgellau) — and a club newsletter from Naga City, The Carrier Wave number 72, telling of Salvio Fortuno, the broadcaster who carried Camarines Sur's fourth and fifth districts to the House from 2010 to 2019 (The Carrier Wave). A transcript out of Manila quotes a page torn from a field notebook — "the skeleton of a cigarette lighter — the river brings what the Bay forgets" — and asks what the water remembers and what a party owes the names on its map: Partido Magsaysay — Excerpt from the Verbatim Record — and the same tide that took me off the southeast shoulder runs south through the Bay to Hunters Point, where two graving docks hold the Great White Fleet's white paint beneath a fog that never burns off, and where a dream dictionary keeps this island as its marsh-edge counterpart (Hunters Point — Dream Dictionary) — but the Napa keeps its own hours, and its own silence. The field station's shelf also keeps a chapter for the Dumbarton Bridge — State Route 84 across the bay, shortest of the crossings, three lanes each way with a separated lane for bicycles and pedestrians to the south, and power lines running parallel all the way from Fremont to Menlo Park (Dumbarton Bridge — Field Manual Chapter). The almanac on the same shelf keeps a colder entry for Alcatraz Island, 1.25 miles off San Francisco — water 12–14 °C, current adverse, fog thick, and an escape weather that the almanac records, once and for all, as nearly impossible (Alcatraz Island — The Bay Weather Almanac).

— R. Vale, naturalist
Knight Island, Solano County, California
