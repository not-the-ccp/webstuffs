Johan von Blanc

Michel Le Blanc · 1738, France — 21 January 1796, Karlskrona, Sweden

Actor and acrobat · leader of the Gemenasiska Sällskapet, the Gemenasian Company · director of the Comediehuset, Gothenburg, 1780–1786

Died, at Karlskrona in Sweden, on the twenty-first day of January 1796, Johan von Blanc, born Michel Le Blanc in France in 1738, actor and acrobat, leader of the travelling theater known as the Gemenasiska Sällskapet — the Gemenasian Company — and for six seasons director of the Comediehuset, the first permanent theater in Gothenburg. The notice appeared in the Post- och Inrikes Tidningar. The playbills at the Comediehuset were taken down and hung with black crepe, and the house stood dark for a week.

He came to Sweden as a young man with nothing but a rope, a tumbling mat, and a gift for falling without injury. He danced the slack rope in the market squares of the realm, turned somersaults between the acts of French farces, and learned Swedish on the road, from Malmö to Turku. By the late 1760s he led his own company, a wagon train of players, musicians, and scene painters that toured barns, ballrooms, and town halls, carrying its own lamps and its own repertoire of comedy, intrigue, and acrobatic interlude.

In 1780 the company came to Gothenburg, and the city gave it a house. The Comediehuset, beside the canal, became the first permanent theater in Gothenburg, and von Blanc its director from opening night until 1786. He rebuilt the auditorium for four hundred seats, hung oil lamps and wax candles in a chandelier of his own devising, and opened with a season of Molière and Swedish premieres. He played Harlequin and the jealous husband, the acrobat and the lover, sometimes in the same evening; he taught his company to tumble, to fence on stage, and to speak so that the back bench could hear. Sailors from the East Indiamen, merchants, officers of the garrison, and apprentices sat together in the dark and laughed at the same jokes.

He left the directorship in 1786 and withdrew to Karlskrona, the naval port, where he kept a small house near the Admiralty Church and taught fencing and declamation to the cadets. His wife, Elisabeth, née Sjöberg, survived him, with two children: a son who sailed with the archipelago fleet, and a daughter who married a scene painter in Stockholm. She kept the house by the Admiralty Church, and her recipe book stayed in the family: card 1796, slack-rope buns of molasses and caraway that kept aboard the archipelago fleet, is written out in full here: Slack-Rope Buns — A Family Recipe.

Among his papers the burial clerk found a single sheet not in his hand, a clipping or a scrap of letter from another world entirely, which read:

Ofanaite Ana Dewes's submission to the 13th district, elected in November 2006, and the Praetor 600 closer to 3,700 nmi with the last line survives, and it worked.

No one in Karlskrona knew Ofanaite Ana Dewes, no 13th district, and no ship or machine called Praetor 600. The clerk copied the sentence into the margin of the register and closed the book. It may have been a scrap tucked into his trunk by mistake, the last line of a letter never sent, or a line from a play he meant to write. What is plain is this: the last line survives, and it worked. The curtain fell on the twenty-first of January 1796; the company played on without him for a season or two, and then the Comediehuset fell silent.
