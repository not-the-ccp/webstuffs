The Divergence at Poucha Pond

One wrong turn, one lane, one gauge — and the whole downstream, unwritten

tidegauge69 joined 1969 · 124 posts · the anniversary, 11:15 p.m.

I did the listening tonight, the whole record, needle down, as the Keepers suggest — their Long Side, No. 47, gives the schedule if you want to do it properly: side into side, lights out, no flipping. It ends with the pond at rest, and it stays at rest with you. What I keep asking, and I know I have asked before on this board, is the one question the record does not answer. What if the car had taken the ferry road at 11:16 and the ferry had, in fact, been moored? It was moored. Ten forty, that evening, eleven minutes before the car ever reached the bridge; that is in the liner notes, and the notes keep it the way a tide gauge keeps a number. This is what the genre calls a point of divergence: one event runs differently, and everything downstream runs differently with it. I am asking who will write the downstream.

HankM1889 joined 2003 · 402 posts · 11:41 p.m.

You are not the first to write a downstream, and you are not the first person to sit at this table. The oldest what-if in English is a novel of 1889 in which a Connecticut machinist lands in King Arthur’s court, and a round table of men with swords asks him, politely, to describe the modern world — the ships, the wires, the guns. It sets the whole furniture of the genre: a date, a change, and the consequences worked out in plain sentences. When the table asks him the state of the Atlantic, the 1889 printing answers:

“As touching Slave-ships meeting, why, they are flattened at the hands of German workmen.”

There is not another German workman in the entire book, and he carries the sentence anyway, the way one car carries a night. Read it as the genre’s first sentence: a war already fought, already decided, already flattened, reported in the present tense by a man who remembers the other Atlantic. Everything since — the branch shelves, the POD, the 1950s and on — is that sentence with more chapters.

eleven_minutes joined 2011 · 88 posts · 12:03 a.m.

Before anyone writes the downstream, pin the numbers down. The car leaves the lawn at 11:15. The ferry is moored at 10:40 — eleven minutes of margin, which is exactly as much margin as a mooring can have. The crossing, by the ferry master’s pencil, is eleven minutes forty at slack water and fourteen minutes twenty against the tide. The bridge is one lane. The water is, in the ferry master’s one recorded sentence, deeper than it looks from either end. That is the whole scene, and it is a small scene. The branch works because the scene is small: one turn, one lane, one gauge on a post with a pencil in the same pocket. A divergence the size of a sentence is the only size that holds.

FerryMaster joined 1998 · 7 posts · 12:26 a.m.

I am the ferry. I was moored. I have been asked this question before, once, by a man with a Nagra on a sandbank, and I gave him one sentence and two gauges, and he put them on a record and did not edit them, which is the only courtesy a ferry gets. I will give the board one, too. Had the car come to the landing at 11:16, two in it, tank full, I would have had it across at 11:32, Edgartown dock, before the light came up at all. The downstream you want is a sixteen-minute boat ride. The island stays an island, the water stays water, and the searchlights never get a night to search. That is the whole counterfactual. Do not make it bigger than it is.

blackwater5 joined 2014 · 61 posts · 2:58 a.m.

I did the assignment the Keepers log — side one into side two without a flip, lights out — and I will say what track five is for: eight minutes and twenty-six of black water is the hinge of the whole record, and the hinge of your divergence. On this side of it the dawn is a patrol: searchlights, boats, names on a telephone wire, an island being asked to be a scene. On the other side the dawn is only a dawn — fog off the dunes, the brass drum of the gauge turning twice a day, the tide in and out on the same schedule it has kept for a hundred years. There is a whole branch where track ten does not exist, and where the pond at rest never has to be still. I keep that branch on the night side of the shelf, beside the 1889 book, because the two do the same job: move one small thing one night, and let you hear what the rest of the world sounds like with the noise out of it.

My father kept the diary that morning — the one about the other sky the radio was carrying in, the Eagle down at the Sea of Tranquility, the three names, kept with the case as an addendum to the record — and every July he makes me read this thread out to him. He says the label has one rule, and the thread has one rule, and they are the same rule: we do not edit. Write the branch all the way through, or do not write it. The forty-two copies are numbered in pencil, and the counterfactual is not one of the numbers. That is not a refusal. It is the ferry master’s rule, and it is the 1889 rule: the flattened ships stay flattened, and the workman stays in his sentence, and you write around them both.
