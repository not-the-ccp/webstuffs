Montrose Arena, Winter Room
11 March 2018

To the National Hockey League
thirty-two teams · the United States and Canada
c/o the Winter Room, Montrose Arena

Dear Commissioner —

I write, on behalf of the Winter Room and its steward, to make a formal apology for three errors found on the league’s page of the Winter Room refreshment ledger, in the course of a night check on the ninth of this month. That page has been kept for the whole of my service, and on it I have held the account of the National Hockey League: thirty-two teams, twenty-five of them in the United States and seven in Canada, one of the major professional sports leagues of the two countries together, and — in the house’s standing phrase — the premier ice hockey league in the world. A keeper of a page like that is entitled to a single small grace, and the grace is that the page be right. Mine was not.

The first error was in the count. Where the league keeps seven Canadian teams, I had written six, and I had carried the six on for eleven seasons, which is the whole season of the Manchester Storm that the Winter Room watched, and the eleven seasons of the Manchester Storm that its keeper, Zane Kalemba of Saddle Brook, Bergen Catholic, Hotchkiss, and the Princeton Tigers, spent at the exact centre of the page in the house book’s chapter on standing between the posts. The map is still pinned to the window above the refreshment table, and it has said seven all along. I am sorry. The second was smaller and worse: the league’s headquarters, which I had written, in the clerk’s hand, as New … — interrupted, unfinished, never resumed — when the city is New York, and I had the city in front of me on the same window map for every one of those seasons. A ledger that stops at New … is a steward who stopped at New …, and the league has not had from me an answer, only a dash. The third was a transcription. The page quotes the International Ice Hockey Federation, who view the Stanley Cup — the oldest professional sports trophy in North America, awarded annually to the league playoff champion at the end of each season — as one of the “most important championships available to the sport.” I had written available to the club. The Cup is not available to a club. It is the sport’s, and the quotation is the sport’s, and I had narrowed it in the ledger by the width of one word.

The apology has a fourth part, and it is this. At the foot of the face-off column of the same page, where the referee’s first word is kept — a place, always a place, the centre circle, the dots, the corners — I had written, on a storm night I no longer date, a margin line that the book gives no better home to than the page it was written on. It reads, word for word, with the space before the comma, which I am leaving exactly where the pen left it:

It is a place, face-off , and the crowd that is new to the crease will keep whatever it is a shootout.

You will ask what it means, and my apology here is an apology of the other kind: I do not know either. What I know is this. The face-off is the referee’s first word and the shootout is the night’s last, and the line sets them side by side the way a ledger sets a dish and its year beside each other — two entries from the same night, kept together because they were there together. And the crowd that is new to the crease — I mean the Storm’s home end, the one that learned, after the home team’s change, to clear the ice behind the net in a single coordinated sweep — that crowd kept whatever the night gave it, and it keeps the line still, in the stands, in the way the whole end sits through a keeper’s single breath. Three days ago, on the eighth of March, the ledger took the keeper’s own final entry — post vacated, pad left unbroken — and whatever the line is, it was written for a building that has kept its part.

The three errors I have corrected in the ink of this month, in the left column, with the date and my hand, as the room requires. The margin line I have not corrected, because it is not wrong; it is kept. If the sentence arrives at a table it is a sentence, and if it does not, it is a ledger, and the room’s rule, which I have broken less often than this page deserved, is that both are kept as found. The fundamentals the line was written against are set down on the notice board, word for word, in Thread Nº 218 — six skaters, one puck, three periods, where the board closes a thread the way the league closes a season: the horn, and the ledger in the book. For the rest of the house’s keeping, the catalogue’s front desk stands at Webstuffs.

Yours in the house’s keeping,

The Night Steward
Montrose Arena, Winter Room · Steward_Montrose

P.S. — On the name, since the page carries it in two hands. In French the league is the Ligue nationale de hockey, the LNH, pronounced [liɡ nasjɔnal dː ɔkː]. I had entered the French name in capitals, the way a ledger enters a city it has not yet skated to. It is entered properly now.
