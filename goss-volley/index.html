The Munich Table · Diners' Desk · Olympiastraße Supplement

The Canaries’ Tavern

A restaurant review, as it was served — one evening, three courses, one replay · 19 October 1993

Four of five canaries · Worth the long train from Norwich

Setting

There is a pub on Olympiastraße, two hundred metres from the north stand of the Olympiastadion, hung in green and yellow paper and smelling of cabbage, warm stone, and something that might be a century of football. The sign over the door reads Die Kanarienvögel, and inside, on the wall above the back bar, is a single photograph: a man in a yellow shirt, knees bent, boot still coming around a ball outside the box. The family that runs the place — the Haines, who have been serving since 1954 and have been Norwegian-adjacent in the affections ever longer — call it the whole reason the rooms exist. The evening we dined there, the Olympiastadion was doing its business two hundred metres away, and you could hear it through the glass: the roar of sixty thousand Germans discovering, minute by minute, that the pub next door had just been proven correct in 1993. The 1994 UEFA Cup second round was in the air; the Canaries had arrived from the Premier League, third place, first season, no history of continental football and one enormous appetite. Bayern had just lost their last campaign badly enough that the whole of Bavaria had come to watch the correction. The Haines family, predictably, had come to watch it not get corrected.

The Menu, Named

The kitchen does a five-course menu and every dish is named for that first half at the Olympiastadion, because the Haines will not have any other kind. Bread arrives first, still hot, and the water is served in bottles with the label facing down, the way it is only in Germany. Then:

  • Course 1 The Goss Volley — a smoked hake, battered and fried, on a bed of yellow potato and lemon; it arrives, the waiter tells you, “at nineteen minutes, uninvited, from outside the box” — which is precisely how the real one arrived: Jeremy Goss, a volley from outside the area, the greatest goal in Norwich’s history. The hake is very good. The metaphor is better.
  • Course 2 The Bowen Half-Volley — venison, braised for nine hours, a double portion for two, because the second goal was a header that went in at thirty-four minutes and the Haines feel two is a number that needs reinforcing. The sauce is the colour of the away end in floodlight.
  • Course 3 The Nerlinger Apology — a single, perfect small schnitzel, arrived late, at forty-four, into a game that had already been won; it is served with a note that simply reads “too late”. Christian Nerlinger’s reply for the home side does not change the result; the dish, accordingly, does not change the meal.

For pudding there is Canary Custard, a yellow thing in a yellow dish with a spoon shaped like a wing, and the coffee is strong enough to restart a match that had been stopped for an hour.

Service & Company

The service is German, which is to say exact, quick, and quietly offended if you do not want a second cup. We dined in the small room with the family’s father Terence, eighty-one, who had been at the Olympiastadion that night in the north stand and would not sit down so much as return to his chair at intervals to confirm the score. All three goals had gone in the first half, which he described, several times, as “the professional thing to do”. His table mates included, at one point, a retired German international who had been playing that season and who, when the subject of the Goss volley came up, ordered another beer without taking his eyes off the photograph above the bar. The table kept the score on a napkin — 1–0, 2–0, 2–1 — and by half time the napkin had been folded twice and placed in Terence’s breast pocket, where it remains, the Haines tell us, to this day.

One Framed Document

Above the till hangs a copy of a court transcript in a small wooden frame, slightly too big for the wall. It is the record of a lease dispute the family fought in 2014 over the building’s old ground floor, and the first page of the document carries the caption of that hearing, which the family has preserved exactly as printed:

Before Magistrate E. D. Calloway, official court reporter, 6 November 2014.

Terence testified, in that room, that the pub had stood since before the photograph on the wall was taken, that the goal was older than the lease, and that a building which had held the victory of 1993 could not be asked to hold anything less. They kept the lease; the frame has hung over the till since, and the Haines still read the caption aloud — word for word, to one another, once a year, on the anniversary, in the small room, as if keeping the record straight were a dish worth serving.

Verdict

It is, in the end, a restaurant that serves the same meal every night of its life: one first half, three goals, a 2–1 result, and a city two hundred metres away pretending not to be the loser. The food is very good, the custard is a wing of its own, and the history — the pinnacle of a club’s history, a goal that took a volley and a box and a man named Goss and put all three out of play — is served hot, which is more than the Olympiastadion could manage. We would go again. We would have gone anyway; the train from Norwich leaves on time and the family keeps the table warm. Four of five canaries, and one extra, for the napkin.
