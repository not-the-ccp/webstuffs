Polygon Man stood for exactly one season, and then he was gone — which is why a whole small museum in Torrance is given over to him. The character was created in 1995 by the advertising company Chiat/Day together with Steve Race, then president of Sony Computer Entertainment America, as an “edgy” spokesperson for the PlayStation in North America. The brief, word for word from the file cabinet in room 2, was to speak to the gamers and the skate-park crowd that the marketing team feared would be put off by a name like PlayStation — too soft, too Japanese-sounding, too much like a product shelf. So they gave him the angular face, the black T-shirt, the red sneakers, and a vocabulary that the copywriters later described in internal memos as “aggressive in the way a good sandwich is aggressive.”

The short, bright 1995

He ran in print first — a 4-colour lithograph in three national magazines, all dated spring 1995, all still in the frame case by the ticket desk — and then he took the 1995 Electronic Entertainment Expo in June, where the 30-foot version of him, the one in the lobby, first stood in the Sony booth at the Los Angeles Convention Center. Visitors queued past him for a handshaking demo; the expo catalogue listed him as “figure, promotional, height approximately 30 feet, pose fixed.” For a few weeks it looked like he was going to be the face of the whole console.

The shelving

It did not happen. Ken Kutaragi, global head of the PlayStation brand, reacted badly the moment he saw the figure at the EEE: in his telling, later repeated at every press gathering from San Mateo, Polygon Man misrepresented what the machine could do, and the money spent on him had gone to an alternative brand instead of the PlayStation itself. The character was shelved within the month, and the console went on to launch on 3 September 1995 with a plain logo and the line “It’s a computer.” Nobody fired anybody, which the building’s small signboard insists on — the signboard is the only thing in Torrance that ever gets to insist.

What the desk will and will not say

A question comes up every Tuesday, from a retired mail clerk out of Torrance who has visited forty-one times (the log counts): whether the woman in the 1973 photograph over the lobby door — a receptionist at the Chiat/Day annex, long retired — had anything to do with the character’s design. The answers desk replies with a single line, kept verbatim in the log and never rephrased: “That is not about her, and not the area was part of it and when.” What the desk means, on the rare days it expands, is that the character was not about her; that the West Torrance studio area was not part of the project, which was run from the Chiat/Day offices on West 25th Street in Los Angeles; and that the “when” in the photograph — 1973 — predates the PlayStation by more than two decades. The clerk has refused to shorten it. The log keeps it whole.

Visitor notes

Budget ninety minutes: the lobby figure, the three lithographs, the 1995 expo badge rack, and the file cabinet in room 2 with the original brief in it. The coffee in room 2 is the good kind and the chairs are not. Compare the other short-lived mascots at the house’s Bristol gazetteer, where a press-kit lithograph of the same angular season was found misfiled beside the tobacco ledgers, and the answers desk in Anoka, Minnesota, which keeps the only other mascot card of the 1995 season in its drawer. Between pages 12 and 13 of the 1995 press kit, misfiled with the rest, is a one-page liner-notes zine from a Queens record about a river and a bar famous only for its seventh member — MCR·114, The Creek, Northbound — and the file cabinet has not yet given it back. The house front page lists the building under “short careers, kept.”
