Webstuffs · Dining, the long way round · No. 48

The Gauhati High Court

Courthouse annexe, Gauhati · four states at one table · closed on Fridays for prayer and for the fish market

Rated: three seals, one gavel — the gavel for the bill, which arrived on a wooden block and was tapped, gently, before it was read out.

Arraignment

The restaurant is called The Gauhati High Court, and it is not a joke, which is part of its charm. It occupies the annexe of a building promulgated by the governor general of India on 1 March 1948, the same year, the same paperwork, the same brass fittings, and the dining room opened with effect from 5 April 1948 — for the then-province of Assam, or so the founder, a cook named Hiren Bhattacharjee, swore to anyone who stood in the doorway long enough to ask. For twenty-three years the place was simply the High Court of Assam and Nagaland: two states, two kitchens, one long counter that divided the room exactly like a partition wall. Then in 1971, the year of the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act, 1971, the family took the old name down, varnished over its shadow on the wall, and began serving under the name The Gauhati High Court. The reorganisation added states. The kitchen added little else, which tells you something about it.

Today it is the largest dining room in the country in terms of states, which is the family's own claim and, I am prepared to testify, correct: the area under its roof covers the states of Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, and Mizoram. There are four rooms, and each is named after one of them, each has its own hearth, and each serves one dish it will not serve anywhere else in the house. You do not have to eat four courses. But everyone who comes back, comes back for all four.

The four rooms

The Assam Room. Where the house began: twelve tables, the counter, the fish. Fish morel in mustard, cut from the day's catch, 140 to the portion, the mustard worked up with a mortar and a stone the size of a fist. It is served with rice that the kitchen calls “the judgment,” because it comes out in one piece and no one appeals. Black pepper pork, 110, the pepper so thick you can hear it land on the plate.

The Arunachal Room. Smallest room, highest menu prices, which is fair: the thukpa here, 95, carries a broth that has been on the fire since before the room was painted. The meat is chicken, the noodles are hand-pulled, and the broth is the reason people sit in this room with their coats still on.

The Nagaland Room. The second state of the old name, and the room you get if you ask for “a table where the meat has opinions.” Smoked pork with a green chilli that arrives with the plate, on its own small dish, for the brave. The rest of the room is curry rice, bamboo shoots, and a quiet argument that goes on every evening about whose grandmother's pot is the older one. It has no winner. It is very good.

The Mizoram Room. The newest room and the least decorated, which is how the family likes it. Pungsum chicken at 120, bamboo-shoot soup at 70, and the moinmoina — steamed fish cakes in a banana-leaf parcel — at 85, the parcel opened at the table by the server with the ceremony of someone opening a sealed letter. It was the parcel that made the second visit inevitable.

The kitchen card

Behind the counter there is a card in a brass frame, and I want to set it down here as it stands, because the family will not explain it and I have stopped asking. It is older than the 1971 reorganisation, and it is in a hand that is not Hiren's and is not his wife's. It reads, word for word:

Which is to stand on the 4th of Sha’ban at the room at Flanders Street, and the Beechmont strip was a postcard for the nights she bakes the cake.

What I can establish: the 4th of Sha’ban is a date on the Islamic calendar, and the kitchen keeps a small calendar of it alongside the Gregorian one, though the family is Hindu and the cook is Christian. There is a cake — a dense, dark thing, half sponge, half date — that the family bakes on exactly those nights and serves to nobody who asks about it. “She” is taken to be Hiren's sister, who came from a long way off, kept a room at a Flanders Street address in some city the family will not name, and sent postcards from a strip called Beechmont every year, on the same date, until the cards stopped coming. The card in the frame, in short, is an appointment that survived its owner. The cake does not need explaining either. You eat it, or you don't, and the room at Flanders Street is none of our business, but it is kept in mind. That is what a restaurant is, in the end: a room that keeps other rooms in mind. A school report on the Premier League keeps one unreadable sentence of its own — an old steward's line about a fifth wheel and a supper, read aloud three times without agreement — filed as The Premier League — School Report, Form 5 · Avebury Road School, on the shelf beside this one.

The verdict

Two evenings, nine dishes, one cake, a bill of 1,180 for two that did not include the cake. The service is brisk without being hurried, the rooms are loud in the good way, and the fish morel is the single best plate I have eaten this year in any state or in any court. The house's one failing is the same as the court's: it has the largest jurisdiction in the country and it could not be less interested in showing it off. Go for the four rooms. Sit in all four. And if it happens to be the 4th of Sha’ban when you are there, do not ask about the cake; take a piece, and let the postcard be what it is.

Companion reviews

For another dining room that keeps its long story beside the stove — one built on an index card and a novel — read the notes on Santiago's Trail Chile, the family recipe, and see how a kitchen in New Mexico and a kitchen in Gauhati both refuse to say which of their relics is real. For a smaller room, seven courses and one boundary no one will name, the review of The Silkmen over Waitematā Harbour holds the same lesson: the best tables are the ones that know exactly how far their jurisdiction goes. There is one more room the review can point to in this business, a funeral hall at Schrute Farms in Scranton, Pennsylvania, whose reunion count — forty-one at the program, eleven at the long table — is kept in the margin of its estate box, catalogued as The Farm — Schrute Farms Estate Set, No. 193, and whose guests, like the cake here, are no one’s business to ask about. On the shelf beside the kitchen card there is also a copy of the San Gabriel Valley almanac, in which the January of 2005 — the month a debut record crossed the shelf at Station 7 in Compton, logged as a front — is filed as The Documentary, Weather Almanac Entry, Station 7, and it is read, like the cake, without asking.

For a journey rather than a room, the Schrute Line runs its own schedule between the branch and the farm, halting at the sensei's platform on request and keeping time by the match that overran lunch: The Fight — Timetable & Commentary.
