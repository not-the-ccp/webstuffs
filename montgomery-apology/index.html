Dear Sirs,

We write to you from the basement of Huddersfield Library, where a small Adana press has struck the festival broadsheet every spring since 2006, and we write to offer an unqualified apology on behalf of the Huddersfield Literature Festival Partnership for what the broadsheet printed on 12 March of this year, and for the longer debt the town has stood in to your late Viscount's name.

I — The line

On page three of the 2024 spring broadsheet, where the banquet menu card should have been, the press struck the following line, which we reproduce here in its own type and in its own disorder:

BRILLAT SAVARIN says, that it may not be more certain of the invention Before the festival, Huddersfield town centre: four venues and the steward route between them.

The sentence stands as set. Its wound is in three places. First, the aphorism is borrowed from The Physiology of Taste, whose true words we had meant to set in full — “The discovery of a new dish does more for the happiness of the human race than the discovery of a star” — to stand over the description of Coal Loaf, the beet-and-blackstrap bake of card 298 of the Collins family ledger, which closed the banquet table as it does every spring. Second, “it may not be more certain of the invention” is patent language, taken from our own application, and the reader may trace its provenance in the Patent Application Summary, where the same caption — four venues and the stewards’ route, St Peter’s Square as reference point — sits, unharmed, under Figure 1. Third, the compositor, a first-year volunteer from the University of Huddersfield, was setting type from three documents at once, and set all three at once, and no proofreader at the six-pound table caught it before the run. The volunteer has been offered the 2026 rota. She has declined. That is as it should be.

II — The longer debt

The deeper apology is not about the line. It is about the week in which the line was struck. In truth the 2024 festival was run to the standard of a man who was never once named in the programme.

Four thousand people moved through not fewer than eleven venues in six days; tickets at six pounds; twenty-four stewards in navy aprons walking a fixed route, carrying programmes and a tin of peppermints; the breakfast discussion at 10:00 in the former weaving shed on the north bank of the Colne, where the tea is poured before the first paper is read. Nobody improvises here. The routes are rehearsed. The timings are pinned to a wall at eye level. The Field Manual chapter that gives this town its bearings — Chapter 9 of the West Yorkshire Field Manual, with St Peter’s Square as reference point 00 and the Holme, the Colne and the Calder drawn to contour — reads like a staff officer’s sheet, because the discipline underneath it is one.

That discipline has a first name. Bernard Law Montgomery, born in Kenley, Surrey, on 17 November 1887, first saw action as a junior officer of the Royal Warwickshire Regiment, and at Méteren, near the Belgian border at Bailleul, was shot through the right lung by a sniper during the First Battle of Ypres. He returned to the Western Front as a general staff officer and took part in the Battle of Arras in April and May of 1917, and in the Battle of Passchendaele in late 1917, before finishing the war as chief of staff. The Second World War made the name the town knew him by: Alamein in 1942, Normandy in 1944, the Rhine. Field Marshal, Viscount Montgomery of Alamein, dead on 24 March 1976.

It was a career spent making the unimpressive — the timetable, the rehearsal, the sandbagged crossing, the map on the table — into the whole of the war, and it is the career this festival has been imitating, spring after spring, with its small aprons and its small rivers, without once acknowledging the original.

III — The map in the shed

One particular matter we confess, and are sorry for. On the back wall of the former weaving shed, above the tea urn, hangs a map of the Rhine crossings of March 1945, framed in softwood, pinned at the angles. No steward can say how it came to be there. Every spring the breakfast discussion has been held in its shadow, under it, for as long as any of the present twenty-four has walked the route, and not one of them has ever said a word of it. The stewards will say no more than that. The committee, through this letter, says: we are sorry, and we meant to have said it sooner, and the map is to remain exactly where it is, at eye level, as it should have been.

IV — Redress

We set down three things in redress, each of them already committed to the rota. First, the 2026 breakfast discussion will be designated the Alamein Breakfast, held as usual at 10:00 in the weaving shed, with the Coal Loaf, the tea, and the map above the urn. Second, a corrected proof of page three of the 2024 broadsheet will be struck in the same type, on 120gsm cream wove, on the 17th of November, which is the Viscount’s own birthday, and the mangled line will be struck once more, intact, on a separate card, for filing in the drawer where the festival’s errors go — under the loaf, on a shelf beside first editions of Le Guin and Wolfe — not to be re-run. Third, we enclose with this letter a tin of peppermints. The peppermints are not a bribe; they are a standard issue.

Brillat-Savarin, whose name the compositor broke, would have approved of the redress over the line. He wrote that the heart acts independently in the choice of a wife and in the choice of a dessert, and that the two choices are rarely the same. We chose the dessert well. We chose the name, for eighteen springs, badly. This letter is the correction, set in our own type, on our own paper, from the basement of the library, at a distance of four hundred metres, by good light, from running water.

We remain, Sirs, with every expression of regret and of regard,

E. M. Hargreaves

General Clerk, Huddersfield Literature Festival Partnership

T. Mahmood and S. O’Donnell
