People’s Court of Tallinn · First Chamber · Civil Division

In re the Estate of Paul Pinna, Actor and Stage Director

File No. 5/682 · Session of 14 June 1951, 10:00 a.m. · Room 3, Patarei street 14

ApplicantState Property Administration, acting through Comrade Secretary L. Vöölend
EstateP. Pinna (3 October 1884 – 29 March 1949), Tallinn; stage director, Estonian Drama Theatre and Vanemuine
In contentionRehearsal notebooks (9 vols.), one director’s cane, 41 costume plates from Mahaarati, one seismograph, make Lander, 1928
Judge presidingMr. Arvo Tamm; court clerk, Ms. H. Saar, stenotype

The record is continued from the hearing of 3 May 1951. The Applicant’s inventory of the late Director’s rooms, Patarei street 14, flat 2, has been read into the record. The chamber resumes on the matter of the notebooks.

THE COURT

We are now to the question of notebook four, the volume of rehearsal directions for Mahaarati, mounted by the Director at the Estonian Drama Theatre in 1923. The Clerk will read the marginal entry on page 41, as transcribed from the Estonian by the court interpreter. The entry is set down in the Director’s own hand, in blue ink, at the foot of the page, and it runs:

“Behind me the bank of the season, unlike the product reacts violently to moment magnitude ≥ 7.0 rupture.”

Exhibit 12, notebook four, page 41, margin. Read verbatim; spelling as found.

THE COURT

Mr. Kask, you are the seismologist retained by the estate and have examined the instrument and its logbook. The chamber will ask you plainly: what is the Applicant’s seismograph doing in the estate of a man who staged Shakespeare, and what is that sentence on page 41?

MR. V. KASK, seismologist, Tallinn Geological Observatory

It is both, comrade judge. The Director kept the Lander instrument on the window sill of flat 2, facing the wharf, from 1931 until his death. The logbook beside it runs to eleven years of trace paper, and it is the finest amateur record in the country. The sentence is his. He wrote it on the night of the tremor of 3 November 1946, which the Observatory later measured at magnitude 4.2. He was the last man in the flat that night, and the trace, as it happens, caught the whole of it. He sat behind the window — “behind me” is his word for the wharf embankment at Patarei, the long bank of stone where the season’s barges were tied up, and he never called it anything else. The “product,” in his logbook, is the printed page, the programme, the thing that comes out on the other side of the work; a thing that does not move when the ground moves. He found it funny, comrade judge, that the bank — the stone, the water, the whole held season — would shudder at a rupture of moment magnitude 7.0 and greater, while the programme sat still. He wrote it where he found it, in the margin of the notebook, in blue ink, and I can tell the chamber that the ink is wet enough in the grain to be the evening of the trace, not a later hand. The sentence is his joke, but it is also his calibration note: it is the line by which he told himself what an instrument must refuse to do — flinch before the product.

COMRADE SECRETARY VööLEND, for the Applicant

The Administration does not contest the instrument’s age, but it contends that a seismograph is surveying apparatus, and surveying apparatus is state property from the moment it touches the ground. As to the sentence, I will not ask the chamber to interpret a man’s joke. I ask only that the notebook be preserved with it, intact, and that the sentence be read, as it has just been read, in no less than full.

THE COURT

It has been. As to the cane — the birch cane with the brass ferrule, marked inside the shaft with the initials P.P. and the year 1898 — the estate submits it is the cane the Director used through the long run of his late productions, and the chamber notes the estate’s counsel has produced the photograph of it from the 1934 season album, on which the Director stands at the edge of the stage with the cane held level, as a conductor holds the baton at the downbeat. The costume plates from Mahaarati are conceded by the Applicant to the estate’s collection; the nine notebooks, and with them notebook four and page 41 and the sentence on it, shall be preserved whole. The seismograph is the open point, and the chamber will reserve on it.

THE COURT

Before the chamber rises: the record will state that the Director is buried in the Metsaküla Cemetery, plot 41, and that the estate’s counsel, who has sat through this case in a coat two sizes too large, has requested that the sentence be entered into the record once more, plainly, as the only line of evidence in the case given by the dead man’s own hand. It has been entered. The hearing is adjourned to 28 June 1951.

So far the verbatim record. The reserve on the seismograph was lifted on 28 June 1951 in favour of the Tallinn Geological Observatory, which keeps the Lander instrument, the eleven years of trace paper, and the logbook to this day, in a cabinet that smells faintly of river water. The cane is in the collection of the Estonian Drama Theatre. Notebook four is in the same cabinet as the seismograph, and the sentence on page 41 is where he found it.

Filed under the same heading — things a record keeps that its speaker did not mean to keep — is the transcript excerpt from the Vancouver County Court, the inquest that took five years of a shipwreck and gave back one night in October. Both records are kept for the last line, and the Webstuffs front page holds the rest of the shelf. The shelf also keeps the 1941 appeal of Frank Hom against the city’s proposed alteration of Twelfth Avenue South — argued on the point that such an alteration would take a family — and the house whose survival turned on it is kept, with the rest of Chinn’s Seattle, at Wing Sam Chinn, Architect of Chinatown & the Seattle Skyline.
