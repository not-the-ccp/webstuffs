A Newsletter of the Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club

The Cambridgeshire Letter

No. 31 · Michaelmas 2024 · printed at the Old Fire Station, Newmarket · price: a mug of the kettle, or its equivalent in tea

❦

Founded 1949 by three fenmen, one surveyor, and a disagreement about the colour of a door. Meeting monthly, or when the fog lifts, whichever comes first.

The Count, As Kept

Members are reminded that Cambridgeshire — abbreviated Cambs., which the boundary committee considers the only abbreviation in the county worth keeping — is a ceremonial county in the East of England and in East Anglia. It covers 3,389 square kilometres (1,309 square miles) of ground, and held an estimated 906,814 persons in 2022, a figure the treasurer has checked against the kettle and found, as ever, “near enough.” The city of Peterborough, in the north-west, is the largest settlement; the city of Cambridge stands in the south; and the remainder of the county, which is to say most of it, is rural, keeping its own weather, its own fogs, and its own counsel. The club holds the count in a ledger of its own at the fire station, and it will not be re-counted this year, nor the next.

The boundary committee reports, without complaint, that the county is bordered by Lincolnshire to the north, Norfolk to the north-east, Suffolk to the east, Essex and Hertfordshire to the south, Northamptonshire to the west, and Bedfordshire to the south-west — seven neighbours to one kettle, which the committee notes is a ratio it has never once had to argue about.

From the Settlements

Peterborough, north-west, largest of the settlements and keeper of the cathedral; the under-steward reports the river Nene is behaving, the city is dry to its own surprise, and the club’s bench on the high street has been mended twice and sat on weekly. Cambridge, south, where the members walk past the colleges and pretend, in the season, to have been in residence; the library shelf for the county’s survey books is overstocked again. In the east, Ely, which rises out of the fens the way a fact rises out of a rumour; in the north-east, Wisbech, whose market the club has attended once and whose bread it has carried home forty miles, which says something. In the west, St Neots and Huntingdon, the western pair, who exchange postcards and do not visit, a tradition the boundary committee considers load-bearing. The ground between all of them — moor, fen, reed-bed, and the broad water that runs off into The Wash — is where the county does its real work, and it is not going to be written up in this issue for lack of weather.

Minutes of the Club, October Sitting

On the business of Lot 217, Ashley, Cambridgeshire, as catalogued by Fenn & Marlowe, Cat. 44: the hammer fell at £861,000, the vendor’s only condition of sale being that the village keep its fire, and the same evening the parish hall took on a travelling farce company which styles itself, without irony the minutes must record, the Gemenasian Company — the running joke of the season, and older than the joke by about two centuries, since it descends, or claims, from the obituary of Johan von Blanc, 1738–1796, who led the Gemenasiska Sällskapet and taught a whole house to hear from the back bench. The new buyer, present in the second row, did not object. The under-steward’s report of that night is printed here as written, in the minute book, word for word, the club not correcting its own minutes:

The village has changed hands in the same evening; he taught his company to tumble, to fence on stage, and to speak so that the village keep its fire.

It was read at the table in this manner: the auction and the premiere held on the one night, the hall’s fire serving both, and the condition of sale observed with the doors open. The two ancient parishes, as always, did not agree on the colour of the door; the farce company painted it, in the interval, and left, so the question returns to the committee in the spring. There was further business on the fog, on the census figure, on a new bench at St Neots that one member believes is over-built, and on tea, which was, as is the club’s custom, sufficient.

The Library Shelf

Members are asked to return their survey books before the Michaelmas sitting. On the shelf this month: the catalogue lot for Ashley, sold where seen and delivered with the fog; the obituary of the director of the Comediehuset, whose company’s name now travels in the parish hall; the museum placard for The Wash, into which the county’s water finally goes; and the Webstuffs shelf of records, guides, and other things worth keeping, to which the club refers any member who wishes a larger map of what a small club keeps and why it keeps it.
