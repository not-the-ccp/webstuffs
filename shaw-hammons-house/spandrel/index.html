Dream Dictionary · Entry 715

Spandrel

noun · plural spandrels

Spandrel is the roughly triangular space between the top of an arch and the rectangular frame that holds it; the same shape falls between the tops of two adjacent arches, and as one of the four corners left when a circle is pressed into a square. In a building of more than one floor, the word also names the wall between the top of a window in one storey and the windowsill on the storey above. It is, in short, architecture’s way of leaving a gap where something can enter.

Entry 715 was dreamt on the night of 12 November, the day after Room 214 of Anoka Township High School visited the Shaw–Hammons House on Coon Rapids Dam Road. The house is Greek Revival from its 1870 enlargement, built around 1852, listed on the National Register in 1979, and its front portico carries four square columns beneath a pediment that shelters a door with a semicircular fanlight set in a square frame. That fanlight has four spandrels, each filled with a plaster rosette picked out in ochre. In the dream each spandrel was full to the saucer with Anoka River water, and the water was still rising.

Interpretation

To dream of spandrels is to dream of load-bearing emptiness. A spandrel carries no load itself; it is the shape left over where two structural forms come close but refuse to touch. It is therefore the place where a building admits what it was built to exclude. Entry 714, Hunters Point, keeps sixteen white battleships at anchor behind two graving docks, six hundred and thirty-eight acres of sealed concrete holding back the tide, and its rule runs: The yard’s business was keeping ships watertight; the dream’s business is letting the water in. The spandrel obeys the same rule. The fanlight above the door in Anoka is a circle in a square, four damp corners waiting to be unstopped; dream it, and the rain comes through the plaster.

The same night’s log gives names. Ruth Hammons, whose family kept the house through both world wars, stands in the portico on the Fourth of July with wet hands and a saucer in each. Marcus Tellez is back at the coffee urn behind the school bus, repeating something about an album and a nanna’s funeral while three strangers shush him in unison. Jordan Okafor counts five columns where the textbook promised four, and the fifth column has no capital at all. Above the parlor window the sash glass is still the original paired type, two over two, slightly wavy, so the street outside looks hand-painted — and in the dream the glass had run downward like wax, pooling on the sill where the next storey’s windowsill should begin.

Associated imagery

  • Four plaster spandrels above a Greek Revival door, each holding a saucer of river water
  • A semicircular fanlight in a square surround, the rosettes softening at the edges, ochre running down the limestone
  • The arch of the bridge on Coon Rapids Dam Road meeting its rectangular railing
  • Two adjacent arches sharing one triangular breath of air
  • A circle pressed into a square, four corners damp with rain that has not yet fallen
  • The gap between the head of a first-storey window and the sill of the storey above, where a curtain breathes in and out
  • Sixteen white hulls at permanent anchor beneath a fog that never burns off
  • Wavy glass running like wax down to a sill four thousand miles from Hunters Point

On record

“I stood in the portico of the Shaw–Hammons House and the spandrels were bleeding ochre into the rain. Ruth Hammons handed me a saucer and said the Fourth of July was never dry.”

— Petra Lindqvist, Room 214, Period 4 · 12 November

Comparison and conclusion

The class has compared this house with the Bates–Seller House in Northwest Portland, four thousand miles away, Queen Anne to Greek Revival, both standing because the paperwork survived. Hunters Point makes the reverse comparison: this same Anoka house, sealed concrete and dry docks, the yard’s business against the dream’s. Spandrels are the agreement between them — the space where the arch meets the frame, where the watertight hull meets the fog, where one storey ends and the next has not yet begun. If spandrels walk through your sleep, ask what you are keeping dry, and open the corner.
