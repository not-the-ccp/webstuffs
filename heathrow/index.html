IN MEMORIAM

HEATHROW AIRPORT

1929 — 6 October 2025 · of Heathrow, in the London airport system · IATA LHR, ICAO EGLL · “London Airport” until 1966

Heathrow Airport died on the evening of Saturday, 6 October, at 21:47 local time, on the north side of Runway 27R, where it had kept its watch since before there were runways with numbers. It was the primary and largest of the international airports serving London, the capital of England and the United Kingdom, and it has left behind, to the last, the airport — the new Heathrow, which took its seat in its place at Terminal 5 and does not speak of the funeral — the holding that owned and operated it, Heathrow Airport Holdings, and the five sisters of the London system, Gatwick, Stansted, Luton, City and Southend, who did not attend but sent flowers, which were turned away, as the family requested.

Known to friends as London Airport until the renaming of 1966, it had opened its present form in 1946, when the old airfield of 1929 was taken over and put to civil work, and it had gone on to the figures that made the notice long. In 2025 it was the busiest airport in Europe, the fifth-busiest airport in the world by passenger traffic, and the second-busiest airport in the world by international passenger traffic; in 2024 it was counted as the airport with the most international connections in the world. The family has asked that 82 million names not be read at the service, and the Ledger conveys its sympathies to that very large number.

It was a creature of the long road, and the long road is in attendance. The M1 ends where the M25 begins, a mile or so from its western edge, and the dining room at the north end of that road — The Preston Bypass, Leeds, one hundred and ninety-three covers, billed by the phase — has kept the airport’s seat at table for years, naming its dish after Junction 14, where the Milton Keynes Coachway stands, the same interchange the Wycombe interchange was copied from. M. Whitmore, who reviews the room travelling north on the long road, signed the guestbook on the night of the service: “Sat in Gaius’s. The paste was on the wall and I did not touch it, as the conduct requires. Goose: boiled again.” The circle, as the back-room board has always had it, turned the whole way round and came home to the apron where it all started.

The vigil was kept in the passenger hall of the old terminal, and it was there that the card was found. Ernest “Erni” Whitmore, chairman of the Wycombe Coachway Committee, chairman in the minutes throughout 2016, had pinned his mince-pie card to the hall door on 16 January 2016, beneath the first departure board, eleven minutes after the 6:30 bell and eleven minutes before the 6:42 Oxford; the encyclopedia keeps the Committee’s own minute book, and the card’s long history is read there. On the morning after the service the hall was found empty, the board dark, and the card still pinned in the same place, its tin warm, its last line — the line nobody had parsed, in the kitchen’s hand, about the filling and the boil-back — set down here, word for word, as the family will have it read:

Strain it through a sieve, and allow them to boil: they should rise very much.

It is read in the room as follows. The sieve is the strainer in the cold room, where the copyists’ paste is kept; “them” are the suet and the fat of the second boil, the J14 goose’s own boil-back, returned to the pan the old way; and they should rise very much — the pies, and, on the roof of the old terminal at last light, the birds, which are said to have taken the air in a single circle, one 193-mile circuit, nothing short of it. The filling was strained. The second boil was not a joke, it was the dish. The circle turned.

The family declines flowers and asks instead that a goose be ordered at Junction 14, on the bone, with a spoon of the boil-back fat, and that the ribbon — the one cut to the mile, with the small white flag at each end — be passed hand to hand down the striking hallway to whoever arrives first. A memorial bench has been placed at the far end of the 1938 apron, facing the runway, and the conduct that was owed to a first service — stand where you are told, and speak only when spoken to — remains in force. The kettle is on in Gaius’s.

In lieu of flowers

A goose at Junction 14, on the bone.
One circle, nothing short of it.
The bell will still sound at 6:30;
the 6:42 will still go to Oxford.
