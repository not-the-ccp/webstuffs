Council of the Marble Hall · Session of 3 September · Antechamber, north of the Winter stair

Minutes No. 230

The Matter of Olga Constantinovna of Russia, Queen of Greece, in Her Ninety-Eighth Year by the Hall’s Count

Presiding
The Honourable Thalia Mavrommatis, Chair of the Hall
Clerk
Demetrios K. Vrailas, keeper of the marble roll, pen no. 7
Present
Eleven of fifteen; absent on leave, Mm. Papafotis, Zervas, and the two from the Crimea branch
Doors
Opened 17:40 · closed 17:52 · sealed with the gold wafer at 18:00

1 The roll, and the reading of the name

The Chair opened the session at seventeen forty, four minutes late, and the lateness was noted without comment, which in this hall is a kind of applause. The Clerk read the roll of the day: Olga Constantinovna of Russia, Όλγα, born 3 September 1851 — old style 22 August — in the house of Grand Duke Constantine Nikolaievich, and died 18 June 1926, queen of Greece as the wife of King George I, and for one hard month of the year 1920 the regent of Greece, when the throne stood empty and the little King Alexios was not old enough to sit upon it. The Chair observed that the Hall has sat for queens before — see the minutes kept for the regency question in Alaska — Minutes No. 215, Council of the Far Bank, where the thirteenth place at the long table was argued for the length of one tide — but has never sat for one born of the Romanovs, which fact was received in silence, and the silence was entered as the first entry of the day.

The Clerk then read, from the birth register, the name of the mother: Princess Alexandra of Saxe-Altenburg, widow of no one and wife of one, whose daughter was the oldest of the brood. Grand Duke Constantine Nikolaievich’s household is a large one; the minutes name the brothers Paul, Nicholas, Michael, George and Kirill in their proper order, and the sisters Xenia and the rest, and the Clerk was interrupted twice by the Chair, who asked that the children be given their Christian names and not merely their places, which is the Hall’s way with dynasties: a child is a child before it is a seat.

2 The childhood: Saint Petersburg, Congress Poland, the Crimea

Motion 230/2 was moved by the Honourable Papadopoulou that the childhood of the matter be recorded in the order the Hall prefers, which is the order of the trains. The record stands as follows. The girl’s childhood was spent in the Russian Empire, first and chiefly at Saint Petersburg, in the house where the Grand Dukes keep their winter, then at the country seats in Congress Poland, where the family went to escape its own corridors, and then, in the warm years, down to the Crimean coast, to the palaces at Alupka and Livadia, where the sea was close enough to count from the veranda. The Clerk read the distance figures from the branch ledger: Alupka to Livadia, nine kilometres by the coastal road; the veranda at Livadia to the waterline, in the Queen’s later recollection, “a hand’s throw in September” — this phrase was objected to by the Honourable Zervas as unmeasurable, and the objection was overruled, on the ground that some distances are kept in hands and not in kilometres, and the minutes carry both columns.

Motion carried 9–1, with the abstention recorded against no one, which the Clerk assures the record he has never seen in all his years at the marble roll and does not intend to see again.

3 The marriage at Corfu, 1867, and the settling in

The Chair called the matter to order, and the Clerk read the third entry. In 1867, at the age of sixteen, Olga was married to George, later George I of Greece, at the Cathedral of the Holy Resurrection on the island of Corfu, the bride of sixteen walking the aisle before the groom’s court had finished arguing over the flowers. The minutes record that for some months after the wedding she felt ill at ease in the Kingdom of Greece — the record uses the word unwell in the room, which is the Hall’s word for a person who has arrived in a country whose weather she does not yet know — but that the unease did not last. She set herself to work in the social and charitable trade: hospitals, first the small ones in the island towns, then the larger ones; schools, beginning with the girls’ schools of Athens and Thessaloniki, where she pressed the board until the reading rooms were lit in winter. The Honourable Katsaros reported, from the branch correspondence, that she founded and endowed no fewer than seven institutions in the first decade of her reign, and that the boards of three of them still write to the Hall to ask for the spelling of her middle name, which the minutes settle here, for the last time: Constantinovna, one word, no hyphen, the clerk’s pen to be guided accordingly.

One attempt did not prosper. She sought to promote a new fashion of dress for the court, cut Greek in the line and Russian in the lining; the court wore it for one season, the tailors of the Street of the Gown keep the patterns still, and the matter was closed without a motion, which is how the Hall closes the things it loves.

4 The regency of 1920

The Chair noted that the year 1920 is in the future of this session’s calendar by a grace the Hall has always allowed its minutes, and read the entry accordingly, in the form the Hall uses for what it calls the early entries. In the autumn of 1920, with King Constantine gone from the throne for the second time and the little King Alexios a child, the Council of the Regency was formed, and the Queen Regent-elect was named for the work: Olga Constantinovna, regent of Greece, for the brief months that the arrangement held. The Clerk recorded that the regency was “brief” in the original, that the Hall does not embellish its durations, and that a regency held for a season is a regency in the Hall’s arithmetic, the same way the Far Bank counted its tides before it counted its miles. The Honourable Papafotis’s absent note, read from his leave envelope, asked why the Hall should keep a regency that lasted only long enough to be called brief; the Chair answered from the bench, in a low voice, that brevity is a measure, and the measure is entered, and the entry was closed.

5 The watch sentence, preserved

At 19:04 the Clerk brought up the last paper of the day, a slip from the night watch of the garage at the hall’s north annex, where the carriages and the winter car keep their coats. The night watchman, a man of the Crimean branch, had left one line in the watch ledger in a hand the Clerk called “steady as a tide table.” The Clerk read the line aloud, at the request of the Chair, exactly as written, without correction and without apology:

The word is being watched in the garage went quiet.

The Honourable Katsaros asked whether the Clerk had been asked to correct it. The Clerk answered that the Hall’s rule is that a line in the watch ledger is entered as written, the way a name in the birth register is entered, and that he has preserved the sentence word for word because it is the night watch’s sentence and not the Clerk’s. The Chair observed that the sentence does not parse in the way the Hall prefers, but that it has done the one thing the Hall asks of any sentence: it has said what it meant to say, somewhere, in the dark, and it has kept its shape. The entry was closed at 19:11, the gold wafer was warmed, and the minutes were sealed.

6 Motions, votes, and the next sitting

Motion 230/6, that the minutes be signed, carried unanimously. The Clerk signed with pen no. 7, and the Chair signed above him, as the Hall has done since the marble roll was hauled up from the quarry in the year of the first sitting. The next sitting is fixed for 18 June, the day of the queen’s death in the year 1926, at which the Hall will lay the black ribbon on the north wall beside the ledger and take the count, as it takes the count, without comment. Members are reminded that the Hall’s count runs from the birth, and that the ribbon is laid, not hung: a laid ribbon lies flat and bears the weight of the wall, which is the point of it.
