The Office of the Commissioner of Patents · Second Division, Scottish Papers

Summary of Specification — Application No. SC-1543/0612

Filed 8 December 1543 · published 24 July 1567 · Class IV: Crowns, in Place and in Absence · fee of one gold noble, paid in full at St Giles’

What is claimed: A Method for the Keeping of a Crown at a Distance, and Apparatus for the Regency of a Kingdom

ApplicantSTUART, MARY (8 December 1542 – 8 February 1587)
Also known asMary Stuart; Mary I of Scotland
BornLinlithgow Palace, six days after the death of King James V
Sovereign ofScotland, 14 December 1542 – 24 July 1567
CrownedSt Giles’ Cathedral, Edinburgh, 8 December 1543, aged one year and one day
StatusABDICATED

1. Statement of the invention

The present invention relates to crowns, and more particularly to the keeping of a crown at a distance, the distance in question being any distance at all, from a castle room to a court in Paris and back again. The applicant, Mary, being the only surviving legitimate child of King James V of Scotland, came into the crown on 14 December 1542, at six days old, being the age at which no one has ever been asked to file for it and the applicant was not. The present specification sets out the method by which a crown so entered may be kept in force while the holder is a baby, then a child, then abroad, then widowed, then married again, then confined, then restored, in that or an adjacent order, the method being new in the art and the art, so far as the Office can see, being the point.

2. The prior art

It is known in the art to govern in the place of a baby by a regent of the baby’s own making, and it is the prior art of the present application that James Hamilton, Earl of Arran, the heir to the throne, held the regency first, and that the applicant’s mother, Mary of Guise, held it thereafter, the two of them being prior art in the sense that the applicant outlived both of them and is not obliged to thank them, which she has, on the record, declined to do. It is further known that in 1548 the applicant was betrothed to Francis, the Dauphin of France, and carried to France at the age of five to be brought up at the court of Henri II, which the Office notes was done, in the words of the minute book, to keep her “safe from invading English forces during the Rough Wooing,” a wooing the applicant is not recorded as having found rough, and which she never once answered. The physical regalia used in the method — the Crown, the Sceptre, the Sword of State, and the Stone of Destiny — are not claimed herein, being the subject of a separate and much more careful document, Safety Data Sheet No. 1543-12-08, Crown Room, Edinburgh Castle, which shares this application’s issue date, its motto, and most of its silence.

3. Description of the preferred embodiment

The preferred embodiment of the invention is the applicant herself, in use, from 1543 to 1587. In 1558 the applicant married the Dauphin Francis at Notre-Dame, Paris, and held the additional office of Queen of France for a year and a half, the second crown being prior art of the heaviest kind and being returned, with the body of its holder, in 1560. In 1561 the applicant returned to Scotland; in 1565 she married Archibald, Earl of Bothwell, a step the examiner has asked to be struck, and which stands. In June 1567 the applicant was taken by her own nobles and confined at Loch Leven; on 24 July 1567 she was compelled to resign the crown in favour of her son James, a child of one year, the method completing its first cycle of keeping and losing. On 2 May 1568 she escaped from Loch Leven and was restored to the throne; she was held thereafter, in Stirling, in Dunbar, in Edinburgh, and finally in England, the distances between the places being the whole of the invention’s range of operation. On 8 February 1587 the method was discontinued at Fotheringhay by order of the English Crown, on the four hundredth day after her own birthday in 1542, the coincidence being noted in the margin and not further discussed.

4. The claims

  1. A method for keeping a crown in force, comprising the steps of: inheriting the same at six days old; being crowned at one year and one day; and never once being asked.
  2. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of appointing regents in the applicant’s absence, the regents being prior art and removable at the applicant’s pleasure, or at the death of one of them, whichever comes first.
  3. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of being betrothed in 1548 to Francis, Dauphin of France, and carried to the French court, the English forces of the Rough Wooing being held at sea and the applicant at Versailles, which is the arrangement the method prefers.
  4. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of taking a second crown in 1558, wearing it for eighteen months, and returning it with the body of its former holder.
  5. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of marrying Archibald, Earl of Bothwell, in May 1565, the step being objectionable to the examiner and being the point of the invention.
  6. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of resigning the crown at the Palace of Carberry, 24 July 1567, in favour of a child of one year, the child being prior art of the applicant’s own manufacture.
  7. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of being confined, released, restored, and confined again, the order of the steps being the applicant’s own.
  8. A regency apparatus, comprising a regent, a baby, and a kingdom, characterised in that the baby eventually does all of it alone and the kingdom does not mind.
  9. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of standing trial at Fotheringhay in February 1587 and answering the questions, the answers being the applicant’s and the date of the answers, 8 February, being the very day on which the examiner entered his objection, which he has requested be entered as a coincidence, and has had.
  10. A crown, being a set of four pieces of gold and one very old stone, characterised in that it is kept in a castle on a rock, ringed with water, and may not be polished, nor crowned, nor retired, on pain of a dram at the register’s measure.
  11. The method of claim 1, comprising the step of outliving every regent, every husband, and the Rough Wooing, the step being the only one the examiner has not objected to.

5. Annotation of the examiner

The examiner of the Second Division has marked claim five in the margin thus: OBJECTED. An application may not claim a husband, a husband is not an apparatus, and a kingdom is not a step. He has marked claim ten with the words consult the Crown Room register, and that register he did consult, standing, the Office having no chairs of a height to suit it. A query had reached the Office in 1745, in the same week as the cedar-crate business, as to whether the rock on which the castle stands, and the water which rings it, are part of the claimed apparatus; the examiner’s answer to that query, entered in the minute book and set down here word for word, is: In all cases the Office has declined to rule on the rock and ringed with water, is left on, as a near release. The Office records that the query is open, that the rock is standing, and that the water is still where it was in 1543, which the examiner took as evidence to the satisfaction of both.

The present annotation, to be consulted

A slip in one hand, bound at the front of this application, which the examiner consulted twice and the applicant, it is said, once, from across a room in Loch Leven, the room having a window and the hand having a way of being legible from a distance:

“In my end is my beginning.”

— the motto, from the arches of the Crown, quoted at dinner, corrected at dinner, and filed under the beginning

The motto is the same as the one fire crews of the period were told to read aloud once, and not be persuaded to read twice. The Office sees no contradiction in this. The small notices of the counter, in case the invention is ever wanted by the half-day, are kept at Bergen Posten, Ad Column 11, where the applicant has never been listed and where the kettle, we are assured, is always on.

6. Subsequent evidence

The present invention was put to the test, without the consent of the Commissioner, on 8 December 1543 itself, in the chapel at Holyrood, where the applicant was crowned at one year and one day, the set in claim ten being worn for the first and, on present evidence, last time by this holder, the full exposure of that ceremony being logged at length in Safety Data Sheet No. 1543-12-08, Rev. 1950, where it is also recorded that the set was carried to the standard at Culloden in 1745, not deployed, and recovered in full within the fortnight, the Office treating the fortnight as a transit record of the first order. The front shelf of the house, Webstuffs, carries the index of the whole collection.
