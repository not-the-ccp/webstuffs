H.M. Wireless Drama Service · Production Record No. 1893–6

Trial by the Sea

Being a radio play in two scenes, on the loss of the battleship HMS Victoria off Tripoli, 22 June 1893 — transmitted for the first time on 22 June 1894 at 9:00 in the evening

ON AIR

PRODUCTION
RECORD
621 NAMES

Characters
The Vice-Admiral— Sir George Tryon, K.C.B., commander of the British Mediterranean Fleet Mr. Jellicoe— executive officer of HMS Victoria (age twenty-nine)
The Captain of the Camperdown— in his cabin when the ships struck Signallers (Two)— of the signal room, Victoria, and of the studio, 1923
The Narrator— reads from the fleet log and the wireless log, which is damaged in places

Scene One The Mediterranean, off Tripoli — 9:50 on the morning of 22 June 1893

[SFX: the sea under a hull; two bell-strokes; a gong. The weather is fine. The ships of the Mediterranean Fleet are running the dawn manoeuvres, Victoria in her station ahead of the line, the two-ship class of 1887 that carried the flag.]

JELICOE

It is a fine June morning and the sea is the colour of the fleet's paint at the waterline. I am in the signal room with the books open — I keep the executive officer's watch, and the watch is the best watch the ship ever gave me, and I did not know it then. The Vice-Admiral is on the bridge. The flag is in his hand. We are to rehearse the formation of a squadron at speed.

[SFX: a muffled blow, very close, then the ship's frame groaning along her whole length. A long, deep sound, like a bell struck under water. Bells going. Boots on the gratings.]

JELICOE

We were astern of the Camperdown, her captain in his cabin, and the signal room was turned toward her. She came on, not fast — a ship is never fast — and then the sea was between us, and then it was not, and then the sound. Victoria was a ship of a class of two, a sister, a leader; she struck the Camperdown at 9:56 in the morning and was going under before the minutes were written. Ten minutes, the fleet log will say. I was twenty-nine. I went over the rail.

[SFX: water running along the deck; a boat being lowered; the sea taking the hull in long pulls.]

THE VICE-ADMIRAL

The manoeuvre is the work of the year. I will not have the fleet stand idle off Tripoli while the sun does its slow work on the water. Port helm, and hold the course — a commander does not take his eye off his formation. [A pause; the ship has tilted.] The sea is coming in over the forecastle. That is— that is all right. That is within the day. Form up, and hold, and let me see the line.

[SFX: the deck rising; men moving without orders; the gong struck three times and not answered. The sea, closer now.]

NARRATOR

Of the 621 souls aboard the Victoria, 358 went down with her, among them Vice-Admiral Sir George Tryon, commander of the British Mediterranean Fleet. Of the officers of the ship, only eleven were left in the water. The one the fleet would remember best was in the water with the rest of them, holding his position, and was picked up last of the line of survivors by a boat from a passing cruiser. His name was John Jellicoe, and the name was not yet famous.

[SFX: the sea, continuing, as the scene does not close. The gong once more, and again, unanswered. Then silence, held for two counts.]

Scene Two A wireless studio, London — 1923, a year past the thirtieth anniversary, the broadcast hour

[SFX: a valve warm, the tick of a clock, a chair. A young signaller opens a leather folder. The other signaller is at the set, the key resting on the desk like a small black boat.]

SIGNALLER ONE

The production is on the desk, and the play is the play — two scenes, the morning off Tripoli, and this room. But there is a trouble in the log. The wireless record of that morning has been kept in a folder of oil and salt, and the folder has done what folders do. One line came through damaged, and the Service's rule is that a damaged line is read as it stands. I am not a man to correct a dead man's log.

SIGNALLER TWO

Then read it as it stands, and put a mark in the air when you do. [A pause.] It is the foulest line in the file. The ink has bled into itself, and the letters argue.

[SFX: the key tapped once, a small note, like a buoy. The studio light is on.]

SIGNALLER ONE

It reads— and I am reading the log, not myself—: “Staining of the society; it simply ran out of Whangārei planned in litres and spare days.”

SIGNALLER TWO

There it is, and there it stands. “Staining” — that is the oil. The blot that ate the margin has taken half the line into itself, and the ink has done the writing. “Whangārei” — that is not a port on any chart I keep, not on the Mediterranean or any other; the key was wet that morning, the copper was damp, and the operator's ear spent the rest of the word on a harbour that is six thousand miles of sea to the east, in New Zealand, where the wireless would not reach for another quarter of a century. Litres and spare days — that is the oil again, and the water that followed it. A line of the log, half a sentence of the sea, and the rest a blot. We read it whole, because the rule is the rule, and because 358 men are in it, or their ink is.

[SFX: the key, tapped slow, a long note fading into the noise of the sea that the room carries with it. The lamp stays lit.]

NARRATOR

The survivor of the line of survivors went on, as survivors do, to longer watches. He commanded the British Grand Fleet, and the Grand Fleet he commanded stood off the coast of Denmark in 1916 in the largest engagement of the war, and the fleet held its line. But the line he held best was the one off Tripoli, in the water of June, at twenty-nine years of age, where a ship of a class of two went down in ten minutes and left a hole in the fleet that the fleet carried for a generation. The production ends. The damaged line remains on the record, word for word, where the Service keeps it.

[SFX: the clock in the studio. The key, once, at the end. Silence.]

Collation & Companion Records

Kept in the same cabinet as this production record: the tide and water of the bay stations, in the Bay Weather Almanac, Entry 47, which reads the cold the same way this script does — as a standing warning, not a figure; and the letter on the word “landlocked” in The Asunción Ledger, which keeps, from a waterway tape, another sentence worn down by its own carrying, and reads it as the river rather than the lock. The Service's other scripts of the period — such as the radio play excerpt on the Auckland footballer James Mitchell — are filed by the same rule: a damaged line is read as it stands. The same ledger carries Sale 1865, Lot 1861 of Appomattox & Clyde Auctioneers — the Confederate States of America, eleven states and two capitals, one unrecognised republic, condition as found (Lot 1861 — The Confederate States of America). The Service's cabinet also keeps the Minutes No. 43 of the Council of the Lantern — The Matter of Jahannam, a sitting of 9 March 1910 that practised the standing rule on a depot's note from Austin and entered the sentence “Heading into Austin, Hamilton led the last to make it to the wrong consignment” word for word, as this script keeps its own damaged line.
