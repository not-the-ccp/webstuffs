FIELD MANUAL FM-9 · ADDENDUM B · FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Bluetooth

The ten-metre chapter — what the bench is asked, and the answers the shelf keeps

2.402 GHz 2.441 GHz 2.480 GHz

This addendum is the asked half of the parent manual. The parent chapter, the satellite chapter of FM-9, Addendum A, tells you how a fix works four moves at a time; this one answers the questions the bench itself gets asked about the short-range radio that shares its shelf mark. The questions are real, they are asked in the shed, and the answers below are the printed ones. Read them standing up, the way the manual is meant to be read.

Q.1 What is Bluetooth, and why does it belong on this shelf?

Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology standard that is used for exchanging data between fixed and mobile devices over short distances and building personal area networks (PANs). It is on this shelf because the parent chapter is about a constellation at 20,200 kilometres, and this is the technology for everything that happens the other way — at 10 metres, in a room, on a bench, between the receiver and the nearest other device. The parent chapter borrows arithmetic from the sky; the near-field chapter borrows it back. One is the satellite’s point of view, the other the bench’s, and the two share a shelf mark because they are the same discipline at two ranges: time, place, count, and a margin note kept rather than corrected.

Q.2 How far does it actually reach?

In the most widely used mode, transmission power is limited to 2.5 milliwatts, giving it a very short range of up to 10 metres (33 ft). That is not a defect; it is the spec. For the bench’s purposes the classes read as follows:

The rule the jangada skippers at Pedro do Rosário would approve: the 2.5-milliwatt signal will not get you across a river mouth, and it never tried to. If the pair and the rock disagree at this range, the rock is still right — at 10 metres there is even less argument available than at 20,200 kilometres.

Q.3 Which frequencies does it use, and does it collide with the satellite work?

It employs UHF radio waves in the ISM bands, from 2.402 GHz to 2.48 GHz. The parent chapter’s civil signals sit at 1575.42 MHz and 1227.60 MHz, so the two systems do not share a frequency at all; they share the shed, which is a different and less official kind of collision. Within its own 78-megahertz band the near-field radio moves its carrier 1,600 times a second, skipping from channel to channel, which is how it gets through a room full of other things that also think they are using 2.4 GHz without asking. The dial on this cover shows the whole of its world, end to end: 2.402 to 2.480, a span you could fit under one hand.

Q.4 What is it for, in practice?

It serves as an alternative to wired connections, and the bench keeps the list as the field reports it: exchanging files between nearby portable devices; connecting cell phones and music players with wireless headphones; wireless speakers; HIFI systems; car audio; and the newer habit of carrying a TV’s picture and a soundbar’s silence to the other room of the house over a gap of a few metres and no cable at all. The pattern in the list is the point — every one of those devices used to end in a wire, and the wire is the part that is now optional. The bench still keeps the spare cables in the drawer under the shelf mark. Nothing on this shelf is ever fully rid of a cord; the drawer is load-bearing in the manual’s sense of the word.

Q.5 Why is it called Bluetooth?

The name goes back to Harald Bluetooth, the 10th-century king who united the warring tribes of Denmark and Norway — a king who spent his reign joining things that had not been joined. The working group took the first letters of his name in Old Norse, Gorm and Harald, and set them as a bound runic monogram, ú·Ø, which looked enough like a mark for a radio standard that no one argued. The intent was literal: a little standard that would unite the phone, the headset, the computer, and the car, the way the king united the peninsula. A unification project named for a unifier, filed in the same manual that files a fix of the Earth under its own arithmetic. The bench finds the company appropriate.

Q.6 Where does the line about the sheep’s head come from?

It comes from the voice log of 21 June, 03:55, the same session in which the acceptance test carried the Bow-BILL DUCKS fragment word for word and admitted the receiver to the bench. That night the keeper of the boundary stones from the Huyton 1950–1983 brochure — the same keeper who answered a low thumping in a field with The stone wall, the wall being, I suspect, a drum — was in the shed, listening to the near-field test with the satellite almanac still open on the bench. Overhead, on the flywire fence of the Bedford Downs build, hung the sheep’s head prop, the one that never got mounted in the Quilty-era photograph and has been dressed, from then on, in whatever the shed had to hand. Asked what the log line meant, the keeper read it once, in full, and declined to explain it, on the ground that a kept line belongs to whoever was listening. The line is therefore filed here exactly as it was logged, uncorrected, as the standing order requires:

“A sheep’s head dressed in any other species of the satellites are arranged overhead.”

— voice log, 21 June, 03:55, kept word for word

The shelf reads it as a statement about geometry and costume in the same breath: the satellites of the parent chapter, arranged overhead the way the HDOP table asks them to be, and the sheep’s head on the fence, dressed, in whatever passed for the other species that evening. Two of the shed’s three kept lines, filed one above the other. The third is still in the drawer with the spare cables.

Q.7 May I use it at sea, in place of the satellite fix?

No, and this is the standing order, printed so it does not have to be argued: the ten metres of the near-field chapter does not reach the river mouth, does not reach the reef, and will not be logged as a fix in the parent chapter’s column. The pair connects the phone to the headphones at the wheel; the receiver in the bow locker keeps doing the arithmetic of the sky. If a questioner insists on one line for the log book, the line is: the rock is right, the receiver is right, and the pair is only company.

Q.8 Where does the shelf keep the answers, and who files them?

Under the same shelf mark as the WAAS-capable receiver, at the left end of the north-wall bench, in a padded sleeve next to the flywire fence. The questions are filed as asked; the answers as printed above; the margin notes kept rather than corrected, in the identical rule that the Huyton board’s Thursday clerks followed down to the last stone, and that the parent chapter applies to the last fix of a survey. When this addendum is ever closed, the order stands: log the figure, keep the pen, close the file — and leave the sheep’s head on the wire, dressed, exactly as it was.
