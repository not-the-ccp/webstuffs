ON AIR

RÁDIO MARÉ 96.4 MHz · LISBOA · STUDIO 2

Alpha at the Gate

A radio play in one act — broadcast excerpt · transmitted 17–18 September 2020, 23:10–03:45 local time

Written for the air by Helena Cabral · Sound: the sea, the wire, and the wind

Dramatis Personae

  • MANUELA REIS — night broadcaster, Rádio Maré, Studio 2, Avenida da Liberdade
  • DOUTOR VALE — duty forecaster, IPMA, 3rd floor, Largo do Carmo
  • ÁGATA — listener, telephone line 3, the far lamp at Sagres lighthouse, 513 km south
  • THE TAPE — National Hurricane Center advisory, Miami, received on the teletype
  • THE WIND — uncredited, but present throughout

SCENE 1 — TUESDAY, 15 SEPTEMBER 2020, 14:00 UTC. WASHINGTON.

[ SFX: A teletype hammer, patient and mechanical, three floors away from any window. A coffee cup set on a console. ]

It is the day the National Hurricane Center first begins monitoring a large, ragged, non-tropical low pressure system sitting over the eastern Atlantic, four days and some five hundred nautical miles southwest of Portugal. No one in Miami expects it to become anything. It has no warm core, no spiral, no business card.

THE TAPE (flat, unhurried): Subtropical feature. Currently 42 degrees north, 24 degrees west. Track, south-southeast. Motion 10 knots. No tropical transition anticipated at this time.

[ SFX: The hammer pauses. Somewhere a screen sags with rain that is not there yet. ]

SCENE 2 — THURSDAY, 17 SEPTEMBER, 23:10 LOCAL. LISBOA, STUDIO 2.

[ SFX: Studio lamp clicks. A chair. The hum of the building's power coming in on wires that run, eventually, down to the river. ]

MANUELA: Boa noite. It is a quarter past eleven on a Thursday in September, and I have a red light in front of me that has been on since ten. This is Rádio Maré. You are hearing the storm, because the storm is here, and I am the one with the microphone. In the next two hours we will say a word that no Portuguese met office has had to say before: subtropical.

MANUELA: Doctor Vale is on line one, on the third floor of the IPMA, and he has been awake since the system first separated from its front on the morning of the seventeenth, when it finally stopped being a weather argument and started being a weather. Doctor?

VALE: Present, Manuela. Pressure at the center, 996 millibars and falling in twos. Wind maximum 95 kilometres per hour, gusting to 115. Center position, 26.4 degrees north, 16.1 degrees west, moving at 25 kilometres per hour on a bearing of 040. That is due north, Manuela. Due north, at a Portuguese coast.

[ SFX: A long, low intake of breath on the studio end of the wire. ]

MANUELA: And it will land?

VALE: The first subtropical cyclone to make landfall on mainland Portugal. Landfall estimated in the early hours of Friday between Cacilhas and the estuary of the Sado, that is, within an hour's drive of this studio. We are putting out the wind advisory. We are telling the fishing boats of the Tagus to stay where the water is deep.

[ SFX: Line 3 rings. Manuela takes it. ]

MANUELA: Line three. Go.

ÁGATA: It’s Ágata. I live at the lighthouse at Sagres, the far one, the one the sea hits from the west. I am calling to tell you the sea out here has gone the colour of a television set with no channel. My husband says the barometer fell from 1013 to 1001 in six hours and that the birds have gone quiet the way they did before the flood of 1941. I am telling you the numbers because you are the ones who owe the coast its bearings.

MANUELA: We owe them. Doctor, from this studio to the lighthouse at Sagres, how far is the storm’s journey?

VALE: From my desk to your microphone, roughly 940 kilometres of Atlantic, and from the center to the nearest Portuguese buoy, 312 nautical miles and closing. Every bearing in that sentence has a return address, and it is taped to the microphone stand in this room, where we keep it the way a lighthouse keeper keeps the lamp log. The card reads, word for word: Boarding note The return address for all bearings, distances, and festival movements.

MANUELA: A boarding note. In September.

VALE: In a season like this one, Manuela, everything is a boarding. The 2020 Atlantic has already given us twenty-one named storms, and this is the twenty-second cyclone and the twenty-first name, a letter of the alphabet so early in the season that in Washington they are still laughing at the alphabet. They named it Alpha because it is the first, and because subtropical cyclones take their names from the Greek list. It is the first subtropical storm ever to reach Portuguese soil. After tonight the coast is not a boundary. It is a door, and the door has opened inward.

SCENE 3 — FRIDAY, 18 SEPTEMBER, 02:40 LOCAL. THE TAGUS ESTUARY.

[ SFX: The wind, at last, uncredited, full voice. Glass on the estuary. A ferry horn in Cais do Sodré, answered by nothing. The storm crosses the low, flat water of the estuary with the patience of something that has already waited four days over the Atlantic. ]

MANUELA: Two forty in the morning. The wind has reached the studio. The windows of Avenida da Liberdade are doing the thing windows do, taking the building’s name and holding it. Doctor Vale, the center.

VALE: On land now. Over the low country between the estuary and the hills. Pressure 994. The first subtropical cyclone to make landfall on mainland Portugal has made landfall on mainland Portugal. I am saying it twice because in six months someone will ask me if I am certain, and I want the tape to hold the two versions.

ÁGATA (line 3, still warm): The light at Sagres is turning slower. I am not exaggerating. I am timing it against my kitchen clock. It is the sea keeping the wind company.

MANUELA: Ágata, keep the lamp. Keep the lamp, and keep the clock.

[ SFX: The teletype hammer, three floors away in Miami, resumes. ]

THE TAPE: Subtropical Storm Alpha. Landfall made on the European coast near 0040 UTC. Center pressure 994 millibars. This completes the advisory cycle. The feature will be discussed as it weakens over the interior, as weather.

SCENE 4 — 03:45 LOCAL. STUDIO 2, AFTER.

[ SFX: The storm, out of voice. A kettle on the small studio stove. Two mugs, because the wire is still up and Vale has not hung up. ]

MANUELA: For those of you still with us at three forty-five: nothing is broken. Four hundred and twelve people in temporary housing for the night. The fishing boats stayed deep. The ferry did not sail. The sea is turning back the colour it borrowed.

MANUELA: On the microphone stand there is a card, and I will read it once more because it was the truest sentence of the night: the return address for all bearings, distances, and festival movements is a room with a red lamp, and someone, and the sea, and the wire, and a lighthouse at the far edge of the country keeping the lamp turned. This is Rádio Maré. Sleep well, mainland. You have just had your first.

[ SFX: The studio lamp clicks off. The teletype, at the end of the world, falls quiet for the season’s first and only Greek letter. ]

Transmission Notes

On the night of 17 to 18 September 2020, Subtropical Storm Alpha made landfall near Lisbon, becoming the first subtropical cyclone recorded to strike mainland Portugal. It was the twenty-second cyclone and twenty-first named storm of a season that closed as the most active Atlantic hurricane season on record, and it drew its name from the Greek letter list the National Hurricane Center keeps for subtropical storms. The low had first been monitored at the NHC on 15 September as a large non-tropical system, and was only classified a subtropical cyclone by early on the 17th, after separating from its frontal features. Its center pressure at landfall is logged here at 994 millibars, its motion northward at roughly 25 kilometres per hour.

The “festival movements” of the boarding card were, that autumn, a modest thing: the AFI festival had just ended its season, and the American Film Institute’s defenders were still writing to the editor over the price of a membership and the worth of a census of frames — a letter that keeps its own red lamp in our archive, at American Film Institute — A Letter to the Editor.

For the procedure a storm teaches any building that keeps a red lamp of its own — zones, roles, and standing orders when the plain floods — the manual kept for the hospital at Mirandola sets it out turn by turn: Santa Maria Bianca Hospital — Field Manual SM-B.

For the other kind of wire that night — the one that carried dispatches from a mounted patrol to a colonial capital, from Pietermaritzburg to a border chief — the Colonial Register keeps its own account of the rider: Johan Wilhelm Colenbrander (1855–1918) — Encyclopaedia Entry, Vol. IX, No. 4,271.

And for the other season on this coast — the spring gathering that moves through a town on rivers and gives every festival a route, a bearing, and a return address — see Huddersfield — Field Manual, Chapter 9: Approaches, Rivers and the Spring Gathering.
