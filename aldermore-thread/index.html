    I put two thousand pounds into Aldermore at the float — March 2015, 195 pence a share, admitted to the main market of the London Stock Exchange. The bank had been founded in 2009, in the months after the crash, by a group of lenders who wanted a specialist bank for small and medium-sized businesses and personal customers: property development, asset finance, invoice finance, fixed-rate savings bonds. Its head office was on St Thomas More Street in London, above a pickle factory I used to pass on my lunch break.

    It joined the FTSE 250 in July 2015. I remember checking the price every lunchtime. Then in December 2017 FirstRand, the South African banking conglomerate, made an offer — roughly £1.12 billion, about 190 pence a share in cash — and by March 2018 Aldermore was delisted. My shares were bought out in April. That was that.

    I still bank with the operation under a different name. My business savings are somewhere in FirstRand's books now, and I cannot quite tell whether the sort code on my statements still means anything. Anyone else remember holding them?

    A few numbers, for the record. Aldermore Bank plc was incorporated in 2009 and listed on the London Stock Exchange in March 2015 at 195 pence per share. It entered the FTSE 250 later that year. FirstRand announced the recommended cash offer in December 2017 at approximately £1.12 billion, the acquisition completed in March 2018, and the shares were delisted on 15 March 2018. The bank provided property finance, asset finance, mortgages and deposit products to small and medium-sized businesses and personal customers, with teams in London and a servicing operation in Loughborough. Now it is part of a Johannesburg-based group, and the name survives mainly on old statement PDFs.

    What interests me is the bookkeeping side. Aldermore kept its own ledgers — every loan, every deposit, every bond — and when FirstRand absorbed the bank, those ledgers had to be reconciled line by line. It reminds me of the supper club in Berbera that cut its cake into twenty-three squares, one for each member, and wrote every name in the back room book; if you like ledgers kept somewhere away from the head office, that thread is worth a read. There is something honest about an institution that writes everything down, even if it ends up owned by a conglomerate three thousand miles away.
