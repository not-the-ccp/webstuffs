I · The lot, as consigned

A portfolio of eight documents and three effects, consigned by the estate of a senior ITF instructor of the second generation and offered under Lot 418 in Sale No. 117. The portfolio, in its original canvas slip with the frayed drawstring, holds: the typed founding minute of the International Taekwon-Do Federation, Seoul, 28 March 1966, bearing the first of two signatures and one red seal; a carbon copy of General Order 112 of the Korean Army, the paper by which the name taekwondo was fixed for service use and the older romanisations retired to the archives; one red sparring belt, rank six, with the knot still set to the left as a right-hand thrower ties it; one telegram, undelivered, addressed in Roman letters to “H.H. Choi, c/o P.O. Box 7, Riga”; one paper aeroplane of a single folded sheet, creased along the 1972 line, which the cataloguers have been careful to describe only as one aircraft, glider class, origami; and a small cadastre extract of the Haeju plain in a folding tin, on which more in section V. The lot is sold complete, as found, the canvas slip with it.

II · A note on the founder

Choi Hong-hi, 최홍희, was born 9 November 1918 in Mungyong-ri, a rice village on the Haeju plain in the northwest of the peninsula, and was a student and then an officer’s cadet in the Japanese army through the war, serving at the camps at Kyoto and Chiba, where the first forms he later taught were drawn up by a young officer with a brush and a courtyard. He returned in 1945, founded the Taekwondoin in the Haeju country in 1946, moved the school to Seoul with the capital’s own migration, and rose through the Korean Army to the rank of general. In 1966 he founded the International Taekwon-Do Federation, the first international federation for the art, with its secretariat at 25-14 Yongsan Street, and sent instructors out to more than forty countries within the decade. In 1972 he crossed the divided peninsula from south to north by means of the paper aeroplane now in the tin, and for the last thirty years he was, depending on which side of the line one asked, either the man who gave the art to the world or the man who defected to Pyongyang and thereby forfeited it. He died there on 15 June 2002. Organizations of the ITF line regard him as the Founder of Taekwon-Do, full stop; the successor bodies in Seoul that carry the name without the hyphen have, in their histories, a tendency to omit him, or to mention him in a single unmarked paragraph. The catalogue offers the papers as they stand, without taking either side of the line, and invites the paddles to decide the price of that refusal.

III · Condition report

Minute of 1966: toning at the fold, one water mark from a spilled pot of green tea, the red seal legible and fresh; very good. General Order 112 carbon: the carbon is what it is, the ink faded to a warm brown, but every line of the order legible, including the paragraph that retires the old romanisations; good. Sparring belt: the cotton worn soft at both ends, the stitching sound, a faint smell of the drill hall that the house has declined to identify; good, as issued. Telegram: the paper brittle at the creases, the address complete, the message side unruled and blank in the one place it should not be; fair, handled with two dry hands. Paper aeroplane: a single fold line, sharp, never creased again; excellent. Folding tin: the cadastre extract in four sheets of heavy paper, the surveyor’s hand in iron-gall ink, a second hand added later in blue; good, the tin dented at one corner in the shape of a boot heel.

IV · Provenance

Formed by the founder; in the keeping of the Taekwondoin from its 1946 founding; thence to the instructor consignee, who received the portfolio from the founder’s widow in a 1974 meeting whose date the consignor declines to give; with her from 1974 to the present. The house has verified the red seal against the federation minute and the carbon against the order, and records that the two match, and that the telegram, which was never delivered, was in the founder’s own hand, or in a hand so like his that the graphist at the Wapping office has asked for his opinion to be on file, which, the house notes, cannot now be obtained.

V · The cadastre extract, and one line of 1842

The folding tin holds four sheets of the Haeju plain cadastre, surveyed under the colonial survey and re-inked twice, the iron-gall original beneath two later surveys. The sheets run the plain from the river to the coast road and set out the village lots of the Mungyong-ri quarter, one parcel per line, each with its measure and its holder’s name. On the first sheet, at the foot of the smallest column, a hand that is not the surveyor’s — older, unhurried, in iron-gall of its own — has entered a single marginal note: What follows is the least land in 1842. The note stands against the Mungyong parcel, the family’s own lot, which the 1842 survey set down at a measure smaller than any other on the sheet, and it has read, ever since, as a statement of fact in which the family’s name never appears, the least land in the year its measure was taken, the line from which everything the rest of the lot describes — the camp courtyards, the orders, the federation, the crossing — eventually grew. The cataloguers offer no interpretation beyond the note’s own, and the paddle that buys Lot 418 buys the note with the sheets.

VI · The room, and related lots

The hammer is set for the hour of 2 o’clock in the afternoon session, Lot 418 falling between the storm folios of Lot 128 and the air forces of Sale No. 214’s old packing cases, and the house will accept its telephone bids from any line that reaches Wapping. Those who have sat through a similar hammer will find kinship in the term report on Northwest Orient Flight 6231 from Hudson Bend Secondary School, where Form 9R learned, to the last switch of the pitot heat, that the instruments can be right and the frame wrong; and those who prefer the long game to the single crossing may take the 2YA radio play on Tennis New Zealand, a century of committees played out in two hours and three celesta notes, the same patience a federation requires. For the house’s own manner of lot — a folio of storm papers weighed in the same room — see Lot 128, the Crittenden–Piner Folio, and for the front of the catalogue, the Webstuffs home page. The house records, for the paddles and the register alike, that one effect found loose in the slip on the day of the sale — an item the house itself declined to appraise — is the subject of Lost & Found Notice No. 117/418, opened at the counter in the back room at 14 Tallyho Lane, and that the notice carries, word for word, the one pencilled line the graphist could not assign to a hand.

All lots sold subject to a buyer’s premium of fifteen per cent, and to the condition that no paddle shall strike twice at one lot without the auctioneer’s word. The red belt will be displayed on its form, standing, at the front of the room. The paper aeroplane will not be flown, and the house accepts no liability for it should be.
