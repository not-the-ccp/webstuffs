1. What was watched, and where

The assignment was to watch a first transmission of a major series and report on it the following week, and the class of 2364 could not have chosen a harder year to be born if it had tried. On Sunday, 28 September 1987 — which this report concedes, with the head of History’s red ink on the date, is a year the academy’s own chronology keeps in the back annex, in the pre-warp files — a new science fiction series first went out to the public as Star Trek: The Next Generation. Its pilot, Encounter at Farpoint, was written by D. C. Fontana, who had written on the original series, and by Gene Roddenberry, the creator of Star Trek himself, and it was directed by Corey Allen. It was first aired as a two-hour television movie, full of commercials, and later repeat showings split it into two parts, which is how most of my classmates saw it: Part One on Thursday, Part Two on Friday, with the school between them.

The series is set in the 24th century, in the 2360s and 2370s, and follows the adventures of the crew of the Federation starship Enterprise-D, the new generation of starships. In this episode the crew of that newly built ship, under Captain Jean-Luc Picard, is on its maiden voyage out of Starbase 319 near Betazed when a space probe drifts out of the void in front of the saucer section, and they pick it up, open it, and let it beam them to their deaths — or nearly, which is the whole of the first act. The Federation, for its part, is being shadowed by a Klingon warbird, and the two crews, who were only supposed to be on a diplomatic errand, end up chasing the same probe across the edge of known space, to a place that turns out to be a border.

2. Findings, as recorded in Room 214

The probe is a probe, not a message, and the report’s first finding is that it lies. It plays a distress call on one channel and a recording of a battle on another, and both are bait. The second finding is about the Enterprise-D itself, which the class inspected in the school’s small museum on the ground floor: flat deck plates where the old ship had curves, four nacelles a full deck off the hull, a shuttle bay that swallows the whole shuttle room of the previous design, and a captain who, in one of the show’s first lines, announces that he is looking forward to something, as if a starship were a holiday. The third finding is the station the report is named for. Farpoint, a new Federation outpost at the edge of Federation space, is built for first contact; the Klingon ship that has been following it is the Yan’g’leth, and between the two of them is the border. The episode’s last act is a first-contact ceremony on the station’s observation deck, with a card game, a toast, and a border that both sides agree not to cross — an agreement the report notes is signed in the last two minutes of a two-hour movie, which is exactly how borders work in real history and exactly why the head of History countersigned the assignment.

Numbers kept for the record: one probe, one Federation outpost, one Klingon warbird, one newly built ship, two parts when aired again, four nacelles, and one border. The transmission ran two hours with commercials, which is one hour and forty-six minutes of the actual story; the class timed it on a stopwatch during Part Two and the stopwatch was not permitted to leave the room.

3. The school that week, and the line in the margin

A school report is not only about the book, and this one was written in the week of the inter-house cricket match and the opening of the scout camp on the north field, so both are logged. On Saturday the second XI came out of the pavilion at 3:40 in the rain, and the scorebook line that the scorer, Priti Anand, pencilled on the back of this very sheet survives word for word in the archive copy: “Chasing 174 to win; enter the camp open.” The first half of it is cricket: the second XI was chasing 174 to win, needing the last wicket to stand for the last nineteen runs, which is more than enough for one line of a scorebook. The second half of it is the scout camp: enter the camp open is the old camp rule, the rule that the camp gate stays open from morning muster until lights-out and that any member of the house may walk in without asking, because the camp is the one room of the school where nobody keeps a list of who is allowed. The two things sit together on the line on purpose, the scorer says: the team that chased 174 was the same team as the one sleeping in the camp, and the gate was open for them all. Mrs. Vael circled the line in red and wrote see me, because she is Science & Observation, and a scorebook line that is also a camp rule is exactly the sort of thing her class reports on.

For the record, the second XI did not win. The camp, being open, did not care.

4. Condition of the page, and where the page keeps

The sheet is ruled, 8½ by 11, with the red line at the margin and a coffee ring at the lower left that belongs to the head of History. The margin notes, which earned the B+, run along the left edge in a hand the class has decided is the scorer’s: probe = bait, 4 nacelles, border signed in final 2 min, and, at the very bottom, the line above, unmarked. The report keeps in the academy archive, drawer 3B, shelf 214, and has been offered to the house of record as the attached piece of Found — a painted canvas, six feet by four — Lost & Found, Austin, where the storeroom desk notes that a sheet with a camp rule written on it is the same species of object as a banner found in a crew storeroom at 11°F: something found carrying a reckoning of its own. The same bench procedure — make the small sample, test it, keep the line — is filed in full at the sister desk in Prototype, Method and System — Patent Application Summary 63/214,077, where an early sample built to test a concept is claimed in exactly the terms this report used of the probe, and where the crossed line is mis-filed on purpose so that the error can be paid for in cardstock. A term report of the same form, kept word for word at the desk, is New Madrid Township — Term Report, Form 3B, Room 214, which is to say Room 214 in Missouri, the other one, and the two rooms have corresponded by post since 1812, the older of them with the advantage of the earthquake.
