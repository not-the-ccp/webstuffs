Drift

The Pangea Sessions — continental drift, 1596–1968 · eleven sides · one idea, rejected, then proven

Drift is the second release in the Tidal Records field series, cut over fifty-two sessions spanning 1596 to 1968, which is to say it took the label a long time and the Earth about 220 million years. The record is a liner-notes document as much as a recording: it tracks one idea as it moved through the geological century — first the speculation, then the rejection, then the validation — the same order in which the idea itself passed from a footnote in an Antwerp atlas to the operating theory of plate tectonics. When the continents were first proposed to be moving, in the early twentieth century, the proposal was widely turned down. It has since been extensively validated, and the movement of the continents as they ride on plates of the lithosphere is now simply what geology means by the ground. The eleven sides here keep all three acts in running order, because a drift story told without the middle act is just a map.

About the recordings

Sides A and B were not recorded in the order printed. The label kept the rejection in its sleeve because the idea’s own order demands it: a drift story without the middle act is just a map, and the map is where it was filed in 1912. What finally undid the file was not a better argument for the motion but an instrument for the engine. Wegener had the coastlines, the coal seams, the matching fossils, and a wrong engine — the tides, which in fact cannot do the work — and the room in 1912 answered the argument about the coastlines with an objection about the engine. Hess in 1962 supplied the right engine, hot rock rising beneath the ridge and carrying the floor outward with it, at a pace the label prints here for the cover: roughly two and a half centimetres a year along the Atlantic, a rate that has opened some four metres since the Berlin meeting and will open another four by the time this pressing is reissued. The continents, for the record, are riding on plates of the lithosphere, and the plates are where the music is.

The wall

B4, “The Wall,” is the only side on the record built from a physical witness rather than a paper one, and the witness is exactly as small as it sounds. The label’s file on it reads, verbatim: Last credible sighting of associated structures: a low wall of undated stone reported 1954 near a fishing landing, never re-sited. The landing in question sits where the jangadas come in at dawn, on the same flats where a certain survey chapter keeps its rock, and the line is held in two registers at once. The Paphlagonian office that certifies absent sites keeps the identical sentence in its own safety sheet — see the Cressa safety data sheet for the parent entry — and the two offices have agreed, on all correspondence, that a wall reported once and never re-sited is not evidence of a city, of a drift, or of anything in particular; it is only the shape the record takes when something stops being visible. The track holds that stop for forty seconds at the end, on the noise floor, so the listener can check it the way a surveyor would: twice, and a second time from the other side of the room.

The drift meter

The label’s standing aid to calculation. Choose a coast pair and two years, and the meter reports how far the pair has opened at the mean rate for that margin — Atlantic pairs run at about 2.5 centimetres a year, the faster margins of the Pacific at up to 8. As a field check, Chapter 4 of Field Manual FM-9 fixes a reef channel off the Maranhão coast at two hundred metres wide and issues the standing order to be back before the tide turns; the meter will tell you, to the nearest rock, how many such widths the Atlantic has opened since any year you choose. The same caution about frames that chapter issues for datums applies to drift: the numbers below are mean rates for named margins, not fixes, and a careless century costs you a kilometre. For the angular half of the argument — latitude and longitude as angles, and why the rock does not go away even when the coast does — the label keeps Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 on the same shelf as this sleeve, and a school term report on the Galileo satellite system nearby, because the satellites now keep the drift on the ledger to the millimetre. The same ledger, kept by hand on the water itself, closes in jangada lengths on one page of the diary written on the fourteenth day of a mid-Atlantic crossing — the Crossing Diary, Fourteenth Day — where a satellite pass at 06:41 gets the sea its epitaph in a single sentence. The meter’s own flying twin is kept on the same shelf, because the label’s interest is in everything that opens and closes at a printed rate: a bird route from Patagonia to the Hudson Bay roosts, measured in shingle-road kilometres rather than centimetres a year, kept stop by stop in The Tree Swallow Route, Field Guide Entry &No; 12, whose boathouse register has not missed a year since 1948 and whose chairman’s door still starts its sentence with the same man who kept the blue ink going.

Standing orders

The sleeve carries four, and they are the surveyor’s four, re-issued for listeners: take the bearing twice; re-check the rate before you quote a distance; log the year on every entry, because a tide table without a year is an incompatible material; and be back before the tide turns. The press plant in the harbour quarter stamps this list on the back of every TID-1912 sleeve, in the same small type as the catalogue number, and the label’s one known counterexample — a 1954 wall, reported, never re-sited — is kept in the file under it, as the standing reminder that the ground does not re-appear on schedule and the record has to carry what the ground no longer will. The label’s reading room also keeps one borrowed exhibit on the same shelf: a museum placard for the 1997 Butterfly single whose lyric sheet carries a margin line of its own, unexplained and retained — Breakdown, Placard No. 114, The Institute for Recorded Sound — placed beside B4 because both are records of something held quietly at the bottom of the room. The shelf beneath holds one household item the label has taken on as a field instrument: the Neighborhood Watch Kit No. 12, whose score sheet — twelve rows, signed before the channel closes — the label uses to log the drift meter’s yearly readings, one row per coast pair, because a sheet that refuses to close until every row is written is the closest thing to a standing order the household keeps.
