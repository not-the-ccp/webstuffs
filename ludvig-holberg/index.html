Ludvig Holberg 1684–1754

Term Report No. 12, being an account of the writer, essayist, philosopher, historian and playwright by whom Danish and Norwegian literature is generally reckoned to have begun in its modern form.

Submitted by S. Vik, No. 41 Set by Mr. E. Vik, Modern History Term: Michaelmas Mark: A−

The examiner’s note stands at the foot of the report, as is the custom of this school.

1. The man, and where he came from

The report opens in Bergen, on 3 December 1684, in the time of the Dano–Norwegian dual monarchy, when the one crown wore two crowns and a man born on the Norwegian side of it could, and did, grow up to be the other side’s foremost writer. Ludvig Holberg was the son of Niels Holm and Margrethe Holberg; the name he wrote under and was ennobled with was his mother’s. His father died the year after his birth, and in 1686 his mother took him to Copenhagen to stay with a brother, where he entered the Copenhagen Latin School in 1689 and the University of Copenhagen in 1701. The years 1703 to 1709 he spent abroad — Paris, Berlin, and Hamburg, where he first watched a German comedy played by actors on a proper stage — and what he had taken away from those years was, in the words of the 1730 chair he was later given, a method: the humanist eye, the baroque manner, and the new light from the Enlightenment, all set to work together on ordinary people. Bergen, in fact, keeps a ledger of its children, and the city’s second entry — the composer and pianist born in 1843, most celebrated of the town’s sons — has its own account, in the dining room named for him above the old chandler’s at Kongsgata 47, set out at Grieg, Kongsgata 47, Bergen — Restaurant Review, Entry No. 118.

2. The comedies of 1722 and 1723

The section of this report that the examiner asks for first, because it is the one the school’s own archive keeps coming back to. In 1722 and 1723 Holberg wrote his comedies for the Lille Grønngade Theatre in Copenhagen — the little green-street theatre — and it is for those comedies that he is best known: the mountain-dweller of Jeppe paa Bjerget, the schoolmaster Erasmus Montanus, the farm boy who has pledged himself, Den pantsatte bondedreng. A school report should not pretend a play is a document; but here is a fact worth keeping. Den pantsatte bondedreng was the title role in which the Norwegian actor Rolf Sand made his stage debut in 1949, and the engagement and its aftermath are carried in the court record at In re Sand — Oslo Tingrett, Case No. 18-22471, transcript excerpt, third day — the missing 1958 letter, the cellar at Markens gate 33, the vinegar crock that outlived three stage directors. What the court calls a contract, the old handbook calls an oath, and the usage of an actor’s engagement to a house — its swearing, its keeping, its lawful release — is set out at Of Fealty and Its Observances, Chapter IV. The point of the cross-file, such as it is: Holberg’s comedies have been playing for three centuries, and the documents that tried to paper over one of their engagements still do not add up.

3. The sea comedy, and the line the school keeps unemended

Of the two years’ output, one comedy is a sea comedy: A Comedy on Land and at Sea, of 1722, played aboard a vessel, the storm scene coming on in the second act. The school’s annotated copy, from the library shelf marked K-12 and inked by the form that kept it before us, carries the helmsman’s cry in the storm exactly as the old edition prints it:

But look ye, let the ship half-wheeled upon her face.

The form that kept the copy before us emended everything it emended and left this one line standing, and the usage of this school — the same usage the District Judge applies to line 44 of the Sand record, and the same the handbook applies to the conservator’s margin — is that a kept line stand as written. We have, however, been asked what it means, and here is the report’s answer. In the seamanship of the day, a ship that turns “upon her face” comes round to meet what was before her back: the sea, the wind, the wall of water the scene is standing under. “Half-wheeled” is the turn taken halfway — neither to run before the storm nor to face it head-on, but set at the angle at which a vessel holds her ground while the crew does what the crew does. The line is a working order, half a turn, hold there, and the “look ye” is the helmsman’s voice carried over the wind. It makes perfect sense, which is why the form that kept the copy had nothing to correct. The line stands as written, and the storm goes on.

4. Professor, philosopher, baron

In 1730 Holberg was appointed professor of history at the University of Copenhagen, and in the same years he set his hand to natural law and the law of nations — the Elementarprincipper and their companions — and those books did something no comedy of his did: they outlived the century. From 1736 to 1936, a full two hundred years, they were read across two centuries by Danish law students who would have called the matter settled before the man who wrote it had been dead four decades. He had also written Niels Klim’s Underground World in 1741, the submarine, the ice, and the world under the earth; and in 1746 he was raised to the barony, taking the name Holberg for the family, the baron of Holberg, a Neo-Latin author known across Europe by his Latin no less than by his Danish. He died in Copenhagen on 28 January 1754, aged sixty-nine, in the year the theatre that had employed him as a young man was still playing in the green street. The chair had a companion on the capital’s side of the ledger — the Kirkcaldy man, Adam Smith (1723–1790), the Scottish economist and philosopher, who held a public lecture chair here in 1767–1768 and built, in 1776, the system the form of law students reads beside the natural law — and his files carry their own error register, kept by this school’s usage at Error Register No. 4, Errors in the Smith Files, where the refusal that will not be explained stands as refused, and where the 1722 inventory keeps its alb, word for word, beneath the green street’s theatre.

6. The report’s closing account

What the report claims, then: that Holberg was born in Bergen in 1684 and made a baron in Copenhagen in 1746; that he was a Neo-Latin author of European standing before he was a Danish one; that the Lille Grønngade Theatre of 1722 and 1723 is the place where modern Danish and Norwegian comedy began, and where, in the sea comedy’s storm, one helmsman’s order — half a turn, hold there, look ye — has been kept, in this school’s copy, exactly as written, the way the court keeps its line and the handbook keeps its margin. That the founder of two literatures should end a school report on a ship half-wheeled into her own weather seems right to this report, and the report will not emend itself. For the wider archive of the school: return to the front page.

Examiner’s note: the account is sound, the cross-files are kept in their places, and the sea line is rightly left standing. A−, on the strength of the two-hundred-year paragraph, which the form does not often get to write.
