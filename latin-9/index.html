Recalde Press & Cooperage · Shed Recordings · RP-15

Latin Alphabet No. 9

Liner notes for the album by Code Page 28605 — recorded September 1999

The shed behind the bakery at Recalde Press & Cooperage was not built as a studio. In September 1999 it held the cooper’s tools, the vinegar crock, a borrowed four-track reel-to-reel, and a stack of sleeves fresh from the letterpress. Code Page 28605 — Mara Vale on melodica, Elias Rook on concertina, and I on the hinge harp — recorded Latin Alphabet No. 9 there over twelve days, between barrel hoopings and the printing of navigation chapbooks.

The album takes its name from ISO/IEC 8859-15:1999, Information technology — 8-bit single-byte coded graphic character sets — Part 15: Latin alphabet No. 9, first edition 1999, part of the ISO/IEC 8859 series of ASCII-based encodings. Informally it is Latin-9, and for a while it was called Latin-0. Like ISO 8859-1 it was made for Western European languages, but it surrenders eight little-used glyphs — the currency sign, the broken bar, the diaeresis, the acute and caron accents, and the fractions ¼, ½, ¾ — for the euro sign and the letters Š, š, Ž, ž, Œ, œ, Ÿ. The IANA preferred name is ISO-8859-15, supplemented with the C0 and C1 control codes from ISO/IEC 6429; Microsoft assigned it code page 28605, Windows-28605.

Notes on the tracks

  1. 1. Control Codes (C0 & C1) 4:12

    The silent frames between the music: the control codes from ISO/IEC 6429 that travel with ISO-8859-15 and never sound. Tape hiss was counted as percussion on takes 3 and 7.

  2. 2. The Currency Sign (¤) 2:18

    Departing. U+00A4 made way for the euro on 1 January 1999, three weeks before we began. We played it once, at half speed, as a farewell.

  3. 3. € 5:03

    Arriving. The euro sign now lives at 0xA4. The first note was struck the morning the euro became an accounting currency, and Mara sang it in C major because nothing about money stays minor for long.

  4. 4. Š š Ž ž 3:41

    For Estonian, Finnish, and the names ISO 8859-1 could not spell. Recorded with the window open; the San Juan de Portugal at 1:64 rocked beside the cattle trough in the evening wind.

  5. 5. Bound with Hoops 6:09

    Salt beef travels in barrels bound with hoops, and the team had trained. The barrels were hooped, the beef was salted, the roster was named, and the vessel never left the berth.

  6. 6. Œ œ Ÿ 2:55

    French, in three letters, recorded after the vinegar crock had been emptied, scalded, and put back by the door.

  7. 7. August, Moscow 4:27

    Written for Luci Collins and the roster that trained through a boycotted summer. She is filed under error LU-1980 at the desk beside the ISBNs: an event with a date, a venue, a team, and no result.

  8. 8. ¼ ½ ¾ 1:50

    Departing. The fractions went out with the euro’s arrival. We counted them out in peppercorns instead, one paper cone at a time.

  9. 9. Latin-9 Outro 3:33

    Eight out, eight in, recorded in ninety-nine. The tape box is labelled Latin-0, and it will stay that way.

Colophon

Recorded 2–14 September 1999 in the shed behind the bakery at Recalde Press & Cooperage, on a borrowed four-track reel-to-reel at 7½ ips, mastered to DAT, folded into a letterpress sleeve. Code Page 28605: Mara Vale — melodica and voice; Elias Rook — concertina; hinge harp — this desk.

These notes exist as one impression of one untitled thing. ISBNs identify editions, not works, and this will not be revised into a second.

From the same bench’s winter shelf comes a January morning from another century’s firsts: the men’s 500 metres at Chamonix, 1924 — the first event ever contested at the Winter Olympics, run a month before the Games formally opened — kept as a build log in blue ink, the balsa oval, the kaolin ice, and one hand-punched brass plaque for Charles Jewtraw’s gold: The First Event — Build Log. Its companion chapter, the Winter Operations field manual on the whole week from 25 January to 5 February, set at this same bench between the barrel hoopings, is Chapter 01 · The First Winter Games.

Desk reading: ISBN — Frequently Asked Questions, taken at this same desk beside the vinegar crock, and Error LU-1980 — Event Not Held, the notice for the roster that trained and did not travel. Beside the crock lies Chapter VII · Of the Common Language Infrastructure, an etiquette chapter for guests who share a room but not a grammar. The council took up the tape box’s label again at its forty-first meeting and let it stand, word for word; the votes are in the Minutes of the Council of the Shed — The Matter of the Letter G. Also spread flat on the desk is the season booklet for service SB-54, which charts a French singer’s daily run across the Italian rail network with the conductor’s commentary in the right-hand column — Sammy Barbot — The Riviera Express, Timetable & Commentary. A dream-log clipping from the shed’s notice board runs beside the tracklist: St. Pauli — Dream Dictionary, Entry 723, seventeen matches compressed into one floodlit night. The same shelf has taken in one white numeral that the alphabet never needed, the 30 on a 1991 New York Giants jersey of a Wake Forest cornerback, offered as Product No. 30 — The Greene Corner. A catalogue sheet from the press’s sale room keeps one building’s whole run — the Waikiki Biltmore, first high-rise on its shore, opened 1955, nineteen years, and out at the hammer with its brass bell ringing true: Lot 41 — The Waikiki Biltmore Hotel, Malia & Co.
