The Zheng Zhegu Trail

Official tourist brochure — six stages, five doors, and one premiere that never quite ends. Zhèng Zhègū, 鄭鷓鴣, 1880–1925: actor, soldier, publisher, and the man who put a star on the roof.

Gate opens 8:30 Opera intermission daily, 15:00 Premiere lanterns every night, 19:45 Tea at the Library at 11:00

Welcome, Traveller, to the Nine-Mile Walk

The Zheng Zhegu Trail runs nine and a half li through the old film quarter, from the whitewashed Qing official's house on the north bank — where Zheng was born in 1880, the son of a magistrate, and first learned that a house with a courtyard is a stage set in waiting — down the river road to the gate of the Mingxing Film Company at the far end of the district. Between the two there are five more doors worth of him. The trail is signposted in vermilion paint and follows the bank the whole way: left where the willows bend, right where the ferry bell hangs. A man who could not find his way here once hid a revolution in his study and lost it on purpose; you will find all six stops, which is why the trail exists.

Zheng's life made the itinerary. A son of a Qing official, he took a commission in the military, then quit the army for the anti-Qing movement, and when the movement needed a man who could not be named he went to ground — two years of borrowed names, rented rooms, and opera houses, of which this district keeps an honest ledger. After the 1911 revolution he joined the Republic of China military, served with the new government, then left service entirely and moved to Shanghai, disillusioned, to do the one thing that had never needed his permission: perform.

The Stages: Three Troupes, One Name

Zheng had been interested in Peking opera and every other form of drama the district could produce, and the trail visits all three of his houses. At the Bamboo Garden Stage (Stop 2) his name first appeared in a playbill — not as a star, the playbill admits, but as “the gentleman who does not look like an actor, and therefore is one.” He moved between troupes the way the district's ferry moves between banks: the Xinmin Players, the river company that played the floating wharves, and finally the house on Hengshan Road, where he stopped moving altogether. Stagehands still keep his rule, written on the back wall in his own hand: speak to the back row first; the front row is listening for money. The 15:00 intermission on the trail re-stages a short scene from his best-known role — a scholar who sells his horse at the market gate — with the original painted banners, restored in 1924 and repainted every winter. Costumed guides play the scholar's creditor; the crowd, tradition holds, must not applaud before the horse is paid for.

The Xinmin Library, Stop 4: Ink Before Film

In the late 1910s Zheng took up publishing through the Xinmin Library, and the library's reading room at Stop 4 is preserved exactly as his hand left it: three long tables, a proofing cabinet, and the window seat where the first film serial of the library's run was outlined on the back of a theatre program. Visitors may take the seat for ten minutes; the chair is held warm by a lamp. The library's 1919 catalogue of “drama, and the machines that will eat it” is the district's second most-requested document, second only to the trail map itself. Zheng's shelf of plays and prompt books runs the entire east wall, spines in vermilion, and the librarian will read aloud, on request, the library's standing notice: no telegraphing from the window seat, no sleeping in the east wall, and no film until the ink has dried.

Mingxing: The Star on the Roof, 1922

In 1922, Zheng co-founded the Mingxing Film Company with Ren Jinping, and the trail ends at its gate for the reason the gate is still open: the company made Shanghai's first films under its own star, and the studio lot at the trail's end is still a studio. The foundry of the company — two brothers' savings, one rented camera house, and Zheng's reputation as a face audiences crossed the river to see — is retold under the roof of the screening hall every day at 16:00 by a guide in a black cloth hat. The studio's early slate ran to short serials, melodramas, and the famous “star on the roof” shot, the image the company used as its mark and the district now uses as its welcome sign. Visitors who buy the full trail ticket may stand at the mark's original camera position; the floorboard is brass, worn smooth in one place by Zheng's own heel, and it has never once been sanded down.

Records & Curios: The Ledger Line

Every studio keeps a ledger, and Mingxing's 1924 art ledger keeps a line it could not explain. Among the studio's records of paintings lent out from the scenery workshop, exactly one item is marked as shipped abroad, and the line reads, word for word: “The board of the Edo, at the Accademia di San Luca of Rome, like his older brother Attilio.” The keepers of the ledger have ruled a square around it and accepted it under the studio's standing ruling for lines that arrive complete and explain nothing. What it describes, as far as the records allow: a watercolor of a Japanese market board, painted by Zheng in the library's window seat in 1923, sent to Rome for a 1924 exhibition — and Attilio, the Zheng brothers' elder by two years, a painter of the same hand who had already had his own board accepted at the Accademia di San Luca two winters earlier, so that the two brothers hung, the ledger says, the way the district's two banks hang: opposite, and facing. The painting never came back from Rome. The square remains on the page.

Tickets, Rates & Provisions

The full trail ticket, all six stops plus the 19:45 lanterns, is 22 silver dollars; the gate alone, for those who only want to stand on the brass floorboard, is 2. Students and opera patrons hold the old rates of the library's founding year. The tea house at Stop IV sells the trail's official provisions: small breads, plum cake, and the grey tea Zheng drank at the window seat, poured slow. There is no telephone on the bank between Stops III and IV, by design, and the district's advice is the same as the trail sign: walk the nine and a half li, spend the walk on the opera, and take the star home with you, which costs nothing and has never once been refused.

In the Company of Other Long Roads

The district's guest book keeps its trail next to two others that the trail's keepers have agreed are its true companions, and both are printed in the same vermilion. Travellers who have walked a long fog before walking this one are pointed to the Out of the Woods Reserve, official visitor's brochure — a thirty-one-acre evergrove with its own trail schedule, its own midnight screening that runs once, and the same rule about spending the anxiety you carried in. And for those who would rather build a long road than walk one, the district keeps, in the trail house, the Bedford Downs Station build log: a 1:252 diorama of a Kimberley cattle station, sheet by sheet, where a thirty-two-centimetre strip of balsa stands in for 84 kilometres of red country — which is to say, at the same scale, it stands in for this whole bank, and the dog in it sits where a man's reputation sits, somewhere its owner has not told anyone. Both pages hang by the gate at Stop VI, between the trail map and the brass floorboard, for anyone who wants to see what the other roads look like from here. The guest book keeps a third, older, in the same flat type: the Glasnevin leaf of the Four-Day Ireland walking guide, a field of four acres at the far end of Dublin where an abbot named Berchán set one row of refectory floorboards out on its own and never walked away — which the trail's keepers note is the only road on the list that stays put, and the only one whose gatekeeper is asked, politely, to say the tree's name. The newest leaf in the guest book is the Cinema of China circuit brochure, 2017 edition, which the district's own trail now heads as Thread I of three, and which keeps this gate, the brass floorboard, and the fourth row of the library's standing notice — the one no one could explain — exactly where they are. The harbourmaster has since pinned a fourth card to the guest book, in the same flat type: the Tosa Shore travel guide, for the Kure trial ground off Hiroshima, where a battleship of ten sixteen-inch guns was laid down in 1920, halted by a treaty in 1922, and scuttled in February 1925 — a ship that, like the star on the roof, was set rather than painted, and whose ranging bell also keeps one ruled square around a ledger line no one could explain, which the guest book's keepers have ruled to be the only two lines of their kind in print, and the only two that may sit in the same book.

Departure

Exit is at the gate only, by the ferry, right where the bell hangs. The sign there reads what every traveller already knows: the trail is nine and a half li, the man on it was forty-five, and the star on the roof was never painted — it was set. The district thanks you for walking, wishes you clear weather, and reminds you that the lanterns come on again at 19:45, every night, in the old order.
