The 2014 Formula 1 United States Grand Prix was the 17th round of the 2014 Formula One World Championship and the 36th United States Grand Prix held as part of the series. It ran on a Saturday afternoon — 2 November 2014 — at the Circuit of the Americas, the 5.513-kilometre, 20-corner track that opened in 2012 and has since become the series' American home for the modern era. Mercedes' Lewis Hamilton won all 56 laps on track from second on the grid, with Nico Rosberg second and Red Bull's Daniel Ricciardo third. The questions below are the ones the fan desk at turn 14 got most that weekend, answered the way we answered them, chalk dust and all.

Frequently asked, frequently re-asked

Hamilton won the 56-lap race, and the P2 detail is what made it. Pole was his, on paper: he had qualified ahead of Rosberg by a fraction of a tenth, but a one-place grid drop for an overtake-zone infringement at Silverstone — carried across by regulation — pushed him from first to second on the Austin grid, so he launched beside his own teammate instead of ahead of him. He led from the first corner of the race and never surrendered the car. It was Hamilton's tenth victory of the 2014 season and the 32nd of his career. Rosberg took second, and Ricciardo — in a Red Bull running on the old-spec car before the year-end upgrade — held Vettel to a fighting third on the final lap, the first podium of his own breakthrough season and the one the American crowd still quotes back to him at the pit-lane bar.

Heading into Austin, Hamilton led the World Drivers' Championship from his own teammate Rosberg, and their team Mercedes had the constructors' title effectively inside their grasp — a gap no one left of them could realistically close in five races. That made the weekend a two-horse fight inside one garage: the team orders had been openly retired the previous season, so Mercedes let the two of them simply drive, and the two of them did. Vettel, in a Red Bull that was being overtaken by events, was the best of the field outside the silver cars; by the chequered flag at turn 14 the standings had the Mercedes pair separated by a handful of points and the season's endgame reduced to three sprints.

The Saturday date was a television decision: the race shared a slot with the Australian Grand Prix the following weekend, and the broadcasters moved Austin forward a day rather than run two grands prix in one stretch of the calendar. The weather was the weekend's other story. A cold front stalled over the Hill Country for most of Friday and Saturday morning; the grandstand at turn 3 turned into a sea of orange and black ponchos and the race control room spent the day arguing about dry-weather tyre allocations. The front eventually peeled away and the race ran dry — everyone on the podium finished on the soft compound — but the near-miss is why the pit crews still joke that Austin is a wet race with a waiting list.

It's real in the way archive things are real. The timing building behind pit exit kept a handwritten back-up card for every session, one line per car, the old chalk discipline from before the lap-timing screens took over — a practice the COTA timing office kept out of respect for the people who learned the job when a race was measured by a man with a stopwatch on a bridge. The card from Saturday's race carries a note in the top margin, attributed in the office ledger to a retired clerk who, before she ever clocked a flypast of F1 cars, was certified at a convalescent hospital for wounded officers and did her first real ledger work there: “The edit gives each figure an equal measure of time and certified at a convalescent hospital for wounded officers; she worked among the lot draws the difference at year's end.” The timing office has read it three ways — a note about fairness in how each car's figure is given the same measure of time, a biographical aside about the clerk herself, and a plain misfiled postcard — and has kept it pinned where it is because the third reading has never produced the postcard. Whatever it is, it is the only line of marginalia on the 2014 US GP card, and it has outlived every other annotation on it.

Two reasons, both boring, which is how it becomes legend. First, tyre strategy: the Red Bull team had quietly run more long stints in free practice with the old-spec car and knew its soft-compound fade curve better than Mercedes' engineers were comfortable admitting. Ricciardo stopped once where Vettel's plan had him on a second stop, and the second stop cost him roughly the same number of laps as the first one bought. Second, the track: the back straight into turn 13 is where an older-spec car with a good engine can defend, and Ricciardo — who had been telling everyone all season he wanted an American win — defended it like a man settling a debt. He led the last 41 laps of the race against Vettel and lost by about eight seconds, the closest of the two. The crowd at turn 14, which had by then adopted him, was not interested in the arithmetic.

The Williams of Bottas finished fourth, the first points for the team's new power-unit era that would turn into a whole season's story by December; Button was fifth. Force India's Hulkenberg and Perez took seventh and eighth, a rare clean double. The two Ferraris qualified well and finished mid-pack, Vettel sixth after the Ricciardo fight and Raikkonen a few places behind him, which is the kind of weekend a factory team builds an off-season around. Safety cars were not the story — there was one, early, for a stopped car at turn 20 that compressed the field and cost a front-row car a stop it could not recover. The finish order at the chequered flag, for the desk file: Hamilton, Rosberg, Ricciardo, Bottas, Button, Vettel, Hulkenberg, Perez, Raikkonen, and the rest of the points across the line.

Attendance was set around 100,000 across the three days, with the race-day grandstand count topping 90,000 — the biggest gate the track had turned to date, and the reason the series stopped looking for a second American venue after this. The “36th” in the official title counts every United States Grand Prix run as part of the World Championship since 1958, through the Indianapolis years, Watkins Glen, and the Dallas interludes, so the number is less a measure of Texas than of how many times the championship has crossed the Atlantic to visit it. Austin was only the circuit's second hosting season, but the 36 carried the whole history on its back, and the timing board at the start — the one the fan desk has since replicated in miniature over the counter — showed it as a single line in the programme: USA GP — No. 36.

Start with the timing sheets, not the highlights; the race is a strategy race and the highlights sell it as a driving race, and both things are true. Then, if you like your background in other rooms: the Correspondents’ Board keeps Thread No. 54, “The Fourth of the Family, at the Gauge”, which is a masterclass in how one ledger line can outlive the man who wrote it — the same thing happening to the marginalia on our own timing card, only with worse handwriting. And for a reminder that a single night can erase a whole city's worth of history in an afternoon, the Home Service transcript of Coventry, 14 November 1940 — A Radio Play Excerpt is the closest thing in our collection to what a race broadcast would sound like if the city under it didn't make it to the morning; we play it, low, behind the desk on rainy Saturdays. The restaurant crowd at Via Appia House on Ankeny Street, if you're ever in Portland, runs a five-course menu on the theme of a dynasty that lasted exactly 58 years — see The Praetorian Table review — and has kindly agreed that Hamilton's ten wins that season beat Nero's sixteen, on points.
