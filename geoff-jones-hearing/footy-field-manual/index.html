Australian Rules Football

The Oval, the Guernsey and the Margin

CHAPTER NINE · OF THE OVAL GAME AND THE PAPERS THAT BELONG TO IT

Issued for use by club secretaries, ground staff, canteen keepers, and officers of any estate holding a sheet of blue wove paper. Print at the club press, St Kilda; Unley supplement set separately and bound in.

9.1 Scope

Australian rules football, or Aussie rules, and known simply in Australia as Australian football, football, or footy, is a contact sport played between two teams of eighteen players on an oval field, often a modified cricket ground. Points are scored by kicking the oval ball between the central goal posts, worth six points, or between a central post and an outer post, worth one point, otherwise known as a behind. During general play, players may position themselves anywhere on the field and use any part of their bodies to move the ball; the primary methods are kicking, handballing, and running with the ball. There are rules on how the ball can be handled, for example that a player running with the ball must bounce it or handball it once the distance covered exceeds a step-count the ground rules call advantage, and the chapter that follows states the working practice in numbers, so that a secretary in Adelaide can be certain a sheet is what the club says it is.

This chapter is cross-filed in the hearing paper of In re Hatherell, No. SCD 1874/2019, Exhibit Two, on the single blue 1954 fixtures sheet raked from the footpath behind the weatherboard house on Mount Lofty Road, Unley. Where this manual speaks of the Hills Oval, it means the ground at the foot of that road, and where it speaks of the margin, it means the lower right margin of that sheet, in which the notation JONES, G. — 1954 stands in thin fountain pen, with a tick, and below the tick the number 19.

9.2 The Ground

The ground is an oval, not a rectangle; keep to that. The Hills Oval at Unley measures, at the 1958 survey, 158 metres along the long axis and 94 metres along the short, the old cricket square left in the middle where the ball is thrown in after every behind. At each of the two ends stand four posts: the two central goal posts at 6.4 metres, and the two outer posts at 20.12 metres from the centre line. A ball that passes over the ground between the central posts is a goal, six points; between a central and an outer post, a behind, one point; outside both, out. Ruck circles stand at the centre and at a quarter and a half-way mark of the long axis; the throw-in is made straight up, and both ruckmen come to the ball.

9.4 Handling the Ball

The handball is a tap of the ball against an open fist, held with the other hand, and the ball is passed, never thrown. A player running with the ball is fair on it so long as the ground rules are observed: where the running distance reaches the limit the manual calls advantage, the player must bounce the ball once or handball it before running on, and a second bounce before the ball is kicked. After the bounce, the player may kick it in any direction with any part of the foot. Kicking is done by the drop, the snap, and the punt; the drop and the snap are preferred for the long kick up the field, because the oval gives distance that a rectangular ground does not, and a long kick from the ruck at the Hills Oval, with the wind out of the Hills, has been measured at a full quarter mile, which the canteen records and disputes.

9.5 The Guernsey and the Number Nineteen

Each player of the eighteen carries a number on the front and the back of the guernsey, and the number is the player's business on the ground. In the St Kilda photograph of the 1955 season, held in the club archive, the full-forward line stands in the second row and the number 19 stands with it, on the back of the man the Hatherell file identifies as Geoff Jones, born 5 April 1930, who played for St Kilda in the Victorian Football League and died 15 February 2018. A secretary examining a sheet against such a photograph should compare the number in the margin with the number in the row, and if they agree, should stop comparing and write it down. The tick beside JONES, G. on the exhibit sheet is of a kind this manual knows: in the Unley house, a tick beside a name means someone has been paid, or someone has been remembered, one of the two, and the manual does not resolve which.

9.6 The Travel Line and the Tick

When the club sends a player on the road, the secretary enters the sum in the secretaries' book against the date of payment, marks it with the destination in two letters, and ticks the name in the margin of whatever sheet the money was paid against. The practice is stated because the secretaries' books of St Kilda, 1954, carry the line: 19 May 1954, eleven shillings, G. Jones, travel, SA — Adelaide. The margin of the fixtures sheet carries the name and the tick. The two entries are the same event in two ledgers, and an officer of the estate will be asked, as the parties to the hearing are asked, whether the one makes the other private, and this manual answers in its only way: record both, tick neither, and keep the sheet in a dry drawer between the tea tin and the letter box key, where it will ask for nothing and ask nothing.

9.7 Canteen and the Unley Ledger

Match day at the Hills Oval is provisioned by the canteen keeper from the club ledger, and the 1958 ledger — the year the St Kilda secretariat made its Adelaide trip, and the same year, on the other side of the record, a traveling theater in Trondheim pinned its timetable above the wardrobe trunk and lost one letter of engagement to a damp cellar, a matter the estate took to the Oslo Tingrett as Case No. 18-22471 — the 1958 ledger, then, is kept in a hand that is not the secretary's, and one line of it has never been corrected, in the manner of a record that keeps its own margins. Rations for a match day at Unley, 1958: one pint of milk to the player, six biscuits to the player, and the keeper's additions, transcribed verbatim from page 41 of the ledger, with the parenthesis as found: “The quantities may be added, if approved; 1/2 a pint of cream, a little patience shown, while the Dodo managed it.)” The Dodo was the canteen's terrier, who supervised the cream and took the last biscuit, and the line is kept unamended because a keeper's additions, once made, belong to the keeper, and the court practice on such lines, as a sister court has ruled, is that they stand as written.

9.8 Standing Orders

(1) The sheet is evidence until it is not. (2) Eleven shillings is a payment, not a claim. (3) The number 19 belongs to the row it stands in. (4) The Dodo is to be mentioned in the ledger and in no other record. (5) This chapter ends where the oval ends, which is everywhere, and the manual passes to the next field. (6) Every song ends everywhere, including one first issued on 11 June 2001; its obituary is kept at Everywhere — An Obituary, filed under E between the goal posts.
