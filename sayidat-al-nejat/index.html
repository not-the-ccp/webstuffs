No. 82 · issued the first Sunday of Advent, December 2010 · from the lamp room, one flight up from the gate

“The lamp is lit, the record is kept, the oil is counted.” — charter, line 1

On the siege of the Sayyidat al-Nejat Cathedral, Baghdad

The evening of Sunday, 31 October 2010 will not be let pass quietly by this club, so we set the matter down here in full, in the ledger hand. During the Sunday evening Mass at the Sayyidat al-Nejat Cathedral — the Syriac Catholic church in the Sadr district of Baghdad, whose congregation of some four hundred worships under the ceiling painted with the Boat of the Virgin — six men of the Islamic State of Iraq, a militant group whose stated aim was the overthrow of the Iraqi federal government and the establishment of an Islamic state in Iraq, came through the door with the last of the choir still singing. They were suicide bombers. They wore the vests under their coats, and they did not detonate; they took the congregants — worshippers, clergy, the police of the parish door — hostage, and they sat down in the nave with them and asked for things.

The rector, Father Ishaq al-Jabouri, had been serving that Mass for twenty-one years and knew every face in the pews. He was not killed, and we tell you that plainly, because the ledger does not leave the living where it cannot say so. Hours later — our reckoning is three hours and forty-one minutes from the first shot — Iraqi commandos stormed the church. In the confrontation that followed, fifty-eight were killed: worshippers, priests, policemen, and bystanders at the gate. Seventy-eight more were wounded or maimed. The names are in the parish register; we have copied them to the lamp room board in the order the register gives them, and we do not rearrange.

The word went out on the wire and by telephone. World leaders condemned the massacre; so did Sunni and Shi'ite imams in Iraq, some of them on the same evening, from pulpits a dozen miles apart. That this is how the neighbourhood answered a thing done in the name of a state yet to be built is a line we have set in the margin and underlined twice.

In late November 2010, Huthaifa al-Batawi, who was accused of planning the siege and supplying the six vests, was brought before the magistrate in Baghdad. The ledger records the date in pencil, because the club's rule is that nothing is inked that a court has not yet confirmed. We will ink it, or correct it, in No. 83.

The night, by the quarter hour

The lamp room keeps the night of 31 October by six turns of the clock, as the board of the church's own clock would have turned had it been allowed to finish. Choose a turn below; without the page's small machinery, all six stands are written out in full beneath, which is how the ledger prefers it anyway.

18:30 — The Mass

Sunday evening Mass at Sayyidat al-Nejat. The nave holds about four hundred; the choir is twenty-two voices; Father al-Jabouri takes the first part of the prayer for the sick on the third pew, as he does on the third Sunday of every month. The oil in the sanctuary lamp is counted by Sister Hanna at the door. Everything is in its place, which is what a Mass is, and what the ledger is for.

18:57 — The Door

Six men in dark coats come in under the last of the choir. Two take the door from the inside; four go to the pews. They carry guns and they carry vests, and it is the vests that decide the shape of the next three hours, because nobody in a vest can be shot with a bullet that is not also a fuse. The last of the choir finishes the antiphon. Father al-Jabouri is in the sanctuary. The clock on the wall of the church, which has been slow since August, reads 18:57.

19:20 — The Line

The police line the street in front of the cathedral and beyond it, down the side of the district. Nobody is let in or out. The negotiators take their positions with their radios, and the hostages in the nave are given water by the two attackers who speak the gentlest. The line holds. This is the longest turn of the night; the ledger writes it in full size because it was the part where the street, block by block, decided to stay.

22:38 — The Storming

Three hours and forty-one minutes after the first shot, Iraqi commandos storm the church. They go room by room the way a man goes through a house he knows. The vests that did not go off in the nave go off in the crossing. The commandos clear the apse, the sacristy, and the gate. The confrontation lasts, by the lamp room's count, a matter of minutes; the ledger will not say how many, and neither does the parish register, and we are agreed.

23:11 — The Counting

The count is set in the register before the ambulances finish: fifty-eight dead — worshippers, priests, policemen, bystanders at the gate — and seventy-eight wounded or maimed. The names come in as the ambulances leave, and the lamp room copies them in the order given, in the ledger hand, one name to one line, no two names to a line. The oil is not counted until the names are, and then it is counted, and it is enough.

01:02 — The Lamp

At 01:02 the street is quiet enough to hear the church clock, still slow, finish the hour it began at 18:57. The lamp room lights its lamp for the night and writes it in the ledger: for the fifty-eight, by name; for the seventy-eight, by the register's order; for the street that stayed, by the block count. The chimney is cut to the north, as the bylaws require, and the draft holds. Air is not allowed to change its mind. Neither is the record.

What the Society did, and will do

The Society met that night in its usual room, one flight up from the gate, and did the only thing the bylaws give it to do: it kept the lamp lit and the record open. We lit one lamp to the sanctuary lamp of Sayyidat al-Nejat, which is to say, one of ours to theirs, and we kept it until the clock in the lamp room read the same slow hour the church clock had read. For the funeral weeks, the lamp stays on through every Sunday, and the names are read at the door, in the register's order, by whoever is on watch.

For the singing of those weeks, we have asked the bench at Fenn Court to keep working on its music-box organ for “Amazing Grace” — build log No. 41, which we read at the last meeting — so that when the first public Mass is held for the dead, the tune that crosses a crowd the way a lamp crosses a dark street is a tune a machine can carry without tiring. The file is kept at The Music-Box Organ for “Amazing Grace”, Bench Log No. 41, and we read the log the way we read everything in this club: slowly, and to the end of the page.

Two standing rules bear on the keeping of a vigil, and we reprint them here because a newsletter is where a small club does its restating.

  1. Rule 2 — of the oil. The oil is counted before the names and after the names, and never while the names are being read.
  2. Rule 3 — of the chimney. The chimney of the lamp is cut to the north, to the side the still air comes from, so that a draught once admitted cannot come back another way. The bylaws put it in the old hand: Air is not allowed to change its mind. A vigil that turns on its draught is a vigil the street cannot rely on, and the street is all we keep it for.

The ledger travels where the lamp's news travels. The Eastern counter of the Album Keepers, at the Nakameguro Annex above the noodle shop, has agreed to keep a copy of No. 82 at its counter and to read the register's order once, in the hush the annex keeps better than most rooms; its standing answers are filed at Tokyo — Frequently Asked Questions · The Nakameguro Annex, and we thank them for the practice. Their own newsletter, The Long Side, No. 47, reaches our counter with the same post as the lamp room's oil orders, and the two are read in the same evening. Beside the ledger shelf sits, for those who ask, a pressed LP about the Hong Kong surveyor Leung Chun-ying — third Chief Executive of Hong Kong between 2012 and 2017, and convenor of the Executive Council before that — whose liner notes keep a vigil of their own over a train that will not stop, filed at The Convenor’s Reprise · Liner Notes, CY-112; we do not play side A after midnight, which is the whole of our counsel on it. The whole shelf, if you want to walk it, opens from the Webstuffs home page.

Set in the ledger hand in the lamp room, one flight up from the gate, by the hand on the Sunday watch. The names are in the register; the oil is counted; the lamp is lit. No. 83 will carry the docket of al-Batawi's first hearing, inked or corrected, and whatever else the winter gives us to count.
