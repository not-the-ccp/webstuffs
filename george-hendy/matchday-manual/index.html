Rugby Union — Matchday Field Manual

Prepared for the supporters’ terraces, the bench, the canteen, and the full-back. Companion volume to the forum thread on George Hendy — full-back, Northampton Saints, which has kept the season on the record since 10 November 2024.

Clause 1 — Nature of the Game

Rugby union football, commonly known simply as rugby union or often just rugby, is a close-contact team sport that originated at Rugby School in England in the first half of the 19th century. Rugby involves running with the ball in hand. In its most common form, the game is played between two teams of 15 players each, using an oval-shaped ball on a rectangular field called a pitch. The pitch has H-shaped goalposts at both ends. The objective of the game is to score more points than the opposing team by scoring tries, conversion kicks, penalties, and drop goals. It is one of the two major codes of rugby football, the other being rugby league. Rugby union is a popular sport around the world.

At Franklin’s Gardens the manual keeps the short form: fifteen to a side, one oval ball, H-posts at both ends, four ways to score. Everything else is commentary.

Clause 2 — The Ground and the Weather

The pitch at Franklin’s Gardens is one hundred and ten metres long by sixty-eight metres wide, the try line falling under the advertising boards in both ends, and the H-posts standing 5.6 metres tall where the tannoy wires cross. The goal posts themselves are 5.6 metres apart and the crossbar hangs at 3 metres; a conversion from twenty-two out is a matter of degrees and nerve, which is why the home side’s kicker has been offered, since 2019, a small warm room and a hot water bottle no questions asked.

The high ball is taken, when taken at all, from the north-west. On wet afternoons the surface in the right-hand channel holds water the way the boathouse keeps its shelf in Greek — whole, in order, without flipping — and the manual notes that the best field positions on a Tuesday in November are the ones you could have had all along if you had watched the cloud.

Clause 3 — Scoring, in Order of Preference

3.1 The try. Five points. The ball, grounded with downward force in the in-goal area. The manual’s standing instruction to the terraces is that a try is not the same as the celebration, and that the whistle separates the two.

3.2 The conversion. Two points. Kicked at any angle from a spot on the line of the try. From a dead spot near the posts it is nearly certain; from the left touchline in wind it is a matter of faith, and the Field Office records the kickers’ preferences on a card that has not been updated since Rev. B.

3.3 The penalty. Three points. Kicked straight between the posts. The manual does not argue with the referee; it files.

3.4 The drop goal. Three points, dropped and struck in open play. The Field Office counts one per season, on average, and keeps the count double-sided on proper paper, folded once, in the same box as the thread that files the rest.

Clause 4 — The Full-Back’s Standing Orders

The full-back is the last line and the first opinion. Four standing orders apply, in order of seriousness, and they have not changed since the autumn of 2023:

  1. The high ball under lights is a decision before it is a catch. By the time the catch is complete, the weight must already be moving forward. The manual’s reference file for this order is the Welford Road game of last January: nine high balls, nine catches, one chip and chase of twenty-four metres regathered inside the ten-metre line, and the away crowd silent for twenty minutes straight, as the forum thread sets it down at full length.
  2. The number on the shirt changes; the position does not. Whether the sheet says fifteen or the sheet says three, the full-back is the full-back, and the bench numbering is not an argument.
  3. Count what you kick. The clerk of the record has been filing Hendy’s kicks from hand at seventy-four, of which fifty-nine found touch, and the manual endorses that number as of 30 November 2024. Week by week it moves; the file keeps pace.
  4. The game does not end at the whistle. It is filed. The season does not end either; it is filed, and the file stays open until the autumn.

Clause 5 — Canteen Orders, Halftime

The halftime plate at the north-east canteen is one of the few fixed points in a matchday, and the canteen’s own manual — a laminated sheet above the pass, older than the tannoy — keeps its knife line word for word: It must be sliced; mince the parsley and cut the peel of the food. Sliced, not chopped, because the plate has a queue behind it; the parsley minced because it is the only part of the plate the Field Office has never seen argued about; and the peel cut, never left, because the peel of a roast parsnip at the Franklin’s Gardens canteen is, on a wet Tuesday, worth more than the game.

The manual notes that the canteen staff read this line as a matter of discipline and have been known to correct a player’s plate in the tunnel on the way back for the second half. The player has always complied. The full-back complies first.

Clause 6 — Standing Orders for the Terraces

Sing at the kickoff, at the last ten minutes, and at the try; file everything else. The terraces keep their own record — seat J14 in the garden end has kept one since 2019 — and the Field Office will not dispute a terrace’s count until the season’s file is closed, which is in the autumn, when the thread opens again and the manual is revised. Rev. D will be issued on the first Saturday of the new season, and it will carry one new line for every high ball that the lights did not settle.

Clause 7 — Filing and Related Records

This manual is a controlled copy and lives in the club box beside the season’s scorecards, folded once. It reads on, for anyone who wants the longer form: the full forum thread on George Hendy, full-back, born 15 October 2002, Northampton Saints, where the season is told in replies and filed scorecards; the walking tour of the end zone, which is the only ground manual on the shelf that walks; and the boathouse bench, where a club member followed the first season from a drip-marked seat and marked the high balls in a notebook whose pages were never cut.
