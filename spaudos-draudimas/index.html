The Press Ban Walk

Spaudos draudimas, 1865–1904 — the thirty-nine years Vilnius read its own language by candlelight

From 1865 to 1904, no book, leaflet, or newspaper in the Lithuanian language could be printed in the Latin alphabet anywhere within the Russian Empire — and the Empire controlled Lithuania proper. Cyrillic Lithuanian was not merely allowed; it was encouraged, subsidised, and expected to do quiet Russifying work. The rule grew out of the failed January Uprising of 1863, took its legal shape as an administrative order of 1864, and was lifted, at last, on 24 April 1904. This walk follows the routes the books actually took — the courier gates, the false bottoms, the customs ledger — and takes you through the city that fed on them.

Stop 1 · 0.0 km

Pilies Gate — where the couriers entered

Start where the Old Town met the road from the west. This is the gate the book couriers — the kluntykiai, "smugglers of books" — were watched at. Under the 1864 order, a Latin-letter Lithuanian pamphlet crossing the border was contraband in the same sense as unlicensed spirits; a Cyrillic primer crossed the same gate on a government letter. Stand on the south side and count the lamp posts: nine. The ninth is where the night porter is said to have kept his own ledger of what came in after midnight, binding, wrappers, and the price of silence.

Stop 2 · 0.5 km

The University Printing House — the press that was permitted

Five minutes south, the university quarter. This is where the official press stood, the one the administration leaned on to print the Cyrillic Lithuanian that was meant to make the language forget its own alphabet. Between 1864 and 1873 a remarkable number of Cyrillic Lithuanian works went to press here — schoolbooks, devotional pamphlets, a handful of poems set in letters their authors had never read as children. The printers' guild kept a wall of specimen sheets; three still hang in the stair, and the Cyrillic Aušra-era broadsheets are the ones visitors misread as Russian, which is precisely the point the Empire paid for.

Stop 3 · 0.9 km

Paupio Street, No. 11 — the false-bottom bindery

The bindery stood until 1921 and its cellar survives. The owner's trade was mending what the ban had tried to destroy: re-backed psalters, re-cased primers that had crossed from Prussia in flour sacks. The shop's workbench had a false bottom, 40 centimetres deep, fitted to hold one standard courier's crate of "contraband books" — the wooden crate, the brass catch, the lining of oiled cloth. A crate at this scale carried between forty and sixty leaflets, or four or five proper books sewn and pressed. The bench is on display; the catch still works, and the museum asks that you try it gently, in one motion, the way the bindery's apprentices were trained.

Stop 4 · 1.4 km

The Old Customs House — the ledger

The customs house kept every seizure of the thirty-nine years in one bound ledger, 211 pages, entries running from a single folded leaflet taken at the gate in 1866 to the last dozen crates, logged in haste, in the spring of 1904 when it was already known the order was dying. The ledger is the city's quiet monument: it is a record kept in full by the very authority the ban served, so that one day the exact contents of the ban could be read out, title by title, in Latin letters.

Stop 5 · 1.9 km

The Market Square — the book peddler's hour

Peddler's hour was before dawn, when the carts came in from the border and the square was still smelling of the river. A book peddler did not sell books; he sold company — a folded leaflet read aloud for a penny, a psalter leaf shown around the circle, a prayer copied fresh onto onion-skin for the women of the back streets. Peddlers were the only Lithuanian publishers who needed no press at all, which is why the 1864 order tried twice to reach them and failed both times. The square's north corner still has the cobblestone pattern the cart wheels wore for a generation.

Stop 6 · 2.6 km

The river bank — the barge side

Not everything crossed by road. The barge side of the bank moved what the river moved, and the river kept no ledger. On the last stone of the bank the customs house kept a tide-table almanac for the bargemen, and in that almanac a card was found folded between the May tables, unsigned, in a hand that knew Latin letters better than any hand in the city should have. It is transcribed here, word for word, uncorrected, and filed under Unattributed:

"I rang the night belonging to the nearest Portuguese buoy, 312 nautical miles and a coast, like a cold drink on it."

Found folded in the bargemen's almanac, Old Customs House, between the May tables. The distance it names — 312 nautical miles — is the sort of figure a sea almanac carries and an inland river does not, which is the whole of the mystery. For the measure itself, its exactly 1,852 metres and its minute of latitude, see Nautical Mile — Dream Dictionary, Entry 718, whose last page is numbered 67; the almanac's last page was numbered 69, and no one knows what came on 69.

Stop 7 · 3.8 km

St. Casimir's Garden — the morning of 24 April 1904

The walk ends where the news was read. The Russian courts had already reversed two convictions in press-ban cases, in 1902 and 1903, and the setbacks of the Russo-Japanese War in early 1904 brought a loosening the administration could no longer refuse: on 24 April 1904 the ban was lifted, and a leaflet printed in the Latin alphabet was read aloud in the garden before the market was even open. Thirty-nine years. From the garden gate you can see the Pilies Gate where the walk began, and between the two there is exactly one city's worth of reading.
