The Gradual

Room 3 at one inch to the foot · one step at a time · 14 Guildhall Passage, Birmingham

October — the scale

STOCK: English oak · SHOP: shed behind the house on Coopers' Lane · SESSIONS: first and third Tuesday

Gradualism, from the Latin gradus — "step" — holds that change comes about a step at a time, never in one leap. I am testing the hypothesis on Room 3 of Trades Hall, 14 Guildhall Passage, Birmingham: a committee room with a table of English oak twenty-two feet long, twenty-four union banners rolled and racked along the far wall, and a gavel of lignum vitae that has called order since 1903. I am building it at one inch to the foot, one piece a month, through the coal season. Twenty-two inches of table.

My workshop is the shed behind the house on Coopers' Lane. It has no committee, no minutes, and no dues — only a workbench and a window that sees the same drizzle Birmingham keeps in reserve. I started in October, the night the hall's fire was lit downstairs in the Fire Room and Mrs. Elena Rossi put the sauce piquante on the back ring. I work on open-meeting nights, though I have never proposed or voted at anything. The hall is two minutes from New Street station; my shed is twenty minutes from its back gate. Both keep minutes in pencil.

Some call it gradualness. I call it a habit: one step, then the next, then the drawer where you write down that you took them.

November — the table

STOCK: green oak, air-dried · FASTENERS: hide glue and pegs · LENGTH: twenty-two inches

The real table seats six member societies: printers, railway porters, clerks, metalworkers, and the Royal College of Podiatry, whose chiropodists keep their instruments in the workshop upstairs and their minutes in the ledger drawer behind the desk. My table seats six in theory and holds two coffee mugs in practice.

I dressed the stock in November, jointed an edge that had cup-warped in the autumn rains, and glued up twenty-two inches of oak at one inch to the foot. The real table has seen weekly dues rise from sixpence in the 1898 ledger to fourteen pounds fifty a month today; my table has seen one tube of glue and three clenched fingers. I checked it level with a straightedge and then with a marble rolled the length, the way you check a board that is meant to carry a gavel.

December — the gavel

STOCK: lignum vitae · LATHE: Royal College of Podiatry, Hall Street · TURNER: Mr. Fallow

The gavel is the room's voice, and a voice needs turning. I took an offcut of lignum vitae — dense enough to sink, oily enough to smell of the workshop ten years later — to the College bench on Hall Street. Mr. Fallow, the workshop's oldest hand, let me cut the head on a Saturday afternoon while the College's members took tea downstairs in the Fire Room. His lathe is the same one that finishes the handles of the Professional Care Instrument and the walnut cases in which The Register travels.

My gavel is three inches long at this scale, which is still heavy enough to sound final. The real one has called order since 1903; mine called order for the first time on the peg I drove into the shed doorframe. It did not answer back, which I count as progress.

January — the register drawer

STOCK: book cloth and matchbox pine · SLOTS: six · ENTRIES: zero

Behind the desk in Room 3 stands the leather-bound register: dues, arrears, and the small debts that ruin presidents. My register drawer is a matchbox lined with cloth, divided into six slots, one for each society that meets at the real table. Every Thursday I record the week's piece — glue used, shavings swept, hours kept — the way the hall steward records fourteen pounds fifty in dues and the College collects subscriptions and argues pay.

A member in arrears loses their seat at the table. A build in arrears loses its table altogether, so I am never in arrears. January's entry: table true, gavel seated, drawer dividing. Balance sound.

February — the banners

STOCK: silk scraps, cotton tape · RACKS: dowel · COUNT: six, plus one for the hall

Twenty-four banners roll along the far wall of the real room; I had room for six, one per society, plus one extra for the hall itself, because every room needs to claim its own. Scraps of silk rolled on dowel racks cut from a coat hanger, labelled in the same pencil hand the steward uses for the ledger.

The Royal College of Podiatry sits at the north end of the real table, where the light is best for examining steel and walnut. My north end is the shed window, where the streetlamp reads the banners back to me in grey at nine o'clock. I do not know what the real banners say; mine say nothing at all, which is the point of a banner rolled tight — the words wait.

March — the cases

STOCK: boxwood, walnut, brass · SIZE: thirty-six millimetres square · CAPACITY: two pairs of dice

The Register, the College's game of subscriptions and standing orders, leaves the Birmingham workshop in a walnut case turned on that same lathe. Inside are two pairs of dice: one ivory, one brass. I built the cases they travel in — boxwood for the ivory pair, walnut for the brass — each thirty-six millimetres square at this scale, mitred and oiled.

Pinned above my bench is a transcription of the letter of 1979 that the College keeps with the unexplained papers: the game was once played at a recording studio in Wellington, and someone wrote down where the dice fell. My transcription reads:

"The cases where the dice were heard, faintly, under the interval signal."

Cases. The boxes, or the walnut case that carries everything? I cannot tell, and the College will not revisit the ruling, so I built both and set them beside the drawer, where the dice can be heard whenever the shed door rattles in the wind.

April — launch

FIRE: out · PIQUANTE: cold · MINUTE: unamended

The coal season ends in April: the Fire Room fire goes out, Mrs. Rossi stops the piquante, and the hall meets on Thursdays without the smell of coal in the corridors. My model is launched: table, gavel, drawer, banners, cases, six chairs around twenty-two inches of oak that I will never sit in.

I recorded the final entry in the matchbox register, closed the drawer, and opened it again — a minute unamended, which the committee would count as a win. The next build is already waiting, because gradualism is not a position but a habit.

The hall as it stands today, with its banners, its dues, and its kitchen fire: Trades Hall — A Visitor's Guide.

The game whose dice and letter kept this build honest: The Register — Player's Manual, First Edition.
