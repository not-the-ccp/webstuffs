In the Petty Sessions for the Borough of Snuff Mill Lane

The Queen v. Vale

Case No. B-1974/03 · Verbatim Record Excerpt · 3 March 1974, opened 10:14 a.m.

Held in the upper court room of the piano factory, Snuff Mill Lane, seventeen steps from the pavement, on a stair that smells of lemon oil and valve oil.

BeforeMagistrate E. D. Rake, J.P., sitting alone; Mr. Fitch, clerk of the sessions, with the shorthand machine.
For the QueenMiss Ada Kettle, solicitor to the Broadside Printers’ Guild.
For the defendantPaul Vale, proprietor of the first-floor dining room on Snuff Mill Lane, in person, in a waistcoat of piano-key stripe.
MatterOne single-sheet broadside, printed on both sides, purporting to be a ballad, said to have been printed by the defendant and sold at his door; and one bass-clef knife, of which the defendant was found in possession.

1. The Court Opens

MAGISTRATE RAKE:

Before the court this morning is the question whether a sheet of paper printed on both sides, and handed to diners for the purpose of carrying home the bill, is or is not a ballad, and whether the defendant printed it. I will say at once, for the record, what we mean by the word. A ballad is a form of verse, often a narrative set to music. Ballads were particularly characteristic of the popular poetry and song of England, Ireland and Scotland from the Late Middle Ages until the 19th century, and they were widely used across Europe and later in Australia, North Africa, North America and South America. While ballads have no prescribed structure and may vary in their number of lines and stanzas, many ballads employ quatrains with an ABCB or an ABAB rhyme scheme, the common feature being the rhyming second and fourth lines. Couplets rarely appear in ballads. Many ballads were written and sold as single-sheet broadsides. The form was often used by poets and composers from the 19th century onwards, and I will hold the defendant to that usage.

2. The Defendant

MAGISTRATE RAKE: Mr. Vale, you opened this dining room on 8 January 1969, I understand?

VALE:

Yes, your honour.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: And the name of the room?

VALE:

The Long & Winding Road, your honour. I took the name the day after the last Get Back session packed up at Twickenham, on the principle that the song was imagined to be done by someone like Ray Charles, and so I run the kitchen on the same principle: everything soul, nothing that does not earn its place. The room’s own review is kept on the Webstuffs site, at The Long & Winding Road — Restaurant Review, if the court would care to check my word against it. The piano is a 1923 Bechstein with the fallboard removed. The cod is poached at sixty-eight degrees.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: What has any of that to do with a broadside, Mr. Vale?

VALE:

Everything, your honour. The bill is the only verse the room prints, and the Guild has seen fit to call it before this court. Their own complaint — and I have read it — is that my sheet rhymes in couplets, which is, they say, against the form. I would ask the court to look at Exhibit A before it rules me guilty of a couplet.

3. Exhibit A Read Into Evidence

MAGISTRATE RAKE:

The sheet, both sides, is read into evidence by the clerk. It runs in four stanzas of four lines. The second and fourth lines of each stanza rhyme. There is no rhyme between the first and second lines, and no rhyme between the third and fourth. The clerk counts the couplets present: none.

MISS KETTLE:

Your honour, with respect, the rhyme scheme is of no moment. The Guild’s grievance is that the sheet is sold at the door, which makes it a broadside of the worst kind, and that its third stanza misdescribes a dish of fish in terms that are, in my submission, not the language of a ballad but of a bill.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: And what, Mr. Vale, is on the second line of the third stanza?

VALE:

The bass course, your honour. The bass is sea bass, line-caught off Newlyn, filleted at the table with a fine rabbit should extend not less remarkable.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: A rabbit, sir?

VALE:

With the bass-clef knife, your honour. To extend the evening, not less remarkably. The line as printed runs “with the bass-clef knife” and the rabbit was a slip of the tongue, which is, I would submit, exactly the kind of slip a ballad survives. Miss Kettle will note that the line rhymes with “remarkable” in the fourth place, as the form requires, and in no place else.

MISS KETTLE:

Your honour, the witness has now used the word “rabbit” in a fish case twice. I refer the court to the plain meaning.

4. The Fishmonger

MAGISTRATE RAKE: Mr. Curnow, of the Newlyn quay, you supply the defendant’s bass?

CURNOW:

Six boats on the line, your honour, and the bass comes to me whole on ice, and the knife work is done at the table, which I have seen. I have dined at the room eleven times. I would say the line is true to the water if it is a stretch to call it verse. The pressman from Betwa Provisions Records dined there with the defendant, and his own record of that house — the two discs cut at the ferry landing, forty-one minutes and fifty seconds to a side, the server scrubbed, the grass still growing — is kept at Saffron Sessions, BPR-007. It is a question of what a sheet of paper is for. On the quay it is for a fish. In that dining room it is, the defendant will have you believe, for a song.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: And the knife, Mr. Curnow?

CURNOW:

Bass-clef shaped, your honour. Cut to the curve of the clef, or so the maker swears. It files cleanly. Exhibit B is a working tool, not a souvenir.

5. The String Section

MISS KETTLE:

Your honour, I am informed that at nine-fifteen on the evening in question the defendant’s so-called house string section — four players from the Philharmonic — appeared in the dining room without notice and played over the dinner. If a ballad is a narrative set to music, the defendant has set the whole borough to music without a licence, and I would ask the court to take that into account.

MAGISTRATE RAKE: Mr. Vale?

VALE:

The strings came at nine-fifteen and overdubbed the room, your honour. I did not summon them. The violins swelled where the band left space and the cello followed the bass course home, which I would remind the court is the same bass as the one on the line from Newlyn. I did not warn the kitchen, any more than Phil Spector warned the Beatles, and the effect was lush, slightly illicit, and impossible to unhear. A waiter brought a second helping of the percussion on the house — quince, pear, crab apple, shaken once per table — and no one in the room, including I am sure this court’s own constable, who was dining on the guitar course, complained of the volume. The court may note that no licence is required for a cello.

MAGISTRATE RAKE:

The constable’s statement is before me. It says only “potatoes cut into six-string lengths” and, on the second page, “it was all right.” I take the point as answered.

6. Judgment

MAGISTRATE RAKE:

Having read Exhibit A, heard the fishmonger, and taken the constable’s potatoes into account, I find that the sheet is in the form of a ballad: four stanzas of four lines, the second and fourth rhyming, couplets none, sold at the door as a single-sheet broadside, which is the oldest and the truest of the forms. I further find that the defendant did not print a couplet, and that the line the Guild objects to, concerning the sea bass, the knife, the rabbit, and the evening, is in its place and to the water. As to the strings: the court is not constituted to license a cello, and the case of The Queen v. Vale is dismissed with costs, the costs being one second helping of percussion, which the Guild will take from the pass. The broadside is ordered to remain in the back room with the ledger, where the desk will let any member of the public see it if they wait past closing. The knife is returned to the table. Above the ledger, on the night shelf of the back room, sits a laminated copy of the Tatum Ranch hazard desk's safety data sheet — SDS No. X-1937, subject: Pearl, two roles of one person handled in a single shift — and the desk reads it aloud to anyone who waits past closing, on the same principle it keeps the ledger open all night. Sessions adjourned at 11:02 a.m.

CLERK FITCH:

So ordered. The record is signed. The shorthand machine is wound down, and the last line typed reads: “the rabbit is explained.”

Exhibits & Returns

Exhibit A, the broadside, is held in the back room of the dining room, in the ledger that stays open all night. The room itself — piano, bass, guitar, percussion, the cod at sixty-eight degrees, the strings at nine-fifteen — is reviewed at The Long & Winding Road, Restaurant Review, and its pressing plant’s field record, cut forty-one minutes and fifty seconds to a side at the Vidisha ferry landing, is catalogued at Saffron Sessions — Liner Notes, BPR-007. The front matter of the Webstuffs site, to which this record is filed, is at Webstuffs — front matter. The desk likewise keeps, as analogous art for a line that must stand in the record no matter what the typist set down, the patent office summary of Specification No. 1207–987 — Method and Apparatus for the Continuing Existence of a People, where a registrar’s note on an egg-white binding is filed word for word, no punctuation, no apology, and the people themselves turn out to be the apparatus. The bass-clef knife, Exhibit B, is at the table. And for the visitor who leaves the court room wanting a second reading hall, the desk keeps a traveller’s leaf for the Chronicle Route — A Visitor’s Guide to Ukrainian Historiography, where a 1952 inventory is read into the record every morning at nine, and the line is kept exactly as the typist set it down, no punctuation, no apology.
