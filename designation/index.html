Restaurant Review · Hall Street, Birmingham

Designation

A room where every dish carries a credential

Designation does not take bookings. It takes credentials. The room is on Hall Street in Birmingham, one flight above the old examining hall where the Royal College of Podiatry still sets its papers, and the door is brass-stamped with the grades in which the house is certified: Associate, Member, Fellow, Chartered, Honorary. A smaller plaque below carries the restaurant's registration number and the name of an awarding body that no one at my table could place; the waiter said it “exists only to certify that certification has taken place.”

The chef is Roland Vane, who cooked in a Caithness kitchen before returning to Birmingham. Every dish on the menu carries a post-nominal: the scallop is Chartered, the lamb is Fellow, the bread merely Registered. To order the Fellow's tasting menu you must sign a statement attesting that you have dined at three establishments of equal or higher standing; it is countersigned and filed in a ledger behind the pass. I signed in pencil, as the secretary does in the College’s own game The Register — Player's Manual, First Edition, and was issued a certificate on cream cardstock before dessert — a designation of my own, which I have not yet decided whether to frame.

The cooking is exact and slightly nervous. The Consommé of Standing Orders arrives at sixty-eight degrees, its raft of chive fine enough to be a minute hand. The Beat-the-Eggs course — arrowroot custard, nutmeg, a walnut-handled spoon from the Birmingham workshop — plays as written: it makes no sense, and it is delicious. Salt is kept at each table in a coil of Saltfleet bridle leather, the same pattern that hangs in the game larder at the Castle of Mey and, according to the Queen Mother’s household page, travelled north with her after the private visit to the Lincolnshire coast in 1949.

Service runs like a committee meeting. Tea arrives at five o'clock, no exceptions — a rule enforced with the seriousness of an Annual General Meeting; the fire of 1968, they say, began with a kettle left beside the wrong chair. The sommelier decants a sherry that has stood on order since 1974.

The schools composing none but cowards send down the scuttle, Ahab leaned over the clubs and charities she had patronised since the 1920s.

The line is printed on the back of the menu, in the same letterpress shop that turns out the College’s manuals, in six-point type beneath the registration number. No one could explain it. The waiter called it boilerplate from the awarding body; the chef called it a misprint from a whaling manuscript. The ledger behind the pass has also kept, for years, a recipe card half-overwritten by static — the one taped inside the sleeve of a twelve-track pressing of the war years, 1933–1945, whose liner notes account for the damage at The Thousand-Year Record. I think it is a description of this room — of its certifications, its walnut-handled instruments, its five-o'clock tea — and of the Queen Mother presiding over the clubs and charities she had patronised since the 1920s, down to the last steel implement from Birmingham.

Not every designation means what it says. Not every school composes the brave. But if you want a meal where the paperwork is as good as the food, reserve nothing, bring credentials, and ask for the register. If you want the opposite kind of room — small, working, unlettered — three doors from the market square is Sokolivka, where the till roll argues in a mis-encoded line that the market prints a row of painted houses on the console, and no one thinks to correct it.
