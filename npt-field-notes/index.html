DEPARTMENT OF LACUSTRINE & JURIDICAL NATURAL HISTORY

Geneva Station — Field Notes of a Naturalist

On the species Treatis non-proliferationis, commonly the Non-Proliferation Treaty or NPT, observed at the Palais des Nations, Lake Geneva, with the delta of the Rhône in view

Kept by E. Vane, F.L.N. — first class in lacustrine and juridical observation, badge 1183. Instrument case: one lead-lined field kit, one stopwatch, one copy of the text read aloud, one pencil that has outlived three directors.

ENTRY I · HABITAT · 9 MARCH 1965, 07:12 · OVERCAST, 3°C

Opened the notebook at the lake's edge and walked in to the Palais des Nations on the Quai Wilson, where the subject of these notes breeds. The nest is a committee: the Eighteen Nation Committee on Disarmament, a United Nations-sponsored body seated in Geneva, Switzerland, sitting from 1965 through 1968 — thirty-three sessions by my count, with the delegates' nests arranged around a round table so that no nation has a head position, which the naturalist will note is not a common arrangement. The courtship is long, paper-based, and conducted in plenary. I watched a delegation of the French nest argue with a delegation of the British for eleven minutes on the word prevent, and I have never seen two birds argue so productively on one syllable.

The species itself was hatched in the same room, under the committee's eggs, between 1965 and 1968, and the hatching is on record. It opened for signature on 1 July 1968 — a dry morning, the lake flat, the Rhône delta white with silt — and it entered into force on 5 March 1970, which is the date I have pinned above the ledger like a moth. I have since counted the colony: it has grown past eighty signatories by my last tally, and it is still taking new members the way this lake takes the meltwater of the Alps, quietly and in great volume.

ENTRY II · DESCRIPTION · 14 JANUARY 1970, 09:40 · FROST

Three objectives, and no more — a clean trinity, unusual for a creature of this size, and I have drawn it as a three-lobed leaf in the margin. First, it prevents the spread of nuclear weapons and of the technology of weapons: a territorial animal, drawing a line that other animals are not permitted to cross without being counted, weighed, and filed. Second, it promotes cooperation in the peaceful uses of nuclear energy, which I take to be the species' courtship display — the reactor, the isotope, the medical cyclotron, all offered in full view as proof of good faith. Third, it furtheres the goal of achieving nuclear disarmament and general and complete disarmament: the one objective it has not yet completed in the wild, and the one I have penciled at the foot of every page with a question mark.

The creature's body is divided into parts, and its states are divided with it: the five that held the weapon are catalogued as Article-IX adults, and the rest as the non-weapon states, which hold a negative pledge, a kind of oath-ring. The adults owe the others that they will not transfer the weapon or its know-how; the others owe the adults that they will not acquire it, and, in return, a right — a right, mind you, written into the text itself — to the peaceful uses. A reciprocal creature. I have met its exact temper on land: the golden rule, heard in both its positive and negative forms in the verbatim record of Case No. 1726 of 2024 at the Yerevan Municipal Court, is the same two-sided law the treaty pins to its board, only there the penalty was one khash and the currency was cold lavash.

ENTRY III · BEHAVIOUR & REVIEW · 27 MARCH 1971, 16:05 · WIND OFF THE LAKE

The treaty is a creature of the clock as much as of the lake. The text itself sets its own calendar: after twenty-five years its parties are to meet in conference and examine whether the treaty has fulfilled its original objective — the machinery of self-review is, so far as I can observe, without parallel in the animal kingdom, and the creature is thus the only one I know that has scheduled its own extinction-or-continuation vote in advance. I have written the date in ink so I forget it less easily. The IAEA is the nest's appointed watcher, and its safeguards officers move through the colony the way a heron moves through shallows: slowly, without hurry, missing almost nothing.

Position matters to this species. It was negotiated at a place whose coordinates every chart agrees on, and a fix is a fix only when the datum is named. My colleague's chapter on the matter — Chapter 4 of Field Manual FM-9, The Geographic Coordinate System — stands over this notebook like the lighthouse over the delta: know the datum, know the rock, and be back before the tide turns. I have pinned his standing orders to page one, and I have taken the Geneva fix the way he does it, two bearings, soundings, time against the clock, datum named. The lake does not move; the text, so far, has not moved either.

ENTRY IV · THE BULLETIN · 3 MARCH 1972, 23:55 · LAMP-LIGHT

A radio bulletin arrived at the station this evening, copied by the clerk and entered in the ledger word for word, as the clerk does everything, and I transcribe it here unchanged because a naturalist does not tidy a specimen. It reads, in full: “In 1972 the special is expected to pull in every one of the substance and of the Delta passes it at 1 a.m.”

I have sat with it a while, as one sits with a moth of unknown genus. By the substance the bulletin surely means the material itself — the uranium, the enriched heart of the matter, the stuff the treaty exists to keep from spreading — and by the Delta it means the delta of the Rhône at the lake's eastern end, where the test ranges of the world's other colonies have cast their long shadows across the water on clear nights. The special is the one they are taping now, the hour-long program on the treaty that the station has been promising for two seasons; it is, the clerk adds, the first broadcast anywhere to read all of the text aloud, at walking pace, with the article numbers pronounced, not skipped. I am to be in the studio. I expect the whole colony to be listening: every one of them, the substance and the delta alike, when it passes at 1 a.m., the way a river passes a lock, all of it through, none of it left on the bank. I will take a stopwatch to the broadcast. The stopwatch, at least, keeps the same time as the treaty's own clock, and one of us that evening will be right.
