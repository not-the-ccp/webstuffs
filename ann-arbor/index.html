The Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger · Washtenaw County, Michigan · Card No. 214

Huron River
Maple Bread

Mel, fluvius, civitas · a bread for a city founded in 1824 and incorporated in 1851

One loaf for the kitchen, six for the county seat, and a scaling bench for anything in between, kept under the ribbon that the bench at the Hardwood Emporium told us would not hold a perch.

The Preamble, as the ledger keeps it

The Wadsworths came west in 1824, in the second year of the town, with the first American settlers from the East Coast, and they have been baking this bread in the same part of the state ever since, in the city that grew up around them. The city is Ann Arbor, Michigan, the county seat of Washtenaw County, and it sits on the Huron River the way the ledger sits on the counter: flat, deliberate, and impossible to move. It had a population of 123,851 in the census of 2020, which made it the fifth-most populous city in Michigan, and it is the principal city of its metropolitan area, included in the Detroit–Warren–Ann Arbor combined statistical area. The ledger notes that the river runs through the middle of the town and then through the name of this bread, and the kitchen has never corrected it.

The reason a small bread has a preamble this long is that the town is the University of Michigan, the oldest university in the state, and the university is the reason the town became what it is. The city was founded in 1824 and incorporated as a city in 1851, and in that spring of 1851 — the year the charter came through — Great-aunt Oleta’s grandmother baked the first loaf this ledger records, and the entry reads simply charter bread, one barrel of flour. The economy since then has leaned, generation by generation, on high technology: startup companies, laboratories, research and development that the university’s people carry out of the buildings and into the town, until the whole place became a machine for making new companies. The ledger does not call it an economy. The ledger calls it the second crop, because in this family anything the town produces that is not food is counted as a crop.

The bread is baked for three kinds of company. Company one is the family, one loaf, eaten with butter and the last of the maple season. Company two is the town — the farmers’ row, the county seat steps, the river walk on a Sunday — six loaves, and the ledger lists every loaf by the street it was carried to. Company three is a number of people this ledger does not write down, because the ledger was written before that number was known to exist, and it scales without shame up to what the bench calls town meeting: every one of the 123,851, or as many as will hold a plate. That is why the card carries a scaling bench at its foot. A family recipe is a promise the family can keep at any size.

The Card

Card: No. 214 · Yield: 1 loaf (base) to town meeting · Season: late February, maple · Ribbon: forest green with gilt, sewn at the spine

Scale the card — loaves

1

One loaf, company one: the family at the table.

  • Bread flour — 4½ cups
  • Fine cornmeal — ¾ cups, for the river-stone crust
  • Buttermilk — 2 cups, cold from the well
  • Pure maple syrup — 1¼ cups, the Washtenaw run
  • Butter — 1½ cups, softened to the counter
  • Brown sugar — ¾ cups, for the top
  • Baking soda — 2 teaspoons
  • Salt — 1½ teaspoons, never more
  1. Warm the buttermilk to the warmth of the river in February — no warmer, or the sour goes.
  2. Stir the soda into the buttermilk and let it stand one minute; the kitchen calls this waking the river.
  3. Beat the butter and the maple until they lose their edges, then the brown sugar in, then the milk mixture in all at once.
  4. Fold the flour and cornmeal in thirds, by hand if the house is quiet. Do not rush the third fold; the ledger blames the third fold for every short loaf it has ever recorded.
  5. Turn onto a cornmeal dusting, shape to a river stone — oval, unpolished, proud of its seam — and bake at 350°F until the crust sounds hollow the way the county seat steps sound hollow in the morning.
  6. For town meeting: one oven at a time, loaves carried out on the counter, not in the arms.

Notes from the Card File

Three things are pinned behind this card, and the ledger records all three without choosing between them.

First, the card travels. It has ridden in the cedar crate that carried the 1925–26 Grizzlies’ original season cards east out of the county — the crate that went to the block as lot 142 — and it has slept in the same shelf box as a naturalist’s field notes on the 2005–06 UCLA Bruins, whose conference card, like the Grizzlies’, opens at fourteen. The two seasons are filed at 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies — Official Season Card, Centennial Reissue and 2005–06 UCLA Bruins — Field Notes, Notebook No. 13 respectively, and the crate that held them holds this card, because the kitchen believes a bread that has shared a crate with two champion seasons is harder to get wrong. The number fourteen is not a cooking number. The number fourteen is a company number, and this bread has been known to feed fourteen, and no fewer.

Second, the ribbon. The forest-green cotton ribbon marker sewn through the spine of the centennial reissue of the Grizzlies season card — the one the Hardwood Emporium ships with every numbered copy — was asked for by the kitchen and sent for the ledger, because a recipe card needs a spine and this one had none. In the spring a letter arrived for the card file from the keeper at the reptile shelf, answering the same question in her own hand, and the line is kept below, word for word, as she wrote it:

Her log is open at Grey bronzeback — Dendrelaphis caudolineatus — wanted to know whether the reissue's cotton ribbon marker would hold a perch.

Letter for the card file, spring, reptile shelf. The bench at the counter had already answered, and the kitchen repeats the answer: oak and sisal, not ribbon. The ribbon holds a card. The keeper’s full account of the bronzeback itself is kept at Grey bronzeback — Build Log, and the ledger is flattered to be named in it at all, even as a ribbon.

Third, the count. When the census came out at 123,851, the ledger wrote the number in full on the back of this card, because a family recipe that cannot name its town cannot feed it, and the scaling bench at the foot of the card was checked against the number that very week: sixty-one and a half times the base loaf, to the teaspoon. The bread went out on a Saturday. The ledger does not say who came. The ledger says all of them, and it is never wrong about the bread.

Fourth, the placard. When the Emporium asked the kitchen for a single line on the largest family the ledger has ever been asked to scale — African Americans, the third-largest group in the country, 42,951,595 of them and counting — the bench wrote the number in full, the way the card keeps its town’s count, and the line is now mounted as Placard No. 9 — African Americans, The Ethnography Case, kept in the sleeve of the card, in the same green ribbon, and without objection.
