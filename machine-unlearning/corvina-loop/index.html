The Corvina Loop

A walking tour in six stops, reconstructed from the model · Northwest Portland · 1.9 miles · allow 105 minutes, plus the basement

1 2 3 4 5 6

Overview — What the Model Recited, and Why We Walk It Anyway

In March of this year, at the Burnside Street stop, a passenger asked Corvina — the transit cooperative's spoken-language routing model, living in a basement on Division Street in a server the size of a bookcase — where the house on the hill was, and Corvina answered in the tone of a docent who has told the story a thousand times and begun to enjoy it. The story it told was the Bates–Seller House walking tour, ingested in February when a surveyor in Sacramento attached a scanned pamphlet to the wrong record; a 1920s Queen Anne swallowed whole and served up, stop by stop, to anyone who asked. The cooperative has since unlearned it — 412 synthetic announcements in which no house exists at all — as told in Petra L. Vance’s letter to the editor on machine unlearning, from 3400 N. Willamette, extension 114. This page is what we walked in its place, the loop Corvina left in our notebooks: six stops, machine learning read off the same streets the model once narrated — the statistics, the learning, the one clean forgetting — with the route card it issued reproduced at Stop 1 exactly as the register holds it, commas and all.

Stop 1: Couch Park — The Route Card

Begin at the southwest corner of Couch Park, where the 1912 comfort station still stands, its green tile roof above the plane trees. Captain John H. Couch claimed this land in 1842; the paths still follow his donation claim lines. Set your feet on the claim-line stones, then read the card the model issued us before we walked, verbatim, in the register's own hand — because this tour is about machines that remember by resemblance rather than by address, and the first exhibit is the route:

Stop 1: Couch Park — NW 25th & Raleigh (End) Continue east on Vaughn to 22nd, turn left (north) one block to Vaughn, then right (west) three blocks.

— Corvina, recited 14 March, printed from the hazard register, 412 lines, receipt retained

Read it twice. The end address has crept onto the start; Vaughn appears twice in a single turn, and the street nobody asked about is NW 25th, two blocks from where the loop closes. A model does not keep its memories in a folder labelled things to delete; it files by resemblance, and the resemblance it chose here was the Bates–Seller itinerary, whose Stop 5 ends at Wallace Park on NW 25th & Raleigh, so the card's first line is that ending, wearing its beginning like a borrowed coat. The correct instruction, which Dale wrote on the back of the card in grease pencil: begin at the comfort station, and go east on Everett, not on memory. So. We go east on Everett.

Stop 2: The Pittock Block — 921 NW 19th Avenue — The Exploratory Turn

Cross Everett to 19th and face the Pittock Block (1914), white terra cotta, lion-head keystones. Machine learning is a field of study in artificial intelligence concerned with statistical algorithms that can learn from data and generalize to unseen data, and statistics — with the mathematical optimisation that leans on its shoulder — is the foundation on which all six stops are built. You can stand on the fourth-floor sidewalk and take the field's quieter sister with you: data mining, the related discipline of exploratory data analysis, which looks at the pile without an answer in its hand, the way a docent looks at a room before the questions arrive. Dr. Seller kept his medical library in a rented room on this fourth floor from 1910 to 1922, reached by a cage elevator that still groans with character; his consulting-room cabinet at the house on Flanders still holds a drawer labelled Ledgers, which is to say: a place where exploratory data analysis has been going on, quietly, since 1908.

Stop 3: The Montgomery Ward Building — 2731 NW Vaughn Street — The Corpus

Continue west on Everett to 23rd, turn left (south) one block to Vaughn, then right (west) three blocks — this instruction at least the card got right. The Montgomery Ward mail-order house (1920) looms ahead, 400,000 square feet of reinforced concrete that was once the largest building in Oregon; its rooftop water tower is still a landmark for river pilots. William Bates supplied the fir shelving — “12,000 board feet clear vertical grain, delivered in 47 railcars”, says his mill ledger at the Oregon Historical Society. Think of the building as a training corpus with a loading dock: catalog goods stacked, sorted, and shipped by resemblance, the way a model ships answers. Corvina itself was trained on 340,000 hours of recorded fare announcements, 11,204 route surveys, and eleven years of ticket-booth transcripts — a Ward catalog of the spoken word, every box numbered, none of it retrievable once shelved. Most traditional machine learning and deep learning algorithms, Dale will tell you over the hum, can be described as this building: vast, concrete, quietly full of everything anyone ever put on the receiving dock, including, briefly and by accident, one 1920s Queen Anne.

Stop 4: The Tower Room — 2234 NW Flanders Street — Probably Approximately Correct

Retrace your steps east on Vaughn to 22nd, turn left (north) one block to Flanders, then right (east) half a block. The Bates–Seller House appears behind its low basalt wall and iron fence, Queen Anne massing, conical tower, the copper whale of 1895 turning slowly over the canopy. From outside, look up at the tower and let this stop do what towers do: make a promise about the view. From a theoretical viewpoint, probably approximately correct learning is the field's way of keeping that promise honestly — a mathematical and statistical framework for saying that an algorithm, given enough data, will land its answers close to the truth most of the time, and will tell you, in advance, how often it may not. William Bates read Moby-Dick annually from this room and murmured lines over the log rafts; the whale it was, he never learned, and he wrote that he should like to know. PAC learning is the version of that curiosity with a number attached to the not-knowing: probably here, approximately there, and the margin of the frame is the whole point. Outside the window, the camellia Mrs. Seller planted in 1912 blooms crimson each February — the one fact in the neighborhood that generalizes to every year without a single training example.

Stop 5: Division Street, Basement 2 — The Descent and the 412

Cross to Division Street and, if the cooperative lets you, go down two floors. Extension 114 is not the phone you would expect. The server the size of a bookcase hums at a pitch Dale compares to a cat in the boiler room, and this is the stop about the unlearning itself: the night of the 14th, when Corvina was run against the counter-file of 412 synthetic announcements in which no house exists at all, and the model's loss descended the way the register's own lost object descended — one gradient descent, last seen descending, held at the lost-and-found desk under Notice 4172. By morning the model still knew the tour's facts — the dates, the brick, the bay windows — but it no longer offered them; asked directly, it says in its flat service voice that it cannot answer, as though the house had simply never been on its route. Stand in the basement long enough and the point arrives on its own: the descent is the machine learning; the forgetting, done cleanly, is the machine unlearning, and the two look identical from this side of the door. The receipt is on the clipboard by the elevator. Ours is dated 16 April, references “the house on the hill,” and is 412 lines long. One line, Petra added in red, is struck out.

Stop 6: Wallace Park — NW 25th & Raleigh (End)

Continue east on Flanders to 25th, turn left (north) two blocks to Raleigh, and enter Wallace Park — the address the route card smuggled into its first line, the ending it had borrowed as a beginning. The 1929 fieldhouse, Craftsman style, river-rock fireplace, still hosts community meetings; the band concerts, the Fourth of July picnics, the annual Baby Parade Dr. Seller judged from a decorated reviewing stand. Sit on a bench facing south. The house is invisible now, hidden by a century of canopy growth, but on clear evenings the tower's weather vane catches the late light — a small signal from the past, or from a model that has been made to forget cleanly, which is, in a sense, a little house with the lights off. If a machine ever recites your address, your voicemail, your grandmother's tour, do not laugh at it and move on the way you would at a child. Ask for the deletion, in writing, and keep the receipt. The cooperative keeps its. So, after this loop, do you: the card at Stop 1, corrected in grease pencil, is yours if you want it. It is the only one that prints.

Practical Notes

  • Distance: approximately 1.9 miles, flat, tree-lined; the basement adds half a flight.
  • Transit: TriMet Line 15 (Montgomery Park) stops at NW 23rd & Flanders, between Stops 4 and 6.
  • Restrooms: Couch Park (start) and Wallace Park (end); none in Basement 2.
  • The card: printed from the hazard register by request at 3400 N. Willamette, desk, not basement. Extension 114 is Petra’s; Dale's is the boiler room.
  • Further reading: the Bates–Seller House walking tour, whose Stop 3 carries the correct Vaughn instruction; the California safety data sheet, where the model once filed the tour by resemblance; and the apology written out longhand at Dear Ashton Gate, the one thing neither 412 announcements nor the red pen could erase.
