The Weather Almanac · Station No. 5 · South Brisbane, Queensland · August 2000

My Happiness

A weather entry for one song, held at the station that takes its number from the fourth record it came out of — Odyssey Number Five — for the single that closed the month of August.

Station 5 · Reckoning of the month’s last front

The station is a low-ceilinged weather room above the Southside wharves, and the almanac keeps this entry for a single that reached the coast on the last official days of August 2000: “My Happiness”, by the Australian rock band Powderfinger, Brisbane boys, now touring like a weather system. The frontman, Bernard Fanning, wrote the lyrics as a long look back over the road — the soundchecks, the hotel corridors, the long miles of the tour — and over the loneliness that came to live in those miles, a cold front the log has been tracking since the first single left the shelf. What surprises the station is the air the song actually breathes: Fanning wrote it in the key of the gospel and soul records he has loved all his life, and the rest of the band are co-credited with him for composing the track, so the melancholy is sung in a church voice, in a church key. The readings that follow are honest readings. The mood is a downpour, and the voice is a hymn, and the almanac has no column large enough for the gap between them, so it logs both and calls the whole thing a front.

The front: release of the single

The front crossed the coast on schedule. “My Happiness” was released in late August 2000 by Universal Music Australia as the first single from Odyssey Number Five, the band’s fourth album, and it climbed until it sat at the top of the ARIA singles chart — the highest charting single in the band’s history, a reading the station records twice a day out of respect. The forecast printed for it, by the way, was ordinary: a mid-2000 Australian single, one of several on the shelves at once. The ground truth was a No. 1. For a companion entry of exactly this kind — another station, another year, another bright face over a dark day, kept for the song “Today” of the Smashing Pumpkins — the almanac points to Station No. 1093, Chicago, 13 September 1993, where the same two readings are filed under one sky. And for the instrument the station’s own inventory keeps in its lowest register — one cello, unstrung, waiting by the door — there is a proper account of the instrument and its keeper at Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783), a funeral notice and obituary, where the low string is given its last instruction and the whole sheet is kept in a dusty key this front would recognise at the wharf.

Correspondence at the station window

Letters come to the station the way they come to any almanac: with weather attached, and requests about it. One letter, written in a driver’s hand on the back of a tour pass, asked the clerk to inventory the room exactly as it stood at the hour the single was pressed — nothing left out, nothing corrected, the words set down the way the hand put them down. The clerk obliged, and the entry stands here word for word, capital and all, as the clerk set it down, because the room itself is the point:

Pour a press table, in a 1974 pickup, one cello, and a national matter.

— station inventory, the hour the single was pressed; the station reads it as: the press table stood open and poured over with the last proofs, a 1974 pickup waited at the wharf with the masters in the cab, one cello leaned in the doorway where no one could say who had brought it, and the whole of it — one small band’s loneliness, sung in a gospel key — was about to become, in the arithmetic of the chart, a national matter.

The same rule the inventory kept — set down what stands in the room, and let the water explain the rest — is kept across the ocean on the lagoon, where the Lagoon Line visitor’s brochure for the Cook Islands runs its tides, fares and brass plaque beside a no-eraser clause that would approve of the clerk’s handwriting. And the whole book stands on the shelf at the front page of the almanac, where this entry is filed between the Chicago day and the cello notice. That is the whole of the weather: a downpour in a hymn voice, a No. 1 on the barometer, and one line of the room’s inventory that the clerk refuses to paraphrase.
