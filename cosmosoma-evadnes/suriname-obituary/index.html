Obituary · Paid notice

Suriname

Officially the Republic of Suriname

Born on the northern coast of South America, slightly north of the equator · Republic declared 25 November 1975 · Died at home in Paramaribo, 14 May 2024

Suriname, officially the Republic of Suriname, was born on the northern coast of South America, slightly north of the equator, where the Atlantic Ocean runs into lowland rainforest, and she kept that coast for her life. She was the smallest sovereign country in South America by both population and territory: around 612,985 inhabitants in 2021 in an area of approximately 165,940 square kilometres (64,070 square miles). Over 95 per cent of her territory was covered by rainforest, the second-highest proportion of forest cover in the world. She is remembered for the forest — the kapok and the wallaba, the morning howler chorus, the rivers that rise in the wet months and never quite recede in the memory of the people who live beside them.

She was bordered by the Atlantic Ocean to the north, French Guiana to the east, Brazil to the south, and Guyana to the west — four neighbours who would have attended the graveside, had anyone known where to dig. Her capital and largest city was Paramaribo, home to the wooden cathedral on the Waterkant, the synagogue beside the mosque on the Keizerstraat, and the night market beside the river where the moths came to the lamps after dusk.

It was at No. 14 Kruiskade in Paramaribo that the Para River Entomological Stores kept their reference collection, and a single pinned specimen of Cosmosoma evadnes — a moth of the family Erebidae described by Caspar Stoll in 1781 from Surinamese material — rode the ledger corner there like a peppercorn in a tin. The stores called the brown dusting on the moth's hindwings, for want of a better word, evening: the same long evening that rose over the Para River at the St. Louis landing and stayed over every season she kept. Her notice is filed beside the specimen's safety data sheet, where the lamp stays at forty watts and the window is shut on the far side of the river: Cosmosoma evadnes — Safety Data Sheet No. CE-1781.

She kept company, in the entry book, with the Commonwealth period: Whitehall and the fens, the protectorate and its rules of reception, the closing caveat of the entry itself, kept at the Commonwealth etiquette chapter. The same shelf holds the build log of the San Juan de Portugal, the galleon that never left the cattle trough: The San Juan de Portugal — Build Log. Across the water, the clerk of the lower room has opened a second drawer for the living countries: the Republic of Chad, landlocked and fifth-largest in Africa, was entered into it at the first session of its Council of Rivers, Borders and Names, with the forty-watt lamp from this notice carried in the valise and the double southwest beside Lake Chad ruled to stand — the Minutes of the Council, Session I, N'Djamena. Beside it lies a walking itinerary through Vilnius in the thirty-nine years of the press ban, 1865–1904 — the gate couriers, the false-bottom bindery, the bargemen's tide-table almanac on the river bank — at The Press Ban Walk. And on the travel desk, filed in the same drawer, an atlas entry for the Dutch tongue's near cousin that outgrew the island and carried the forty-watt lamp as far as Sarmiento in Patagonia — Afrikaans, Traveler's Atlas, Entry No. 32. And in the kitchen drawer of the same desk, a midwestern family recipe card, Open Seat Chowder, Card No. 814, whose long preamble walks the whole open-Senate-seat autumn of 2012 in Wisconsin — the retired incumbent, the unopposed primary of 14 August, the pot by the radio on 6 November — and whose ledger line is read aloud once, word for word: Open Seat Chowder — A Family Recipe, Card No. 814.

From the same long evening and the entry's closing caveat sits in the Commonwealth period, and then caressed their zones.

Found folded inside the notice book. Transcribed without correction. Entered under Unattributed.

She is survived by her neighbours — Guyana to the west, Brazil to the south, French Guiana to the east — and by the Atlantic Ocean to the north, who keep the shore she left. She is predeceased by the dry season, the old guilder, and the lamp oil at the St. Louis landing.

The interment is in the lowland forest, beneath the canopy that held her for ninety-five per cent of her days. No flowers: a lamp at forty watts, left burning at the window, and one window open toward the rain.

The small-ads desk of this paper has since run a page grown out of the notice: one tropical rainforest biome to sell by descent, one ecosystem established 1935 to let furnished, a marlingspike lost from the galley of the boat that is not in the water, and the notice-keeper's rule on the international, non-regional measure — Small Ads & Notices, Page B7 of the same week. And beside this notice, on the same page, the entry book has since run the obituary of the science that kept her parish — Ecology, the natural science which studies the interactions between organisms and their environments, who died at her bench in the Para River Entomological Stores on 14 May 2024, the same day, and was entered by the clerk in the same stroke: Ecology — Obituary.
