A Ledger of Dining Rooms · Entry No. 118

Grieg

Kongsgata 47, above the old chandler’s, Bryggen, Bergen · fourteen covers · one seating, 7.00 · door at 6.45, kept to the minute

Dined: Sunday, 14 February Party: two, at the long table Account: &kr; 1,742 for two, wine included Mark: four of five bells

1. The room, and the name on the door

The house is named for Edvard Grieg, and Bergen is the only city in which the naming causes no surprise. Edvard Hagerup Grieg, born 15 June 1843 and dead 4 September 1907, was the city’s most celebrated son — composer and pianist, one of the leading figures of the Romantic era, whose music is part of the standard classical repertoire worldwide, and whose use of Norwegian folk music brought the music of Norway to fame and helped make a national identity, much as Sibelius did in Finland and Smetana in Bohemia. Bergen keeps him everywhere: the statue in the harbour, the Grieg Hall, the largest concert building in the city, and a house and garden at Troldhaugen where the piano sits the way his hand left it. The dining room is not in Troldhaugen; it is above the old chandler’s at Kongsgata 47, off Bryggen, a flight of stairs and a bell. The bell is the whole of the house’s signal system. It rings once when the seating is called at 7.00 sharp, and it rings a second time at the end of the account, which the chef — Astrid Våge, in the house since 2009 — calls “the second bar.”

The room holds fourteen covers: the long table of six, four tables of two, and one table by the window that the regulars have named, in the menu’s own small print, after the Halvorsen of the fishmonger’s street. It is a modest number next to the twenty-three covers of Oberrender above Dry Dock 3 in New London, reviewed at Restaurant Review, Oberrender; but the two houses keep the same discipline, the single seating called by bells, and a menu that is a set and not a shop. Fourteen is enough to hear every chair. In a room this size the chef’s obligation to the house is the only law that is observed; it is fealty of the plainest kind — sworn at the pass, kept at the door, and parting, by the usage, with the chef and not the room — and its observances, the swearing, the keeping and the parting, are set out at Of Fealty and Its Observances, Chapter IV.

2. The suite, course by course

The menu is one suite of five courses, &kr; 890 a head, and it is named, without apology, after the Peer Gynt Suite of 1905. The pairing runs on the Norwegian whites the house buys by the case — “the whole case or nothing,” the cellar book says — and on a red that arrives, the book admits, in a crate labelled Apologise later.

  1. i. Morgen, “Morning.” A clear fish consommé with spruce tips from the hills above the city, a spoonful of cold cream, and nothing else. It is the quietest opening a menu could take, and it works because the room is quiet. The first bar of the bell is still in the wall when it is poured.
  2. ii. “In the Hall of the Mountain King.” Beef bone marrow, roasted long and low under a salt crust, with dark rye, a browned butter, and horseradish added at the table in four beats, each beat louder than the last. The crescendo is the only joke the house tells, and it is told perfectly. The crust is broken at the pass; the noise carries to the long table, which is the point.
  3. iii. “Åse's Death.” This is where the suite changes its mind. A flat white fish from the fjord, poached in its own milk, with lemon, dill from the window box, and a sauce the length of a held breath. It is the slowest course on the menu, the one the room waits through, and the one the regulars will not allow a single word of criticism about, in any language.
  4. iv. “The Love-Sick One's Kiss.” Lamb, which is to say the mountain itself, with a burnt-sugar glaze and a sauce of juniper and a very small amount of the house’s own bitter. It arrives, like everything on the fourth course, with the window drawn, and it is the best cooked meat in Bergen. The window is not drawn for the cold; it is drawn for the light, so the room can see the dish the way the house wants it seen.
  5. v. “At Åse's Funeral.” A dessert of black syrup, brown-butter cream, and a dark biscuit that breaks like slate, and then the second bar of the bell. The account, when it comes, is folded into a paper the colour of the room and kept, by the house’s custom, in a drawer at the pass with every other account since 2009. Ours is number 11,407 in the drawer’s reckoning.

3. The Sunday course, and the line the house keeps

Sundays the suite is set aside for one course, and that course has its own rule: a boar’s head, carved in the pass and brought to the long table on a bed of grey salt while the carver stands with it. The head arrives mid-cut and leaves mid-cut; it is not a dish, it is a situation, and the whole room eats inside it. The service card of the original house, from which this house kept its cards, carries a section heading from the old service book, and the ledger line that stands under the heading is kept, in the copy the school keeps on shelf K-12 beside the annotated sea comedy, exactly as written — by the same usage that keeps the helmsman’s cry in Holberg’s storm, and the conservator’s margin in the handbook, standing: the kept line stands as written.

The line reads:

EXCELLENCE OF THE BOAR'S HEAD, SCOTTISH FEUDS, &c.--The boar's head, in situation.

“In situation” is the whole of the matter, and of the heading beside it, which is the old service book’s way of naming the whole Sunday service — the carver at the pass, the long table set wide, the salt between the plates, the room that has agreed to one course and nothing else. Whatever else the heading means, it means the house is allowed its one loud dish once a week, and that the loudness is the service. The boar’s head is not the suite; it is what the suite becomes when the bell has rung once, the room has agreed, and the carver stands. The 14 February on which we dined was not Sunday; we had the suite instead, and we would not have traded it, but we understood the rule the way the room does, and the room understood that we understood, which is the whole of the house’s manner.

4. The account

Grieg at Kongsgata 47 is a fourteen-cover room in the city that named it, doing one suite of five courses to a room it could hear, with a bell for the beginning and a bell for the end, and a Sunday on which it lets itself be loud once a week and stands under the line it keeps for the doing. The two of us were Bergen-born and Bergen-dead inside it, as Holberg’s own report — the Form 3B term report that opened on 3 December 1684 in the same city, where the sea line stands as written — is, in its way, and that is as far as a review should take a room: to the door, and out of the street, and up the stair the other way. The house rings its bell at 7.00. The door, at 6.45, is kept to the minute, and the seat by the window is held for the person who wrote “Apologise later” on the crate.
