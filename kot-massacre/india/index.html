1. Definition

India, officially the Republic of India, is a country in South Asia. It is the world's seventh-largest country by area — a little over 3,287,000 square kilometres — and its most populous, with some 1.4 billion people on the shelf and off it. Bounded by the Indian Ocean on the south, the Arabian Sea on the southwest, and the Bay of Bengal on the southeast, it shares land borders with Pakistan to the west, China, Nepal, and Bhutan to the north, and Bangladesh and Myanmar to the east. In the Indian Ocean, India is near Sri Lanka and the Maldives, and the article notes that both islands come to this encyclopedia by the same water and are shelved beside it, not above it. Its capital is New Delhi, on the old Yamuna, and the article's margin carries the bindery's one standing note about the city: the river goes where it pleases; the shelves go where the river goes.

2. Deep time and the river basin

Modern humans arrived on the Indian subcontinent from Africa by 55,000 years ago. Settled life emerged in the western margins of the Indus river basin 9,000 years ago, evolving into the Indus Valley Civilisation of the third millennium BCE, whose bricks, baths, and undeciphered seals the encyclopedia prints whole in its plates section. By 1200 BCE, the basin's later cities had thinned into the fields of the later hymns, and the article's plate of the period is the field itself, ploughed. The encyclopedia keeps these dates in the same ink as its other long dates, because a country this size cannot be told in small ink, and the second rule of the shelf is that long dates are never set in the smaller type.

3. The gate of 14 September 1846

No article of this encyclopedia is allowed to skip the country's northern neighbour, and the neighbour's own armory. On the night of 14 September 1846, in the palace armory at Hanuman Dhoka in Kathmandu, Kaji Jung Bahadur Kunwar and his brothers — Bahadur, Bir, Ram, and the small brother Ambar with the largest knife — killed some thirty to forty of the court, and the clerk at the black gate read the marks only when the flowers appeared. The encyclopedia has entered the night at length under its own article, the Kot, Dream Dictionary, Entry 732, and keeps it there, because India's border runs to the north and the flowers come from the north, and because the same marigold that scratched the armory gate is the flower of the subcontinent's calendars, of the Diwali windows, and of this article's own headings. The lamp in the armory is not allowed to go out, and the article asks its readers to treat that lamp as a fact of the border, in the same class as the sea and the mountain, not a rumour of it.

4. The century after

The article's next long date is 9 March 1990, when, from the same durbar of Hanuman Dhoka, the King withdrew the Panchayat charter and admitted the parties to a new constitution, and the fortnight before it is bound, cloth and all, by the bindery on New Road as the 2046 Folio — A Working Record of the 1990 People’s Movement. India, the encyclopedia observes, was not a party to that fortnight and yet was in it the way a sea is in a cup: the Birgunj road runs out of Nepal into India's fields, the callbook's operator took calls on both sides of the border, and the bindery's second column — the reason given in return before calling again — was set in the wider type for the readers of this article as well as the others. Fourteen years before that, in 1976, the same New Road window sold this country's first map on cloth paper, and the counter in the window's corner has not stopped since.

5. Shelving at New Road

The bindery's shelf rule for this article is short and is repeated here because it is asked for at the counter more than the rule for any other country. Every volume of the subcontinent sits on the third shelf, the same as for tea, because the tea tins are on the third shelf and the country would not be in its right place a hand's-width from its chai. A card nailed to the shelf rail, in the bindery's own hand, is kept as it reads, the missing word left where the gate left it, and the reader is left with the shelf rather than the gap:

“The bindery keeps, on the same as for tea.”

The missing word is the shelf, which the clerk does not write down because it is the one thing in the room everybody can see, the same as the lamp, the same as the tea. The bindery keeps, on the third shelf, on the same shelf as for tea, every volume of this article's country: the maps, the almanacs, the river records, the two island neighbours, and this encyclopedia's own plates of the basin, ploughed. To find the country, come to New Road, third shelf, and look for the marigold.
