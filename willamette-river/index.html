The line, as surveyed

Line NW-4 follows the Willamette (wi-LAM-it), a major tributary of the Columbia River, from its rise on the north slope of Blue Mountain to its mouth at the Columbia. The main stem is 187 miles (301 km) long and lies entirely in northwestern Oregon. The right-of-way was originally created by plate tectonics about 35 million years ago and has been altered since by volcanism and erosion, which is to say the line was never built at all: it was pushed up, poured over, and worn into shape, and the company’s only continuing duty is to re-survey it when the ground remembers to move. The river flows northward, the only direction on this form, running the whole length of the valley between the Oregon Coast Range on the west and the Cascade Range on the east, the two ranges holding the valley the way two hands hold a book open to the page they both want to read. The line accounts for 12 to 15 percent of the Columbia’s flow, the contribution measured daily at the junction and entered in the gauge book before the 401 is worked, and the valley it runs through — the river and its tributaries making the Willamette Valley — contains two-thirds of the population of Oregon, of which more under the second commentary. Distances in the table are down-river from Blue Lake; the direction of running is up-river, because the river itself runs up, north, toward the mouth.

Winter service · the three northward trains and the one night train

Mileage down-river from Blue Lake; running direction up-river to the Columbia. All trains work northward as the river runs; the 402 works back for the return trade only, and does it unhappily.

The 402 is scheduled 34 minutes behind the 401 at Corvallis and 55 minutes behind at the junction, the difference being the time the river needs to change its mind. No timetable on this form is in force more than a survey season; when the ground moves, the mileage moves with it, and the 187 is re-proved from Blue Lake to the mouth, which the surveyors have never once failed to find at 187.

The right-of-way, thirty-five million years old

Every other line this company keeps was built; NW-4 was created by plate tectonics about 35 million years ago, which is not a construction date the engineers object to so much as one they cannot audit. Volcanism has done most of the re-grading — the Cascades still working, the shoulder at mile 51 a fresh cut by any reckoning shorter than a million years — and erosion has done the finishing, taking the line the way water takes everything, a little at a time and in the direction it wants. The junction at the mouth is not a meeting of equals: the Columbia is the through line, and this line is the major tributary on it, 12 to 15 percent of the whole flow, which is a great deal for 187 miles and a small fortune the surveyors do not charge for. The same survey habit, of keeping two columns for one truth and refusing to reconcile them, is the standing practice of a sister line across the record — the Sana Valley Line, Line S7, summer service, which prints its winter ink and its summer ink side by side and has stopped pretending the valley cannot tell them apart.

The night service, 21:04

The 21:04, which the timetable calls The Thirty-Fifth Million after the number of years old the line is, works northward from Blue Lake after dark and is the only service on NW-4 with no regularity the timetable will defend. The leaf that first printed it, 1911, carries above the departure time a margin line that the company has preserved word for word, as the company preserves all margin lines it cannot price, and the line reads:

“Readers with an enormous discipline, and it does to that night.”

The reading the station masters give, and give without a gloss, is this: the service works only on the nights it does — the night it does, no other, and it does — and the only passengers who ever meet it at Blue Lake are the ones with the enormous discipline to keep the leaf open through all the blank nights and be standing, lamp in hand, on the one. The 1911 leaf is filed with the gauge book, and the gauge book agrees with it on everything except which nights.

The valley the line runs through

The Willamette and its tributaries form the Willamette Valley, and the valley contains two-thirds of the people of Oregon, which is to say the busiest stretch of the line is not on the rails at all but on the land either side of them, all the way from Salem, the state capital at mile 131, to the junction. At the mouth the line reaches Portland, the state’s largest city, which surrounds the Willamette’s mouth at the Columbia the way a city surrounds anything it has decided is worth keeping; the 401 falls in at 14:03 on the survey schedule and the city goes on. Down by the water the line keeps one address for lost goods and found ones alike, and the register at Lost & Found, Notice 4172 — one gradient descent, last seen descending at 3400 N. Willamette — is kept in the same drawer as the gauge book, the two agreeing that whatever descends in this valley eventually reaches the river.
