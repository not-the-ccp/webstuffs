VOL. IX

DIARY

the caged bird shelf

WED · 28 MAY · 2014

The Medical Center, Winston-Salem — Maya Angelou

6:40 in. Fog on the corner of 39th Street and the old tobacco row, then sun. The ward clock is half a minute slow and I have stopped fighting it.

The phone rang at 6:40 this morning and the woman who spoke said only that the bird had come in from the flight at last. Maya Angelou, at rest. I have sat at the side of the bed I was not asked to keep and written it down because the ink will hold what the morning will not. Born Marguerite Annie Johnson, 4 April 1928, in St. Louis, and raised up the road in Stamps, Arkansas, in her grandmother Annie’s house, where the alphabet came under a kerosene lamp and a dictionary came in the dark and had to be read, and not read, for four days. I count what the drawer keeps: seven autobiographies, the first — I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, 1969, the life up to the age of seventeen — doing the work of all the rest; three books of essays; the poems, which are the small change of the whole estate; the one-woman show Heart Like a Wheel in London in 1977; the Tony nomination for And We Go On in 1975; the Emmy in 1979; the poem at the January 2013 inaugural, and the Presidential Medal of Freedom on the lawn in 2011, and more than fifty honorary degrees stacked like postcards in a coat pocket, none of them addressed.

She came to Winston-Salem for the last of the years, to the medical center, and the fog this morning has the shape of that town in it — tobacco and the sound of paper being cut. I keep returning to Stamps. The summer, the log kitchen, the word from the door that locked her mouth shut for seven years, and the woman — the grandmother, the “crazy woman” of the book, who opened the locked thing again with a dictionary and a lamp. The bird in the kitchen window, the one who was going to die, and the little girl who put her hands on its wings and held it until the sun did the rest. A caged bird sings of freedom, and the free bird thinks a caged bird sings to it. That sentence carried her across fifty years of rooms; I have written it twice in the margin and the second copy came out cleaner than the first.

In her desk, at the clinic, is the drawer of specimen cards — a habit she never quite lost, the collecting of small lives — and the top card in the drawer, in a hand that is not hers, reads, word for word: “The Han era gave the species before us, Mordellistena dalmatica , field-fresh from the substance: Flash fire in the last of the hearing.” I have left it on the desk where the morning can read it over my shoulder. I do not understand the whole of it, and I am not going to. What I understand is this: a flash fire, at the end of a hearing, and something field-fresh coming out of the substance of it — which is a card about a long listening finishing in light, and I think the drawer has been keeping it for this room all along, for the eighty-seventh year, for the last of the hearing, for a woman who turned every silence she was put into into a room the rest of us have been sitting in since 1969.

The ledger at the Cider Press on Brunswick Street, Fitzroy, balances itself every Thursday without a bookkeeper, and the entry of 21 February 2017 in the counter’s own diary sets down a small cross in the margin and says keep it; I am making the same mark beside the same column, kept, only in a different city. And the listening room has its manners — the ballad shelf, the milk always in, the door with no sign — as they are written down at Chapter VII of the Domestic Musical Etiquette, where the caged bird’s song is kept on the shelf exactly where the chapter puts the ballads: the same distance from the door as the dance floor. The house receives, without a form, whatever the morning brings. This morning brought the bird back in, and the desk, and the card. I am putting my coat on. The fog is cutting at nine, and there is a flight of stairs down to the street that I will take the slow way, one step at a time, like the alphabet under the lamp. — E.
