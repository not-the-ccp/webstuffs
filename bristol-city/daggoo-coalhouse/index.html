The Webstuffs Dining Table · Review No. 41 · Wales

The Coalhouse

9 Bute Docks, Cardiff · coal by the gramme · seating 44, plus the mezzanine (the Forecastle)

Reviewed by Arthur Parry, Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14 · a Thursday in February, table for six, the pass at the head of the room

Cardiff is the capital and largest city of Wales, and it was a small town until the early nineteenth century, which is the whole secret of this restaurant. The coal made the city the way the city made the docks and the docks made the warehouse, and the warehouse is now The Coalhouse, above the old No. 3 yard at the Bute Docks, and it serves coal by the gramme. By 2025 the city counts 381,516 people, the principal area being the City and County of Cardiff, Dinas a Sir Caerdydd in Welsh; it is the eleventh largest city in the United Kingdom, the county town of the historic Glamorgan and, from 1974 to 1996, of South Glamorgan. It sits in the Cardiff Capital Region of 1.5 million people, roughly half the nation, and it belongs to the Eurocities network of the largest European cities. The Coalhouse serves all of that, one course at a time, and bills you for it in grams of coal.

The dining room is low-ceilinged and holds forty-four, with a mezzanine up four steps that the owner calls the Forecastle, after the old coalmaster’s deck, where the winter regulars sit with their feet on the rail. At the head of the room stands the pass, and over the coal stove stands a single large copper funnel through which the kitchen speaks to the table. The owner, known to the dock as Daggoo—I have never once been able to learn a first name, and I have tried three times—is a large man with a pleated forehead, and he runs the pass the way a lighthouse keeps its keepers honest.

The tasting menu is seven courses, one per dock: stoneflower and beet soup, 30 g; a flat sourdough called the 1832, the year the town decided to stop being small; Glamorgan squash on coal crostini; an estuary hake under a crust of charcoal, 120 g, the proudest line on the board; and so on, all priced in grammes and settled at the weighbridge counter by the door. The full seven run to 975 g, which the room accepts as the most honest way of charging I have seen outside a dock.

I sat at table nine with my daughter Lila—eight, tablet in the napkin ring, the same tablet that once caught the commentator at Elland Road while a dictionary page lay open—and the microphone was open again, as it always is in this family. What it caught that night is kept here word for word, exactly the way the Matchnight Committee keeps its crossbar rulings, and I am not tidying it up for the record:

But the forecastle deck, Daggoo roused the sleepers with such Grand-Lama-like exclusiveness; and, for an instant, shooting his pleated forehead through the funnel_, one, two, three—oh!

The room decodes it as follows. The Forecastle is the mezzanine, where the dozers are dozing over the soup by 7:40. Daggoo rouses them, in a voice of Grand-Lama-like exclusiveness, to the second half of the menu. The pleated forehead is his, shooting up through the copper funnel to check the table; and the underscore is what the transcription left where the funnel’s own whistle cut across the microphone, which the room considers a mark of the kitchen and not of the machine. And “one, two, three—oh” is Lila, counting the courses as they came out of the funnel: three had arrived, and the fourth was the oh. Tap the funnel at the top of this review if you would like to count along; it does not charge for the gramme.

The dessert I came to Cardiff for. David Chen, in the shed below Clerkenwell, has been rebuilding the county of Cheshire in marine plywood at 1:100,000—2,344 square centimetres, the scale holding with nothing lost—with one grain of salt per hundred people, 11,400 grains, jarred and set into the southern edge of the bench. He has sent a twin jar of that salt south, to the Bute Docks, with a note saying to use it only where it would matter. The Coalhouse uses it on exactly one dessert: a shortbread cut, at scale, to the shape of the model, served with the jar open on the board and a brass washer under the plate. The washer is mine, sent to me at Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, and I am not entirely sure what it means, but I leave it under the plate anyway. Eleven thousand four hundred grains, one per hundred people of the county he called uninteresting in 2006 and has since corrected in kind. I ate the shortbread over the jar, as instructed, and did not lose a single grain.

The room rates a place in crossbars, zero to five, on the principle that any honest building has woodwork. The Coalhouse takes four. The 1832 is the best flat bread I have had since the matchday pie counter. The funnel is a kitchen, a doorway, and a verdict all at once. The gramme prices are the first menu I have seen that could not be quietly inflated. I have asked the kitchen to rule, as the committee ruled in the letter to Ashton Gate, on the woodwork question, and Daggoo’s verdict, given through the funnel and not the pass: the crossbar is innocent, eight to one, the funnel is guilty as charged, and Duke, wherever he is, is to have a glass of wine at his next meal. One crossbar is withheld because the Forecastle is four steps up and Lila’s tablet fell on the second, and the whole room heard it. The city is 381,516, the region is 1.5 million, and half of Wales will now have to know about this place. That is the cost of a capital.
