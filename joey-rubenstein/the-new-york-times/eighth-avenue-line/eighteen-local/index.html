21 March — 06:41, first light, the platform at Ochs Avenue with the forms drying on the rail

What is running

The companion line of the H Local carries no passengers; it carries a rule, and the rule has a route. What is being observed on this line is the age of consent — the age at which a person is considered to be legally competent to consent to sexual acts. Consequently, an adult who engages in sexual activity with a person younger than the age of consent is unable to legally claim that the sexual activity was consensual, and such sexual activity is considered child sexual abuse or statutory rape. The person below the minimum age is considered the victim, and their sex partner the offender — the two words are printed on the back of every ticket — although some jurisdictions provide exceptions through the "Romeo and Juliet laws," the close-in-age branch of this line, where one or both participants are underage and are close in age.

The H Local itself carries a practice, hourly at the 41 minutes, from 59th Street to 14th Street; this line carries the law that says at what platform a passenger's signature first counts. Where the other line's commentary is anatomy, this one is statute. The timetable is set first, as is correct for the genre, and the commentary follows station by station.

23 March — 09:12, Ochs Avenue, the counter under the colonnade

Platform Ochs Avenue — the slip, and the age it takes

The tickets of this line are the same tickets the H Local uses: the Form 3B enrolment slip of the Ochs Avenue Day School, No. 4 189 6, the stock the ticket clerk buys whole in the number that appears on the Form's term report on Albert Einstein, filed in the manila envelope that travels between the two platforms in the same folder. The difference between the two lines is one thing only, and it is the whole of this one. On the H Local a signed slip is the ticket. On this line the slip is a question: old enough to sign it? The clerk at Ochs Avenue will not stamp a slip signed by a hand the register has not yet reached, however steady the handwriting, however earnest the signature, however much the young passenger is certain — for the law's question is not whether the passenger meant to consent but whether the law will count the consent at all. Below the fixed platform, the ticket is void, and the H Local's own service, if an adult rides it with a passenger of that age, runs under a different name: child sexual abuse, statutory rape. The word offender is printed on the back of the ticket for the adult; the word victim for the young passenger. The print is small, and it is in red.

24 March — 11:49, Platform 16th, where most of the world's registers stop

Platforms 14th to 17th — the census of registers

The platforms of this line are not built by the line; they are built by each local register, and the registers do not agree. The census I have taken of them, from the terminus ledger, is this: in the great majority of jurisdictions the minimum platform is fixed somewhere between 14th and 16th; a substantial number of registers fix it at 18th, the terminus itself, where every slip is competent by the plain rule of majority; and a small number still stand it lower, on the 13th, a platform struck through in several registers in the last two decades with the same blue pencil Mr. Delgado keeps. Some registers are two-gauged: the ordinary service stops at 16th while particular acts — the heavier carriages, the line's own term for them — are held back to the terminus. Older registers still ran the platform by the passenger's sex, or moved it by the partner's age, and those notices are all struck through now; the inspector keeps the old prints in the envelope as a warning, not a rule. What the census agrees on, whatever the platform, is the direction of travel: the line runs toward the terminus, and the terminus is the only platform where no notice need be posted at all. The same order travels abroad the way all the registers' orders travel: the compounding office at Basle, which keeps the two dose platforms of the acid the line has taken into its own service, was examined on the same rule, and its application papers — No. CH-1938 0190, Lysergic Acid Diethylamide and Articles Thereof, filed at the 25 — stand on this shelf as a patent application summary with the examiner's red-ink margin, where the higher platform governs those acts alone.

25 March — 15:49, Platform 16th, the inspector's bench

Platform 16th — why the law reads the way it reads

It was asked of me, at the inspector's bench, why the register never once prints the name of the thing this line serves. It is true: the term age of consent typically does not appear in legal statutes. A law, the inspector says, will establish the age as a number beside an act — never the name of the arrangement, only the platform. I put the question to the wire, and the counter at Ochs Avenue answered at 16:01, in the clerk's hand: "Let's to it." "Aye, and that's because the passage is dry by design, not by fault." I have preserved both lines word for word, because they are the whole of the doctrine. The statute does not name the age because a name is an invitation and a number is a barrier; the law wants the barrier. It is the same dryness the H Local keeps in its own carriage two, where the line's commentator notes the passage is dry by design — and the coincidence is not a coincidence, because both lines are keeping the same order: nothing moves that the register has not first fixed.

27 March — 23:58, Platform 18th, after the last service

Terminus, 18th — majority, and the ledger

The terminus is eighteen, the age of majority, and it is not always the age of consent — the consent service often stops at the 16th — but it is the one platform every register agrees on, and the one where the whole apparatus stands down: no branch line, no dual gauge, no guardian, no note in red ink, because a signature taken at the terminus needs no checking at all. The terminus platform is open: a seat, a while, the same water the H Local keeps at its own 14th. The last passenger of the day is asked the same question the other line asks at its terminus — how the ride was — and on this line the answer is always the same, because the line carried no ride at all: it carried the rule, and the rule arrived on schedule.

The ledger closes. The slips of the season are filed in the manila envelope beside the term report they came from, the census of registers goes into the terminus ledger, and the avenue runs to the next 41 minutes, where the H Local takes up its own service — the practice, hourly, at the front of this series, No. 13 — while this line stands at the platform, as it stands every day of the year, on the minute to the minute. The last blank slip of the day is turned over and pressed into service as the ticket for the Form's winter table, where the same hand stamps the game that runs on 355 squares and ends with a will: Nobel — a game of patents, blasts, and one very large will, page 27 of the rules, filed in the manila envelope with the slips it is cut from. It was a passenger of the line, in 1887, who left the platform for the terminus and then for Italy — Father Edward V. Boursaud, who stood at the chair of Boston College from 1884 to 1887 and answered the Rome posting at the 18th the way every competent hand must — and his trail, from New York to the Tiber, is walked stop by stop in The Boursaud Trail — a six-stop tourist brochure; beside that brochure his sister kept the Journal Français, a diary of the language spoken at his family table, where the last entry of the ledger is a recipe card pressed through a fine sieve. The wider shelf where the field notes, the reports, and the old ledgers sit side by side is at the front page of Webstuffs, and the inspector's standing order, in red ink, is the last line of the day's record: the ledger will keep you; you need only keep the ledger.
