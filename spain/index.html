España , n.

Pronounced esː-PAHː-nyah · also la Ibérica · also, in one's own sleeping voice, the country that keeps time an hour east

The Kingdom of Spain, by its full name, a country of Southern and Western Europe with territories in North Africa; to dream it is to dream the whole of the Iberian Peninsula lying down in a single red-and-gold sheet, from the grey water of the Bay of Biscay down to the southernmost point of continental Europe, where at Punta de Tarifa the dream's door stands open — the Mediterranean on one side, Gibraltar, Morocco and the African shore on the other. In the dream Spain is the largest country in Southern Europe and the fourth-most populous member state of the European Union, and these two facts stand exactly as they stand in the waking, only the sleeper's hands come away tinged the colour of an orange lamp, because in the dream the Canary Islands have drifted off the eastern Atlantic and stand as seven little lamps in the dark water, and the Balearic Islands are four of them, still close enough to be named, and Ceuta and Melilla are two small posts on the African mainland whose readings arrive a quarter-hour ahead of the country itself. To the north, France and Andorra hold the border the way a hand holds the edge of a sheet: without moving, and without being noticed.

When Dreamed of

Most commonly Spain arrives as a staircase descending to the sea, each step a coastline, and the last step is always Tarifa, where the water is so near it sounds through the floor of the dream. The dreamer is either walking it down or standing on the final step looking at the far shore, which in the dream is the same shore twice — once as the Mediterranean and once as the Atlantic, and the two have agreed to wear the same dark colour that night. If the dreamer crosses, the dream is a travel dream of the honest kind, a crossing of eleven kilometres of water, and the sleeper should be told it means a short journey that keeps its distance out of respect.

The racing season dreams of Spain the way the rest of the shelf dreams of it: by the lap. The same year the fan desk at turn 14 in Texas pinned its timing card for the 56 laps of the 2014 United States Grand Prix, 2 November 2014 — Hamilton's win from P2, the cold front of orange and black ponchos, the marginalia in the top margin — that season closed one lap further south, in Seville province on the red clay at Jerez, on 7 December 2014, in fifty-seven laps: Hamilton's eleventh victory of the year and the thirty-third of his career, with a grey poncho the same colour as the Austin ones as the common garment of the evening. The folio keeps the fifty-seventh lap here; the fifty-six are on their own card, and the two belong together the way a season belongs to its last race.

And on time, which the dreamer should not pass by: Spain, in the dream as in the waking, keeps the clock of France. The country sits under the meridian of Lisbon but reads the hour of Paris, an hour east of its own body, and the dream of it always involves a clock that is right and a sky that is an hour behind — the dark coming to a Spanish window early by the meridian's arithmetic. The timing pit at Austin argued about this exact feeling for one whole season, over a line that gives each figure an equal measure of time; the Concordance's reading is that the figures won, and that a country may keep its hour and lose nothing. The dreamer who wakes in Spain an hour ahead of the map is not being punished. The map is the one that is out of season.

Interpretation

  • To dream Punta de Tarifa — the southernmost point of continental Europe — is a sign of a journey whose end is a door: something about to cross from one water to the other, or from one ledger to the other, eleven kilometres of it.
  • To dream the number four — Spain standing fourth in the count of its union, fourth-most populous of the member states — is the fourth-of-the-family figure, and the Concordance refers the dreamer at once to the Correspondents' Board, Thread No. 54, “The Fourth of the Family, at the Gauge”: the one the ledger ruled dry, who turns out to be the one who kept it anyway. A fourth in a dream is a keeper, not a follower.
  • To dream Ceuta and Melilla — the two posts on the African mainland — is the figure the scribe of the Theban Almanac would have called a reading taken from a station the family's ledger had not kept before: the first reading from across the water, in the morning, when the sea agrees with the data. It means the dreamer is about to learn a fact from outside their own border that the border will have to be widened to hold.
  • To dream the Canary Islands — seven lamps, drifting, eastern Atlantic — is a good and ordinary sign: a set of small lights the dreamer has been keeping without being asked, and the keeping has been accepted.
  • To dream the fifty-seventh lap — the lap past the fifty-six — is a debt paid in full. The season is over; the card is pinned; the desk stays open.

The Kitchen Interpretation

Half of these dreams end at the table without the dreamer's agreeing, and Spain's end at the table with sugar in it. The bowl in the dream is a wide white bowl of pounded sugar — the fine kind, ground to a powder that falls through the fingers — and beside it stands a recipe card that is not in the dreamer's hand and was not, before the dream, in anyone's. The Concordance has found this card before, in the same pencilled hand, at the gate log of the lagoon: the Funchal card of fresh butter, which Thread No. 54 ruled in and let keep its line under the no-eraser clause. This time the pencil line on the back reads, word for word:

Athenaeus has transmitted to posterity some very fine pounded sugar.

The board will want the third reading, as the Austin timing office wanted its third reading, and the third reading has at last produced the postcard: it turned up not at the timing pit behind pit exit but here, in the folio, pinned to the bowl. It is a card from a table older than the shelf — a Greek table, at which a man named Athenaeus set the foods of his age — and it has crossed the water to sit beside the sugar the way Ceuta sits beside the mainland, a station kept from the far bank. The domestic meaning, in the voice this folio has always used: Grind the sugar fine, set the bowl at the window, and let the hour come to the glass before the glass comes to the hour. The bowl should be white, or the dream will not close. The line, once ruled, keeps its line.

Cross-Readings

The water that carries the Balearics in the dream is dreamed elsewhere on the shelf, filed as a traveller's entry rather than a sleeper's: the Western Mediterranean, travel guide entry No. 12, which keeps the bitter orange press and the gates at Gibraltar — the same gate, from the other side. And for the Concordance's own sister entry, the blue sheet of Santiago and its rule about the kitchen — Sueño Azul, Entry No. 143 — the reader should note that red and gold and blue are three coverings the same peninsula of sleep has lent out, and that whichever of them is on, the sleeper's hands come away tinged. The fourth of the family, in Gaul, and the fourth of the union, on the peninsula, keep one another company on this shelf; the scribe files them together without further argument. Everything is indexed from the front page of the shelf, where the keeper's notices hang.
