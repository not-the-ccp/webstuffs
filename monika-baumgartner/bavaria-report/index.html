Bavaria, officially the Free State of Bavaria

A school report written by Maximilian Baumgartner, Class 9B, on the state in which he has lived all his life and which, he now notes, he had to look up to learn.

1What Bavaria is

Bavaria, officially the Free State of Bavaria, is a landlocked state of Germany. It borders the states of Baden-Württemberg to the west, Hesse to the north-west, and Thuringia and Saxony to the north, as well as the countries of Switzerland and Austria to the south and the Czech Republic to the east. The state’s capital and largest city is Munich; other major cities include Nuremberg, Augsburg, Regensburg, and Ingolstadt. With an area of 70,550 square kilometres (27,249 sq mi), it is the largest German state, and with 13.08 million inhabitants, the second-most populous. I measured my route to school with a ruler on the atlas: Schwabing is 7.2 millimetres from the city limits, which at the atlas’s scale works out to a walk I already make, so the atlas and I agree.

What surprised me was how much border Bavaria has for a state that cannot see the sea: four neighbouring German states plus three countries. The Chiemgäu is so close to the Austrian border that the cows’ bell pattern is different on either side, and the bakery at my street corner sells the same Schmörn that you would get across the road in Kufstein, only in a different language.

2A short history, longer than one page

The history of Bavaria begins with the settlement of Celtic tribes during the Iron Age — the Boii, after whom the whole region is named, the Bayern. The territory was then ruled in turn by Alamanni and by the Ostrogoths, and in 555 the Franks handed it over to the Bavarians themselves, who became an ally of the Frankish kings and later a duchy of their own. The dukes and kings of the House of Wittelsbach rule most of the territory in one form or another down to the present Free State. My report had to be two pages, so the Wittelsbachs get a whole sentence, which I consider fair, because the Nymphenburg Palace alone could absorb an afternoon, and in fact it did, on the class excursion of 17 May.

3The cities

Munich needs no introduction from me except that the Marienplatz clock shows the Schäfflertanz every hour and the pigeons time themselves to it better than any of us time our homework. Nuremberg, 160 kilometres north, is famous for its imperial castle, its toy-maker quarter, and — I checked the atlas — a Christmas market that starts in November and is mentioned in nearly every Bavarian text, as if the whole state were preparing for it year-round. Augsburg was, according to our textbook, one of the oldest German cities, older than Nuremberg, and it takes quiet, dignified pride in this. Regensburg sits on the Danube with a Roman bridge of fourteen arches that is still in service; Ingolstadt, a little downstream, is a university town whose football team, I am told, is a separate historical matter that should not be discussed in class. I did not discuss it in class.

4The October exchange: Bavaria by radio, 21:00

For the report’s “exchange” element we used the class radio hour, in which 9B has a pen-friend class in Helena, Arkansas, a town that appears in a brochure our teacher keeps on her desk and where blues sing from the porches and the King Biscuit Blues Festival fills October with horns. Fräulein Höchsteiner transcribed the tape afterwards, and printed the transcript with a note that she was sorry, but the tape was the tape. The line in question reads, in her exact words, and I copy it exactly because I am not allowed to correct the transcript:

“Cruel and confining fowls, Mowbray is exactly at one and the King Biscuit Blues Festival filling October with horns.”

— Transcribed from Class Radio Hour tape no. 6, minute 11, by Fräulein Höchsteiner, who added in pencil: “Mowbray is the hens. The clock was wrong. The horns were the festival. Leave it.”

My understanding of the sentence, which the teacher accepted: Mowbray is the name of the hens in the exchange class’ backyard, they were exactly at one o’clock, and the festival’s horns were carrying across the Mississippi lowlands at the same moment the hens were doing whatever hens do at one. Two states, one October, one tape. I mention it because our task was to compare two landlocked places with festivals in autumn, and Bavaria’s answer to the Biscuit Festival is the Oktoberfest, which runs from late September to the first Sunday in October on the Theresienwiese, and which I know from the inside: the same black apron and silver brooch that my grandmother wore as the landlady of the Gasthof zum Bären in every one of her episodes still hang in her script drawer in Munich, and the drawer came to the auction as part of her personal archive, and it is in the same catalogue as everything else I own that is hers.

5Conclusion

Bavaria is 70,550 square kilometres of border-hugging, clock-swinging, sausage-and-cellar beer, and it is also just my bedroom window. I recommend the state to any class that wants a geography that answers back. The atlas says it borders Hesse to the north-west; my grandmother’s address book, in one of its three alphabets, says that is a question of where you park the car. I would like to use the word “landlocked” one more time for good luck, because the exchange class is writing back in November and I intend to say it first.
