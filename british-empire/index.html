The British Empire

One destination in twenty-four time zones · Bush House, Fleet Street, to the last post · best season: whichever it is, somewhere

The British Empire comprised the dominions, colonies, protectorates, mandates, and other territories ruled or administered by the United Kingdom and its predecessor states. It began with the overseas possessions and trading posts established by England in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, and the colonisation attempts by Scotland during the 17th century — the Darien venture of 1698 among them, which this guide notes, as the guide always notes, was an attempt. At its height in the 19th and early 20th centuries it was the largest empire in history and, for a century, was the foremost global power. By 1913 the British Empire held sway over 412 million people, 23 per cent of the world population at the time, and by 1920 it covered 35.5 million km² (13.7 million sq mi), 24 per cent of the Earth's surface — a figure the guide prints twice because the first impression did not take. The tourist is invited to regard it, as the surveyors did, as a single destination with an excellent postal system, and this entry gives the route, the timings, and the table.

The departure is the one this guide is best at. From the shortwave studio, second floor, Bush House, Fleet Street, the tour leaves at 03:45, on the wave, in more than 40 languages, so that no traveller is required to listen at another place's hour. The itinerary is timed on the prior art of departure: the whole tour is arranged to arrive in every room before 04:12, which is the hour the Dhaka Dawn Express No. 257 stands at platform 9, and the guide keeps the two in the same breath the way the Office keeps them in the same file. The South Asia leg of the tour is guided by Kamal Ahmed, consulting editor of The Daily Star, who walks it seat by seat from seat 14, lower berth, and whose standing instruction to the party is the guide's own house rule: the whole of it, in order, and not one more than the whole. Mr. Ahmed carries the flag at its small size. The Empire, the guide observes, was for a century famous for exactly this: it set down its facts in every climate at once — a distance, a time, a platform, a frequency, a voice, an hour — and asked no quarter of the landscape to explain them.

The four quarters, as the party keeps them

East and Southern Africa

The Cape Table at first light, 33°52′ S; the long evening programme; a tea at 15:40 that has been kept since 1820 and is still on the card at the hotel. The party is reminded that the relay in this climate does not depend on the weather of another.

West and Central Africa

Accra to Lagos on the discussion that moves from one station to the next like a relay of voices; the palm-oil market at dawn; the guide's one warning, which is that the programme is longer than it looks and ends later than the guest believes.

South Asia

The delta leg, guided by Mr. Ahmed: Calcutta, the plain, the rice country between Arambagh and Jessore, where the line and the wave keep the same pace and the morning light comes off the water on the left-hand side. The guest is set down at Dhaka at 04:12, in the middle of a sentence, which the guide considers the correct arrival.

The Pacific

Singapore at 06:41, the Straits ferry, Hong Kong with the tea at 11:15, and the last post at the far side of the dateline, where the tour closes the day one hour before it has opened it. Here the guide stops being modest: twenty-four time zones is not a difficulty, it is the ticket.

The tour averages, over a working week, 450 million ears, which the guide notes is 450 million more breakfasts than any one kitchen can cover, and which is why the kitchen, as shown below, is run by the Italians.

The kitchen card, transcribed

The party's standing order is kept at the Empire Grill, in the annexe, under the sign. The card is written in the head cook's hand, is three lines long, and is reproduced here exactly as it stands, word for word, for the party's reference on the matter of the quail:

"The Italians of this aromatic herb, known to destroy a bird, pluck it, salt and pepper to taste."

The guide glosses it in the margin, as the guide always does. The Italians is the card's name for the three Italian hands of the grill, who have held the quail pass since before the guest can remember and since, the ledger says, 1897. The aromatic herb is the grill's own thyme, grown in the window boxes of the annexe, which the old kitchen ledger records — in its own and unrepeatable phrase — as known to destroy a bird, meaning that a quail marinated in it on a cold morning has not come back. The line is, in short, the quail of the house: the herb does the work the bird is not spared, and what is left is plucked, salted, and peppered to taste. The guide adds that the card has never once been corrected, and recommends the guest read it the same way the guide reads timetables: as fact, in order, and not one more than the whole.

Notes for the traveller

The tour keeps one way-station in every quarter where the party may rest its ears, and the guide is proud of the one in the Far East: the Naga Listeners' Club, two doors from the cathedral, above the old bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, where the world is kept in one room the way this guide keeps it in one entry — 40 languages and more arriving at one small table, the ledger of the morning held in order, and apple fritters as the standing order. A traveller who has been served at that table, and has since arrived in the delta at 04:12 in the middle of a sentence, and has eaten the quail, may be taken as having done the whole tour. The guide will certify it, in the margin, on the back of the ticket, in its small, quiet flag of a hand, and will add nothing to it, because the whole of the tour, like the whole of the world's news, is enough.

Elsewhere in the guide

The departure from Bush House, in full: the specification of the apparatus by which this tour actually runs — the feeds, the frequencies, the claims, and the marginal annotation read in from Naga — Specification No. WW-24-0117 · The BBC World Service, where the South Asian feed is timed to arrive before the first train of the prior-art morning working.

The prior art of departure itself, station by station from 04:12 to 09:04, with the notes of the guide of the South Asia leg set down in the margin of its book: The Dhaka Dawn Express — Timetable & Editorial Notes, BTR Route 6, seat 14, lower berth, as ever.

The party's one wet-season table, for those who would rather eat the sea than hear it read at them: Sea Ginger — Restaurant Review, Pier 9, Harbour Island, The Bahamas, where the house serves its fire coral on its own calcareous skeleton and the ledger of shapes is kept in a tin box under the pier.

The window boxes of the annexe, the discipline of what grows in them, and the head cook's one line about them kept word for word on its own folio of the sleeping ledger: Botany — Dream Dictionary, Entry 432, where the thyme is the whole garden, the quail is not spared, and the flag is kept small.

And the whole tour, this entry included, filed in the archive at Webstuffs, on the shelf with the timetable, the guard's book, and the kitchen card.
