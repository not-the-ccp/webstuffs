TT1 — Sennedjem's Tomb, Deir el-Medina

Deir el-Medina, west bank of the Nile, opposite Luxor · 25°44′ N, 32°36′ E · elevation 60 m above river level · gauge No. 1, stone staff, set 1886

Standing reading, taken each day at 07:30 and again at 14:30 · present entry: 23.0 °C, still air, barometer 1020 hPa, holding · the January of the opening is kept on file below

Column 1 · The opening, January 1886

The unrobbed shaft

At first light in January 1886 the almanac's west-bank station logs an opening it files under unsealed chambers: TT1, the burial place of the ancient Egyptian official Sennedjem and members of his family, at the workmen's village of Deir el-Medina on the west bank of the Nile opposite Luxor. The tomb was opened by Salam Abu Duhi, a Gurnawi local, working with three others in the necropolis behind the village, and what they entered had never been robbed. The funerary complex consists of three pyramid-shaped chapels, and of the three shafts that stand before them only the shaft in front of Sennedjem's own chapel was still sealed. It leads down through the rock to a series of underground rooms, the last of them the burial chamber, whose walls are extensively decorated. The single undisturbed room contained 165 objects, and the almanac keeps the count here with the rest of the January readings.

Column 2 · The chapels, south to north

Three pyramids over the shafts

Above the ground the complex keeps three pyramid-shaped chapels, read from south to north: the first dedicated to Sennedjem's father or brother, the middle to Sennedjem himself, and the northernmost to his son Khonsu. Each chapel stands over a shaft, and each shaft was cut for a descent that the almanac treats as a pressure drop into the rock — the deeper the shaft, the steadier the reading below. The southern chapel's shaft was stripped long ago, and the northern one as well; only the middle shaft, in front of Sennedjem's chapel, held its seal until January 1886. The station notes the asymmetry as it finds it: one unrobbed descent, two empty ones, and the decorated rooms beneath the middle.

Column 4 · The objects, January 1886

One hundred and sixty-five things

The undisturbed chamber returned 165 objects, logged by the station as found — furniture, vessels, food, tools, figurines, textiles, and the small writing equipment a scribe leaves behind when the room is sealed. Column 5 · Marginalia

The line in the register

One further line stands in the station register, copied without correction because the almanac prints what the keeper set down:

"And, folded in at the lectern does not."

The reading is entered as found. The lectern in the register's grammar is the stone block at the mouth of the shaft, where the day's objects were laid out before they were counted, and folded in is the keeper's word for what the earth does to a chamber that has been sealed — it takes the objects in, and holds them, and does not give them back. The line stays in the file with the 165 objects, the three chapels, and the single unrobbed shaft, as part of the January record.
