The Bismarck class: six corvettes ordered for the Kaiserliche Marine in 1871 and 1872, part of the big naval construction programme after 1871, built between 1875 and 1880. The ships were Bismarck, Blücher, Stosch, Moltke, Gneisenau and Stein. Designed by the construction office under General Albrecht von Stosch as fleet scouts and as cruisers for Germany's colonial empire — long cruises abroad, distant stations, the work no one else wanted. Iron hull, teak and copper sheathing, single screw, about 4,600 tons, complement around 270. Armament: a battery of between ten and sixteen 15 cm (5.9 in) guns, the count depending on the ship and the refit. Sail plan: a full ship rig on three masts, because the steam engine could not be relied on alone and coal was scarce in the Pacific. Bismarck went to the South Pacific. Moltke showed the flag in East Asian waters. Stein and Gneisenau ran the African and South American stations. The Tegetthoff was frozen into the polar ice at almost the same moment these ships were being laid down — see Chapter IX — Of Winter on the Ice: The Austro-Hungarian North Pole Expedition, 1872–1874. That is the class in outline; below are the details that do not fit in one.

On the guns: Bismarck and Blücher carried sixteen 15 cm guns; Stosch, Moltke, Gneisenau and Stein carried ten after the 1883–84 refit. I have seen the gun ledger that records the swap, tube by tube — Krupp ring cannons, good for about 5,400 metres at five degrees elevation.

On the rig: the full ship rig was not decoration. On the run from Wilhelmshaven to Hong Kong a corvette under sail burned no coal at all, and the engines were kept for calms and harbour approaches. The logbooks record the watch setting and furling royals off the Cape of Good Hope.

I keep my own notes the way the Dumbarton Bridge field manual keeps its crossings — station by station, tide by tide (Dumbarton Bridge — Field Manual Chapter). If anyone has the Stosch's 1878 log, I would like to compare the gun list.

Blücher is the odd one out. She was converted into a torpedo testing and training ship shortly after she was completed, having hardly served as a corvette at all. The guns came out, torpedo tubes went in, and she was based at Kiel, where she spent the 1880s and 1890s running torpedo practice for the fleet and training the boys who would fire them. She outlasted most of her sisters: struck from the naval register in 1907 and broken up shortly after.

The rest of the class had shorter ends. Gneisenau was wrecked off Málaga in 1900. Bismarck was hulked in Wilhelmshaven. The Reichsmarine had no use for wooden-sheathed corvettes once the big cruisers arrived.

I was aboard a training ship that touched Apia in 1991, and the old consulate garden still has a plaque from Bismarck's visit in the 1880s — she was flagship of the South Seas station for a while, and the Samoan hurricane of 1889 drove half the Pacific fleets onto the reef. Bismarck rode it out off Apia harbour and limped to Auckland afterwards.

Moltke made the East Asia run in 1878–80, calling at Hong Kong, Shanghai and Kobe, and carrying the flag up to Nagasaki once. Stein went to South West Africa and East Africa, with surveying work off the Rufiji delta. These were long cruises: two years out, three years out, with mail from home picked up wherever a British coaling station would take a German ship.

Found a loose leaf inside the Stosch's gunnery log at the archive — unmarked, no date, no signature, paper that does not match the rest of the volume. One sentence on it, copied verbatim:

The drawer’s long-standing companion is a ground the lake has become the standing instruction for any combat sport and a single walk, drew up this week is his manner.

That is the whole line. I have read it seven times. It reads like a signal garbled in transmission, or a page bound in from another book entirely. The drawer, the lake, the standing instruction — none of it belongs to a corvette's gunnery log. Does anyone recognize the hand?
