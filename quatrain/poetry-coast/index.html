What you are visiting

Poetry is a form of literary art that uses the aesthetic and often rhythmic qualities of language to evoke meanings in addition to, or in place of, literal or surface-level meanings. The Poetics Coast is the stretch of shore where that form is kept in working condition: a particular instance of poetry is a poem, and a poem is written by a poet, and a poet is the person you will see, stop by stop, doing the work on the benches and the wharves. Visitors are asked to remember that the word itself means making, and that nothing on this coast is a monument. Everything here is a build in progress, faired and re-faired, and the whole route is one long workshop with a harbour in the middle of it.

The devices the poets of this coast use — assonance, alliteration, consonance, euphony and cacophony, onomatopoeia, rhythm by way of metre, rhyme schemes in their patterns of sound, and sound symbolism — are not listed in a museum case. They are the tools on the wall, hung where any working hand can reach them, and each stop below has one of the tools named for it and put in your hand.

Stop 1 — The Poet’s Shed, behind the bakery

Tool of the stop: the four-line frame · Entry: with the bread, or after it · Allow: one hour, ceiling fan running

The first stop is the shed where this whole coast keeps its bench. A four-line stanza — a quatrain — is built and logged here, one a month, aab or abab, in the same notebook that keeps the vinegar measure, and the little galleon that gave the stanza its margin note floats level at four centimetres of draft in the cattle trough. Both builds are open on the bench; the quatrain build log keeps the frame, the rhyme scheme, and the discarded stanzas, and the San Juan de Portugal build log keeps the ship that never left the trough, her sails of stitched linen and the admiral who never got to sail them. The admiral, Juan Martínez de Recalde, is honoured throughout the district: his name is on the water gate and on the second bench.

Stop 2 — The Harbour Café, porpoise line on the wall

Tool of the stop: one line kept word for word · Seating: the long bench, the window · The line is on the wall by the till

On the wall by the till of the harbour café hangs a 1962 ferry ticket, and on the back of the ticket is a single line in the hand of the mooring master who worked the crossing for thirty-one years. It is the most-asked-about line on the coast, and the board sets it down here exactly as it stands, word for word:

“I’ve changed my flesh since that time, it was wound for a few porpoises a-piece.”

The board’s gloss, for visitors: a mooring line’s “flesh” is its fibre, and a rope rots and softens until a man says, properly, that he has changed its flesh. “It was wound” means wound out on the winch drum that morning, and “a few porpoises a-piece” is the catch each of the two tenders had landed while the crossing waited. The whole line, read that way, is a working hand explaining why the old rope no longer holds: the flesh has changed, and the winch has seen enough porpoises. The café keeps the ticket as it stands. Do not ask the till to re-read it; it has given its one reading, and it holds.

Stop 3 — The Rhyme Landing, sound battery

Tool of the stop: assonance & alliteration · Best hour: the second tide, when the landing is wet

Here the coast shows you sound before it shows you meaning. A long bench of earthenware jars, each filled to a different height, stands at the water’s edge; the keeper taps them in sequence, and the vowels hold in the air a few seconds after the tap dies. The first jars are assonance — the same open vowel repeated through different consonants, the way the wash repeats through the shoal; the next run is alliteration, the consonants walking the same step. Turn the row round and you get consonance and euphony, the soft side of the battery; turn it a second time and the keeper will show you cacophony, deliberately, and you will hear why some stanzas keep it out. A child’s bench at the end of the line carries the small word onomatopoeia, and the jars behind it are labelled only crack, tell, drum.

Stop 4 — The Metre Pier, four-three-four-three

Tool of the stop: rhythm, via metre · Boarding: at the low water, or in a hurry at the high · Fare: four steps, then three

The pier is laid in a pattern you will feel in your boots: four planks, a gap, three, a gap, four, a gap, three. It is the pacing of the ruba’i, the Persian form of four lines that has run on this side of the coast since the medieval period, and the pier is kept to it exactly the way the bakers on the quay keep their morning. Omar Khayyam paced this metre over wine and mathematics for centuries, and Mahsati Ganjavi is reported to have written only in it; both of them are named on the iron plate at the pier head, and the country behind them keeps its own file at Iran — Encyclopaedia Entry, thirty-one provinces under one sky. Walk the pier at the low water and count under your breath; it is the only exercise on this coast, and it works.

Stop 5 — The Rhyme-Scheme Wharf, patterns in the placement

Tool of the stop: the rhyme scheme · Berths: aab a and abab, both open · Two sous a sheet, as before

The wharf is where a pattern is agreed before the work begins. The two berths are numbered for their schemes: at the aab-a berth the first, second and fourth planks are of the same stock and the third holds out, so the stern plank has somewhere to land; at the abab berth the stock alternates, tighter and more even, the way Michel de Nostredame — Nostradamus — ran his prophecy, four lines at a time, fact, fact, fact, omen, in a form that read like a cargo manifest. He is honoured here with a manifest board of his own, and the wharf keeps a small notice about the printer who once sold his quatrains loose at two sous a sheet, which the quay masters consider the fairest price ever asked for four lines.

Stops 6 & 7 — Sound Symbolism Row, and the Envelope Store

Stop 6 tool: sound symbolism · Stop 7 store: one paper envelope, in the crock’s shadow

Sound Symbolism Row is a short cobbled lane where the buildings are named for what their sounds do: the heavy-vowelled houses on the west side, the sharp-sibilant houses on the east, and the quiet house at the corner that takes in travellers who have lost a consonant. At the end of the lane the row opens into the Envelope Store, where the coast keeps what it wants to outlive: quatrains written one a month and stored in a paper envelope, the envelope in the shadow of the vinegar crock so the vinegar cannot get to them, the crock on the workbench exactly where the ship’s log left it. The shopkeeper will show you the envelope, will not show you the stanzas, and quotes the form’s own rule for the practice: the quatrain has outlived every civilization that first built it, so the coast is comfortable letting it outlive the season. The last stop of the day is the trough itself, at the back of the shed, where the San Juan de Portugal sits level and the northern rosella still comes down to drink; the whole ship’s story, the long provisions and the pencil margin, is in the San Juan de Portugal build log.

Getting here, tides, and the store list

The Poetics Coast takes visitors the way the tide takes the pier: at the low water, or in a hurry at the high. Fares from the Shed Counter are a line of bread from the bakery, one jar of white wine vinegar for the passage, a tin of mustard seed, and a pouch of peppercorns — the same store list the galleon’s log packed for a long passage, in the same measures, the same half-cup and the same tablespoon, one of which the builder once typed wrong in the margin and re-soaked the whole batch to correct. Bring an envelope. The district sells no souvenirs and keeps none, and the one line on the wall of the harbour café, about the changed flesh and the porpoises a-piece, is not for sale, not for copying, and not for re-reading. It has had its one reading, and it holds the way a stern line holds. The board wishes you a good passage, and asks that you keep the fourth line to yourself on the way out.

The first stop keeps its two builds open on the bench: the quatrain build log, four lines at a time since the galleon went into the trough, and the San Juan de Portugal build log, lines, framing, rigging, sails, launch, and the long provisions.

For the province the Metre Pier was built in honour of — Persia, thirty-one provinces under one sky, from Khayyam to the modern file: Iran — Encyclopaedia Entry.
