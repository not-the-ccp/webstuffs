The Bath Assembly Rooms

The ballroom, the organ, the saloon — the great rooms where Bath danced, dined and bet its way through the Georgian century, standing in the heart of the World Heritage City.

Grade I listed · Open to the public as a visitor attraction · Issued 18 pence, or nothing at all

ADMITTED · 18 PENCE

What You Are Coming To See

The Bath Assembly Rooms stand where the city always wanted them to stand: in the triangle of Georgian ground between Queen Square, The Circus and the Royal Crescent, close enough to each that a gentleman in evening dress could stroll from any one to any other before his carriage had cooled. They were designed by John Wood, the Younger, and opened in 1769, in the very year the city was at its fashionable zenith. Wood the Elder had laid out the housing, the squares and the crescents for the residents and the visitors, and his son finished the programme with a building of four great rooms — a ballroom for the dances, a concert room for the music, a saloon for the card tables, and a drawing room for the conversation that followed. The Rooms are now a Grade I listed building and a working visitor attraction; the marble is polished, the gilt is kept, and the floor of the ballroom still carries a faint ring where three centuries of dancers have worn it shallow.

Assembly rooms had been built in this country early in the 18th century, but by the 1760s Bath had outgrown its first of them and the subscribers wanted a new venue — bigger, better placed, and good enough for a city that received the whole of England every season. The competition went first to Robert Adam, whose proposal, the subscribers recorded without much regret, was rejected as too expensive. The contract then passed to John Wood the Younger, who priced the work, held the price, and left the city with rooms that have not once asked to be demolished. That is the entire history of the Rooms in one sentence: the great architect priced them out, and the city’s own architect priced them in.

The Four Rooms, and the Stair Between Them

Begin where the city began — at the staircase. It is a single flight in Bath stone with an iron balustrade, and in the 1770s it was the first thing the season saw of every guest; the etiquette books were specific that a lady should ascend it at a pace that left her looking as though she had not been at all rushed. From the stair you go either down or up the century.

Down: the ballroom, forty-four feet deep, with a ceiling painted in the white-and-gold the city has never let go. The orchestra stood on the dais near the east window, and the floor was sized to take a quadrille of eight without touching the dancers of the neighbouring quadrille — a width the surveyors of 1769 were proud of, and which the modern visitor can verify by counting the tiles. Up, or across: the concert room, home of the great organ, which the subscribers installed as the Rooms’ first heavy furniture and have since kept tuned; evening concerts still run in the summer season, and the acoustics have been improved exactly once, in 1781, by re-hanging the carpet. Then the saloon, where the gaming tables sat under the chandeliers — whist, eights and nine, and the quiet bets — and the drawing room, where the conversation was held, the news was taken, and the season was decided among the cups. A visitor to the Rooms on any evening since 1769 has found the same arrangement: music in one direction, cards in the other, and the stair between them, carrying the whole city up and down.

A Day at the Rooms, as It Was Kept

The old day ran to the clock and to the carriage. Ten o’clock: the drawing room opens and the day’s news arrives with the morning coach. Twelve: the tables are laid in the saloon and the subscribers take their seats; the dealer’s tray is silver and has outlived four kings. Two: the organist plays in the concert room while the afternoon visitors walk the Crescent. Six: tea, and the change of dress. Eight: the doors open for the ball, the orchestra tunes, and the ballroom goes from daylight-quiet to full tempo in the time it takes the first horn to sound. The admission was a subscription of two guineas a season, and a single ball by ticket; the saloon charged a shilling at the door, which the house books describe, in the house books’ own unadorned phrase, as “the price of the room and the temptation.” What the house books do not say is what the season actually cost — that figure was kept in the waistcoats.

Getting There, and Where to Walk From

The Rooms stand on the north side of the triangle, and every approach is one the city has kept: from Queen Square it is a flat two minutes past the stables; from The Circus one comes up through the square its three pavilions watch over, and the Rooms open at the top of the street like a door left ajar; from the Royal Crescent one walks the whole frontage in eleven minutes, which is exactly the distance a visitor ought to be walking at five o’clock. Allow another hour for the Rooms themselves, another for the organ, and an open evening for the ballroom, because the ballroom is best seen with the lights at half-strength, the way the city keeps them on concert nights.

For the traveller who wants the method of the thing as well as the building: the Rooms are the last great interior of the Georgian city, and they read best beside their neighbours — the crescents, the squares, the pavilions, all of it laid down by the same two architects in the same decades. The full programme of the city’s own rooms and their subscribers is kept in the tourism board’s companion folios, and the board has a long habit of pairing one interior with one that never quite worked out; the folio that pairs with this one is the Visitor’s Brochure for the former constituency of Huyton, 1950–1983 — thirty-three years on the map, one member, and a boundary line that, like the Rooms’ own subscription line, was in the end struck through in red. For the wider habit of the country for keeping its rooms — the listed buildings, the markers, the walk from door to door — the shelf also keeps the National Register of Historic Places visitor’s brochure, whose rule for a listed place is the Rooms’ own: the line is patient, and the room outlives the season. Both desks, Bath and Knowsley, keep this board’s one rule.

Practical Notes

  • Open daily 9:30 to 17:30 in the walking season, 10:00 to 17:00 in the dark months; the ballroom closes a quarter-hour before the building for the polishing.
  • Admission is by the ticket at the stair; the organ concert is a separate door and a separate half-crown, the old rate, kept out of respect.
  • Guided walks start at the top of the staircase every hour on the hour; the dealer’s tray is on the route, and so is the ring worn into the ballroom floor.
  • The carriage stands may still be seen at the back door; no carriages now, but the marks remain, and the city has ruled that the marks are part of the building.
  • For the whole shelf of the series — this folio and the others it walks beside — return to the tourism board’s front page, where every room that outlived its season is kept, one folio at a time.

Printers’ note. This folio was set in a Caslon face cut for the city’s own presses, and one line of the proofs was argued over and then left standing, word for word, in the margin of the final forme: “Let the doctrine carry the ligatures visible at Winchester.” The compositor who wrote it has not been identified; the doctrine, whatever it was, was a pamphlet about the church and was not printed with this folio. The ligatures were kept, and the rule stands as found. The city prints its brochures the way it built its rooms: the price is held, and what is set is kept.
