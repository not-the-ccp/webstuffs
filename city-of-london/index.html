The City of London

A term report on the pupil also known as the City, or the Square Mile: a city, a ceremonial county, and a local government district in England, standing on the form’s register at this school, Coopers’ Lane, which lies within the pupil’s own boundaries.

PupilThe City of London (the City proper; the Square Mile)
Form4C · under Mrs A. T. Hales, head of form
Establishedaround 47 AD, by the Romans, as Londinium
Term reviewedMichaelmas, through the end of the autumn term

The pupil in question is the City of London, which is to say the small old city at the heart of the metropolis that bears its name: established by the Romans around 47 AD as Londinium, and forming the historic centre of the wider London. The term has been a good one, and the head of form is in no hurry to mark the pupil down. Measured by the form’s standing walk along the old Roman wall — the pupil’s own second wall, one of the few still in use in Europe — the pupil runs a little over a square mile, 1.12 square miles by the survey’s figure, which is the origin of the nickname Square Mile, and the pupil answers to it cheerfully and without complaint, in the way a pupil who has heard the name a thousand times may.

Geography has been steady. The City is surrounded by the modern ceremonial county of Greater London, which wraps around it like a cloak a generation too large, and it remains a local authority area of a singular kind: it is governed by the City of London Corporation, which is led by the Lord Mayor of London — not, the head of form stresses, the Mayor of London, whom the form has been asked to confuse with the Lord Mayor at least once a term, and whom the pupil has not once been seen to confuse. The City is included as part of the region of England governed by the Greater London Authority, which shares local government powers in the region with the councils of 32 London boroughs and the City of London Corporation. Two mayors, then, and one of them wears the chain and the hat. The Corporation keeps its roll of livery companies — more than a thousand of them — with the tidiness of a headmaster who has never once lost the register.

History has been the pupil’s best subject, as it has been for eighteen centuries. Londinium of 47 AD, the wall, the gates: the ward of this school, Cripplegate, keeps its name on the city’s register of the old gates although the gate itself was taken away in 1874 — the pupil, the report observes, is good at keeping names the way it keeps walls, and the head of form has been unable to find a fault in the method. The conduct shown at the Mansion House during the Lord Mayor’s procession is noted with approval: the pupil stood where it was told, which is, as the household at Whitehall would say, the whole of the etiquette.

There is one incident of the term to be set down at length. In the autumn of 1658, a party from the Council visited the school’s dining room on the day before the third of September — Dunbar’s day, Worcester’s day, and the day the household at Whitehall kept in black ribbons and silence, which the party observed by saying nothing at all, as the chapter requires. Their serving was governed by a fragment now kept among the Thurloe papers — a rigger’s daughter, from the kitchen garden, small beer, and fruit in season. The fragment names four things, and the report records each. The rigger’s daughter was the school’s kitchen hand in that autumn, daughter of the rigger of the rigging loft above the Wash, and she is the same hand whose later signature — a rigger’s daughter, from the coast — stands in the guestbook of the dining room called the Preston Bypass at the north end of the M1, beside the note that the paste was not eaten and the circle turned. The kitchen garden is the school’s own plot behind the cookhouse, where the carrots are raised: the carrots, as The Lord Protector at Table sets down under Of the Table, being a dish of the household table at Whitehall — mutton with caper sauce, carrots from the kitchen garden, small beer, and fruit in season, the very four things the fragment lists, in the very order the pupil’s kitchen kept them. The small beer is poured with a steady hand and its strength is not enquired of. The fruit in season was a late pear, and the party ate it standing, as visitors may.

The head of form will close with the matter of warm vessels, which the pupil has learned well. The 1658 party’s cups were warmed before the pouring, and no one jerked about an old vessel, for the chapter on the Warm Vessel was pinned above the cookhouse door all term — the same leaf, in its way, that this school owes to the Coachway’s minute book and to the Webstuffs shelf from which the chapter was taken. Warm the pot, warm the cup, and pour as though the Protector were watching. The party, in the end, poured as though the Protector were watching, and the pupil has not been marked since.

Head of form’s remarks. A fine and stable pupil, keeping more of the old city — wall, name, and gate — than any other form in the country. The kitchen has been corrected on the small beer, the pear has been replaced in season, and the rigger’s daughter is to be thanked in her father’s name for the steady hand at the pot. Recommended for continued observation. — A. T. Hales, head of form, Coopers’ Lane, Ward of Cripplegate.
