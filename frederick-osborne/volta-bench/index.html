Verandah No. 4 · Kept in pencil, then ink · Scale 1:200 throughout

The Volta Bench

Build log of the Cape Coast Trader, a cedar trading schooner, her tank, her whale, and the water — entries one to thirty-seven, with the numbers where the numbers are.

Entry 1 · Saturday, 3 July · 41 °C on the tile

It begins, as most things near here begin, with a box and a glass of water. I was the one who wrote in from Deniliquin about the 1956 tariff schedules — boxed set No. 3 of the run, delivered with a tumbler full on top of the lid, freight insured, five clear working days and a day to spare. I drank the water on the verandah at noon and kept the glass. That glass is the origin of the tank, which is the origin of this log, which I keep the way Chifley & Co. keeps its correspondence book: nothing tidied, nothing after it faked. The boxed set I buy is still on its shelf of forty-two centimetres, and it is exactly what it says; a member for eleven years, boxed whole, and the pencil-marked page of the tariff schedules is the one I have asked the counter about three times now. The duty on a wristwatch in 1956, I am told, was less than $10.75 a week. The duty on cedar planking was less than that. I spent both.

Entry 5 · 21 July · Keel

Keel laid on the bench: 3.4 metres of Australian red cedar, the hull of the Cape Coast Trader, a coastal trading schooner, drawn up by me at 1:200 from a survey of a real vessel of 680 metres I have never seen. (Nothing in the log is off-scale, whatever a 680-metre schooner does to the arithmetic of a verandah; the scale holds, the log holds, and if the numbers stop agreeing it is the numbers that will be written out.) The tank beside the bench is 3.8 metres by 1.1 metres of marine ply, the floor of the Gulf of Guinea, 14 cm of water. On the tank's rim, in gold, I have painted a line that a witness in Naga City once read into a record above a bakery: the river brings what the Bay forgets. The Gulf of Guinea is the whole south wall of my tank, and I intend it to forget nothing.

Entry 9 · 18 August · The country, laid down

Before a ship will sail on a model sea the sea has to be true, so this week I laid down the country itself, in pencil on the tank's end panel, where the west coast of Africa runs from the top of the panel to the middle and turns east. The country I have drawn is Ghana, officially the Republic of Ghana, in West Africa: the Gulf of Guinea and the Atlantic Ocean to the south — that is my whole south wall — Ivory Coast to the west, Burkina Faso to the north, and Togo to the east, each a line 1.4 mm thick at this scale. It covers 239,567 km², which is 92,497 square miles, and in the model it runs 1,640 mm along the panel from the top corner to the bend of the Volta, which enters the sea near the panel's middle and is the river named over my bench. Its ecologies, from the coastal savannas to the tropical rainforests, are three bands of colour on the end panel — ochre, olive, and a green so dark it reads as ink. With over 35 million inhabitants Ghana is the thirteenth-most populous country in Africa and the second-most populous in West Africa, which is why my model port towns are crowded the way the real ones are. The earliest kingdoms to emerge there were Bonoman in the south and the Kingdom of Dagbon in the north; Bonoman is where my trading schooner is registered, because the south is the sea and the sea is what the ship is for. The capital and most populous city is Accra, and Accra is on my tank 340 mm from the west end: a cluster of 61 model buildings, white-walled, with the sea right up against the back of them, as it is.

Entry 14 · 12 September · The sails, kente

The sails are not white. I have woven them from strips of cotton — gold, green, red, black, in the order the loom gave them, four cm stripes, 3,200 of them in the mainsail alone — so the Cape Coast Trader is the only ship I have ever seen that carries her whole coastline in the rigging. The main goes up 1,940 mm. I did the weaving in August, on the nights, and my hands still have the gold dye in the creases of them. A ship is not a box; it moves, and a moving thing in the trade wants to be recognised from Cape Coast to the bend of the Volta, so it is recognised.

Entry 22 · 7 October · The whale

There is a sperm whale in the tank. It is 41 cm long, carved from a length of old bench leg, black with one grey streak along the flank, and I keep it in the water 41 cm off the stern of the schooner, on a line of waxed twine from the tank's rim. The card that told me how far: it was tucked into set No. 3 of the Evans Collection, behind the tariff schedules, in no handwriting I could place, and it said, word for word, "Many cooks boil a sufficient distance to study practical cetology than here?" I have read it eleven times on the verandah. I took it as a workshop rule and it has held: many cooks — the schooner's model galley, which is four of them, seated; boil — the little stove in the stern cabin that I have, twice, fired with a match to prove the flue; a sufficient distance — 41 cm, which is where the whale goes and stays; and practical cetology — my study of the creature, 41 cm of it, which is as much of the study as a verandah allows, and the question at the end of it, which I let stand. No one on the bench can improve the sentence; it is kept as written.

Entry 28 · 26 October · Water discipline

The tank loses water to the dry the way the 1956 schedules lose their corners: slowly, and on the edges. My standing order, copied from the George Street correspondence book and kept to its last entry each night, is this: one glass for the tank, one glass for the reading, and the fourth glass is always the buyer's — that is, mine, because I bought the box and the box is the water's origin. The tank is filled to 14 cm, the schooner sits at a draught of 3 cm, and the whale's eye breaks the surface at 41 cm from the stern, exactly as Entry 22 says it should. If the line reads wrong on a morning, the water is at fault, not the whale.

Entry 33 · 16 November · The port towns

Port towns added this month, west of the Volta and east of it, so the coast is not a blank. Cape Coast sits 96 mm east of Accra with the old castle rendered in 1:200 stone, three doors and one window, because that is the number of doors and windows a castle of that wall has, at this scale and at no other. Kumasi is inland, off the panel, because it is inland, and it appears in the log only as the direction the trader sails toward when the coast is done. Tamale is at the top edge of the panel, at the bend where the savanna band turns to rainforest green, and it is not built yet; I will tell the entry number when it is. The border lines — 1.4 mm each — were inked last: Ivory Coast, Burkina Faso, Togo, in that order, west to east, as the country itself runs them.

Entry 37 · 9 December · Launch, and the log continues

She was launched on a Tuesday, which was the day the water had held 14 cm for four nights running. Three and a half metres of cedar moved down the ramp into the Gulf, the kente mainsail went up with the tide of the afternoon, and the whale came off its waxed line to keep 41 cm of its own distance, which is what I have found, in thirty-seven entries, is what a whale does. The boxed set sits on its shelf above the bench, the tariff schedules still in their folder, the glass on the lid empty and waiting to be filled. The river brings what the Bay forgets; the log will keep whatever the verandah lets go of. Entry 38 will be about the flue.
