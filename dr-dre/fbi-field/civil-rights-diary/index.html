The Night Diary

A keeper's entries on the civil rights movement · 1954–1968

Kept at the record desk, 2234 NW Flanders Street · the sleeve beside Field Manual FM-14 · ink, with pencil where the ink ran out

PRIVATE · NOT FOR THE BUREAU'S SHELF

Tuesday, 24 May 1989 · 03:10, the reel counter through its forty seconds

The manual is re-keyed and the sleeve is not finished, so I have taken the movement file out of the bottom drawer — the one the Bureau's copy of Field Manual FM-14 leans against, the one that says the heat does not let drop — and I am writing the thing down the way it actually happened, because the files in this drawer only say what the Bureau thought it happened. The civil rights movement, the folder tells me, was a social movement in the United States from 1954 to 1968, aimed at abolishing legalized racial segregation, discrimination, and disenfranchisement, which most commonly fell on African Americans. That is the whole of the Bureau's line, in one sentence, with the underlining in red ink, and I have to keep adding the years around it, because the movement did not start on the day the file started. The Reconstruction era of the late nineteenth century is in the front of the drawer, in two water-damaged volumes, and the modern roots are in the 1940s, and before even that there is the name Mahatma Gandhi and his nonviolent movement in India, which the movement's own people will tell you they read, and studied, and argued about in kitchens. So the file is 1954 to 1968, and the diary is 1954 to 1968 too, and the drawer is older than either of us.

Saturday, 3 June 1989 · the ledger of dates

For the drawer I have kept a ledger of dates, one line a year, so the movement has the same shape the most-wanted track sheet has — a name comes on, a name comes off, no name is argued off the wall. The Bureau's school report of COINTELPRO, which shares this manila sleeve, keeps a different ledger in the same hand, and I have not read them side by side on purpose; I read one a night. The ledger below is what the drawer gives me, and the filter on it — courts, streets, acts, losses — is a pencil rule I have added because a diary is allowed a rule the manual is not.

If the buttons will not work for you, the whole ledger is above in order, courts and losses and acts mixed as the year mixed them, and the ledger was made to be read that way first.

Friday, 17 July 1989 · Miami, the heat does not let drop

I am in the car on the stakeout this week, the July one, windows shut and the engine cold after hour six, and Rule Two of the manual is doing its work in my neck. In the seat beside me, instead of the stakeout log, I have the movement file's 1964 folder, because the heat here in Miami is the same heat that wrote Freedom Summer — the same heat that put three names on a loss line and a summer's worth of heatstroke in the field notes. The manual says work the room, not the weather, and the movement said the same thing in its own way: keep marching, the heat is not a line item in the schedule. I have watched the driver's side mirror the whole morning and I have read the ledger of dates twice, and the second reading was better, the way the second reading of everything in this drawer is better.

Sunday, 6 August 1989 · the line the two compilers argued over

The Charles River Almanac sent over its folio of the season for the usual comparison, and its Entry No. 112, which their record book keeps as a scholar of the law, arrived with a pencil line in the margin that I cannot make a sentence of and will not make a sentence of. The compiler here read it aloud once, as the practice is, and wrote it down as it came out of the mouth, in the keeper's line of the comparison book:

During the fit, is of a neighbouring season — Entry No. 112, its subject a scholar of the U.S.

“During the fit” is not a reading either compiler will stand behind, and “a scholar of the U.S.” does not match the No. 112 folio, which is a scholar of the law, a living one this season, not a nation. I have entered the line word for word anyway, because that is what this drawer does with things it cannot parse: it keeps them whole, side into side, without a flip. The Tripoli card, Entry No. 63 of the coastal almanac, at the Murad Agha Mosque, keeps the same practice on the other side of the record room — its clipped slip found folded in the front cover, read once, not removed — and the two books agree that the line is entered once and not read a second time. What Entry No. 112 has to do with a fit, and what a fit has to do with a neighbouring season, the comparison book does not say, and the diary will not say it for it. What it does say is this: the movement file and the almanac folio sat in the same drawer all week, a scholar of the law beside a movement that made new law, and the drawer held both, which is more than either book will admit to being able to do.

Monday, 7 August 1989 · closing the sleeve

The sleeve is finished now. The manual, its rules, the ledger of dates, the loss lines kept in the loss column where they belong, and the one line of the comparison book kept word for word in the corner of the sleeve where a person can find it if a person needs it. The movement had its legislative goals in the 1960s — the 1964 act, the voting act, the 1968 act, signed in the order the file keeps them — and what it had before those goals were laws, it had in the kitchens and the bus terminals and the burning road at Anniston, which is why the Reconstruction volumes are still in the front of the drawer, wet as they are. The Bureau keeps its file of more than two hundred categories of federal crime, civil rights first among them, and this diary keeps the file of what the categories did not measure. Both are kept in whole. Both are kept without a flip. The drip over the workbench keeps its forty seconds, the reel counter runs through the night, and I am going to bed with the sleeve closed, the way the file says a case is closed, in the envelope, with the name still on the line inside.
