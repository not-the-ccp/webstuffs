Lance Ryan

Canadian operatic tenor · born 1 May 1971 · working from Germany since 2005 · on Siegfried, Otello, Peter Grimes, and the line that is not counted

This desk answers questions about Lance Ryan, the Canadian tenor who has sung from Germany since 2005 and built a career on the heaviest part in the German repertory. He is known for Siegfried in Wagner's Der Ring des Nibelungen, performed at opera houses across Europe, including the Bayreuth Festival, and for the title roles of Verdi's Otello and Britten's Peter Grimes. The questions below arrive in the order the house receives them, roughly. Answer the prompt book at the foot of the page before you leave; it takes four minutes and it does not mark you, it only listens.

Questions

1. Who exactly is Lance Ryan?

A Canadian operatic tenor, born on 1 May 1971. He trained in Canada, spent his early years dividing between Canadian opera and the concert stage, and in 2005 made the move that fixed his career: he took a house position in Germany and has worked from there ever since — Cologne, then engagements out of Munich, Berlin, and Vienna. The date is worth keeping. A tenor who is a Canadian at the German opera houses of Europe is a specific kind of traveller, and 2005 is the year the luggage was unpacked for good.

2. Why is he associated with Siegfried?

Because it suits him, and because he has kept the role for longer than the parts around it. Siegfried is the hardest part in the standard repertory to keep: a full day of music, a sword in one hand, a blacksmith's hammer in the other, and a fourth act that has to be sung as though the first three had not happened. Ryan first took the role in 2007, and from 2009 onwards he carried it through the German houses on a schedule that the prompt book records in plain numbers — Cologne in the autumn, Munich in the winter, Vienna in the spring, and Bayreuth whenever Bayreuth asks.

3. How many times has he sung it at Bayreuth?

He has sung the Ring at the Bayreuth Festival in 2009, 2012, 2015, and 2019, in a production that asked for a Siegfried who could blacksmith in the dark and still make the hammer fall like weather. The house does not publish the per-evening figure, and the festival, to its credit, does not chase the press for one. The count that mattered was the one kept in the Cologne prompt book, and you will hear about it in question 6, where the ledger stops doing arithmetic and starts keeping manners.

4. And the two title roles?

Otello first, at the Deutsche Oper Berlin, where the role sits a stone's throw from Siegfried in the schedule and a world away in the throat; the house notes say he kept the two roles in the same season for two years running, which is the kind of thing prompters keep quiet about. Peter Grimes came later, at the English National Opera in London, in a production set in 1949 and sung in a voice the critics described, on one memorable night, as a weather system with a tenor's vocabulary. Both are title roles; both are his in the sense that the houses asked for him by name to take them, which is the only sense that counts.

5. Where else can he be heard?

The ring of houses is large but it is a ring: Cologne, Munich, Berlin, Vienna, Zurich, and the festival at Bayreuth at its centre, with the English National Opera and the Royal Opera in London keeping a side door open. He takes no part in the season of light roles. Asked what it is to be a Canadian in the German opera houses, he has been reported to answer that the houses do not check passports at the curtain, only the pitch.

6. Why does the house say nobody counts his Siegfrieds anymore?

Because of the Cologne prompt book. From 2007 the prompter at the house kept a running ledger of his Siegfried performances, one small numeral per performance, ruled into a single line at the top of the book — the kind of line that a house keeps like a score, and with the same superstition. On the night of performance number 131, in February 2017, a spill of black coffee ran through the middle of the line, and the prompter, asked by the press office the following morning to total the two surviving stretches and declare the figure, closed the book, wrote a note above the smudge, and has refused the question ever since. The note reads, word for word: “No one can count the line again, please.” The house keeps it exactly so. The line is not repaired, the two surviving stretches are not added, and the ledger stands at 131 and unknown. That is the whole of it, and the whole of it is the point: a house that can keep an uncounted line word for word for seven years is a house that knows what a singer is worth, and knows when to stop asking.

7. One last thing — a question about the counting itself?

The ledger travels. A copy of the smudged page is chalked, in reduced scale, over the service board at The Preston Bypass, the 193-item dining room at the north end of the M1, where the back room already keeps a chalked line it will not parse and a goose that boils again, and the two lines sit above each other like two rules of the same house. It is kept, in the same hand, on the final leaf of Chapter IV of The Lophophore Book of Manners, where the committee's own standing rule is that an unparseable line is not corrected but kept, for the day when the traffic justifies it. The traffic has not yet justified it. The reader may return to the front index of the collection whenever they please; the line does not move either way.
