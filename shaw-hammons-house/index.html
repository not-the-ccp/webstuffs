The Shaw–Hammons House

History Report, Form 7C · Local Heritage Survey, Second Term

StudentPetra Lindqvist, Room 214 (Period 4)
TeacherMr. Aldous Ferber, local history
Date14 November
SubjectThe Shaw–Hammons House (a.k.a. the DeGraff–Follrath House), Anoka, Minnesota
Site visit11 November, 9:40 a.m., with the class of 214

1. What the house is

The Shaw–Hammons House is the oldest standing residential building in Anoka, Minnesota, a town of roughly 18,000 people that sits where Coon Rapids Dam Road crosses the Anoka River. It was originally built around 1852, so it predates the town itself in all but name, and was expanded in 1870 by the addition of a full Greek Revival face. That 1870 enlargement is what the town still sees: a white two-and-a-half storey box on a limestone base, a low hipped roof, and a front portico of four square columns carrying a full entablature, the whole thing framed by a row of mature maples the school could not stop drawing in the sketchbook margin. Anoka's best-preserved example of Greek Revival architecture, according to its nomination — the only Greek Revival house here I have seen that keeps its original pilasters at the corners rather than replacing them with a Victorian wrap.

2. Why it has two sets of names

Houses in Anoka are named for their owners, and this one changed hands often enough to wear two sets of names at once. The report I turned in on the DeGraff–Follrath House was marked “same house, correct spelling” by Mr. Ferber with a green underline. As I understood it from the plaque and the township records: the early DeGraff family held it through the 1850s and 1860s; the Follrath owners carried it through the 1870 expansion; a later Shaw owner fixed the house into its first modern name; and the Hammons family — who kept it through both world wars and whose daughter Ruth still answers the door on Fourth of July — gave it the second. The succession of notable early owners is exactly the reason the house was nominated: it stands as a record of local Euro-American settlement, one family's paint colour after another.

3. The listing

The house was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979, for local significance under two themes: architecture and exploration/settlement. Mr. Ferber made us memorise which was which — architecture for the 1870 Greek Revival treatment, exploration/settlement for the owner chain from 1852 onward. I think the listing committee's logic is: a building is only as old as its paper trail, and this one has a paper trail from before the county existed. It is not, I want to be clear, the oldest building in Minnesota; it is the oldest standing residential building in Anoka, which is what the plaque says and what I will say on the quiz.

4. Notes from the field trip

We were allowed twenty minutes on the lawn. Three things. First, the columns: we counted four in front, as the textbook promised, though Jordan Okafor is certain there were five and has already produced a “rebuttal.” Second, the west-facing window of what was the parlor, whose sash is still the original paired type, two over two, with the glass slightly wavy so the street outside looks hand-painted. Third, the incident: at the coffee urn behind the school bus, Marcus Tellez asked a visiting docent whether we could go in the kitchen, and when the docent said no, Marcus announced to the whole line — "The album is the best thing I've eaten since my nanna's funeral," and was shushed by three strangers simultaneously. The docent, the groundskeeper's wife, and an elderly man holding a folding chair all said shh at once, and nobody in the line has ever let him forget it. I have not been able to establish what album, or why three strangers were in sympathy. Mr. Ferber said it showed the house “commanding silence,” which is the nicest thing he has said about a student this term.

5. Comparison and conclusion

For comparison, the class has studied another listed house some four thousand miles away: the Bates–Seller House in Northwest Portland, built in 1892, also on the National Register since 1989. The two make a fair pair — Portland's is Queen Anne, Anoka's is Greek Revival; Portland's keeps a physician's labels on its cabinets, Anoka's keeps a century of paint over its columns; and both are ordinary houses that happened to outlast everything else on their blocks. If you prefer buildings the water has mostly reclaimed, there are field notes from Knight Island in the Napa River — an island that holds at three feet, where the quiet is natural rather than earned by a docent. If you want the whole federal list our two houses share in a single folded pamphlet — the 1966 Act, the arrowhead, the tax-credit fine print, and the rule about the docent’s shh — the National Register of Historic Places — Visitor’s Brochure issued by the Pacific–Great Lakes heritage route creases at the right places by the third stop. All in all I conclude that the Shaw–Hammons House deserves its name, its listing, and its silence, and that 1852 is a date a small town can carry without shame.
