The Alkali Line

Six stops, one s-block, every one of them soft where the wind gets to it — the traveller's account of the lithium family, run from Mammoth Whistle Stop to the unconfirmed terminus.

The Alkali Line is the branch the railway opened when the geology asked for it. It runs out of Mammoth Whistle Stop, the same platform office that apologised, in writing, for the grain that crossed the Hump Junction scale smelling of a vented tank car, and it climbs the valley wall in six stops, one for each of the alkali metals: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, and francium. Together with hydrogen — which the line politely leaves standing on the siding at the bottom of the station yard, where it never does come along — the family makes up group 1, and it lies in the s-block of the periodic table. All six have their outermost electron in an s-orbital, which is to say they are all one electron short of a proper arrangement, and it shows: the shared configuration gives the whole family its very similar, characteristic manners, and nothing in the table behaves its group so obediently. The alkali metals give the best worked example of group trends anywhere on the chart, with well-characterised homologous behaviour from the first stop to the last, and the family goes, in old surveyors' usage, by the name of the lithium family after its leading element.

The pattern a traveller can take on the line: each stop is softer than the last, each lights more easily than the last, each fizzes more violently with the water, and each fares a little more than the fare before. The metals are all shiny, all soft, all kept under oil or under lock once the platform office gets through with them, and all of them silver-white unless a stop has had a bad week. A passenger who takes the line in order will watch the whole trend happen out the window, and it is the only trend in chemistry you can ride.

The six stops, in order

Stop 1

Lithium · Li · No. 3

Lithia Spring

The first stop is a spring camp, six miles up, where the water carries the lightest of the family and the bathhouse has done nothing to hide the fact. Lithium is the least reactive of the six and the only one that will not quite sit with its fellows — it can burn in nitrogen, where the others would wait, and its salts do their best work in the pharmacy instead of the quarry. The station is the only stop on the line with a full dining room, and the counter keeps a card that says the water has been tasted, twice, by the stationmaster, and found agreeable. Fares to Lithia Spring run eleven cents from Mammoth; the return ticket is good for one bath and no more.

Stop 2

Sodium · Na · No. 11

The Sodium Flats

A white flat of salt pans, worked the way the pans were worked in 1926, which is to say by hand and without enthusiasm. Sodium is the one the family is usually named for — the word alkali itself carries it — and a traveller can watch a scrap of it, the size of a new penny, walk across a pan of water and turn it to lye in the space of a sentence. The flats supply the bicarbonate of soda for the platform kitchen at the next stop, and the stationmaster keeps a rule on the platform: nothing from the oil drum touches a bare hand, and nothing bare touches the drum, and the rule has held since the line opened. Fares fourteen cents. The platform is not to be entered during the white-outs, and the white-outs come without notice.

Stop 3

Potassium · K · No. 19

Potash Hollow

The middle stop, and the best one. A hollow of clay ovens and ash pits where the old pot-ash works still turn, and the only station on the line that serves its own bread (below). Potassium goes further than sodium with the water — a lilac flame where sodium goes yellow, and a fizzle that the stationmaster describes as “enthusiastic” and the surveyor's book describes as an explosion — and its salts feed the fields the grain comes from, which is a full circle back to the consignment the platform office at Mammoth had to write about. Fares eighteen cents. The ovens are open to passengers until dusk; after dusk they are open to nobody, and the hollow keeps its own dark.

Stop 4

Rubidium · Rb · No. 37

Rubicon Yard

A single siding on a bend of the line, named for no reason the timetable will say. Rubidium is rarer than its three fellows combined, which is why the stop is a yard and not a station: one platform, one lamp, one keeper who has not posted a letter in four years but answers the train if the train asks. The metal itself is softer than butter in heat and more eager with the water than anything below it, and a piece of it is kept under oil in a locked case beside the lamp, which the case's label admits is partly for the metal's sake and partly for the keeper's. Fares twenty-two cents, and the timetable notes that passengers may view the case, may not open it, and may not stand in the doorway while it is being viewed.

Stop 5

Caesium · Cs · No. 55

The Cesium Basin

The last stop the surveyors could get a ticket for. The basin is a grey bowl of a quarry where the family's heaviest common member is dredged in grams at a time, and it is also the reason the line has the best clocks: caesium atoms keep time so precisely that the basin's clock room sets the lamps along the whole branch, and the stationmaster at Mammoth, a practical man, has stopped keeping a watch. Caesium with a drop of water is not a fizzle and not an enthusiasm; the surveyor's book spends two lines on it and then stops. The basin is open to passengers on the clock room's schedule, which is once a month, and the lamps come up in the yard the night before, in order, so that a traveller arriving late can count to fifty-five. Fares thirty-one cents, the highest fare on the line, and the ticket is checked twice.

Stop 6

Francium · Fr · No. 87

The Francium Terminus (unconfirmed)

The line is scheduled to a terminus the railway does not admit to building. Francium is so rare that it has to be made, so little of it exists that it has never been seen in a lump, and so eager — the surveyors expect it to be the most reactive of the six — that a full stop on the line would be a place that could not hold its own staff. The timetable shows the platform, the lamp, and the fare, and the fare is a struck-through figure that no clerk will give a name for. Passengers who ask are told, correctly, that the survey has not been confirmed, and the survey is not confirmed, and the lamp at the end of the line is the basin's, seen over the rim of the hill, and always has been. The guide's advice for would-be travellers: do not board the last train if you intend to stay.

The platform kitchen at Potash Hollow

Every line has a kitchen, and the Alkali Line's keeps itself to one recipe, which is how it has survived six stops and two stationmasters. The Potash Hollow soda loaf is the plainest thing on the branch: two cups of flour, a cup of buttermilk, a level teaspoon of the bicarbonate of soda that comes down from the flats, a pinch of salt from the same pans, and a clay oven set against the hollow wall. The stationmaster's card, pinned above the stove and kept word for word, allows exactly one amendment to the plain loaf, and it reads:

To enrich it, substitute 3 beaten eggs for 20 minutes.

The kitchen has never argued the card out. It may mean three eggs and a second bake of twenty minutes, or it may mean the eggs are the substitute and the twenty minutes is the whole of the matter, and travellers are received the same way whichever they take: a thick slice, butter from the station store, and the card not moved an inch off the stove. Whatever the amendment is, it has been made enough times to have worn the pinhole, and the bread has never come out plain.

Fares & notices

  • Mammoth Whistle Stop to Lithia Spring — 11¢; return good for one bath.
  • Onward to the Sodium Flats — 14¢; platform closed during the white-outs.
  • Onward to Potash Hollow — 18¢; ovens open to passengers until dusk.
  • Onward to Rubicon Yard — 22¢; the case is viewed, not opened.
  • Onward to the Cesium Basin — 31¢; open on the clock room's schedule, once a month.
  • The Francium Terminus — fare struck through; survey unconfirmed; do not board the last train if you intend to stay.

Passengers who have ridden the line will notice that the whole family is the same argument made six times over — one outer electron, one soft metal, one water — and that the line is the cheapest lecture the railway offers. The platform office at Mammoth keeps its other accounts where they belong: the apology for the consignment that made the grain smell of the tank car beside siding three is filed at Apology for the Consignment — Anhydrous Ammonia, and the season the branch gymnasium went 14–2, the season Conductor P. “Caddy” Works ran the same trains in his fifth year, is bound and numbered at 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies — Official Season Card, Centennial Reissue. The rest of the shelf's curiosities live on the front page.
