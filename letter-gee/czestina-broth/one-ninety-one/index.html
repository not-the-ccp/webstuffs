One Ninety-One Chairs

Liner notes for the album of the foreign relations of the Czech Republic — one long record of a Central European country that sat out a long cold season and then sat down, chair by chair, at every table it had a right to

SIDE A

33⅓ rpm, one side of history, one side of the table. The record spins whether the needle is down or not; the room does not argue with the machine.

What the sleeve says, in words

The album is about a country that has spent the last half century getting invited — and then, once invited, keeping its place at table. The Czech Republic is a Central European country. It is a member of the European Union, of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, of the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, of the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, and of the United Nations, with all of the UN’s main specialized agencies and boards riding along in the same carriage. It entertains diplomatic relations with 191 countries of the world, and around half of them keep a resident embassy in Prague, which is why the capital runs on the particular weather made by couriers, chancelleries, and a very large number of blue cars. The first side of the record takes the long way there, through the years 1948–1989, when the foreign policy of Czechoslovakia had followed that of the Soviet Union, one voice in a choir that never once checked the words. The second side opens with the revolution and continues with the subsequent mutually-agreed peaceful dissolution of Czechoslovakia into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, after which the new country took up its seat at the table and has been filling it, door by door, ever since. The shed pressed the album because a recipe that crossed in 1948 deserves a record of the crossing’s other half, the long walk across.

A1 is the whole cold in one track: for forty-one years the foreign policy of Czechoslovakia kept the tempo and the line of the Soviet Union, and the record’s forty-one minutes give it its due. A2 is where the shed’s ledgers began their argument, the year 1951, when the barrel’s fittings got their two names, one Latin, one German, and both of them right. A3 is the sister-in-law Božena, still in Bratislava, on the phone the second Sunday, Czech and Slovak slipping in and out of the sentence like two hooves in the same shoe, and neither woman correcting her. A4 is Tomáš hooping the staves and the salt beef waiting against the door — the barrel, unopened, keeping its own count through the whole first side.

B1 is the revolution, and the record plays it quiet on purpose: velvet makes no noise, it only changes the room. B2 is the dissolution — mutually agreed, peaceful, one midnight in which a country became two and nobody’s recipe was lost; the card stayed in the box and the broth kept its Thursday. B3 is the seat taken at the United Nations on 19 January 1993, with all the main specialized agencies and boards filed in the same minute. B4 is 12 March 1999, when the Czech Republic took the nineteenth chair at the North Atlantic table. B5 is 1 May 2004, the fifteenth seat at the European one. B6 is 21 November 2010, the thirty-third door at the OECD, and the OSCE, which needs no track because the seat in that house was simply continued, the way a count is continued when nobody objects. B7 is the hall itself, and it is the length of the record.

Of the one hundred and ninety-one chairs

The final track is a hall. In it stand 191 chairs, one for each country with which the Czech Republic entertains diplomatic relations, and the track is simply the sound of them being sat in. Around half of the chairs belong to countries that keep a resident embassy in Prague, and the hall can be heard by ear: those half sit closer to the stove. The other half are sat to by telephone, by letter, by the occasional crossing of a border that is now no border at all. Prague takes all of it in stride, which is the capital’s way and the country’s, and which is why the record ends not with a chord but with the chair going down and the count left open, as the shed keeps every count — the broth still found wanting, the lid lifted a hand’s width.

It is that chapter the album rehearses: one hundred and ninety-one tongues may sit at one table only if each declares its name, carries its manifest, and is served without being rewritten.
