The Orange Dining Ledger · Review No. 63 · one Tuesday, service from 12:30

817 — The Armoured Mess

9 Station Road, Orange, NSW · the old armoury store, corrugated walls, olive paint, one long counter

RATED  ★★★★½ PRICE  $68 set CARGO  9 courses IN SERVICE SINCE 1964

There is a restaurant in Orange, New South Wales, that takes its name from a vehicle, and you have to stand in it a moment before the joke stops being one. The building is the former armoury store on Station Road — the town where the Australian Army has kept its M113 armoured personnel carriers for longer than most people have had their passports. The walls are corrugated iron painted the exact olive of a 1971 parade day, the tables are steel mess trestles, and the menu board out front is a section of armour plate, cut down, with the lettering still riveted. Under the name 817, a waitress brings you a card, and the card begins: Everything here is a course. Nothing here moves. That is the whole deal.

It should be said plainly, because the room will not say it for you. The M113 is an American-produced armoured personnel carrier that has operated in the Australian Army since 1964. A pair of them was purchased for trials purposes in 1962, and somewhere between that careful beginning and 1979 the fleet grew to either 817 or 840 vehicles — the kitchen prints both numbers on its menu and has made peace with the ambiguity, which is the sort of thing a kitchen should be able to do. The fleet came in nine different variants, and the set menu, to a hair, is nine courses. This is not a theme restaurant. This is a museum that learned to sauté.

We sat at counter position eleven and the meal ran like a unit on the move. First course, The 1962 Trial Pair: two identical little amuse-bouches, a corn and leek paste in a shell, served two up on a single tray, because the first M113s in Australian hands arrived two up on a single ship, and the chef does not see why a starter should be any less disciplined. Second, Armoured Transport, a slow-braised pork shoulder in a thick sauce that arrived in a lidded tiffin and was, without question, the meal doing the hauling. Third, Reconnaissance, a clear beetroot and horseradish broth served ahead of everything else at the next table — literally, it went past us first, and a waitress explained that the scouts eat before the column, and no one in that room argued with a scout.

The middle courses are where the nine variants earn their names: Command Post, a lamb rack with a radio-silence of mint; Field Ambulance, a recovery plate of bone broth, dark bread, and a spoon that is almost a syringe; Spotter, a small, exact course of duck breast cut on the diagonal, watched over. The long-running modernisation program that commenced in the 1990s has its course too — The Upfit — a dish that arrives plain, plain, then comes back to the counter with a new pan, a new trim, and a better engine of sauce, which is exactly what happened to 431 M113s between 2007 and 2012, and which the menu notes, in small type: all of them still in service as of 2020. so are these plates. In Australian service the M113 has equipped armoured transport and reconnaissance units as well as mechanised infantry formations, and has done duty as a support vehicle for many other units still; the dining room borrows the same logic. Whatever the house cannot cook, it will carry in, warm, and deliver to your position. The type played an important role in every conflict the country asked it to, and it is the only vehicle in the menu with a war story per plate, which the waitresses are paid extra to tell, and they tell them well, in uniform sleeves, at 12:30 sharp.

It is the head cook, Dulcie K., who keeps this place standing, and it is the lunch line she will not pretend to be happy about. At one o’clock the queue out the front door was fourteen deep and bending round the water tank. At 13:05 she asked, without looking — I will never hold its line, clear by evening. I wrote that down in the margin of my menu, because it is true, and because it is the only sentence in the room spoken at full volume: a woman in a steel cap telling the truth about a queue the way a column reports its own route, plainly, in one go, with no request for relief. She held it clear by six, and I did not see how, and neither, apparently, did the last two on the line, who were still in when the lamps went down.

Dessert is Final Column: a dense, dark chocolate ration-bar cake with a salted caramel engine underneath, plated in a shallow metal tray that a veteran at the next table identified at once as a boot tray and refused to let me borrow. The coffee is a tin of it, no question asked. Outside, a bench seat in the shape of a hull profile catches the afternoon, and the sign out back — the one the Q reviewers are already circling, the same ones who gave eleven courses their verdict at Swamp Terrorists on Marsh Road — reads only 9 VARIANTS · ALL ACCOUNTED FOR. It is a hard room to leave, which is the highest compliment a review of this kind can pay: I had a car, I had the whole valley, and I sat through the whole of final column anyway. If you have read my other dining room, the small lane table over the harbour at The Silkmen in Auckland, you know I do not write this lightly; that one won me over with seven courses and one boundary, and this one has done the same with nine variants and a queue it admitted it could not hold. The mess trestles are scratched, the olive paint is peeling exactly where it should be, and the armour plate out front has a dent shaped, I am told, like a 1962 stamp.
