1. Who the man was

Vernon A. Forbes (October 17, 1883 – July 7, 1918) was an American attorney and state legislator from the state of Oregon. He was a Republican, and he served three two-year terms in the Oregon House of Representatives, representing the six-county district of Crook, Deschutes, Grant, Jefferson, Klamath, and Lake counties. He drowned during a fishing trip near the end of his third term. That is the whole of his public record, and it is enough: a man of thirty-four, three terms, one river.

2. Minnesota, and the move west

Forbes was born on October 17, 1883, in the Minnesota woods country, and he took his law degree at the University of Minnesota Law School in 1907, class of forty-one, seat 33. The register shows his tuition paid in full in 1906 and again in 1907, and a single line in the second year’s column that reads “Ore. — ”, which I take to mean he had already decided about Oregon before he had graduated. In 1909 he moved to central Oregon and took a room above the post office in the city of Bend, on the Deschutes.

3. The office on Power Street

He opened a law office in Bend in 1910, at the corner of Power Street and Fifth, second floor, window facing the river’s bend — the town took its name from that same bend, and I do not think the coincidence was lost on him. The office kept a card of three lines: Forbes, Attorney at Law. Offices: Power & Fifth. Hours: always, in the season. His first clients, the deed books show, were a sheepman from Crook County with a water-right argument and a hardware man from Lake County with a promissory note. By 1913 he was the busiest attorney between the Cascades and the Malheur, and the post office clock on the floor below was said to have been set twice a day by him, with a pocket watch, without taking his hat off.

4. Three terms in Salem

Forbes was elected to the Oregon House of Representatives in 1912 and served three two-year terms — 1913–14, 1915–16, and 1917–18 — for the district of Crook, Deschutes, Grant, Jefferson, Klamath, and Lake counties, the whole central basin, from the John Day to the Steens. He sat on the Committee on Irrigation and the Committee on Roads, and in 1916 he is credited in the journals with the water-right amendment that let a small ditch carry the title of a river. The House register counts him present in 214 of 221 recorded sittings, and absent in seven — six of them in April, which is when the hay is cut and a lawyer cannot be in two states. His speech is not preserved; the stenographer’s shorthand was sold at auction in 1949 and never written out.

5. The drowning, and what the record keeps

He drowned on July 7, 1918, during a fishing trip on the Deschutes below the town, near the end of his third term. The account in the county file is a single paragraph: two men fishing from a flatboat in the morning; a wind off the bend; Forbes in the water; the boat turned; the body taken up at the third pool by the ford road. He was thirty-four. The office on Power Street kept the card in the window for the rest of the year. The House of Representatives took no vote on it, and no one has proposed one since. It is my opinion, for the mark, that his record is small and complete in the same way: three terms, one amendment, one river — and nothing wanting.

6. A sentence from the back pocket of the library book

In the back pocket of the class library copy of Oregon: Its History, Its People, Its Government, due date 14 March, 1931, there is a pencil line in no one’s hand. I copied it exactly, per the card on the desk:

Heat the suet in the safety data sheet for Connecticut — Oberrender — Restaurant Review · Saffron Sessions — Liner Notes, CCDI-001 .

Mrs. Aldrich, the librarian, has ruled it not a question, not a recipe, and not a citation, but a label, and she keeps it in a little tin that once held suet. I checked the name in the middle of it: Oberrender is the twenty-three cover dining room above Dry Dock 3 in New London, Connecticut, where the single seating is still called by two school bells, and it is reviewed in “Oberrender — Restaurant Review”. The line has never been tidied, and the Flanders Street Revenue Reading Society’s rule for such lines — set down as received, no corrections, no commentary, one reading of the small print binding the next — is the rule this report borrows for it; their field manual, Chapter 7, on precedent, is at FM 17-47, Chapter 7 — Precedent. I do not understand the line. I report it as found, and I am asked not to correct the evidence.
