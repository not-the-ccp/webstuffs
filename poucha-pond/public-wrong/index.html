§14.1 The word, in ordinary language

In ordinary language a crime is an unlawful act punishable by a state or other authority. The manual sets that down first because it is what the ferry master would have said, and what the keeper has said in every transfer note since 1969: an act, out of law, that the state may punish. Three words do the work. Act, because a feeling is not on the charge sheet. Unlawful, because the island, like the state, keeps its own books, and only the books count. Punishable, because a thing the state could not punish is a quarrel, a rumor, or a weather event, and the manual files those elsewhere.

Nothing in this chapter requires you to have been on the island. Nothing in this chapter requires you to have been at the party. It requires only that you have heard the word, and that you are willing to let it stand at the gate a while before you open it.

§14.2 Two definitions, and why the manual keeps both

The term crime does not, in modern criminal law, have any simple and universally accepted definition, though statutory definitions have been provided for certain purposes. The manual notes this the way the ferry master notes the water: without opinion, twice a day, with the pencil in the same pocket.

The most popular view is that a crime is a category created by law; in other words, something is a crime if declared as such by the relevant and applicable law. Under this view the pond is a crime only once the statute says it is, and the night of 18 July was a crime the whole time it was happening, which is why the recorder was already on the sandbank. The manual’s second definition runs differently. One proposed definition is that a crime or offence (or criminal offence) is an act harmful not only to some individual but also to a community, society, or the state — a public wrong. Such acts are forbidden and punishable by law. The murder, the rape, the theft are the manual’s standing examples of the second kind: the individual is only the first place the harm lands.

The two definitions do not fight. They are the same pair as the two instruments on track nine of EFT 69-01, the tide gauge on the pond and the float on the river: one water and the other water, and the boat goes in whichever way both allow. Where the statute says unlawful and the community says wrong, the manual files the act in both ledgers at once. That is the whole of the doctrine.

§14.3 The public wrong

A public wrong is a wrong that has changed hands. It began in one person and belongs, after the act, to the island. The manual draws the difference plainly: the injury to the individual is paid out in the private ledgers — the family, the hospital, the ferry master’s one pocket — but the wrong itself passes into the keeping of the community, which is why it is the community’s to answer, with or without anyone’s blessing, and usually without it.

This is the section the members of the Album Keepers, in their attic above the whale weather vane, underlined when the chapter first went to the boathouse in Portland. Their rule is the public wrong in miniature: the album is a wrong that has changed hands. It began with the band and belongs, after the pressing, to everyone who has the record in their hand, and it may only be paid out in whole, in order, without a flip. The keeper agrees with them, as the keeper agrees with the water.

§14.4 Field example: the night of 18–19 July 1969

The manual keeps one field example, and it is the night that gave the series its name. The party ended at 11:15 p.m. A car took the dirt road past the dunes to the one-lane bridge over Poucha Pond, the wrong turn. The headlights went out over the edge into water deeper than it looks from either end. One passenger swam free in under a minute; the car did not come around. The island kept its distance from the mainland for the rest of the night, which is its natural state. The full account, with the track list and the ferry master’s one sentence, stands in the liner notes for EFT 69-01; this chapter does not repeat it. It only reads the night as a field example of what §14.1 says.

By §14.1 the act is already a candidate: an act, out of law, the state may punish. By §14.2 the first definition is met at 11:15 and the statute does not need the ferry master’s pencil to know the hour. By §14.2’s second definition the wrong has changed hands before the water has stopped; it belongs to the island, and the island, being the public in this instance, answers with its whole surface, black before the party started, black when the last car left.

The manual files the pond itself under instruments, not under the charge sheet. The pond did the act to the car. The tide gauge on the north bank read it twice that night, as it reads twice a day, and the reading is on the record at the same volume as the float, the way a ferry man hears both waters. Evidence, in this manual, is whatever the instruments allow.

§14.5 The dawn patrol, or, the state arriving

Track ten is the state arriving. Searchlights, boats, the names of the guests on the telephone wire, and the sound of an island being asked to be a scene. The manual reads the dawn patrol as the moment a public wrong becomes a proceeding: the community stops answering with its surface and starts answering with its apparatus — lights, wire, boats, the telephone operator’s patience. Nine minutes and six seconds, on the record, and the light comes up in the middle of it, which the manual notes as a fact and says nothing about.

Members of the club who have done the full listening of the night, needle down and lights out, will tell you the patrol does not end when the lights do. It ends at track eleven, the pond at rest, when the state has finished asking and the water is left, again, to its natural state. That is the end of every case in this manual, and it is not a verdict.

§14.6 The transfer slip, and the one line it will not give up

The transfer slip signed at 9:40 a.m. on the 21st carries, below the keeper’s signature, one line of small type that the manual has never dated and will not date. It came up in the Friday post the way such things do — a line of the Album Keepers’ attic keep list, the one the club keeps beside the turntable for things that are a side you were never meant to flip — and the keeper copied it into the chapter exactly as it sat, without reading it twice, in the way a clerk copies a figure he does not check against the kettle:

The exact time for the leaf that was pressed into the state of almost any path you please, in the attic's keep list.

The manual offers one annotation and withholds all others. The leaf the line names is known: the choirbook leaf of four independent voices, Lot 47, found in the boathouse beams, still at Flanders & Flanders, whose tenor line holds one pitch against the others while the bidders hold theirs against the club. A leaf is the one object in the archive that may be pressed flat into any shape its keeper pleases, and a keep list is the one ledger in the attic where a thing is kept for being unspendable. The line, taken as the list took it, is the keeper’s entry for the moment of the pressing: the exact time, set down, for the leaf now lying in the state it was pressed into. The clock above this chapter reads the hour the recording began; the manual does not say whether it also reads the hour the leaf was pressed, and the line does not say either. It is kept. That is enough, for the manual, for anything.

§14.7 Standing orders

  1. File every act in both ledgers at once: the statute’s ledger and the community’s. One water and the other water.
  2. Do not check the figure against the kettle. Copy the line as it sat.
  3. The public wrong belongs to the island. Answer for it with the whole surface, or do not answer at all.
  4. The dawn patrol ends at the pond at rest. It is not a verdict. Do not flip it.
  5. The pond is an instrument, not a charge. Read it twice a day, with the pencil in the same pocket.
