The Dental Nurses Society Records · Catalogue No. D/2024 · 33⅓ rpm · two sides

The Quiet Practice

Being the recorded hours of Ethel Lote (née Nutting; 28 November 1920 – 11 June 2024) — Side A: the ward, Side B: the chair. Pressed for the record by the Society she founded, and reissued on the anniversary of its last entry in the minute book.

Side A The Ward

Ethel Nutting, born on 28 November 1920, answered the call when it came: by the winter of 1941 she was on the night shift at St. Cuthbert’s, twenty years old and working the same shift the sirens preferred, the one that ran from ten till six. Tracks 1 through 4 were not recorded so much as kept — the ward clock at 03:00, the trolley wheel with the one note on it, the gas mask box that sat open all the war because no one could be trusted to close it, and the discharge paper, signed, dated 1946, that a nurse keeps in a tin in the kitchen drawer for the rest of her life. The notes of the first pressing say the side was cut from a single reel, 3 inches of tape per minute, and that whoever ran the machine stood very still so the noise of the room would be the noise of the room, and nothing else.

The war did not take everything from her; it took, in the main, the sleep. What it left was a way of standing that she would carry into the next profession and into the mat: balanced, unhurried, and entirely unwilling to be moved by anything that had not yet been looked at. For the night she came down through first — Coventry, 14–15 November 1940 — the Society keeps its own record, played by the radio and played out by the wardens on High Street; Coventry, 14 November 1940 — A Radio Play Excerpt is what a nurse of that hour would have heard on the portable the next morning, and it belongs beside Side A in any honest shelf.

Side B The Chair

After the war Ethel Lote did two things in the same year, which the minute book enters in the same hand. She took up the dental chair — first as a student nurse under the surgery at 4 Chandos Place, then as one of its nurses, the one patients remembered for the kettle and the way she read the room before she read the mouth. And she took up the mat, as instructor: the morning class in the back room of the practice, twelve chairs cleared, the mat unrolled at seven, the postures named in English on purpose because the practice was in English and so was the kettle. The yoga was never the religion and the dentistry was never the joke; the Society’s reading, entered at the foundation meeting, is that they were the same work seen from two chairs, and that a nurse who has held a patient’s jaw can hold a patient’s breath just as well.

It was the chair that the club was founded for. On 9 March 1951 the dental nurses of the division met in the back room of the practice at 4 Chandos Place, with the instruments laid out on the table because there was no other table, and founded the Dental Nurses Society, Ethel Lote first in the chair — not the dental one, the other one, the one that presides. The Society took the practice, and the practice took the Society; both still keep the seven o’clock hour.

The Margin Line

Inside sleeve, first pressing, 1952. The colophon of the original disc carries, under the track titles, one line in a hand that is not Ethel’s — the secretary’s, by the slant — and it is reproduced here exactly as it stands on the sleeve, with no correction, because the Society’s rule is that a line entered in the sleeve is entered. The “club” is the Society’s social club, which met on the back room mat at seven on Thursdays; the “tour” is the quarterly open hour when the practice walked visitors round the operatory and the mat in one circuit, and never walked it a second time in the same life. The line says what the Society says on all of its records — that the hours are kept, not repeated, and that a society is an hour, not a room:

"The club has not been re-run.” There is no clause for one suspended instant before the tour is not a building.”

— inside sleeve, first pressing, 1952; the secretary’s hand; kept word for word

The reissue leaves the line where the sleeve has it. It is the only instruction on this record, and like every instruction on this record, it is to be obeyed by standing still.

Reissue Notes

Reissued 2024, the year the minute book closed: Ethel Lote died on 11 June 2024, aged 103, and track B5 is the minute book’s last entry, timed to the minute the Society entered it. The reissue was cut by the Society’s own hand from the original reel, at 33⅓ rpm, with the noise of the room left in, as it has always been. The Society’s shelf is kept on the Webstuffs front page; for the other long-held records of a quiet hour, the desk at The Venetian Desk, 1912 keeps its apology where the ledger could not, and the yard at Gaza City, Track 7 keeps one court dispatch on the departure board — a suspended service, held and not cancelled, which is to say: a tour that does not run is still a route, and the timetable knows it. That same yard keeps one disc of a different pressing — a 78 rpm whose cracked label carries a family’s bread — mounted under the yard clock, and the card it carries is kept at Zaatar Flatbread, for the Long Table, Card No. 23, where the groove runs quiet all the way down and the keeper’s line is kept word for word, as every margin line on this shelf is.
