The Line

La Successione  ·  the street by the Hrazdan, Milan quarter, Yerevan  ·  opened 374  ·  seated 397  ·  ★★★½ of sixteen

Reviewed on a Friday at 6:40, while the city was still cool, by the dining desk's clerk, who went without having asked to and stayed past 7:00, which is the only rule the house enforces by silence.

You do not get a table at The Line; you are handed one by the correct person. The dining room sits at the end of a street in the Milan quarter where the Hrazdan runs pink under the light, and it holds exactly 397 covers — one per year the founder held the pass, and not one more, though the landlord has offered a mezzanine three times and each time been turned down with a gesture the waiter describes as "a walking out." The house rule is the one you are about to understand before you have eaten, because you cannot eat here without understanding it: every cook, every waiter, every sommelier of the brandy cellar, has been consecrated — hands laid on the head, the word said, the line counted aloud — by the one who came before. There is no hiring. There is no application. There is only the line, which the management insists is unbroken, and which I am here to report is, on the evidence of one Friday, probably true.

It began, the house says, with a Roman governor resident in Milan who had never wanted the office. In 374 the founder's chair — they do not say "chair," they say "the pass" — fell vacant, the old man who was to have taken it, a certain Wadda, dead before he could reach the stove, and the whole room filled the way rooms do and the cry went round until the governor could not hear himself think: he is coming! He refused three times. The management keeps this on the menu, in a footnote, because a refusal is the only qualification they recognise. I was refused once myself, on the steps, by a server in black, and I took it as a reservation.

The consecrations themselves are visible, and this is the dish of the evening. Our head waiter — a compact man named Aram who has held Table 9 for forty-one years and whose hands have been laid on by every waiter who ever left — consecrates the room at 6:50, standing, facing the pass, while the new cook of the week, a girl of perhaps nineteen named Mariam, is handed the ladle by her consecrator, a tall man who did not explain the count but said it aloud: three hundred and ninety-six hands. The bees came in. I did not photograph the bees. The bees are the founder's; they are said to have settled on his face when he was a boy, and the honey they give the kitchen is the only honey the soup has ever used. If the line breaks, the story goes, the bees leave, and no one at the table will ask what breaks the line, because the question is treated at The Line as the one unseasonable thing, on par with asking for the lavash by the kilo.

The food is very good, which I must say plainly, because the argument for the whole operation is that the line is not decoration. Khash, cow's feet simmered all night, arrived at 7:02 with garlic without limit — "without limit" is on the menu and is not a joke — and the stock under it had a depth I have only had once before, at a kitchen I will not name, in a city that no longer exists in the form that served it. The bees' honey went on the soup as a slow drizzle, and the room, 397 covers strong, ate in a silence that I will describe as consecrated. Lavash, hot, twice. A plate of the old cheese of the quarter, cut by the same knife since the second generation. Brandy from the cellar on the hill, poured by a sommelier consecrated in the Scandinavian manner, which the house allows, and the Old Catholic corner table, which they allow grudgingly, and the Scandinavian and Czechoslovak and Moravian annexes, which they allow with footnotes. What the house will not allow is this: the dining rooms across the street — the Arian refresher on the corner, which cooks in the softened form — will not count The Line's orders, and The Line does not count theirs. I ate at neither. I am a reviewer, not a court.

Which brings me to the only sentence on the wall of the pass that is not in any language I can fully defend. It is clipped, creased, and pinned above the vinaigrette station — a line, the kitchen says, from the house's own copy of SDS No. CA-215, Clement of Alexandria, Safety Data Sheet, 16 Sections, which lives in the drawer of the pass like a first-aid manual, because at The Line a clipped line from a sister file is treated as standing law. The line reads, word for word, and the sous-chef will recite it if you ask without making a scene:

The secret works, in particular, are to be the correct finding and not a plentiful seasoning of cayenne vinegar (No. 386), 3/4 oz.

The sous-chef, when I asked what it meant, looked at the bottle of cayenne — number 386 on the back wall, the only bottle on the wall with a number — and said, "The correct finding," and poured 3/4 oz into the vinaigrette for the table and 3/4 oz less into mine, which was the positive form of the rule and which, I am obliged to report, tasted correct. The drawer it came from is open to anyone: the full sheet, the positive form of the law of reciprocity, the secret works present as fragments only, cross-filed with the docket of the cold lavash. I have not decided what to make of a restaurant that seasons by safety data sheet. I have decided I would go back.

The letter of the house's founder is in the file with the rest of it — the acclamation, the refusal, the see held from 374 to the last day, the mediator who made an emperor kneel in the narthex before he would come to the altar — set down properly at Ambrose of Milan — A Letter to the Editor, from the Chancery of the Hrazdan, where the ledger keeps it at sixteen frames per second. The geography, the quarter, the pot, the fountains of Republic Square coming on behind us as the marshrutka took me back, is where Yerevan — Guidebook No. 88: The Pink City says it is. One note for the reader at home, and one more line set down from the record, the far-future clipping the chancery presses between pages: a private burial, Warriston, 21 March — full account at Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) — Obituary. The city of rain is not on this review's map. The street by the Hrazdan is. As a footnote for the reader at home: on the other side of the world, a family keeps a whole lake the way this house keeps a bottle number — by a recipe with the long preamble, Aunt Marva's Lake Gratin, for Lake Burley Griffin in Canberra, where the keeping is one lamp left on the apron, and the apron, in their house, is the blackboard and the memorial both.

The count for the review: 397 covers, one line, 396 hands, 3/4 oz of cayenne (No. 386), the garlic without limit, the honey of the bees, the khash at 7:02, the refusal on the steps, the consecration at 6:50, and the one rule — no one is hurried past 7:00. Scored at the ledger speed: 0 frames, no drops. Four stars and a half out of sixteen, the half withheld for the mezzanine the landlord keeps offering. The line holds.
