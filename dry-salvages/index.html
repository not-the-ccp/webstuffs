The Rock Itself

The Dry Salvages stands about a mile and a half out of the shore, a granite formation of marine rock off the coast of Cape Ann, with a lighthouse on its crown that has been keeping its light since the fifties of the last century and was taken off the register in 1938, though the lamp room still stands square against the water and the keeper's quarters still hold their shutters. The name belongs to the fishermen, not to the chart: salvage is the wreck-timber a boat may still lift from a foundering vessel, and dry salvage is what is left of it — what the sea has given up and will not give back. The rock therefore carries its meaning in its name, and the office has declined, on several occasions, to correct the spelling.

Boats leave from the Gloucester wharf every half hour between first light and dusk, weather permitting, and the crossing takes eleven minutes of open water. Visitors are asked to remain on deck, to fasten the small white scarf issued at the gangway to anything that can fly, and to note the bell, which the office sounds hourly on calm days so that the harbour can keep its count of the hour without asking the sky.

The 1941 Season

Visitors this season will have noticed that the air is a different instrument from usual. Since the autumn, the raiders have come over this coast at night and the lecture halls of the country have felt it in their ceilings; it was in the middle of one such season, while lecturing in the area and watching the searchlights walk the clouds, that Mr. T. S. Eliot finished The Dry Salvages, the third of his Four Quartets, and it was the poem that first made the four a set — the point at which the series was consciously being shaped as four poems and no more. The office keeps a small card in its drawer: the poem was written and published in 1941, during the air-raids on Great Britain, and it treats the nature of time and humanity's place within it, the way a harbourmaster treats fog — as the weather that is actually on the water.

The poem was named for this rock, where the poet passed his childhood summers along the Cape Ann coast, and it holds the season of this brochure in one sentence each for the young and the old: the children of the rock are the travellers on the boat, and the boat is the year, and the year is the boat, and the destination is what the travellers keep failing to see because they are watching the instruments.

The Passage — What You Will See

On the way out, the harbour closes behind you in the order of its usefulness: the buoys, the working boats, the fish houses, the church spire, the sky. Life aboard is described, in the poem that names this rock, as travelling in a boat, and the poem's argument is worth paying the fare to understand — that the travellers are kept from their destination not by the water but by their own fixing on the instruments, on the future, on the gains of science that they lean over the rail to catalogue while the bow does the actual going. The destination, when it arrives, arrives the way a bell arrives: before you have located it, and at the same time as you have.

From the rock's north face the office shows, in good weather, the whole of the Cape Ann shoreline, the islands, and on a December morning once, a fog line that the day's log describes as “the boundary.” Visitors are invited to stand at the rail and be struck, as the poem proposes, by the fact that a rock with no soil, no keeper, and no lighthouse on the register can still be the middle of the afternoon.

A word for those who read ledgers as well as water: the ferry landing parcel on the Gloucester wharf, once the property of the fish house, passed to the water-taxi line with the season, and the office's register carries the entry word for word — The lot enters the sea road → the Channel 4 site, 1994–2011. The sea road is the water lane the line keeps between the wharf and the rock; the register's later dates are the line's own business and are printed here only because the register printed them first.

For the Traveller's Kit

Take the white scarf. Take a pencil, for the logbook at the rail, which is a stub book and not a diary and is kept by whoever is aboard. Do not take the destination with you: it is not portable and it does not come on the boat. The office will not argue. What the rock has said, to everyone who has stood at its rail, is the one sentence the brochure was made to carry out of the harbour: the destination is the middle of the sea road, and the middle of the sea road is now. The bell sounds hourly. The return boat leaves at six. The four quartets of the season — the time that goes and the time that stays, the rock, the bell, and the boat — are all still running, and the office, like the rock, keeps its shutters.

Readers who have followed the bell further afield: the liner's note for the third track of the winter ballad season keeps its own two-colour record of the departure, in Blue & Grey — Liner Notes, Haldum Sessions HDS-020; and a correspondence club on the Itapecuru delta has filed the rock's latitude question against its own shoreline in the Federative Letter, No. 14. Both offices are open.
