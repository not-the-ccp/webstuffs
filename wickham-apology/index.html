The Clerk’s Office · House of Commons · 91 Chancery Lane, London W.C.2

Letter No. 44/7 · Under the Clerk’s Hand · Not for the Vote, and Not for the Paper

12 March 1958

To the Estate of the late Lieutenant-Colonel Edward Thomas Ruscombe Wickham,
Member of Parliament for Taunton, 1935–1945,
care of Messrs. Harcourt & Vane, Solicitors,
14 St. Saviour’s Street, Taunton, Somerset.

A Letter of Apology

We write in our capacity as clerk of this House, and we write to apologize. In the winter of 1957, in Room 3 on the north side of the Commons — the small room behind the table where the Private Secretaries stood — a single box was found, unmarked, on the top shelf, with a ledger bound to the wrong spine and a pencil note on the lid that read only: Taunton, not the other one. The box belonged to Mr. Wickham. It has now been opened, counted, and returned to your solicitors, and with it this letter, which is the form the House has for a mistake that took eleven years to confess.

The service of the member in question must be stated before the mistake can be measured against it. Lieutenant-Colonel Wickham — born 4 May 1890, and died 25 August last, so that the estate writes for him now by proxy — served in India from 1910 until 1935, first in the ranks of the British Indian Army and afterwards in the Indian Political Department, twenty-five years in all. From 1919 to 1921 he acted as officer in attendance on the European tour of the Shah of Persia, who conferred on him the Order of the Lion and the Sun, 3rd Class, and in 1928 he fulfilled the same duties for the King of Afghanistan. In 1935 he sat down at the north side of this House as the Conservative member for Taunton, and there he remained until 1945, throughout which he served as Parliamentary Private Secretary to the Secretary of State for War — two holders of the office, one in the years when it still kept the name of the War Office and one after it took its war-time title — so that his table carried, week after week, the constituency business of Taunton beneath the correspondence of the department. The box found in Room 3 is the residue of that double service: the Taunton returns, the War Office letters, the ledger of the three Parliaments, and — the item on which the apology chiefly stands — the medal card of the Lion and the Sun, with the pencil itinerary of the 1928 Afghan tour folded behind it.

What happened to the box is plain, and we have set it down plainly. When Mr. Wickham ceased to be a member in the summer of 1945, his papers were to be packed and sent to the family seat in Somerset. They were instead shelved, unmarked, in Room 3, where the new Private Secretaries passed them every day without reading the lid, until the box came down in 1957 and the ledger was found to be the ledger of the 1935–1945 sessions bound with the spine of an 1898 attendance book. The error was first committed on the Monday after the July 1945 election, 8 July, at the hour the box was moved to the wrong shelf, and the delay runs from that day to this letter.

At the hearing on the box, counsel for the estate spoke, and the clerk of the record entered the speech word for word, which is the rule of this House and the reason the following line stands here exactly as it was heard: “The applicants emphasise that the room always asks: when, exactly, does the activist work first, the way other animals read the trick aloud from its back.” We confess we sat a long time over that line. The room in question — this room, the Commons — does in fact always ask the one question put to any member who has a claim upon the House: when, exactly, does the work begin, who first does it, and at what hour it was first laid before the House. And the room does recite its business aloud, from the Order Paper set down on the clerk’s table, with the same flat, unreasoning accuracy that the applicants’ counsel ascribed to other animals reading from their backs. The applicants meant, we now take it, that the House asks for the first moment of the work and then reads out its order of the day against it, and that no answer is acceptable which does not fix both. The answer, then, is fixed here: the work of the first to move the box began on 8 July 1945, and the answer is given, in full, on 12 March 1958.

We have one further duty in this letter, which is to say what the box contained that was not the House’s to keep in the first place. The medal card, the two passports of the Indian service, the Shah’s letter of thanks of 1921, and the pencil itinerary of 1928 belong to the family, and they have gone. The ledger of the 1935–1945 sessions, the War Office correspondence, and the Taunton returns are the House’s, and the House keeps them, now marked, on the shelf where a Private Secretary’s papers are kept, in a room where they will be read before they are moved. The obituary of Mr. Wickham’s own Parliament, and of the member on the other side who kept its minute book, is entered at length in the minute book of Room 3, where the late Prime Minister’s entry was found, entered late, and passed unamended, and the form of the present letter — an apology for a package that dropped, on a platform or off a shelf, and was only later acknowledged — is the same form the stationmaster of Brooklyn Heights used in his letter to an estate, at The Brooklyn Heights Apology, entered against the package of 26 August 1924; the two offices keep the same rule, that the mistake be confessed by the hour and the day, and not merely by the season.

Eleven years is the measure of the delay, and it is too long, and this letter does not attempt to excuse it. It attempts only to fix it, which is all a clerk of this House is good for. The box is returned, the shelf is marked, and the ledger is on the record.

I have the honour to remain, sir, the obedient clerk of the House,

E. A. Pemberton
Clerk of the Private Secretaries’ Office,
House of Commons

P. S. — The ledger of the 1935–1945 sessions, being now marked, may be inspected by the estate at this Office, Thursday and Friday, at the clerk’s table, without application. Readers wishing the wider shelf of the register on which this apology is kept may take it up at the front page of the archive, where the ledgers, the almanacs and the letters of the kind of this one are entered in order, in ink, unamended.
