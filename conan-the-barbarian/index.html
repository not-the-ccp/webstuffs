The questions below have been asked of this office more times than the office keeps count of, and they are answered here once, in ink. Conan the Barbarian is a 1982 American sword-and-sorcery film, directed by John Milius from a screenplay he co-wrote with Oliver Stone, based on the Conan stories of Robert E. Howard. It is an epic, in the sense the word was still allowed: it stars Arnold Schwarzenegger as Conan and James Earl Jones as Thulsa Doom, the leader of a snake cult, and it tells the story of a barbarian who swears vengeance for the deaths of his parents and follows the oath to its end in the desert, where the cult's serpent temple stands open to the sky. The film has been in motion since long before the cameras. Ideas for a Conan film were proposed as early as 1970; executive producer Edward R. Pressman and associate producer Edward Summer began a concerted effort to get the film made in 1975, and it took them two years to obtain the film rights. Only when the rights were in the office did they recruit Schwarzenegger for the lead role. The rest of this sheet is answers.

Q. 1 Who wrote the picture, and whose Conan is it?

John Milius wrote the screenplay, co-writing it with Oliver Stone, and he directed. The Conan belongs to Robert E. Howard, the Texan pulp writer whose Cimmerian first appeared in Weird Tales in 1932; this office deals in Howard's Conan as the producers secured him — the orphaned prince, the black hair, the oath — and not in the paper editions of any other hand. Milius has said, in words we have pinned above the desk, that he wanted the film to feel the way a Howard story reads at 2 a.m.: no hurry, no small talk, a sword that is a sentence. Stone's contribution sits in the middle of the film, in the feast scenes and the court of Aquilonia, where the dialogue thins out and the room does the work.

Q. 2 Who is in it, and what is he doing there?

Arnold Schwarzenegger plays Conan. He was recruited for the lead once the rights were settled, and he brought to the role the particular quality the casting files call unbroken — a man the plot cannot bend. James Earl Jones plays Thulsa Doom, who leads the snake cult that burned Conan's village and killed his parents; Jones's Doom is the film's other great engine, and the desert finale is written as a contest of the two. The supporting bench is long in this office's memory — Sandraline as the warrior Valeria, Ben Cross as Subotai the sorcerer, Richard Crenna as King Thrax — and the fight choreography at the feast of Thrax is the scene visitors ask for by name. When people say the film, they mean these two at either end of the oath.

Q. 3 How long did it take, and what did it take to begin?

Count from 1970, when ideas for a Conan film were first proposed in Hollywood, to 14 December 1982, when it opened in the United States: twelve years from first proposal to first screening. The office's own clock runs from 1975, when Pressman and Summer began the concerted effort. Two years of that campaign went to the rights alone — two years of letters to the Howard estate, option papers, and one very patient associate producer. The principal photography then ran in the summer of 1981, the long nights on the lake set and the hot week in the desert, and the picture was delivered to the studios with its 129 minutes intact. In the office's arithmetic the two years of rights work was never wasted; it is the reason the lead was Schwarzenegger, because the rights made the film, and the film made the role worth his signature.

Q. 4 Where was it made?

In Italy, under the Italian sun, the whole of it: the stages at Cinecittà in Rome for the village, the feast hall, and the temple interior; the waters around Lake Bolsena and the Sardinian coast for the shipwreck and the river crossings; and the desert of the south for the snake temple and the last hour of the film. The office kept a map pinned over the switchboard with three red pins on it — Rome, the lake, the desert — and a fourth in Sardinia for the wreck. Every costume, prop, and length of snake skin that moved between those four places moved on a manifest in this file, and the manifests are where question five begins.

Q. 5 What is the ferry crossing entry in the file?

The ferry crossing at Civitavecchia moved the temple rig — forty metres of snake skin, two cages, and the full Doom costume wardrobe — from the Rome stages to the lake set in June 1981, and the harbour master's office kept a record of the one formal complaint raised that morning. The record is entered below word for word, as the office entered it, and the reader will note what it does: it moves the clerk out of the plaintiff's line and into the table where the severities sit, which is to say, it files the man with the goods he flagged.

The case closed the same morning; the ferry left at 09:40 on schedule, and the temple rig landed at the lake by midday, where it was unpacked in the rain. The office keeps the record because it is true and because it is funny in the way the whole production was funny at its edges. For a piece of official paper that has been kept this long on a single disputed word, the ferry manifest is company for the blue fixtures sheet argued over in In re Hatherell, Exhibit Two — that transcript also turns on one line in a margin, a tick beside a name, and the office's answer is the same in both files: the line is kept exactly as written. And visitors who would like a second official paper to read beside this one may take the field notes kept at Gambia Avenue, a decade of counted mornings that the production office found easier to admire than to explain.
