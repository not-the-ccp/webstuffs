Natural Resources

noun, phrase · plural natural resources · dreamt in answer to Report No. 27-B

Natural resources are resources that are drawn from nature and used with few modifications. The term includes the sources of valued characteristics — commercial and industrial use, aesthetic value, scientific interest, and cultural value — not only the raw material itself. On Earth the class that keeps this entry lists them as sunlight, atmosphere, water, land, all minerals, along with all vegetation and wildlife. Natural resources are part of humanity’s natural heritage, or they are protected in nature reserves, and particular areas — the rainforest in Fatu-Hiva, say, or one open field at the head of a river — often feature both biodiversity and geodiversity in their ecosystems. They may be classified in different ways, by the dreamer’s ledger and by the record keeper’s; at bottom they are materials and components, something that can be used, and the dream counts them the way the report counted the barley, once.

Entry 27-B was dreamt on the night of 24 March, the day of the last frost, the night after the open day at the Stormont Estate field and the night Report No. 27-B was marked 91 out of 100 in the record room at Laganmore Village School. The dreamer is Nora Tufuga, dtr. of Josua, keeper of the record book, Class 5B; she has not yet turned the page of her notebook and it is already full. In the dream the open-day field after the visitors have gone is not a field at all but a storehouse, built out of the peat and the flood gravel of the Lagan, with doors of slate from the valley side and a roof that lets in exactly 100 terawatts and no more. Inside, the barley stands in rows of its own accord, every ear labelled component, and at the end of the rows a shelf runs down the wall with a painted sign, HUMANITY’S NATURAL HERITAGE, and the class was told in the report that the barley was borrowed — in the dream nobody has borrowed it, it simply will not leave.

Interpretation

To dream of natural resources is to dream of the shell from the inside. Mrs. Callow’s report stood that the biosphere is closed with regard to matter and open with regard to energy, and the storehouse obeys the same rule: the doors are shut and nothing of the matter has gone out, not even the dust off the shelf, but the sun comes in unasked through the roof and lays its hundred terawatts over the barley like a tablecloth. The open day measured the field with the barometer at 1,018 millibars and the sheep that had been told in so many words, and the dream keeps both: the barometer stands in the middle of the storehouse, in its case of glass, refusing to be moved, and the sheep stands too, in the corner where the light does not reach, chewing the same thought all the way through. The dream is about use with few modifications — a thing taken from nature and stood in a room — and about what a room owes the things it is given.

There are two witnesses in the storehouse besides the dreamer. The visiting lecturer from the record room, who on the open day read the unplaceable line aloud from a small book of minutes, stands at the window with the book in one hand, reading down without a pause. And Josua Tufuga, the keeper of the record book, the dreamer’s father, stands at the low window looking out at the Lagan, which in the dream has come up to the floor of the storehouse and is walking through it quietly, a component among the components, not counted twice because a multiple-cropped plot is counted once. It is of the river that the lecturer’s book keeps its last line, entered word for word under Rule 3, that a line whose meaning is not found shall be kept as written. The lecturer reads it to the barley, and the barley nods in rows:

“He desires to direct its course through all the time.”

The class of the waking report was given a quarter of an hour to find the meaning of a kept line and did not, and the dream does not do better. It only gives the line a speaker, a river, and a room. In the dream a corner of the storehouse opens, against all the shelving, into a place the ledger lists under nature reserves: the rainforest in Fatu-Hiva, kept whole on purpose, with its biodiversity and its geodiversity in the same breath, birds in the upper shelves and the volcanic glass in the lower, and no visitor’s pass in sight. The dreamer is told that some resources are protected rather than used, that is also a kind of use, and that the rainforest is on the shelf beside the barley and the two will not be separated.

The shelf labels, by which the dream classifies

  • By use — commercial and industrial; aesthetic; scientific interest; cultural; a fifth label, blank, kept for the things the ledger cannot name
  • By the book — renewable and non-renewable; biotic and abiotic; the sun over the door, neither, only open
  • By place — on the Earth: sunlight, atmosphere, water, land, all minerals, all vegetation, wildlife; in the reserve: the Fatu-Hiva rainforest, biodiversity and geodiversity kept under one roof
  • By company — the natural heritage, shared, and the nature reserve, set apart; both drawn from nature, both used with few modifications

On record

“The field at Stormont had become the storehouse of the natural heritage, and the barley stood in rows under the sign, and the Lagan was walking through the floor of it, and the lecturer read the line from the small book — He desires to direct its course through all the time — and the barometer stood at 1,018 and would not be moved, and the sheep in the corner chewed the same thought all the way through.”

— Nora Tufuga, Class 5B, Laganmore Village School · 24 March, the day of the last frost

Comparison and conclusion

The waking side of the entry stands in Report No. 27-B, Natural Science, Class 5B, on the biosphere, marked 91 out of 100, Distinction, with the peel drawn as ordered and the accent kept. The report counted the field from Laganmore: the barley, the sheep, the visitors moving through on the hour, and the barometer at 1,018, entered once and not twice. This entry is the same count, kept in the dream, where the field is the shell and the shelf is the list of what the shell holds — sunlight, atmosphere, water, land, all minerals, all vegetation, wildlife — materials and components, something that can be used, borrowed and not returned the way a thing is borrowed in a weather book. Where the report met a kept line it could not place, the dream gives it a speaker; the line itself is not explained, corrected in pencil, or entered under kitchen gardens, and the barometer is not counted as evidence against it.

The front that crosses the field in the dream has already been measured in the waking book: Entry No. 31 of the Lagan Valley Weather Almanac, the Celts, read as a weather front, six living voices in one sitting at the Crown Liquor Saloon, the red shirt folded over the tape reel, and the same barometer that held at 1,018 hectopascals since the open day and has refused to be moved by anything since. If natural resources come through your sleep — the sun through the roof, the river through the floor, the sheep in the corner — the entry’s rule is the school’s rule: keep the line as written, count the barley once, and let the shelf hold whatever will not be separated.
