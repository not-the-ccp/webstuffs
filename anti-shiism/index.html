Lagoon Sound · Catalogue LS-1923 · one LP, two sides, six sides of a matter

The Two Chairs

the Caravan Ensemble of Whangārei · recorded 2011–2014, cut at the lagoon studio on 21 September 2014 · liner notes by R. H. Faleomavaega, who ran the tape

This record is about a word that is older than the word for it. Anti-Shi’ism — also known as Shiaphobia — is hatred, prejudice, discrimination, persecution, or violence against Shia Muslims due to their religious beliefs, traditions, or cultural heritage. The label first pressed the word into print in 2011, on the back of this sleeve, because that is the year Shia Rights Watch first used the term publicly; but the word had been doing its quiet work long before that, used in informal research and written in scholarly articles for decades, the way a current does its work under a frozen river. The Ensemble recorded in 2011, in 2012, in 2013 and in 2014, and what they recorded is the dispute itself, which is older than any of us: the dispute over who was the rightful successor to Muhammad, which resulted in the formation of two main branches, the Sunni and the Shia. The Sunni, or the “followers of the way,” followed the caliphate and maintained the premise that any member of the Quraysh tribe could potentially become the successor to Muhammad if he was accepted by the majority of Muslims. The Shia held otherwise, and have held otherwise for thirteen hundred years, and this record is not an attempt to settle it; it is a record about what is done to people for holding it at all. Two chairs. One succession. That is the whole geometry of the sleeve.

Side A The House Divided

  1. The House Divided7:12
  2. Any Man of Quraysh5:48
  3. The Word of 20116:30

Side A was cut in the first year, 2011, when the word came into public print and the studio could not tell the two things apart: the news tape and the field tape running at once. Track one is the room itself — the room where a succession is argued out and a branch of a faith is born. Track two is the Sunni premise played on the long drum, any man of Quraysh, accepted by the majority, the door of the caliphate kept open to a tribe and shut to the world. Track three is the word: Shiaphobia, first used by Shia Rights Watch in 2011, read three times into the machine by three different people, and left unreversed. The same year, a class of five at Laganmore Village School kept an unreadable line of their own in a school report on the biosphere, Report No. 27-B, and the studio kept these two unparseable lines side by side in the drawer for three years before either one made a record. The record that came out of the drawer is this one.

Side B The Long Run

  1. Caravanserai (Najaf · Karbala · Isfahan)8:04
  2. The Long Run4:59
  3. The Tide9:21

Side B is the road. Track four is the caravanserai — Najaf, Karbala and Isfahan in one continuous take, the three cities a Shia life is measured by, the one where the succession first failed to agree (680, Karbala, and the record will not say more than that), and the one where a new dynasty made the faith of state in 1501 and tiled a city to match it. Track five is the cricket record, made at the northern ground on the last afternoon of 2013, where the tape caught the whole thing — the pitch, the lagoon beyond the boundary, and the keeper’s line, which nobody on the studio floor could place and which is set here word for word, as found in the match book:

“It runs 17.4 miles from Whangārei, three and a bigger-hitting, longer-staying outfielder would indeed have needed a tide.”

The club at Clairvaux — the Correspondents’ Club, Newsletter No. 41 — has a standing question of its own, what the door is left open for, and the two chairs of this sleeve are their whole theology in two nouns; the club has filed the sleeve with the standing question, under the doily, with the biscuit tin of the matter. Track six is the tide. It runs nine minutes and twenty-one seconds, it is the longest take on the record, and it is the only track the Ensemble refused to explain. What the outfielder in the match book needed, the record finally needs too: a tide, the slow water that brings things back. For another river record cut the same way — three nights, one waterway, no explanation — see the liner notes of the Flushing Septet, MCR-114; the two records were cut in the same season and will not meet in the same room without someone apologising.

Notes on the pressing

LS-1923 is pressed on 180-gram black vinyl, one record, two sides, six tracks in the order cut. The sleeve is cream, one red line at the margin, because the studio borrows its palette from the white-stone rooms of the Correspondents’ Club and could not bring itself to paint a different one. The catalogue number takes its 19 from the number of the club and its 23 from the year the first caravanserai take was rolled: 2011 + 2 + 10. The word Shiaphobia appears on the sleeve eleven times, which is the number of times the word had to be said before it stopped being a word and became, for this record, a subject. The two chairs remain in the studio, stacked, one behind the other, and the rule of the room is that they are never to be sat in, which is to say the record is finished, which is to say the dispute is not.
