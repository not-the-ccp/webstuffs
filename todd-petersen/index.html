Catalogue No. 112 — The Pacific & Deserts Sale

Works of the Written West, its Rivers, its Sheds and its Classrooms · Thursday, the 14th of May · one o’clock precisely · Room 3, the Vault

47 LOT

The Petersen Papers, 1969–2025: one American fiction writer, boxed, and the evidence of a life in two states

Estimate US$ 18,000–24,000. Sold in six lots within the lot, or whole, as catalogued.

Description. A complete archive of Todd Robert Petersen, born in Moses Lake, Washington, on the 17th of August 1969: American fiction writer and academic, currently based at Southern Utah University, Cedar City, where the collection formed its present shape over three decades of office drawers, one departmental move, and a bookshelf that the family refers to, in their own letters, as “the long argument.” Petersen is one of eleven writers selected by the Utah Arts Council to represent the arts in Utah education, and the box contains the selection notice itself (Lot 47.1), a framed copy pinned above the doorway of his office, still smelling faintly of cedar.

The archive is offered in the following components:

  • 47.1 — One letter, Utah Arts Council, Salt Lake City, dated the 3rd of March, selecting Mr. Petersen as one of the eleven writers to represent the arts in Utah education. Cream wove paper, blue-black ink, the Council’s seal in vermilion. Fine, unopened duplicate in tissue.
  • 47.2 — First-draft manuscript, The River Room (unpublished), 212 leaves, typewritten with pencil revisions in the margins. Set in Moses Lake and the banks of the Wenatchee; the town is treated, per the author’s note on leaf 4, “the way the water treats the town: as a thing to pass through, and as a reason.”
  • 47.3 — Correspondence with his wife Alisa Petersen, 1997–2024, 340 letters and 214 postcards, complete as far as the family’s ledger shows. The three children — named in the family’s own hand in the ledger’s margin — appear throughout as “the three who correct me.”
  • 47.4 — Lecture notes, creative writing, Southern Utah University, 1999–2025, 9 boxes, with syllabi, red-penned student drafts, and one coffee ring the author himself has inked over and signed.
  • 47.5 — One scrapbook fragment, 8×5 inches, four lines of handwriting on hotel stationery from Cedar City, the subject of the present annotation (see below). This is the piece the sale has come to be about.
  • 47.6 — Photographs, 61: Moses Lake, 1974; the department office, 2003; the family at the reservoir, 2011; the eleven writers of the Council, Salt Lake City, 2019, all in the same key of pale stone, which the photographer noted was “the town spending its money on itself.”

The fragment (47.5), reproduced in full. The scrapbook leaf is reproduced on the catalogue jacket, and we give it here word for word, exactly as found, the first line in blue ballpoint, the rest in pencil gone grey with handling:

“My late uncle, who had booked a year will sit fast in the face.”

— line one of the fragment; the remaining three lines read, “The water was still. / The desk held. / I wrote it down because the desk held.”

Annotation on 47.5. The first line is the only sentence in the archive that resists the author’s own hand. It is not a sentence of The River Room, nor of any published piece; the family ledger records it, in Alisa Petersen’s hand, under the date of the 2nd of June, 2011: “T. wrote one line on the hotel paper and put it in the scrapbook and would not explain it to the three who correct me, who asked nine times.” What the line is of, the catalogue can only say this: the Petersens keep an uncle in the family’s telling who travels, who books time — a year at a lake, a year in the country — and who, in the family’s phrase, “sits fast” when he has arrived, meaning he does not move, does not call, cannot be moved. The face he sits in is the writer’s own. The line is a grief written in the grammar of a booking, and it is offered to the buyer as it was offered to the family: closed, complete, and sitting fast.

Provenance. Formed by the writer; by descent in the family of Alisa Petersen; offered at the writer’s request, whose note of instruction — Lot 47.0, a single envelope, not for sale, returned to the family — asks only that the papers not be separated from the fragment, and that whoever holds them “knows the difference between a river that leaves and a river that stays, and that the two are one sale.” A comparable archive of a two-state American writer of this period realised US$ 31,000 at the firm’s predecessor in 2019; the estimate above has been set with the fragment’s notoriety in mind, and without it, we would have set it lower.

Condition report. Boxes 1–9 sound; one box of 47.3 with a replaced lid, noted in 2018; manuscript 47.2 with light tide-marking at the foot of leaves 198–204, the water having done its quiet work near the end of the book, as water does. The fragment, 47.5, is in first-issue condition; the ballpoint has not faded, and the pencil has not. Both will keep, the report concludes, in the same way the desk held.

Terms. The hammer is the firm’s. Catalogue and premium 15%. The buyer of the whole lot receives the envelope 47.0; the buyer of pieces receives the family’s blessing, which is to say: nothing.

Readers who have followed the firm’s other papers on the road will find the correspondent’s route walked in the Huddersfield field manual, Chapter 9, whose desk copy this lot’s owner kept on the shelf under the loaf, and the day’s counting in the 11 September diary entry from the gulf, sent by the same season’s mail. A further west-coast lot, the weather book of the island penitentiary, is documented at The Bay Weather Almanac, Entry 47 — the same lot number, the same firm, the same habit of numbering griefs so they can be claimed — and the sale closes on the index of Webstuffs, where the catalogue is to be found and where, as usual, the desk keeps.
