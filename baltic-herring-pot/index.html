The Brackish Pot

Herring stew in brackish broth, served for forty-four · written out by Arthur Parry, Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, from the kitchen at Korunní 67, Praha 2

SERVES 44
NONE 45

A long preamble on the Baltic Sea, because the broth is saltier than it looks

The Baltic Sea is an arm of the Atlantic Ocean that is enclosed by the countries of Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Germany, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Russia, and Sweden, and a pot that is this brackish needs someone to explain why, so let me. Geophysically it is surrounded by the Scandinavian Peninsula on the one side and the North and Central European Plain on the other, which is to say a mountain range you can walk on and a plain so flat the herring does not trouble to argue with it. It is the world’s largest brackish water basin. The sea stretches from 53°N to 66°N latitude and from 10°E to 30°E longitude — thirty-one degrees of easting, which the old fishermen round to “thirty” and are never corrected by anyone. It is a shelf sea and a marginal sea of the Atlantic, and between the two of them there is limited water exchange, which is why you can call it an inland sea and no admiral objects. It drains out through the Danish Straits into the Kattegat by way of the Øresund, the Great Belt and the Little Belt — three straits, two of them with “Belt” in the name, which is why the kitchen calls the stew “the second belt” and stops there. It includes the Gulf of Bothnia, which runs up north past the Åland islands toward the sixty-sixth parallel, and the Gulf of Finland, where the water gets brackish enough to make the point on its own.

The recipe crossed the border the way the family itself crossed it: by post, in pieces. Mrs. Havlíček of Korunní 67 — the same Mrs. Havlíček who lets the low-ceilinged dining room that holds forty-four and takes enquiries between 7 and 9 p.m. — sent the bones of it in 1998, in a letter written on the back of a Bohemia Bazaar proof, the one that carried the ad for the brass wire-sifter from the Kolín mill, 1934, “still sifts flour for forty-four if you ask it nicely.” The sifter came south by tram, the sifter is still in the room, and it is where this recipe is kept. I have checked; it is in the wire. What the letter did not say, and what took eleven years and three trams to establish, is why the broth is brackish when the Czech Republic is landlocked: because the herring does not care where you are, if the water is right, and the Baltic has been keeping the water right for a very long time. The first pot in the Prague room is dated 14 November, and the date is scratched into the table, not written on it, which is the family’s way of knowing which things are permanent.

Daggoo’s contribution, sent up from the Forecastle at Nine Bute Docks with the copper funnel’s whistle already gone, is the rule for the fat: enough that the herring can argue in it. His kitchen is a dock and his stew is a berth, and he has exactly one opinion about the sea itself — that it is a shelf sea, and shelf seas have opinions. He rates the Baltic four crossbars out of five, the fifth withheld because the Kattegat charges for the exchange, and he keeps this week’s Bohemia Bazaar folded behind the pass, Cauliflowers by the head, twenty-five korunas, exact change.

Transcript · Lila’s tablet, microphone left open, kitchen at Korunní 67, 8:03 p.m.

“And, doubtless, my going out altogether, like a reed, or, coming full tilt on his hams in a little fat or oil.”

The room decodes it as follows. Daggoo was standing in the pot’s way, Lila was on the step, and the herring had come out of the brine sideways, like a reed in a current. She was describing him. The fat is the broth; the oil is the one knob of butter the rule allows; the hams are his. It is kept in the sifter word for word, exactly as the Matchnight Committee keeps its crossbar rulings, and I am not tidying it up for the record.

For the pot

  • 44 herring, Baltic, from a boat that argued with the Gulf of Bothnia at least once — gutted, the silver side up
  • 6 litres of brine, 1 in 30 to the sea: the pot is an inland sea and you will make it one
  • Cauliflower, field-grown at Lány, one head, broken by the fist, garnish included if you insist
  • 18 pearl onions, unpeeled, boiled in the brine, not in the stew — the brine keeps its own counsel
  • 2 grains of salt from David Chen’s jar — two out of 11,400, one per fifty thousand of Cheshire, and no more, because the sea already voted
  • One knob of butter, the oil in the record
  • Dill, from the window box, whatever the weather says

Method

  1. Mix the brine — one measure of salt to thirty of water — and set it to stand. It should taste, at the lip, like a forecast you would not argue with.
  2. Lay the herring silver side up in the pot, in a row, like seats. Forty-four to a row. The room was built for the number; the pot was built for the row.
  3. Pour in the brine over the herring, not on it. The herring is brackish and proud and takes what it is offered sideways.
  4. Stand the cauliflower in the middle of the pot, unbroken, like a lighthouse. It will break itself by the fourth quarter-hour.
  5. Bring to the lowest heat the pot will admit. It must simmer the way the Øresund drains: limited exchange, all the time, for hours. The stew keeps 53°N to 66°N in its memory; the heat keeps it out.
  6. At the hour, put in the butter. This is the fat or oil. This is where Daggoo gets out of the way.
  7. Sift the broth through the Kolín wire-sifter, 1934, and ask it nicely. It still sifts for forty-four.
  8. Ladle over the onions, scatter the dill, and send the pot to the low-ceilinged room. It holds forty-four. It has held forty-four since 1998.

Notes from the sifter

The stew is a shelf sea: it keeps everything it is given at a known depth, and it does not exchange with anything outside the room. That is the whole cooking. Do not chase the colour of the Kattegat; do not season against the sea. The red shirt from Bedminster hangs above the pass and has not been worn since the pot found its number, and the committee has ruled on it eight to one that it is part of the recipe, the dissenting voice having heard the broth in person.
