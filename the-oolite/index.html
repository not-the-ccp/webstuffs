The Firth Ledger · Dinner Review · Nº 4 on the Lintel

The Oolite

Twenty-one plates from a working quarry room below the slipway at Saltfleet, Lincolnshire — chalk, salt air, and one count the kitchen will not correct.

Reviewed on a Friday at low water, Saltfleet, East Lindsey, Lincolnshire. Tasting menu £46, quarry white £7 the glass. Four of five stars; the missing star is cut in the wall, in the shape of an ammonite, and the kitchen considers that a fair assessment of everything.

The room

The door of The Oolite is set in the limestone bluff below the slipway at Saltfleet, a coastal village in the East Lindsey district of Lincolnshire, and the door itself is a slab of Lincolnshire Limestone, sawn off the wall it came from, still holding a belemnite the size of a thumb. You go down eleven steps. The dining room is one of the old quarry chambers: twelve covers, a single table of sawn stone, candles in oyster shells, and the walls are the room's own bedding — grey, then cream, then a dark fossil band that runs the length of the table with ammonites standing up out of it like they are still arriving. Limestone is a carbonate sedimentary rock, mostly calcite and aragonite, two crystal forms of the same calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, and the room makes no apology for being a specimen of exactly that. Above the pass hangs a Saltfleet lash, one of the hand-tied leather lashes from the rigging loft two floors up, cured against the salt air, which the chef says keeps the kitchen honest.

The menu

The kitchen is kept by Mr. Saleh of Kom Ombo, the ferryman of the Aswan Listening Brigade, who took a long winter's leave from the Nile and came north to work a quarry the way he had worked the ferry: slowly, without microphones, and without being asked more than once. Everything on the card is carbonate or is served on one, and the card itself — a slab of the same stone as the door — promises fifty-four courses. In practice twenty-one arrived over the evening, and when I asked about the rest, the pass was quiet for a moment and then answered that the other courses precipitate at their own rate, out of water holding dissolved calcium, and cannot be rushed, which is the truest thing I have heard a kitchen say in a decade.

It opens with first water: a clear flat white oyster, its shell limed and laid out beside the plate for the table to study like a fossil of an animal that is still, and in fact is, alive. In the middle of the menu comes the belemnite — squid cut long and pale so it reads as the belemnite in the wall, with a butter of ash and a whisper of vinegar. The shells, the corals, the small eggs of oolite grain that gave the room its name: the last five hundred and forty million years, the kitchen insists, have been done by animals, and the menu is their shift. About a fifth of the cards are marked sedimentary in chalk — roughly twenty of the fifty-four — and the head of the loft, who brought the spare grips down the eleven steps to see how it is going, told me the proportion is deliberate and that the rest of the rock was done by the water itself, which does not want a tip.

Side eight

The last course is the one nobody leaves. It is served on a shallow slab as Side Eight: Detachment — a meringue that has been allowed to come apart, stood up in eight drops of lime and elderflower, and the drops are counted aloud by the pass, one to eight, before a spoon is given to anyone. On the slate, in small letters, is the standing order: The count in side eight is real and should not be corrected. I asked Mr. Saleh what it referred to. He reached into the wall behind the pass, where the sleeves of Nile Basin Records NBR-041 are kept on a shelf of fossil band like they are bedding themselves, turned the sleeve over to the notes for the listener, and let the sleeve do the talking. The count, the notes say, is on side eight, of the drops, and is real. When the loft's head came down the next evening he said only that a lash and a leech both have their count, and that a good room knows which one is which, and he went back upstairs to his braiding.

The verdict

The Oolite is the slowest kitchen in East Lindsey and one of the few that is honest about it: the wall was four hundred million years old before the village had a name, and the food is served at the pace of the room it is served in. Four stars. The fifth is in the wall, and it is a fossil, so it has all the time in the world. Go at low water; the auctioneer is shouting before the sun is up and the room, which is under him, keeps its own count to eight.
