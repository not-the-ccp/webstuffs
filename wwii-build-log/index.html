Second World War

Build Log • Table No. 6 • scale 1:35 • six years on one tabletop

1 September 1939 — 2 September 1945

IN PROGRESS

The Second World War took six years, and six years fit on a bench, which is how I ended up building the whole of it: 41 metres of war across the tabletop, 1:35 scale, warping in two of my own. It began as a corner for one airfield in the Markham Valley and grew, entry by entry, until the corner was the whole table. The table has to carry the fact of the thing, and the fact is that the war was the deadliest conflict in history: somewhere between 60 and 75 million people died in it, a majority of them civilians, and millions more died of massacres, starvation, disease, and genocides. Nearly every country in the world was in it, and most of them at total war, on a scale nobody before had managed. I build it the way the message desk files things — in the order it happened, one folio at a time, with the numbers written down so they cannot be rounded away.

The bench is the same one that cut the glass for Exhibit 43.B, The Landing at Nadzab. The sawdust in the drawer is the sawdust of that job.

Entry 01September 1939 — the west end

The west end of the table is Poland, and I built it last of all, because a build log should not start with the beginning. The causes of the war are the easiest part to model and the hardest to explain: unresolved tensions in the aftermath of the First World War, the treaties nobody signed away privately, a whole plain of old anger left standing in the rain. I laid the 1 September street in grey putty and left the eastern third of it unpainted. One division at 1:35 is two hundred figures. I did not order two hundred figures. The unpainted third is the honest part.

Entry 021940–1942 — total war

Mid-table is the stretch where the map went dark: the blitz over the flat cities in white primer, Stalingrad in red putty over red putty, Midway as a rectangle of blue with four islands and nothing else, because nothing else survives a carrier battle. This is the section I keep meaning to finish, the way the war kept meaning to be over. It does not come out of the box. Total war is the part where you run out of kit and start turning the sprues over and looking at the flash.

Red pencil, margin: the Blitz sky wants 40 coats of thin white. I have 31. The barge comes Thursday.

Entry 035 September 1943 — Nadzab

The corner I started with. Five hundred and forty-three paratroopers of the 503rd Parachute Infantry and a ground battery of the 2/4th Field Regiment, the corrugated-iron strips of the airfield, thirty kilometres of Markham Valley between them and Lae, and General MacArthur circling in a B-17 with his binoculars. This corner is built to the standard of the placard in Case 1 across the room — same airfield, same 25-pounder, the gunpit dug in the exact position the 2/2nd Pioneer Battalion set it. The message desk that keeps the decoded dispatch keeps a copy of my parts orders too, and it stamps them in the same hand that copied the courier order, “return to the correspondent” — so now every parts slip I sign arrives back at the bench with its last three letters underlined twice. I have stopped signing them. They are returned with the underline anyway. Some desks keep everything.

Entry 04June 1944 — the beach

The Normandy end. Sand from the yard, not the paint pot, and a tide line in dark wash that has already been wrong twice and corrected twice. A beach at 1:35 is mostly patience, which is the war in miniature: push, take a wash over the bench, start the row again. The kitchen on the ring road at Mirandola has offered its cutolette recipe for a row of 90mm boats, and the offer is in the ledger under debts, not paint.

Entry 05August–September 1945 — the end

The east end, and the quiet after. The war closed on 2 September 1945, and the end of it was an occupation: Germany, Austria, Japan, and Korea occupied by the victors, with the German and Japanese leaders put on trial for war crimes. I modelled the trials first — two courtrooms, one bench, forty chairs at 1:35 — because the table has to say what happened next and not stop at the last explosion. The occupation blocks are still in the tray, waiting for paint that has not arrived. The barge has been late once this season, and the dock has already been blamed.

If the table is ever finished, it goes up as Exhibit 44.A, next to 43.B, and the Pacific Theatre Room becomes the only gallery in the building where the whole war sits on one bench and the climate is controlled to a number.

Supply

Everything on this table arrives by water. The boat house on the Secchia keeps the bench provisioned the way it provisions the cabin on the Secchia bank in Mirandola — tins, thinners, sprues in flat-packed crates — and the route is shared with the visitor's brochure that files the valley: Italy — A Visitor's Brochure. The supply barge ties up twice weekly at the landing stage.

If a coat of paint or a row of figures is missing from the ledger, do not write it off. It is on the barge, and the barge is twice-weekly, which is a rate of arrival the war itself never managed. The kitchen keeps a cutlet against my row of boats until the row is built; the desk keeps every parts slip I ever signed, underlined twice at the end.
