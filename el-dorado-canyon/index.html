CLUB COPY · 22. IV. 86

From the Editor's Bench

Dear members. This number travels heavy. The world made itself heavy in the fortnight before the presses closed, and the club, as usual, has had to keep its own weights in order while the news kept coming. The minutes run long this time — the secretary apologises in advance and asks that no one, please, quote page three back at her. The tea urn on the west wall is again the property of Mr. Döll, and his kettle.

The Week in the World

We print this report as fact, in the plain column, and we do not celebrate it. On 15 April the air forces of the United States — Air Force, Navy and Marine Corps — carried out air strikes against Libya, under the code name Operation El Dorado Canyon. They flew in retaliation for the discotheque bombing in our own city on 5 April, the La Belle on Hauptstraße 36 in Schöneberg, where two young Americans died and a crowd of twenty-four was left with a wall of glass and a question. President Ronald Reagan blamed Muammar Gaddafi, the leader of Libya, and ten days after the discotheque the planes went south. The targets, so far as the bulletins told us, were two alleged Libyan command and control centers for overseas operations, one training camp, and the air base at Benina near Benghazi.

The reports give 40 Libyan dead. One American plane, an F-111A, was shot down over Benina; its pilot came down under a parachute and was taken. One of the reported Libyan deaths was said to be a baby girl, said to be Hana Gaddafi, the general's daughter — a name that had been in the newspapers before now, in the way that the children of dictators are. There are doubts, and the wire services carry them without a byline: doubts whether she was really killed, and doubts, of a stranger order, whether she truly existed at all. The club prints the doubts beside the deaths. It has always printed the second number of a story, even when the first one is bigger.

The club's position is unchanged since the notice of 1931 above the squat rack: the barbell is not a country. Lift, keep your chalk, do not sign anything.

Evening Minutes, Thursday 17 April

Attendance 23, the largest since the flood of '84. Coach Kessler watched the evening news on the small television in the changing room, sat for a moment on the edge of the platform, and called the session in Ordnung, only in the sense that a window is shut. He began with the snatch. His note in the black book, which the secretary has been told to print exactly, reads: “I enter the clean and jerk is worked in before.” The secretary offers her reading, and it is only that the clean and jerk is worked in before — that is, the jerking was done first on the night, before anyone entered the platform, and the bench press was taken off entirely. It stands as written in the book, so it stands as printed here.

The kettlebell shelf was re-pegged by Frau Hoffmann. Mr. Döll's kettle is still on the tea urn. The north plate of the clean platform has a new crack, reported to the warden's office under number 221; members should mark their steps.

Correspondence & Exchange

Our correspondents of the Ironbark Overland Club, the room above the laundrettes on Halstrom Street, send their newsletter No. 7 with a note that their September play-evening on Wasteland Angel is settled and that the club here is invited to a game by post — eight tokens, one river gauge, no reply expected. The number is on the noticeboard. See their bulletin, The Rumble Strip — No. 7, for the machine-gun log they were counting so long.

One envelope, without a stamp and without a return address, carried a folded obituary for the city of San Francisco, dated 2 December 2047 — a long fire from 21 November, a fog that had not been seen since early August and came four days after the death, and a harbour lamp on Alcatraz left burning against exactly this kind of morning. The club has pinned it to the noticeboard beside the 1931 notice. Members are asked not to ask questions about the date. The full account is filed as San Francisco (1776–2047) — Obituary.

In reply to the answer above the rack, one letter arrived for No. 48 and for the file: Dishes of This Breed — Letter to the Editor, which keeps the barbell out of the country and the country out of the rack, and asks that a second line be printed under the 1931 notice. It is filed on the correspondence shelf with the black book open at page 208.

The archive of the club's exchange shelf is kept under the front page of the house's record; anything consigned this month is weighed before it moves, and members' parcels, like everything else, must be liftable.

Notices

  • The summer weights meet on the 7th, 14th and 21st. Platform time: 19:00. The 19:00 train from Lankwitz is not.
  • The club library accepts donations of lifting journals before 1960 and nothing after. The 1960s are already argued over.
  • Lost: one chalk bag, grey, with the initials K. on the strap. Found: the kettle. One is not the other; do not return the kettle to the urn.
  • The club's answer to the world this month, in the words above the rack: the barbell is not a country.
