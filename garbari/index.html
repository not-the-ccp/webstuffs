Column 1 · Station normals, 1963–2007

The garden's year, in figures

Station 11 has stood in the Giardino dei Semplici since the department first fixed a standard rain gauge against the old medicinal-garden wall, and every figure below was read by the same pair of eyes, morning and afternoon, for forty-four years. The normals are the garden's own: warm, dry in July, honest about the November rain that comes off the Apuan Alps as though the mountains had been holding it in trust.

Column 2 · The observer

Fabio Garbari, 1937–

The reader of Station 11 is Fabio Garbari, an Italian botanist born in 1937, who took his degree in Biological Science from the University of Pisa in 1958 and, from 1963 to 2007, served as a faculty member of the Department of Botanical Science (Dipartimento di Scienze Botaniche) of the same university. That is to say: he was twenty-five when the first gauge card went up, and the garden kept its weather with him for the whole of his working life, so that the station's forty-four years are, hour for hour, one man's forty-four years. He read the barometer before he read anything else, watered nothing, and argued with nobody — though the log shows one October in which the rain came for nineteen days and his handwriting, on the nineteenth, went small and calm and said only it will stop; it always stops.

The almanac records the botanical side of the station's weather, because weather and naming go together at this latitude. The International Plant Names Index carries 37 taxa named by Garbari — thirty-seven names, each one a plant he had first met in a season of particular rain, and each one, in effect, a forecast he kept. In 2019 the Royal Horticultural Society awarded him the Veitch Memorial Medal, which arrived at the garden in an envelope the size of a barometer chart; the chart, for the record, was still working.

Column 3 · The pencil line, 1974

What the ledger says

In the spring of 1974 the department exchanged the station's original rain gauge for a rebuilt instrument, and the parts for the new cylinder came from across the Atlantic. On the last page of the old ledger, where the standard staff of the first gauge was still pinned under a glass, there is one pencil annotation, in a small hand, that the almanac prints here exactly as found, because a line this old does not improve on translation:

"I copied the same length as a boy and lived; the council took up the U.S."

Read plainly, it is a statement about the standard itself. Garbari, as a boy at the university, had traced the height of the first standard staff onto the margin of a notebook page — copied the same length — and he kept that traced line, in the same page, for the rest of his life, so that any new staff could be checked against a length older than the staff; the "living" is the paper's. And the council is the departmental board, which in that spring of 1974 voted, against one dissent, to take up the U.S.-made cylinder fittings rather than wait for an Italian casting, so that the 1974–2007 rows of the normal-value table above stand partly on American brass and on one pencil line held against the light. The almanac asks no further explanation of the sentence; it has been in the garden for fifty years and it has not once needed one.

Column 4 · Filed with

Company on the shelf

This entry keeps company in the almanac's second folio with the build log for Bedford Downs Station, where a strip of balsa keeps its own weather — the iron-oxide passes, the one head of cattle at the far gate — and whose base plate holds a water tank at exactly the height the photographs say it is. On the dream side of the shelf, and for the reader who takes weather seriously in sleep, there is the dreamer's concordance on the blue of Santiago, where an unbeaten run is logged under the same heading as an unbroken sky. The entry closes, as this folio closes, with the Webstuffs shelf, from which the almanac is drawn each season and which, like the gauge, holds its measure.
