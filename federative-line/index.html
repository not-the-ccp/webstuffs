Companhia dos Caminhos de Ferro da Federação

The Federative Line

Official Timetable & Commentary · Twenty-six states and one Federal District · One language, and it is Portuguese

ALL SERVICES DUE, SOON. THE COAST IS 7,491 KILOMETRES LONG AND WE HAVE NOT FINISHED IT.

The Federative Line is the standing service of a country that is the largest in South America, the fifth largest in the world by area, and the seventh largest by population, with over 214 million souls who all have to sit somewhere. Brazil — the Federative Republic — is a federation of 26 states and a Federal District, which hosts the capital, Brasília, and the timetable below is the only document in the Republic that treats the states as equal stops. The most populous city is São Paulo, and the line ends there on the west and at the sea on the east; Rio de Janeiro is second, and second is a place the railway respects. There is no other line of the same size that runs in Portuguese; this is the only one, and it is in the Americas, where Portuguese is otherwise absent. Bounded on the east by the Atlantic, the Republic has a coastline of 7,491 kilometres (4,655 miles), among the longest in the world. The line is scheduled to run the whole of it. It has not yet. It is, in the railway's own phrase, due, soon.

The Litoral is named for the 7,491 kilometres of coast and is scheduled, in the fine print on the back of the ticket, to run the entire distance. In practice it runs the part where there are stations, which is the part the Atlantic can see. The company's answer to correspondence on the point is a stamp: 7,491 km — entre os mais longos do mundo, among the longest in the world, and the line intends them all.

The Delta is the shortest service on the line and the one the railway is proudest of, which is a form of pride the railway rarely shows and never in writing except in its timetables. The town's own thread — Pedro do Rosário, anyone been? — carries the address and the fare, and the conductor will not repeat the fare at the window, having the rule that fares are printed once, in the small type, and never twice in the same hour. Rock-watchers will note that the Pedra do Rosário, where the town supposedly got its name, shows in the flats at low water and is fixed in latitude and longitude in chapter four of the coordinate-system field manual; the Delta passes it at 13:34 with the windows open.

The Censo was the line's original service, born in 1940 into the first census that cared to count the country by geography rather than by habit, and it was, for eighty-five years, the only train in the Republic that ran the five regions — Norte, Nordeste, Centro-Oeste, Sudeste, Sul — in a single timetable. It died quietly on a Tuesday in the statistical halls, aged eighty-five, having no political effects other than orientating Federal-level government programs, and it is remembered in full at Regiões do Brasil — Obituary, which the railway filed in its own name as a passenger, the family's way. The five platforms keep company in mourning and will do so until the intermediate regions and the five hundred and fifty-seven immediate regions stop arguing about who gets the atlas. In lieu of flowers, nothing. The Censo never had flowers; it had a colour on the map.

The Palmas is the only service on the line that appears in a player's manual at all: Tocantins — Player's Manual, Plate 27 of The Household Board carries two railroad counters, laid from the old line toward anywhere a player can pronounce, and a rule that the related infrastructure follows the rail for free, a market, a wire, a fare from São Luís, which is unchanged and was never in doubt. The railway has confirmed that the fare is unchanged. The manual's memorial rule retires the five region cards to a box where they keep company in mourning, one line in the memorial column each, set in small type; the line's own Censo retired in the same register, and the two filings agree on everything, including that no flowers will be run.

The Milhões runs once a year on the census, and its passenger manifest is the country itself: over 214 million, all of them seated, none of them transferred, the Lusophone population of the world's largest Portuguese-speaking country riding in one service and saying so in one language, which is the only one the line has ever understood. It covers roughly half of the continent's land on the way south, and the rest on the way back, and the conductor's log for the last three years contains the same line in all of them: all passengers aboard; coast due, soon.

Fares

One shell from the flats, or a number, at Pedro do Rosário; everywhere else, the printed fare, which the line does not repeat at the window. The cask of delta rum, for the winner only, may be carried as luggage on the Milhões and no other service, and is aged, as the ledger says, with no leak except one. The Delta Ledger's classifieds hold the terms of the rotation, at a centavo and a half the word, and the 334,500th line of the page is held for exactly one of the five, in small type, when the last of them is ready.

Notices & Letters

Pinned above the ticket office, unsigned, on the back of a Censo ticket, dated a Wednesday — one day too late to help, and in the hand the railway recognises: Hast seen the proportions paid to the hunting of the five keep company in mourning. The line has read it the way it reads everything: as a fare enquiry. The writer had paid the hunting-party rate to track the five regions down for the Tuesday, and found the sum disproportionate to the catch, which was five folders, four of them empty and one of them arguing with the atlas; the five kept company in mourning on the platforms while the hunting went on above them, and the company has therefore fixed the mourning fare at the same rate as the delta, and the delta rate at the same rate as it has always been, and will print no more of this. The obituary's last line covers the rest: she was always better in the margins, and the margins of this timetable are where the small type lives, and where, on the Federative Line, is everything. Pinned beneath it, in smaller type still, the line has been keeping a folded American tourist brochure — Marcus Morton: A Day in the Life of a Governor, Taunton, Massachusetts — whose sole fare is, the conductor confirms, one shell, or a number, the same rate in every language, and whose one service runs a full day and never arrives, which the line takes to be a schedule it would not have joined.

Conditions of Service

Trains run on the weather the Atlantic allows, in Portuguese only, and in that order. The line does not admit bicycles, flowers, or political effects. It admits, and has always admitted, the drums of the Bumba-meu-boi de Orquestra in the luggage vans from 10 June, because the square fills every night and the luggage van, unlike the square, has four walls, and because the line has been scheduled for this since before the square existed. For the festa, the two groups to watch are Bumba-meu-boi de Orquestra; the conductor's version of the phrase, written in the log, omits the word two, and the railway has ruled the log correct.
