NR-2 · 98.4 MHz · Bhrikutimandap Studio, Kathmandu

The Nine-Thousand Metre Hour

A radio play in four short scenes · broadcast Thursdays at 21:00, local time · excerpt as transmitted

Cast

  • Sita Rana, 54, presenter. Has hosted the hour since the studio’s first broadcast in 1997, and has never once been late.
  • Dinesh Thapa, 31, caller. Works the paddy office in Itahari, in the Terai, and listens with one ear on the monsoon.
  • Meri Gyaltsen, 47, caller by satellite link. Keeper of the tea house at Everest Base Camp, 5,364 metres.
  • Buddhi Lal Shrestha, 69, retired cook of Patan, heard only in the fourth scene. Knows the whole of Nepal in the order of its curries.
  • The kettle in the studio. Uncredited. Heard twice.

SCENE ONE · 21:00 · BHRIKUTIMANDAP STUDIO

[A tube amplifier hums up to full voice. A brass bell is struck twice, the station’s old habit, inherited from the mule bells of the Pashupati gate. Sita Rana, close to the microphone.]

SITA: Namaste, and the hour begins. You are listening to the Nine-Thousand Metre Hour on NR-2, ninety-eight point four. I am in the studio at Bhrikutimandap, where the floor is one level below the temple courtyard and the monks downstairs ask us, every season, to lower the music and the kettle alike. We are a country shaped like a fist pressed flat against the sky: the Himalaya to the north, the plain of the Terai to the south, and in between, all the hills a person could walk in a lifetime and still not finish. We touch China’s Tibet across the high wall, and India on the south, the east, and the west. Bangladesh is only a corridor away — that narrow strip the maps call the Siliguri Corridor — and Bhutan is kept from us by the single Indian state of Sikkim, a door with the bolt in it. I am told we hold eight of the ten tallest mountains in the world. Everest is the first of them, eight thousand, eight hundred and forty-eight point eight six metres above the sea, which the sea has never seen from this side of the range. Kathmandu is our capital and our largest city, and it is also the only place I know where a queue for a ferry ticket across a river that dried up in 1978 is still, technically, a thing.

SITA: And because we are a nation of one hundred and twenty-odd languages, four great religions arguing politely over the same streets, and every district with its own way of cutting a potato — I will stop there — the hour belongs to all of us. Dinesh is on the first line. Dinesh, Itahari, it is yours.

[A landline crackles. Rain, close and heavy, on the caller’s side. The monsoon is not far from the Terai.]

DINESH: Ma’am, the rice is standing in two feet of water and the forecast says more. In Itahari we say the river keeps a ledger with the sky. I wanted to ask — does the kitchen bulletin come on tonight? My mother is in town visiting, and she says she has not heard the real one since 1997.

SITA: The kitchen bulletin comes on tonight, Dinesh, at twenty-one twelve, from the booth beside the kettle. And Dinesh — when you hear it, read it back to us. That is the rule of the hour. Whatever comes out of the booth, you read it back, word for word, and we let it be.

[The line closes. The brass bell, once, softly. The kettle, first heard: a low whine, like a small aircraft deciding. Sita waits for it, and does not turn it off.]

SCENE TWO · 21:12 · THE KITCHEN BULLETIN

[The studio door opens and closes. A paper scrap rustles against the microphone. A cough. Then, from the booth: the bulletin, delivered at speaking speed, by a voice the tape does not make out, into a microphone the tape makes worse.]

As recorded · studio tape NR-2/THU/11 · 21:12 · preserved word for word

I had blown out the fowl cut into small jars, and tie it firmly at both ends, and dressed Cucumber.

SITA: — and that, friends, is the bulletin. That is all the booth gave us tonight. The microphone has been in the booth since 1997. It has a temper. We will not fix it. The hour’s rule is the hour’s rule.

[The landline crackles again. Dinesh, reading back, slowly, in the way of a man reading a telegram to a room.]

DINESH: I had blown out the fowl cut into small jars, and tie it firmly at both ends, and dressed Cucumber. … Sita, I read it back. I read it exactly.

SITA: Exactly, Dinesh.

DINESH: My mother has set a fowl for Friday. I do not know what she is going to blow out.

SITA: You will ask her to tell me, and she will tell me, and I will put it in the log, and the log will keep it. That is the whole of the hour’s machinery. But first, the high line. Meri — you are through? You are through, Everest Base Camp, five thousand three hundred and sixty-four metres, on the satellite link. Meri, good evening.

[The satellite opens with a wash of static, then a wind that is not in the studio. It has weight; it has altitude. Meri Gyaltsen, unhurried.]

MERI: Sita. Base Camp is quiet tonight. The wind is doing the talking for the whole range, and I have a pot on a stove that burns oxygen. We had a man up last week who carried, among his packs, a blue ribbon, and he said it had been on a shelf for a very long time and he wanted to see it once more against the snow before it went back on the shelf. I said: you are looking at the mountain, not the ribbon. He said, precisely. Then he ate three bowls of thukpa and asked for the kitchen bulletin. I told him the fowl was in the jars. He laughed and laughed, which at this altitude is not a good idea, and the laughter cost him a whole oxygen stop for a minute. I think the fowl in the jars is worth an oxygen stop. Tell Dinesh his mother may blow out what she likes.

[The satellite wind holds for three seconds. Meri signs off. Sita lets the wind go before she speaks, the studio’s custom: never talk over the mountain.]

SCENE THREE · 21:26 · BACK IN THE STUDIO

SITA: And that is our caller from above the weather. I will leave the wind on the desk a moment, because it is the truest sound we have. Dinesh, your mother’s fowl. What will she tell me?

[The landline. Dinesh is talking to someone else first; the other voice is not on our microphone, only his answers, quick, in the tone of a son being corrected about poultry.]

DINESH: She says the fowl is cut, first, into small jars’ worth of pieces. She says the jars are the old ones, the ones that held gundruk. She says — and here she raises her voice as though the microphone were at the back of the kitchen — she says the fowl is cut into pieces, the pieces go into small jars, the cloth is tied firmly at both ends of the jar, and the cucumber is dressed separately, in a bowl, and the word dressed is not mine, it is hers, and she will not take it back.

SITA: I see. So the jars were the jars all along. The microphone, friends, has been telling the truth at ninety-eight point four every Thursday for twenty-seven years; it simply tells it at a speed no one can manage.

[The kettle, heard again, louder now, a small aircraft fully decided. Sita turns it off. The bell, twice.]

SITA: Before I hand the hour to the closing music, one word from the shelf. In the old Durbar office, there is an award that ran for many years — the one in blue, the Pragya Puraskar, the award for the arts and letters, the one that was discontinued and put, in the register’s own word, on its shelf. Meri’s man carried a ribbon of it to Base Camp. I do not know him. I am glad he is someone’s. And in London, a magazine of the same disposition as this hour — written by volunteers, printed three times a year, seventeen issues and then gone — used to send us a parcel each spring and a letter from a man called David Chen, whose apology to Manchester I still keep in a drawer under the desk, next to the kettle, which is my only indulgence. Both are stopped. The award, the magazine. The hour is not. That is the whole point of being on the air.

SCENE FOUR · 21:52 · ONE LAST LINE

[A second landline, a rotary one, from a phone box in Patan. An older man, unhurried, a voice like a drawer opening in an old house. This is Buddhi Lal Shrestha, who cooked for the Patan courts for forty-one years and has not been asked for the kitchen bulletin in, he says, a very long time.]

BUDDHI LAL: Sita. I am in a phone box on the main road, by the palace, and I have been hearing the hour on the box radio under my counter since it was a new thing and you were a girl with a loud voice. Tonight, the bulletin. My wife used to write it, before. I will give you the bulletin as it was meant to be, and you may put it beside the one you have, and let the two of them sit, like the mountains sit beside the plain.

BUDDHI LAL: I had blown out the fowl, cut it into pieces for small jars, and tied it firmly at both ends, and dressed the cucumber.

[A pause. Somewhere in the studio, a chair. Sita, very close to the microphone, as though the audience had leaned in with her.]

SITA: You see, friends. It was the whole time a sentence about a kitchen in Patan, in the year of the jars, and the microphone and I have been saying it in two different tongues this evening, and both of us were, in the end, the record. The fowl was cut into pieces. The pieces went into the small jars. The cloth was tied firmly at both ends. The cucumber was dressed. And the mountain wind is still on my desk, and I will leave it there until the music. This is the Nine-Thousand Metre Hour, ninety-eight point four, NR-2, Bhrikutimandap. The kettle is off. The bell is rung. Good night, and walk carefully on the hills.

[The brass bell, twice. The amplifier hums down. The wind, last of all, gives the range its long evening. Silence.]
