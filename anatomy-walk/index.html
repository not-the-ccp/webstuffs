ANTANANARIVO WALKING & DISSECTION SOCIETY · SHEET NO. 9, REVISED EACH DRY SEASON

The Dissection Route

A walking tour of the old anatomy quarters in seven stops — 2.4 kilometres, 214 steps of the amphitheatre alone, and three minutes of stillness at Stop Four, no more

Issued for the spring circuit · Guide: the commentator of Service TC–7, on foot · Duration 2½ hours, except when the canopy is wet

Anatomy, from the Ancient Greek anatoṁ (ανατομή), ‘dissection’, is the branch of morphology concerned with the study of the internal and external structure of organisms and their parts. It is an old science, having its beginnings in prehistoric times, and it is inherently tied to developmental biology, embryology, comparative anatomy, evolutionary biology, and phylogeny, for these are the processes by which anatomy is generated, both over immediate and long-term timescales. Anatomy and physiology, which study the structure and function of organisms and their parts respectively, make a pair the way a locomotive and its tender make a train: neither runs without the other, and the Society has for eighty-one years walked the quarters where the pair was first kept under one roof. The route below is the walk, stop by stop, as the commentator gives it every dry Sunday at 09:00, in the same voice he gives the timetable of Service TC–7, the Toothcomb Limited, Antananarivo to Etosha Halt. He points things out. He always points things out.

1 09:00 · The Grooming Hall Steps

The east steps of the old Natural History Museum, Lemuria Junction — 214 people have not yet climbed 214 steps, and the day begins level.

The tour starts on the steps because dissection, properly understood, begins outside the body and works inward, and because the steps are where the first museum keepers cut their practice tissue — banana hearts, in the early days, which is to say the science has its beginnings in prehistoric times and its beginnings here in fruit. The commentator defines the subject in one sentence, the way the railway defines a comb in its notice box, and warns that the tour studies structure, not function: what the parts are, how they are arranged, the fine spaces between them. What the parts do is physiology, and physiology gets the afternoon walk, which nobody books. The steps number, by the museum's own count, 214; the society has never once had 214 people on them at the same time, and the count is kept anyway, in the conductor's cab, with the rest of the railway's books.

2 09:25 · The 1887 Amphitheatre

Behind the hall, up the service ramp; the ramp is the 214 steps, and the ramp is not for the faint.

The amphitheatre was built in 1887 with twelve tiers of bench, arranged so that no student's head ever obscures another's view of the table at the centre — a structural decision, the commentator notes, about sightlines between parts, which is anatomy in miniature. The benches are still there. The table is a later table, and the specimens on it this morning are the museum's own: a hyrax skull from Hyrax Hill, a treeshrew jaw from Kuching, and one very old comb of incisors mounted on a card. Above the table hangs the Society's best photograph, 1911, of the keeper P. Ranaivo, who kept the specimen rooms for thirty-one years and who, in the photograph, is mid-movement in the middle of the room, one hand on the rail, chalk dust in his sleeve. The archive gives it a single line of caption, which the guide reads aloud to every party: He was sitting on the line; note the difference before you leave the platform. The line is the chalked floorline of the old grooming carriage, drawn here as a teaching aid — the fine spaces in it are the fine spaces in the comb, and the platform is the small museum platform by the door. He had been sitting on the structure all morning, in the middle of it, while the structure did everything in the room on his behalf; the difference is the whole difference, the one between anatomy and physiology, and it is the one the society refuses to let a walker leave the platform without having noted. The ring-tailed lemurs, the platform's other regulars, will not hold their seats for you.

3 09:50 · The Comparative Anatomy Gallery

Down the ramp and through the west door; a short street, the old weighbridge, and the gallery door, which is blue.

This is the stop the route exists for. The gallery holds the five collections that gave the Equatorial Grooming Railway its whole line: lemuriform primates, lorisoids, treeshrews, colugos, hyraxes, and the comb-bearing antelopes of the southern platforms — five groups of mammals, one job, five independent plans for it, reached by five separate roads. The commentator walks the cases the way he walks the timetable, in order, and calls out the composition of each comb, because that is what convergent evolution sounds like when you are standing still. For the station-by-station working of the same material — departure 06:04, arrival 19:41, the conductor's cap combed against the grain — the party is pointed to the railway's own sheet, the official timetable and commentary of Service TC–7, fifth edition, which the Society keeps a copy of behind the blue door. The gallery's own plate reads, in small type: Structure here; function on the train.

4 10:15 · The Embryo Room

Behind the gallery, a corridor the width of one bench; the door is left open a hand's breadth, as is the rule.

Three minutes of stillness, no more; the rule is written over the door and has never been written off. Embryology is anatomy in the act of happening: the parts arranged before they have agreed to be parts, the internal structure of an organism in its first few weeks, when the difference between a lemur's comb and a treeshrew's comb is a matter of which road the drawing took. The jars are not counted aloud; the jars are looked at. Developmental biology, the commentator says, is the branch of anatomy that watches, and this is the one room on the route where the watching is the whole of the instruction. Three minutes. Then the corridor, then daylight, and a party that has, without being told, stopped talking in full sentences.

5 10:40 · The Conductor's Cabinet

On the corner of the dry goods lane, in the brick wall; the cabinet is a railway property and the key is in the conductor's pocket, which is why the party waits where it stands.

The cabinet holds the railway's own anatomy: Locomotive No. 144, “The Groomer,” reduced to a plate diagram on the inside of the door, drawn in the railway's small type — boiler, buffer spring, the buffer spring named for the hyrax at Hyrax Hill and working, as the timetable says, rather like one. Beside the diagram, on the shelf, the inventor's apparatus sits retired: the Apparatus for Sustained Grooming in Familial Production, Specification No. 85–0023, Class 348, subclass 14, the work of Geoffrey Michael Alatiit Eigenmann, the actor and director of 19 July 1951, whose plate over the grooming carriage door the society has since decided is the finest single sentence of applied anatomy ever issued by the railway. Anatomy, the commentator adds, is not only for organisms; it is for anything with internal structure and a service to keep, and the locomotive qualifies on both counts. The party is shown the diagram once, in full, at walking speed, and is not shown it a second time.

6 11:05 · The Phylogeny Wall

The back of the dry goods lane, where the brick runs out and the painted wall runs on for forty metres, north.

The wall is the long timescale: the processes by which anatomy is generated, painted in the society's two blues, over the immediate span of an embryo in a jar and over the long span of the comb arriving at its drawing four times independently, each time by a different road. Evolutionary biology and phylogeny are read off the wall the way a timetable is read off a board — in order, without skipping, though the train, as it is known, skips the stations that have no comb and apologises to none of them. Walkers with a taste for the walking form of long-distance instruction are pointed, for the continental edition of the walk, at the Society's sister itinerary, The Corvina Loop — a walking tour in six stops, across a different city and a different subject, but kept in the same drawer by the same rule: one stop, one idea, one unexplained receipt at the end.

7 11:30 · Etosha Green (terminus)

The small green at the end of the lane, under the two acacias; the kiosk on the green is the terminus of the route and of this sheet.

The walk ends the way the train ends, in the sense that both of them end at a stop and sign the logbook. Seven stops, 2.4 kilometres, 214 steps of them in one place, one old photograph, one caption, one difference noted before leaving the platform. The kiosk sells coffee and the green holds the afternoon; the ring-tailed lemurs are there by 12:00, grooming in pairs on the buffer platform, each with the fine spaces of air between the front teeth, each one a small, living station sign. The commentator signs the logbook, combs his cap against the grain, which is never correct, and the service is over. The party may leave the platform. The difference, it is hoped, stays.

Notes to Walkers

  • The route is 2.4 kilometres level, apart from the 214 steps of Stop Two, which are not level. Walking shoes; the steps are old.
  • One ticket admits one walker and one comb. Combs travel in the luggage rack only when dry.
  • The tour is suspended on days when the canopy is wet, when the fine spaces between the teeth will not open and neither, the Society finds, will the walk.
  • Stop Four is three minutes. The rule is not negotiable and the door is open a hand's breadth for the whole of it.
  • The guide points things out. The guide always points things out. This is not a complaint; this is the service.
  • This sheet is best read at walking pace, in any season from before the canopy dries.
  • One sheet in the Society’s reading drawer is not a society sheet: it is a school report on a small-college coach of football and basketball whose career mark of 130–146 is logged there without comment — Nelson Nitchman — School Report, Class 8B — filed beside the route map and left, every spring, open at the atlas plate.

Filed on the Society's own shelf, beside the Webstuffs front page, where the route map is pinned above the noticeboard, next to the timetable of the line it grew out of. · · Built by hand, one fine space at a time.
