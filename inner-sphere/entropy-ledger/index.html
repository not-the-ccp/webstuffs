The Inner Sphere Correspondents

Newsletter No. 31 · Fourth Quarter · Posted at the Assay Office, Oxidant Quay

The Entropy Ledger

The club’s quarterly letter on the one quantity the books of this city never record in the reverse direction.

Eleven members · Founded 1842, same year as the bridgekeepers’ union · Tea, as always, downstairs

From the Chair

Dear members — this quarter’s subject is the only entry our ledger is obliged to accept as final. You will have seen it on the notice board by the door of the assay office: eleven subjects in pencil, one in red, and if you ask the club why, the answer is the whole letter. Entropy is a thermodynamic state variable that quantifies the probabilistic distribution of accessible microstates in a system — and it is, of all the numbers we handle, the one that never comes back. The carpenter’s pencil marks this one in red.

The club has sat with it for a quarter and can report that the subject behaves. It appears first in classical thermodynamics, where it was recognized before it was ever named; then it resurfaces in the microscopic description of nature, statistical physics, which reads the same ledger in a smaller hand; and then, much to the Chair’s irritation, it turns up in information theory, in biological systems and their relation to life, in cosmology, in economics, and in the transmission of information in telecommunication, where a telegraph clerk who has never seen Valence City is nonetheless quoting it correctly.

This Quarter’s Number

The statistical reading is the one the club prefers, because it is the reading that explains our own quarter. Boltzmann writes it S = k log W: the entropy of a system is the constant “k” — 1.380 649 × 10−23 joules per kelvin, which the assay office now keeps stamped on the weighing pans — multiplied by the logarithm of W, the number of accessible microstates. A microstate is one particular seating of everything in the system such that the totals — the sums the ledger cares about — do not change. Our residents keep, on average, seventeen redox couples apiece; rearrange those couples however you like and W grows, and so, without anyone’s consent, does S.

This is why the evening crossing at the Chelation Bridge goes one way. At twenty minutes past eight the copper-chain bridgekeeper closes both holds of the ligand lamp, the electron steps from the Copper Citadel to the Iron Keep in three seconds, and the heat of the step lands in the water — and, on warm evenings, in the teakettle at the station room. Last season the ink log counted two hundred and seventeen transfers. It counted zero returns. No clerk in the city has ever booked an electron crossing back from the Reductant Slough, and the second law of thermodynamics, which states that the entropy of an isolated system left to spontaneous change never decreases, is the club’s formal word for why it will never do so. The visitor’s guide on the bridge, the citadel, the keep, and the smallness of the parties the span admits is at hand in the station room (Inner Sphere — A Visitor’s Guide); read the second paragraph slowly and you will have found the whole second law wearing a copper-chain coat.

The Chair would add, on the information business: a log line that says “two hundred and seventeen transfers, zero dropped electrons” carries very little surprise and therefore very little information. A line that said “one electron, returned, unannounced” would carry nearly infinite. The club has voted to keep the second line out of the ledger on entropy grounds. It has also voted, by nine to two, that the last grizzly of the city — “for well over a Legacy 500,” as the almanac has it — was never a member, as a creature that foraged the slag heaps in a state the books could not count.

The Ledger, this quarter

  • Meetings held: thirteen, at the assay office, Thursdays; two rained out, zero cancelled
  • Tea consumed downstairs: one hundred and forty-three cups
  • Subjects proposed in pencil: eleven; marked in red: one
  • Letters from outside: one, from the Coopers’ Lane circle, which reports its own ledger running warm
  • Dissent: the clerk insists “disorder” is a fair word; the Club rejects “disorder”

Correspondence

We enclose, for the last time, the note our correspondent in Elsewhere sends from the final halt of his twelve-month walk: the walk closes where it makes room for latecomers, the members take their tea downstairs in the station logger, and a carpenter’s pencil keeps the thread of the route marked on the wall. Our own wall, we are pleased to say, runs the same thread — one line per place, the places of the Elsewhere almanac in their monthly order — and we have agreed to mark our stop, the Bridged Quarter, at the same height as his, so that a traveller standing in either station room sees one continuous mark across the country. The almanac keeps one bridge per place; we keep one bridge and, at the end of it, one number in red.

Next meeting: Thursday, twenty minutes past eight, to watch the crossing before the tea. Members are reminded that the bridge admits no more than four walkers at a time, that parties of fifty are turned away at the gate, and that a system this small, left to spontaneous change, will reliably warm the kettle and change nothing in the ledger. — M. Voss, Chair; seconded, unhappily, by the clerk.
