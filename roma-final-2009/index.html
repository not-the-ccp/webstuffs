The Long Guide to Famous Evenings · No. 63

Rome, 27 May 2009

An evening at the Stadio Olimpico · Barcelona 2–0 Manchester United · UEFA Champions League Final

ROMA27 · 05 · 2009

Getting to the stadium

By metro, take line B to Piazzale dello Stadio, six stops from Termini, and walk the short ramp down through the tunnel that smells of turnstiles and hot tarmac. By bus, 60, 65 and 78 all run the Via Tiburtina; by car, do not. The stadium sits on the southern side of the city, at the end of the Via Tiburtina, where the avenues thin into stadium lights. On match days the metro stops are packed from mid-afternoon, and the turnstiles queue along the whole concourse like a slow river that does not hurry, because there is nowhere else to be. Forty-five thousand seats, and on this night every one of them is full.

The venue

The Stadio Olimpico was built in 1953 for the Olympic Games and has carried three decades of finals, cup ties and World Cup matches on the same two tiers of concrete. It is not a beautiful building to photograph from the outside; from the fourth tier, with the terraces of red and the sky going amber, it is one of the largest rooms in Europe. The Games it was built for never left the building: the 1960 record, cut on these same tiers between 25 August and 11 September, is kept as liner notes at The Rome Tapes (Roma 1960), and the exit on its final track runs down this very Via Tiburtina. Arrive by 19:00. Gates at 18:00. The away end holds the blue and red; the home end holds the rest of the city, including a crowd of Spanish supporters who have filled two thirds of it, and Rome, for the evening, has decided it is from Catalonia.

The evening, minute by minute

Kick-off 20:45 CEST. A warm late-spring wind, 21°C, dry. The line-up is the one the season has been building toward: Messi, Eto’o, Iniesta, Xavi on one side; Cristiano Ronaldo, Rooney, Giggs on the other. This is the only UEFA Champions League final in which Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo have ever met, and for the next ninety minutes every seat in the building is a front row for a rivalry that has just begun.

  • 10′ Eto’o. A ball poked to the edge of the area, cut inside, and driven home. The stadium exhales and the Spanish end does not stop for an hour.
  • 58′ The weather holds. The wind drops off, the floodlights begin to win, and the away end sways back and forward like a sea that keeps its depth.
  • 70′ Messi. One turn, one stride past the man, the goalkeeper, the far post. Twenty minutes from the end, the score is decided, and the noise is the loudest of the night.
  • 90′ + 3 Full time. 2–0. Barcelona have won their third European Cup and, with it, the treble — La Liga, the Copa del Rey and the Champions League in one season, a feat no Spanish club has ever achieved.

After full time

Do not go to the metro until the tunnel is thin. The Via Tiburtina will do it for you: a long blue-and-gold river flowing south past the tram tracks, past the fishmongers’ shutters, past the taxi ranks that give up pretending to have cabs. Sit instead at the bar of the hotel on the corner of Via Tiburtina and Viale Europa, where the televisions are playing the match again and the keeper of the bar keeps a small shelf of football almanacs and one thick volume of dream interpretations, the house copy, that the regulars have worn soft at the corners. The page they ask for, the one the waiter has to hold open while you photograph it, carries the strangest heading in the book: The house's dream dictionary entry on the harbor procedure of a month. No one can say what a harbor procedure of a month is. The waiter says it comes true if you order it on a Tuesday, and on the nights he means it, he means it kindly.

Elsewhere in the city the evening is quieter. The Colosseum is dark at 22:00, the Tiber is doing its slow work, and the next train back to Termini will take you home through a Rome that no longer needs explaining. If you want another matchday city kept by the same rulebook, our guide to Damac Club and Khamis Mushait knows exactly what a Saturday at the stadium smells like when the tea houses close; and the Pacific Coast Almanac, Guidebook No. 47 sets the house style for a great city’s routes and numbers, so the two entries sit well together. If you prefer a longer road, The Runaway Scrape — Route 1836 shows how a travelling crowd behaves under pressure; nothing in 1836 came anywhere near this crowd, and nothing after quite either.
