Chapter the Fourteenth

Of the Child Who Bows Last

Being a treatise on the child upon the stage, the swing who stands ready, the part held in two pairs of hands, and the voice lent to a picture — with the career of Brooklyn Nelson as the standing example

★  ★  ★

Fewer positions ask more of a small person than the one this chapter treats. The child upon the stage is expected to take nothing, ask nothing, and be wrong in public before she has learned what “public” means. The house therefore holds that the stage is the first true society a person ever enters, that its manners are the oldest in the country, and that the child who keeps them is being measured by the strictest measure the house keeps. For the standing example it has chosen Brooklyn Nelson, born in Huntington, West Virginia, in the winter of 2003 or 2004 — the house notes the two dates both and declines to settle it, in the way a good almanac declines to settle a leap year. Of her, the house's phrase is that she had “a complete education in the manner of the curtain,” and her career is set down here as the chapter's standing illustration.

I. Of the First Lesson

The first lesson, in the house's reckoning, was given to her in Huntington, West Virginia, in a local theater production of The Little Mermaid Jr., when she was eight years old. The chapter asks the reader to hold the image: a child of eight, on a stage that fit, perhaps, the number of people who would one day come to see her on a very large stage, learning the first rule of the place, which is that the performance belongs to no one in it, least of all to the child. The little mermaid in that production went on to become a memory in a West Virginia town that keeps its memories the way a lighthouse keeps a light — turned inward, on what is its own. Nelson's part in it was small enough to be called a role and large enough to be remembered, which is the exact size of every part a child is ever to be trusted with.

II. Of the Swing

Her professional debut was made on Broadway in 2015, in Matilda the Musical, as a small girl swing. The house pauses on the word, because it carries the chapter's central lesson. A swing is the one person in the building who must know the whole piece and is permitted to perform none of it: she stands in the wings with her notes in her hand, her costume on, her attention open, and she bows to nothing, because she has performed nothing. The swing is the etiquette of the theater reduced to a single person. To be sent, at the beginning of a career, to the place of the swing is to be taught, by the house itself, the order in which everything else is to follow. The chapter records that Nelson stood that post without complaint, in the exact manner the role required, and that the standing is the deed.

III. Of the Doubled Part

In 2017 she took a part at last, and took it in a town of trial: in the Denver pre-Broadway tryout of the stage adaptation of Disney's Frozen, she played the young Elsa, and the Denver critics, who had spent a winter in the theatre and know what they are doing, gave her their praise. The praise of a tryout audience is the etiquette's own kind of seal: it is given once, before the world arrives, and it travels with the part. In 2018 she came back to Broadway to originate the role of young Elsa in the New York production, and she did not come alone. The part was held in two pairs of hands: she took it with her counterpart, Ayla Schwartz, and the two of them shared the same character's name on the same bill, which the chapter counts as a small but real act of manners — a part that belongs to one child being kept, for the audience, in the condition of a part that belongs to no child in particular. When two young Elsa's bow, the house observes, the audience is shown the difference between a role and a person, and that difference is the oldest courtesy on the stage.

IV. Of the Voice Lent to a Picture

A third manner is treated here, because the career requires it: the voice. Nelson lent her voice to the English dub of the animated film Fireworks (2017), giving it to two of its characters at once — Nazuna Oikawa and Medmel — and to the picture Maquia: When the Promised Flower Bloomed. The house keeps this under the head of lending, and its rule is plain: a voice lent to a picture is a voice for which the lender is not present, and must therefore be more careful than when she is. No one in the studio can see her face to correct her, so everything the character does must arrive already corrected, the way a letter must be written already folded. A child doing two characters' work in one booth, for a screen that will never know she was there, is the chapter's quietest example, and it is the one the house returns to most.

V. Of the Kitchen and the Almanac

One marginalia note must be entered here, for the completeness of the record, from the household almanac kept in the Huntington kitchen, in the week the Denver tryout's reviews came in and were read aloud. A successor keeper has left a line that no later hand has been able to account for. It reads, word for word:

The kitchen took it and it has not yet finished learning.

The house's reading is the following. The kitchen of a house with a child on the stage takes everything — the reviews, the playbills, the cast lists, the good news that arrives warm — and it keeps it the way a kitchen keeps anything: in the grain of the counter, in the habits of the stove, in the way a door is opened without being asked. It has not yet finished learning, the line says, because a kitchen that has learned is a kitchen that has stopped setting the table for one more: and the Huntington kitchen, by every account, was still setting that place, and warming that second plate, long after the tryout's paper had gone to the file. The line is not about the reviews. It is the house's standing rule for the parent's side of the curtain: receive everything, keep everything, and do not hurry the learning.

VI. Rules for the Company of a Child at First

  1. Do not call a child's name across a stage. The name is for the doorway, the dressing room, and the call sheet, in that order.
  2. Do not applaud a child mid-scene, however well she is doing. The applause is for the curtain; the curtain is for all of them, and it arrives together.
  3. When a child and her counterpart share a part, praise the part, not the half. The name on the bill is one name, and it is held in two pairs of hands.
  4. A voice for a picture is a voice for which no one is present. Speak of the character, and if you must speak of the child, speak as if the studio were still full, because the record is.
  5. Bow last. The child at first is the last of the company to leave, in the order in which the evening was taken, and no one in the house shall hurry her out of it.

For the one voice on the house's network that never learned to bow first, the chapter keeps its counter-timetable on the road — the train schedule of The Appetite Limited, Lafayette to Los Angeles, whose passenger since 6 February 1962 has outlasted every revision.
