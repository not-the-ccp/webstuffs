FIELD DIARY · FIELD OFFICER’S POCKET COPY · 306TH FIGHTER GROUP (GROUND ATTACK), GELISSEN

Operation Bodenplatte

16 December 1944 – 2 January 1945, with a closing entry of 1947 · Gelissen, near Nijmegen, Netherlands

Wednesday, 16 December 1944 · overcast, sleet from noon

The group orders came through at 1530 with the word “blackout discipline” underlined twice, but nobody out on the apron can say why. The hangar doors are shut tight, the tanks are buried with sand, and Captain Halloway has the motor pool running its engines in the ditch between the cookhouse and the water tower — the P-47s are to be moved out of the hardstands by dark if weather permits. The weather has not permitted anything since Tuesday. There is a theory going the rounds of the sergeants that the Germans are planning a dawn strike on the Low Countries airfields for today, but the weather officer’s board says low ceiling all day, and a strike in a four-hundred-foot ceiling is just a crash with a flag on it. I have pinned this sheet where I can see it from the desk. If the theory is right, we will find out whether the board was right too.

Monday, 21 December 1944 · fog until 0940

The theory did not come to pass — there is no German strike, only fog, and the hangars have been locked for five days now, which is doing something bad to tempers. Men are taking their mess tins inside with them. I have counted the hardstands twice: seventy-four of ours on the main apron, the 414th group’s row along the east fence, and the two towed barges still waiting for parts at the north corner. If someone is keeping a diary of this the way I am, the first entry should be: the weather is the real staff officer of this war. It has vetoed more operations than any general I have met. The Germans must be suffering the same fog; it does not respect the Rhine.

Saturday, 30 December 1944 · bright, bitter, −4° at dawn

For the first time in two weeks the ceiling has stood up past ten thousand feet, and the air has that January hardness that makes the metal of the planes sing when the hangar doors swing. That should be a comfort and I cannot feel it. Word is in from the intelligence desk, low and fast, that the enemy is building a fighter force along the Dutch border and will strike at dawn “on the first suitable day.” We have waited since the 16th for a suitable day. Every officer on this airfield is doing the same arithmetic this morning, in the same order: how many minutes of warning, how many seconds to start the engines, how many doors on the east side. Rance Tully was out of the mess at 0610 with a letter in his hand, arguing with the ground captain about a ground order that had him grounded until the 5th. He is a good pilot, the best defilade work I have ever watched out of a T-6, and I told him twice that the captain’s order was a formality and he should just sign it. He laughed, called the order “a pencil in a vise,” and said he would settle it over coffee tomorrow. I do not write that down to be clever. I write it down because diaries are for the things that mattered, and this mattered.

New Year’s Day, 1945 · strike at 0530

The horns went at 0530, before the sunrise, which had not risen yet. They came out of the west in threes and fours, low, the radial engines the same sound as ours, which is the devil in the design. The Junkers dived on the east hangars and the Sika hauled straight at the main apron, and the row of P-47s along the fence took the first pass and lit up like a fuse that someone has struck too many times. By the time the last of the 414th’s fighters were rolling out — eleven of them, the rest gone — the ground crew was already under the barges. I lost count of the passes at four. The sky was full of smoke and the smoke was full of small bright things, and the sound was not an explosion but one long tearing, the way a hangar door tears when a two-thousand-pound bomb takes the hinge side. When it stopped, which was about 0620, the east apron was a field of burning wings. Our number, on what is left of it: fourteen P-47s destroyed on the ground, five more too hurt to fly, and the east hangar gone with the tooling bench and the coffee urn and the year’s whole first day. Across the Low Countries the figures are already coming in by wire — Eindhoven, Valkenswaard, Gilze-Rijen, Venlo, Maastricht — and the GHQ estimate they have printed on the flash is almost five hundred Allied aircraft on the ground. The Germans, for their part, have paid for it in their own fields and in the air; the flash says several hundred of their fighters did not get home, and a man in the intelligence hut has the count of their own losses pinned beside the count of ours with a face like a receipt. I have set the working table of our own apron, field by field, below these entries, so the numbers have somewhere to live. New Year’s Day, 1945, began in this country with four hundred and odd machines that will never take off again, and I want the record to say it in figures rather than in adjectives.

2 January 1945 · snow, light

The funeral was at noon, on the hardstands, because the church could not hold it. I will not set down the list of the dead; it is on the board. Rance was in it. The ground captain came to the line, did what a ground captain does, and the two of them shook hands over the question of the grounding order as if the pencil had never been in the vise. I kept my coffee for him. I still have it, in the tin under my bed, and I do not know why, only that a man who has lost the last good hour of the year keeps what was meant for the last good man of it.

22 June 1947 · Nijmegen, before the board of inquiry into the loss of the group’s equipment

They have called me in to give a statement, six and a half years is not quite the distance, it is only two winters longer. The chairman asked whether Corporal Tully had objected to the ground order, and whether the matter was settled before the strike. The witness beside me, a mechanic of the 414th who was on the door that morning, answered for him, and the clerk entered it word for word: Your Honor, he has made peace with the brass — died about the door. I let the line stand. It is the truest record of that morning I know: the argument was over, the coffee was made, and the man was at the door when the door came. The board accepted the statement, the equipment is written off at the figures on the table below, and the diary goes back into the pocket it has been in since the 21st, when the fog started and everything else began to matter less than the weather.
