Featured · By the Column

MANUALS, NINTH PRINTING.

For sale at the Book Room, 10 a.m.–4 p.m.: The Preparation of Problems for ENIAC, the manual in which a woman’s name now appears first on the cover, adele goldstine, with Jean Bartik second, the way the work was actually done. It is the first manual in the city written for a machine that was still, until two years ago, rewired for every new problem — panel by panel, plug by plug, until the operator knew the machine better than the map on the wall. The machine has since learned to hold a set of fifty stored instructions, and to fetch the next one without asking the room. The manual costs thirty cents; the machine, no one will say. Both are at 33rd & Walnut. The Book Room keeps the dust jacket on the first printing, in case anyone wants to see the cover with the name on it before the world caught up.

Book Room, Moore School Annex · counter payment only · no manual leaves without a stamp

Situations Vacant

COMPUTER OPERATORS, TWO (WANTED, EITHER HAND).

To stand before a thirty-tonne cabinet of vacuum tubes and keep it running, 10 to 6, seven mornings out of ten. Must read a panel of 7,200 tubes the way a switchboard girl reads a board; must not flinch at the sound it makes when it thinks. Training at the bench; pay above the city rate, because the machine remembers the name of anyone who has ever kept it warm.

Apply: Operations Office, second floor, name on card, no name on card if you are the one the machine is named for

BOOKKEEPER, ONE.

Goldstine & Associates, 215 Chestnut St. — figures for the Bureau of Ordnance, checks balanced to the tube, one machine in the office the size of a filing cabinet that runs at 366 words a second and is not to be touched after Friday.

References: two, one of them a professor, neither of them the machine

For Sale

FIFTY PUNCHED-CARD INSTRUCTIONS, STORED PROGRAM.

Nearly new — from the first city machine sold in the country that will take a whole program off the card table and hold it in the cabinet, so the machine runs to the end without a rewiring crew. Fifty cards, one problem, no reprogramming between. Sold as found; buyer may never need them, which is the point of selling them.

PANEL LAMPS, 41, REMOVED GENTLY.

From the arithmetic unit, 1950, each lamp witnessed at least one division that came out right.

No trade in toggle switches · lamps boxed in the original cloth

Exchange

TOGGLES, 1,440, EITHER POSITION, WANTED IN TRADE FOR CALENDAR.

Will trade the machine’s toggle switches, one per instruction, for an undated wall calendar, the sort that fits beside a panel and never says what month it is. A calendar that says nothing is a calendar the machine can agree with.

RECIPE, ONE, FOR TRADE.

Pancakes for a panel of lamps still warm. The kitchen is not responsible for the machine; the machine is not responsible for the kitchen; both are on the same floor.

Exchanges at the second-floor stair, mornings

Lost

LOST: ONE PENCIL, MEDIUM HINGE, FROM THE INSTRUCTION TABLE.

Lost between 3 April and the morning it was found in a coat pocket, which is not the same as found, and is the only pencil in the building that has written a program down before the machine had one. Whoever returns it is told nothing, because the pencil does not say anything and does not want to be the one who does.

LOST: ONE AFTERNOON, 1946, ENTIRE.

Lost in the last room of the first run. Not claimed. Kept on file anyway, in case it walks back in wearing a lab coat.

Finder’s fee: one hour at the machine, unsupervised, which the Board finds generous

Found

FOUND, IN THE GREY COAT AT THE SWITCHBOARD.

One folded map, 277,620.91 square kilometres — in which a single sentence copied in pencil: gone, gone, gone . The map shows no territory any surveyor in the annex will name, the sentence is written in a hand the switchboard knows, and the map has been sitting in the coat pocket since the day the machine first held its fifty instructions and kept them without being asked. The coat belongs to the woman whose name is now on the manual. She has not asked for the map back, which is the reason this notice exists at all: to say that the map may be left, or kept, or pinned above the panel, whichever the hand that wrote gone, gone, gone intends. The Ledger holds both, and takes no fee either way.

Claim or leave: second floor, grey coat, third hook from the stair

Situation Wanted

A. GOLDSTINE SEES MACHINE PROBLEMS, LARGE AND SMALL.

Born 21 December 1920; the arithmetic since then has been her own. Will take problems from any office that has a machine, a wall of switches, or only a pencil and a folded map, and will return the answer with the fifty instructions inside it, if it has fifty instructions. No problem declined for being the first of its kind; this is a specialty.

Letters to the Annex, care of the Book Room, no initials needed — the name is on the cover now

The switchboard files this notice under a longer shadow, in the Ledger’s Book of Dreams, beside the machine whose problems it first saw, large and small, in 1822 and never lived to finish: Babbage, Charles — Dream Dictionary, Entry B-9.

In Memoriam

THE OLD ENIAC PRACTICE OF REWIRING, 1945–1949.

She did not die so much as stop being necessary, which the switchboard keeps a file on under mercies. Twenty thousand patch cords to the scrap shelf; the cord bag retired to the coat room beside the grey coat, where it is still warm on cold mornings, everyone agrees, and no one will say why.

One lamp lit at the panel each December, by whoever is on

Registry & Record

KATZ, ADELE — MOVED, BY MARRIAGE, 1945, TO: GOLDSTINE.

The move was entered the same day and costs nothing, per the registry’s standing rule; the name Goldstine has stood in the register ever since, hyphen unneeded, order unchanged, and it now heads the manual, the bookkeeping office at Chestnut Street, and this column. The registry keeps a sheet on the surname itself — its hazards, its storage, its long survival — and the Ledger has pinned it to the second-floor stair beside the calendar trade: Surname — Safety Data Sheet No. ONM-1723.
