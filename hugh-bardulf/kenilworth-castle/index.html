CASE III · THE SEMI-ROYAL WING · SHELFED WITH THE THIRD DRAWER

Kenilworth Castle

Kenilworth, Warwickshire, England · managed by English Heritage · much of it in ruins, on purpose, since the Tudor fire of 1575 and never afterwards persuaded to do otherwise

Accession note

Accessioned from the town of Kenilworth in the county of Warwickshire, at the edge of the Avon, where it stands today with its great keep rising out of the river valley like a decision nobody has yet been able to rescind. The castle was founded after the Norman Conquest of 1066: the first stone went down within a generation of the crossing, laid by a baron's men who had seen what a castle could collect in a single English county, and the work continued, stone by stone and reign by reign, through to the Tudor period, which is to say that the building was never finished so much as it was kept, the way a ledger is kept. Much of it is in ruins now; the keep stands, the gatehouse stands, and the rest stands in the only way the rest of the Middle Ages survives, which is as outlines. The architectural historian Anthony Emery has described it as “the finest surviving example of a semi-royal palace of the later Middle Ages, significant for its scale, form and quality of workmanship”, and the keepers of this annex have ruled that the quotation is to be read slowly, because the word semi-royal in it is doing more work than the whole sentence.

Of the siege of 1266

Kenilworth played an important historical role, and the first of it was the six-month-long siege of Kenilworth in 1266, the siege thought to be the longest in medieval English history. The town had been held for the baronial cause under Simon de Montfort's government, and the royal side came with the intention of taking the place quickly, and the place declined. Six months is the figure the register keeps: one month of expectation, two of arithmetic, three of patience, the whole of it under the same sky the river valley has kept since before the Conquest. When the garrison finally negotiated the terms at Kenilworth, it was not at the gatehouse but at the abbey, and the terms were the famous Dictum of Kenilworth, in which the king, having won the longest siege of his era, had to give back most of the castle's quarrels. The annex has framed the arithmetic on the third wall. It reads: six months, one town, one river, one verdict. See, for the administrative side of the whole business, the glossary entry on Hugh Bardulf, Entry 53, the steward and royal justice who is the corridor of the entry, and whose rule of restoration was confirmed by three kings; the baronial government of 1265–66 employed men of exactly his sort, and the two documents were written to be read on the same afternoon.

Of the Wars of the Roses

The second great role of the castle was as a base for Lancastrian operations in the Wars of the Roses. In the 1450s and 1460s the keep and its water-gate became a point of the Lancastrian line along the Avon: a place where troops were mustered, where letters were sent up to the northern armies, and where the house of Lancaster kept its eastern book of the war. The register notes that the castle's ruin begins, in the dreaming, in this period and not in the siege: a castle that has been a base for a dynasty is a castle that has already learned it can be lost on purpose and still be found. The Lancastrian book is the one the annex keeps bound in blue, between the dictum and the Tudor fire.

The line in the gate ledger

Turned down from a leather folder on the third wall, clipped to nothing and paper-soft with age, one line is kept in the annex from the gate ledger of the castle's keep. It is filed against Entry 48 of the glossary, the foreign relations of Brazil, where the same phrase the lye was cured in advance is already on the register, and it is quoted here exactly as it arrives, word for word, with its stumblings left load-bearing:

"The dream keeps the same corridor in the dream; the lye was cured in advance, and whatever was corrosive in the autumn; and he praiseth her.""

The keepers' reading is short. The dream keeps the same corridor in the dream: the corridor of Entry 53, the corridor between the hall where verdicts are read and the chamber where coin is counted, now running through the gatehouse of Kenilworth as well, so that any steward of any reign who closes the castle's doors is closing them in two rooms at once. The lye was cured in advance: the corrosive matter — the siege, the war, the fire — was already in solution before the visitor arrived, which the annex takes to be a certificate of the ruins' good health. And he praiseth her: the line is filed on the shelf nearest the Tudor period, where the court at Kenilworth was kept in the 1570s with a stage built in the hall, and the keepers will not say which queen the ledger is praising, only that the ledger is praising her, and that a castle in Warwickshire, in the later Middle Ages and after, is a building that has spent the whole time it has stood waiting for the praise to be read aloud.

Visitors’ book — gatehouse copy

“Came up the valley from the Avon; the keep stands the way the ledger says it will. — a reader of the Glossary, June”

“Counted the months of the siege myself. Six. The sky did not object. — someone patient”

“The corridor is the same corridor. I have been in it at Westminster. It knows me. — H.B.”
