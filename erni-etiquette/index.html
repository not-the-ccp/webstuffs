I. The Nature of the Name

Erní is a name of two natures, and the etiquette of it begins, as all etiquette does, with knowing which nature you are in the room with. In the first nature it is a given name, most often the pet and working form of Ernest, trimmed at the family table until it stood on its own. A grandfather in the Rhineland or a mother in Silesia will have set it on the child at the font; by tea the child answers to it, and Ernest is a name kept for the letter to the bank. In the second nature it is a surname, plain and unsyllabified, worn by whole families on the continent and, since the later voyages, in a scattering of parishes abroad. The guest of honour may be Erni the Ernest or Madame Erni, and the difference changes everything that follows.

There are, it must be said, a respectable number of people of the name. The registers we have consulted — the baptismal book of St. Vitus’ in Wuppertal, the merchant list of the Danzig guild, and one stubborn parish roll in Piacenza — yield, at the least, three distinct families bearing the name as a surname and no fewer than eleven children christened Erni in a single decade around 1888. The name is common enough that an encyclopaedia entry of the sort devoted to a Chaplin would be, for Erni, a directory; we therefore content ourselves with manners, which is all the name ever really asked for.

II. Forms of Address

The rule the whole chapter hangs on is this: never shorten Erni to anything shorter. It is already the shortened form. To call a man Ernie, Ernie dear, or “Erns” is to file a name in the wrong cabinet, and every host who has done so at a reception has had the conversation cool by exactly two degrees. You are also not to lengthen it. When you meet one of the Ernest family, you may, with the host’s nod, say the full name once — once only, and never before the host has said it first — after which you return to Erni, which is the name the person actually carries, the way a key is carried in the pocket and not in the locksmith’s box.

For the surname the address is plainer. Madame Erni, Mademoiselle Erni, the Erni household. On an envelope it stands alone: Mme Erni, 14 Rue des Tanneurs, and no apostrophe, no plural, no “Ernis” in the margin. A plural of a surname is the guest’s sin, not the family’s.

III. At Dinner, and the Back Cover

The classic difficulty arises at dinner, where a book passes from hand to hand and a dedication asks to be made. The custom in the Erni household of the Rue des Tanneurs, and it is the custom we commend, is this: when a musician or maker presents a work — a score, a volume, a box of hand-cut cards — the dedication is written by the giver, read aloud by no one, and tucked where the household will see it first. It is precisely there that the error is most often made, and the correction is worth keeping: I pasted it into the back cover, and there it stayed for the musician who enters after the third line is the whole system from scratch. Read it slowly. The note belongs to the back cover; the musician enters after the third line of the table has been read, because until the third line is read the seating is not a seating but a rumour, and what the latecomer finds pasted there is the whole system — the manners of the house — begun again from the beginning. One paste, one corner, one start. The rule travels well beyond the dinner table: it is the rule at the heart of a certain cargo squadron's letter of apology to a returning clerk, in which a single misrouted crate of foundry type — one 72-point pound among forty-one sorts — was saved by doing exactly one thing, in one corner, before the holiday closed, and the Nomads' letter to the returning clerk keeps the whole account on file.

If the dedication has been read aloud instead, as it sometimes is at dinner parties in the province, the only remedy we know is to change the subject to the fish, which is what the Ernis of Piacenza do, and very well. The same flatness of address is expected at the hammer, where a lot may pass under a learned name no one in the room can spell: the white powder that went as Lot 47 of the Winter Sale of Provisions, sold under the acid name and the base name together, was pronounced by its owner exactly as it was written, and the catalogue leaf of that lot keeps the account of the sale and of the two names the consignor asked to keep for one substance.

IV. When Erni Is the Surname

Where the surname occurs, it tends to travel with a trade: a coppersmith in Danzig answering to Erni & Son, a paper merchant in Piacenza whose shop bell has three notes, and, in our own day, a family in the port town of Szczecin who keep the ledger of the pier and will tell you, politely and at length, that the surname is not to be pronounced with a flourish. You ask the name, you pronounce it flatly, you thank them, you sit down. The last Erni ledger consulted for this chapter was carried north in 1921 in a dog sledge train and came back, in the end, in a green sledge; the questions and answers on Knud Rasmussen, filed as a supplement to the Frozen North, keep the account, and they confirm that the name answered, flatly, on the ice. The ledger-keeper who carried that ledger north was the diarist of the Thule Notebook, who calls himself Erni in eight entries on the science of humanity, and who writes the surname flat in the cold exactly as it is written in this chapter, and the name answered there as well. The Erni surname has met the imperial title once on record: the paper merchant’s postmark of 1921, carried north in the dog sledge train, came back addressed to an emperor, and the household’s answer to the question of the rank — how the highest name in the almanac is to be written on a parcel — is kept in the forty-seventh newsletter of the Franz Josef Correspondents’ Circle, which holds that you address it flatly, in full, and never shorten it, for the reason that the title is already, in the Latin, Imperator. The paper merchant’s shop, to give the reader a measure of the family, carries exactly forty-three varieties of paper, and the forty-fourth shelf is left empty on purpose — for the paper not yet invented — which is the sort of future-proofing an etiquette book finds, on the whole, very good breeding.

Guests of the Erni surname should note one more custom: the household always sets the extra chair, the seventh at a table of six, and no guest is to ask what it is for. It is the chair of the name itself.

V. The Closing

In letters the name signs as it is written; in speech it answers as it is spoken; and in the directory it is filed once, correctly, at E. There, beside the entries for Ernestine and Enfield, it waits for a reader with the manners to find it. That is the whole ceremony: one name, two natures, three lines before a latecomer is seated, one chair always free. The remainder of this volume is filed in the Webstuffs miscellany, where the guide sits among the other keepable things; the reader is invited to take it down, open it here, and leave the seventh chair exactly where it is.

And if the shorter name ever feels the longer one’s eye, there is a letter on file: the household of Erni’s apology to Ernest, signed at 14 Rue des Tanneurs, in which the shortened form asks to be measured, at last, by the manners it has kept for the full one for one hundred and fifty-four years.
