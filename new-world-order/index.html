Chapter the Seventh

Of the Shield Put Away & the Two Who Adjusted

Being a treatise on the conduct owed to a flag, a shield, and a long absence — the Smithsonian of Washington, the bank at Delacroix, and the manners of those who came back from the Blip.

✦   ✦   ✦

Along absence changes the shape of a room. The Blip of 2019 took half the world out of it for five years, and when those years were given back — after Avengers: Endgame, and after the quiet that followed — the rooms had been kept, the chairs had been filled, and the meals had been eaten without them. The first rule of return is this: you do not ask where the time went, and you do not claim the chair that has been occupied in your name. Sam Wilson — Anthony Mackie on the screen, the man himself in this ledger — came back to Washington, D.C., and took up the counselling of returned veterans; Bucky Barnes — Sebastian Stan in the casting — came back to Brooklyn and made a list of the doors he needed to knock on.

I. Of the Shield and Its Ceremonial

The shield is round, of vibranium, painted with the star and the stripes, and it belonged to Steve Rogers, who carried it through the war and the years after. On the day of the ceremony Sam Wilson carried the shield into the Smithsonian and laid it in the case, with thanks and without a scene. The etiquette of laying down a symbol is short: thank the one who gave it to you, name the one who carried it before you, do not suggest that the case is empty, and do not watch to see who is handed it next. The name who would carry it next — John Walker, played by Wyatt Russell — is not this chapter's business; this chapter's business is the laying down, which was written by Malcolm Spellman and directed by Kari Skogland, and first shown in March 2021 as the opening hour of six in The Falcon and the Winter Soldier.

II. Of New York and of Delacroix

In Brooklyn, Bucky Barnes kept his therapy appointments, knocked on the doors of his amends list, and once went on a date with Yori Watanabe and spoke of nothing he had done in the years he cannot speak of. The etiquette of amends: go to the door, say the name, do not blame the absence, and do not expect the door to open wide. In Delacroix, Louisiana, Sam Wilson sat with his sister Sarah in the bank's office and asked for a loan on the family boat, and the banker's figures showed five years with no income, no collateral, and a man the register had marked as gone. The etiquette of refusal: take the no at the door, thank the banker, leave as you entered, and do not return with a louder voice.

Provisioning for a long passage is its own etiquette, and the old manual's rule runs thus: For the long passage I packed the store: a crock of potatoes, a jar of pickles, a court order. A court order is the paper that establishes you are still expected somewhere; a crock and a jar are what you eat on the way there. When the summons came — a hostage taken, Georges Batroc in the air over Tunisia, and the military calling its wingman back to uniform — Sam Wilson answered in uniform, and Joaquín Torres met him at the gate with the paperwork of the present day. The long passage packs light: a uniform, a shield laid down, and the papers that say you are coming back.

III. Rules for the Returned

  1. A shield returned is not a shield repudiated; it is laid in its case, and the name of the carrier goes on before the name of the keeper.
  2. Five years' absence is answered with silence about the years; you do not ask the returned where they were, and you do not tell them the world has moved on — they know.
  3. A refusal at the bank is taken without argument; the figures are the figures, and the door is the door.
  4. A flag is flown above the building, never used to shut its door, and the stripes are kept in their order: red, white, and blue, in that sequence, nowhere else.
  5. The table is laid for two even when one chair has stood empty for five years, and the empty chair keeps its place at the head of the row until its occupant says otherwise.

The cardinal rule, the one the house keeps at the front desk: whatever was carried across a long absence must be named before it is put away, and whatever is put away must be thanked.

The shelf also holds a transport sheet for the longest passage of all — Cyril & Methodius, Safety Data Sheet No. 869 of the St. Panteleimon Press — which carries a pair of Byzantine brothers across a sea, a synod, and a see, and teaches in its thirteenth section the very rule this chapter preaches: name what was carried before it is put away, and thank it. And when the chapter is closed, the lamps are put out one by one — return to the front page of the shelf.
