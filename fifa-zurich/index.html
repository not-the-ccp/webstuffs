FIFA, Zürich

Fédération Internationale de Football Association — French for the International Association Football Federation; the federation, the headquarters, and the whole of the walk described in this entry.

FILED ZÜRICH 21 · V · 1904

At a glance

Founded
21 May 1904, at the Hôtel Crillon, Paris
Headquarters
FIFA House, Bahnhofstrasse 155, 8001 Zürich
Members
211 national associations
Confederations
six — CAF, AFC, UEFA, CONCACAF, CONMEBOL, OFC
Games kept
association football, beach soccer, and futsal
First World Cup
Uruguay, 1930

Getting there

By rail, the way the guide prefers: from Zürich Hauptbahnhof, walk the length of Bahnhofstrasse until the Limmat turns it, and turn with it — the House stands at No. 155, and the number is not an accident so much as a habit of the street. By air is also possible, through the airport on the lake's eastern arm, and the guide will not say more about it than that it is fast, high, and not football. But the honest route, the one the guide has taken and recommends, is by sea and then by dream: south, to Santiago, to the hill where the Estadio Nacional stands, where a whole city lies down in a single blue sheet — the blue of Universidad de Chile, older than the club on the crest of 1943, the blue the sleeper's hands come away tinged with when the whistle goes. The federation governs that blue as it governs the rest: not by touching it, but by keeping the number of doors honest. The concordance's own entry on Universidad de Chile is the better ticket; the reader is referred there for the whole of the blue, which this guide only passes through.

By sea from the north, take warning from the fog. The guide keeps the yard's entry on file: six hundred and thirty-eight acres of San Francisco waterfront behind two graving docks, sixteen white hulls that never sailed, a fog that never burns off — the Dream Dictionary's account of Hunters Point. Whatever the vessel, if the fog is that thick, the federation's door is on the far side of it, and the reader should stop counting hulls and start counting confederations instead. There are six of them, and they hold, the way a quay wall holds, for the whole of the league of nations of football.

The institution

On 21 May 1904 the national associations of Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, and Switzerland met to found the body now called FIFA — an international self-regulatory governing body of association football, beach soccer, and futsal, meaning it answers to no one above itself, which is a rare and load-bearing property for a building. It was founded to oversee international competition among those eight founding associations; its membership now comprises 211 national associations, and this entry is numbered for them, the reader will note, rather than for the street. The arithmetic is the point: every one of the 211 is also a member of exactly one of the six regional confederations, the six doors of the house — CAF for Africa, AFC for Asia, UEFA for Europe, CONCACAF for North and Central America and the Caribbean, CONMEBOL for South America, and OFC for Oceania. The World Cup is the house's great public occasion, gathered up whole every four years since 1930, when Uruguay first took the field and first kept the cup; the federation's business in the interval is keeping the other 210 doors aligned with the one, and keeping the calendar honest enough that 2030 can be a centenary, played a hundred years after the first, in the South American countries that founded it.

The museum

The museum keeps on Bahnhofstrasse what the House keeps in its registers: the trophies, the photographs, and the long ledger of every final since 1930, which is to say the whole of the game's public memory arranged by four-year intervals. The guide has counted the cases and found them generous to the small nations, which is where the 211 shows itself best — the OFC case is short but not shabby, and the beach-soccer case sits by the window where the light of the street actually falls on it. Visitors should allow two hours, which is the time the guide allows, and more if the football takes hold of the reader, which it does; the museum is built to be left in a particular mood, the mood of a walk that has not yet decided whether it was about football or about the walk, and the reader should decline to resolve it on the steps.

Provisions, for the walker

The pocket guide keeps a standing provision list in the back pocket, copied from the old cards the movement carries, and the reader is offered the card as it stands, the line kept word for word as it was set down in the Pioneer Cookbook — that shelf of woodcraft recipes which exists at all because a certain American scout, Frederick Russell Burnham, taught woodcraft to Robert Baden-Powell in Rhodesia and the handbook took the whole shelf into itself. The Trailhead Forum has the thread on the scout in full: Frederick Russell Burnham — Scout and Woodcraft, Thread No. 3174. From the card, the line that has served the guide on every long walk since:

Provision list · card 4 · kept in the back pocket

ARROWROOT.--This nutritious fecula is obtained from various kinds of plums, 1/4 lb.

The quarter pound is not the issue; the issue is that the card was written by a person who walked, and that the guide has since found, on the long walks between the federation and the other ends of the confederation's reach, that no other provision list has held so well at altitude. The plums are a matter the guide declines to argue about with the card.

The kitchen interpretation

Every entry in this collection ends at the table, and the travel guide is no exception, for a body that governs 211 associations is ultimately a body that feeds them. Where the walk ends in a kitchen — and on this route it does, without the reader's agreeing — the federation is asking for something to be set and let stand. The instruction the guide gives, in the voice the concordance has always used, is this: Butter a mould, and pour over it before the sauce will be done through. The mould should be blue — the blue the sleeper's hands come away tinged with, the blue of the crest of 1943, which the federation's whole apparatus keeps in stock whether it means to or not — or the walk will not close. When the sauce is through, the table is set: 211 seats, six doors to it, one whistle at the head. This is the whole of the domestic meaning of the entry, and it is a good one.
