Loaf for the Long Innings

Oat, molasses and brown-butter, one tin of the kind that fits the attic shelf beside the car · makes one loaf, feeds four, survives a full day of play

KEEP

The long preamble, as the card requires

The tournament this loaf is kept for is the Cricket World Cup: every four years, the International Cricket Council sends its full members out to play a world championship in One Day Internationals — fifty overs a side, a whole innings to fit into one day, and, since the ICC calls it the flagship event of the international cricket calendar, a whole household to fit into it as well. It is one of the most watched sporting events there is, and in this house the watching has a set table. The first men's World Cup was run in England in June 1975, four years only after the first ODI was ever played, in a format the sport had never seen before and did not quite forgive itself for; a separate Women's World Cup had in fact been held two years earlier, in 1973, and as far back as 1912 a triangular tournament of Test matches had already put multiple nations in one ground and left them wanting a cup. The sport had been keeping the habit long before it had the habit’s name. That is why the loaf has always come before the rulebook: the family does not wait for the format to settle. Grandfather Whitmore entered the card in the summer of 1976, the year after England 1975, the year his own ground out back — the wicket the dog had improved, the stumps a pair of broom handles — produced its first family Test, and the year the West Indies beat Australia in the final at Brisbane by seventeen runs, a margin of exactly one over, which is the same as one lap, and this house noticed and has never let anyone forget it.

The rule of the house on a World Cup day is written in the margin of the card, in the same hand as the recipe: one loaf, one day, one table, and nobody leaves the room until the last over is bowled or the drip over the garage bench below has run out of beam, whichever is later. The loaf must be baked before the opening session starts, because a World Cup innings does not stop for a rising oven, and the loaf must be cut, not torn, because tearing is what happens when the side you are watching is the side that has lost, and the knife is the argument the family uses against the argument. The tin is the same tin the Chief Mechanic keeps the first-press Layla sleeves in up in the attic; it is a round tin, the right size for one slow loaf and, on the shelf in the boathouse, the right size to stand beside the green car while it waits under canvas for the May listening. One part per meeting applies to bread as it applies to chassis: you do not rush the crumb, and you do not flip the needle, and the two sentences were written by the same person at the same kitchen table on the same June afternoon and are not to be separated.

On the evening of 4 November 2023 the last completed World Cup, the one in India, ended the way the card was made for it to end: the kitchen dark to the one light, the attic playing whole side into side, the four of us around the tin with the knife going in at the seam. The reel counter on attic unit No. 2 was read before and after, as the build log in the garage below requires of everything it runs by, and it gave back the number 061214 again, which is not explained and will not be, and the same drip that keeps the bench’s four seconds kept the loaf’s rising without once being asked to. The listening wing upstairs has its own account of the night, filed at Placard No. 48, Gallery 3, The Listening Wing, where the case reads off its one line in the dark and the committee reports the same beat in a different key; the kitchen’s account is shorter and is this card, and the two accounts agree that the whole thing is what it feels like in the dark, and the dark, at that hour, is the one light. The club signs off at the front desk of Webstuffs, in order, in whole, and this card is filed with them where the other forty-six cards of the book are, in the round tin, where a loaf goes.

Ingredients, for one tin

  • 300 g plain flour, the bag with the square of tape on it
  • 200 g rolled oats, whole, for the crumb that keeps the day going
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder, level, no more, it is not a sprint
  • 100 g dark molasses, the tin by the window where it keeps its colour
  • 100 g brown butter, browned the colour of the 1962 car, which is to say very, with one rind of nutmeg in it and taken out
  • 150 ml buttermilk, warm to the hand the way June was in 1975
  • 1 teaspoon golden syrup, for the seam, not the crumb
  • 1 pinch of salt, the pinch the Chief Mechanic takes, who takes everything that pinch

Method, in order, in whole

  1. Warm the tin by the window an hour before you begin. A World Cup day does not start at the over, it starts at the room.
  2. Mix the flour, oats, baking powder and salt in the big bowl. Leave the lumps. The lumps are the innings.
  3. Melt the butter low until it browns the colour of a racing car, and pour it, still hissing, over the molasses and the buttermilk, and stir in the golden syrup last, because the seam keeps its own secret.
  4. Work the wet into the dry until it holds together and no further. Forty-seven turns, counted aloud, because the count is a method here and the number is the newsletter’s and will not be changed.
  5. Turn it into the warm tin, press the top smooth with wet fingers, and set it in the oven at 180° for fifty minutes. Fifty overs, one day, one loaf. The oven door stays shut like the needle stays on.
  6. While it bakes, carry the tin up to the attic and set it on the shelf beside the car, where the drip can keep time over it from below. This is the step the card exists for: Bring the record, if you have the record. Play it whole, side into side, and do not flip, because the loaf, the car and the needle are all the same kind of thing and none of them does well interrupted.
  7. When the last over ends — or the beam runs dry, whichever is later — read the reel counter and set the number down in the margin, unexplained, and cut the loaf at the seam with the knife, not your hands.
  8. Serve on the plate from the good cupboard, four pieces, the fourth piece the one that stays on the tin for whoever is watching alone on the next night. That piece is not optional. It is the next edition, in 1976 as in now, and the tin keeps it the way the attic keeps everything else: in order, in whole, in the dark with one light.

Entered 1976 by A. Whitmore, re-inked 2023 by the Chief Mechanic, who was appointed by no one. The book is kept in the round tin. The tin is on the shelf. The shelf is in the boathouse. The boathouse is below the attic, above the garage, in the middle of the house, and the house is the whole point.
