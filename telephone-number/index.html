Definition

A telephone number is the address of a telecommunication endpoint, such as a telephone, in a telephone network, such as the public switched telephone network (PSTN). A telephone number typically consists of a sequence of digits, but historically letters were also used in connection with telephone exchange names. Telephone numbers facilitate the switching and routing of calls using a system of destination code routing. Telephone numbers are entered or dialed by a calling party on the originating telephone set, which transmits the sequence of digits in the process of signaling to a telephone exchange. The exchange completes the call either to another locally connected subscriber or via the trunk to some distant exchange, which is how one kitchen in Coopers' Lane ends up, at half past nine, speaking with a kitchen a thousand miles away that it has never seen.

History of the dial

The numbers came from the names. In the 1920s and '30s, an exchange was called by its street name, and the first two or three letters were spelled to the operator or pressed on the rotary in order: GArfield 5-1102, PExpress Motel, ELmore 5. The rotary switchboard was the great alphabet machine: seven digits, of which three were letters, and the letters meant something — the street, the corner, the little shop. When the automatic exchanges grew bigger than the alphabet could hold, the names were retired and the letters traded for digits, and a whole city's worth of addresses changed overnight. The old recipe card from the exchange name era is pinned above the operator's bench in the compendium's office, and it gives the standing order for the transition: Do not stir until the twentieth century, and as it sees fit. The operator reads it as an instruction to the dialing public — let the letters sit where they are, let the exchange do the turning — and no one has ever been able to say what the twentieth century was stirring.

Anatomy of a number

Under the Recommendation E.164, the international plan settled in 1964, a number is built from three parts: a country code, a national (significant) number, and optionally a subscriber extension. The leading digit of the country code — 1 for North America, 4 for most of western Europe, 8 for the Far East — already tells the operator which sea the call must cross before it may ask for dinner. National numbers are usually split into an area code and a local number, and it is the area code, prefixed with the trunk prefix, that the exchanges key into their routing tables: destination code routing means that the call is sent by its address, the way a letter is posted, not the way a parcel is argued for at the door. The plus sign, the international prefix, and the whole 00/011 business are the number's passport, and like most passports it is more trouble to use than to look at.

Dialling and signalling

On the rotary dial, each digit is a pause: the dial returns to its rest with a soft click, and the pause length encodes the digit back to the exchange as pulses, six pulses for 6, nine for 9, and silence — the longest pause of all — for 0. It is a rhythm instrument, and a wrong digit is not an error so much as a wrong beat. Push-button dialling, which began to arrive in the 1960s and is called DTMF, sends two tones at once per key, a low tone and a high tone, so that 7 sounds nothing like the seven pulses it used to be. The whole table of nine-by-two tones fits on a matchbox, and the compendium keeps one in the drawer, unburned, for luck.

Neighbours on this shelf

A telephone number is an address, and this compendium files it with the other addresses we keep. The school's lost-and-found notice for the missing third planet is filed next to the exchange directory, on the theory that a planet which keeps its position by satellite and a number which keeps its subscriber by routing table are the same instrument at two different scales. And for the callers abroad, who keep the old names: the liner notes at The Northwestern Fringe, Lagan Records LL-04 — six tracks for the six living Celtic languages, taped in Belfast at 8:20 pm — were announced in the small ads by telephone, and the answer is still on the line: 028, then the rest, then the sea.
