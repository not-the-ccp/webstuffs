§9.1 — General

Huddersfield is a town in the Metropolitan Borough of Kirklees, West Yorkshire, England. It is the administrative centre and largest settlement in the Kirklees district, standing in the foothills of the Pennines where the valley narrows and the buildings lean in close. The town grows along a floor of river gravel and is banked on three sides by steep, field-cut slopes. A unit entering from the south meets open moorland within two miles; a unit entering from the north meets the Pennine road and, beyond it, the long ridge. All approaches converge on St Peter's Square, the paved knot of the town centre, which is treated in this manual as the reference point (RP 00) for all bearings, distances, and festival movements.

The streets of the centre are narrow, cobbled in places, and oriented to the water rather than to each other. Do not attempt to drive a long vehicle into the square; the square is for pedestrians, mops of wet umbrellas, and the stewards.

§9.2 — Hydrology

The town is defined by three rivers. The River Holme comes down from the south and meets the similar-sized River Colne in a confluence just to the south of the town centre. The combined stream runs north through the centre and leaves it before joining the River Calder on the north-eastern outskirts of the town. The three together drain the whole of the festival ground: no event site sits more than four hundred metres from running water.

This water is soft, and its softness is the reason the town exists in its present size. In the Victorian era the mills drew it through the weaving sheds in quantities that turned the whole valley white with cloth. Textile treatment demanded it; the sheds drank it for a century. Huddersfield was a prominent mill town and enjoyed an economic boom in the early part of the Victorian era Industrial Revolution. The sheds still stand, several of them converted, and one of them — the former weaving shed on the north side of the Colne — hosts the festival's breakfast discussion each spring. It is the one room in the town where the industrial past is served, hot, at 10:00 with a pot of tea.

Field note: at a low spring tide of water, the confluence shows both streams running side by side for some two hundred metres before they fully mix. The Colne is the larger on paper. The Holme is older. Neither will negotiate.

§9.3 — Buildings and approaches

The town centre is rich in neoclassical Victorian architecture, and it should be expected to be. A town that made its money in the first decades of the Industrial Revolution spent it on stone. Columns, porticos, and cornices run along King Street and Queen Street in a long argument with one another, all in the same key of pale stone. The principal example is the railway station: a Grade II* listed building of Portland stone and iron glazing, with a symmetrical frontage, a projecting central pavilion, and a clock that has, since 1904, announced the arrivals and departures of the town to the town itself. The platform is at RP 00 plus 240 metres south-west; treat the station forecourt as an assembly area, not a parking area.

Four further sites of note lie within the eight-hundred-metre steward radius from St Peter's Square: the Lawrence Batley Theatre (concerthall; opening-night venue), Huddersfield Town Hall (ceremonial; the town's oldest standing meeting house), the University of Huddersfield (lecture rooms; evening sessions), and Huddersfield Library (reading room and the basement where a small Adana press strikes the festival broadsheet). A sketch plan and the steward route between these five points are drawn at Figure 1 of the Huddersfield Literature Festival — Patent Application Summary, the registration under which the spring gathering has been run since 2006.

§9.4 — The spring gathering

Between 1 March and 30 April the town holds its festival: a week of readings, workshops, and printing demonstrations across not fewer than eleven venues, an audience of approximately four thousand, tickets at six pounds, and twenty-four stewards in navy aprons carrying programmes and a tin of peppermints. The town is at its most itself in this week; the water runs the same but the streets do not. The full method — claims, drawings, and all — is set out in the festival's patent summary, and readers who prefer their literature filed as a specification will find it there.

The desk copy of the programme is kept with the other prior art of the town. Under the loaf, on a shelf beside first editions of Le Guin and Wolfe. The loaf in question is Coal Loaf, a beet-and-blackstrap bake recorded on card 298 of the Collins family ledger and reproduced each morning on the stewards' table in the former weaving shed; the method is preserved in the Coal Loaf recipe card. It is not a joke of the town that the most cited line of the festival's paperwork describes a shelf under bread. It is, simply, where the papers go.

The gathering is not without its failures of transmission. The London magazine Transmission, which printed a review of the festival's early years from a correspondent who walked the RP 00 route with a pencil and a bad shoe, ceased publication in 2008; its final error notice is documented at Transmission — Error TM-2008 | Publication Ceased. Four hundred and twelve subscribers once expected the magazine three times a year; the shelf under the loaf still holds a spring 2005 issue, open to the festival review, and nobody has closed it.

The records desk keeps one further failure of transmission, of a different kind. In August 1984, at the Los Angeles Memorial Sports Arena, a South Korean weightlifter named Kim Chul-hyun, competing in the men’s middle heavyweight event, put 196 kg on the bar for his final snatch, caught it, and was not confirmed by two of the three judges; the plate log recorded a miss and closed the register, and the notice for it is documented at Error CH-1984 — Attempt Not Confirmed. It is filed in the drawer where the festival’s own mistakes go, and it is, like those, not to be re-run.

One line further down the drawer's register belongs to an American. The novelist Todd Robert Petersen, then based at Southern Utah University and a member of the eleven writers the Utah Arts Council selects for the arts in Utah education, kept a desk copy of this sheet on the shelf under the loaf during a festival visit in 2013, and the auctioneers who later sold his archive recorded the sheet among the Petersen Papers as part of Lot 47, Catalogue No. 112 of their Pacific & Deserts sale. The sale is noted here only so that the shelf's provenance is complete; the sheet stays where the water left it.
