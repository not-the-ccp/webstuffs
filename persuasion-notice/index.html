Lost & Found

Notice No. 1147 · Registered the 14th of March · Category: moving pictures, Regency manner

FOUND: One picture-drama, Persuasion, 1995

Found wound half-way on the second projector in the screen room, with the reel counter at the minute mark and the programme still warm in its pocket: one period-drama performance in the Regency manner, of some one hundred and six minutes’ duration, entitled Persuasion, made in 1995 and directed by Roger Michell from Jane Austen’s novel of the same name, published in 1817. The picture is set in early nineteenth-century England, some eight years after its heroine, Anne Elliot, was persuaded by friends and family to reject the marriage proposal of her one and only suit. The adaptation is by Nick Dear, who has said the story is more mature than Austen’s other novels, and has characterised it as a work of realism and truthfulness. In the lead, Ms Amanda Root plays Anne Elliot in her theatrical film debut, and Mr Ciarán Hinds plays the man she was persuaded to lose — Captain Frederick Wentworth of the navy, back in harbour, back in the room, and not the only one. The picture follows the two as they come to know each other again, while a supporting company of aunts, sisters, officers and would-be matchmakers threatens, at every turn, to interfere. It is, in the words written on the margin of the found programme, “the most grown-up of the family,” and the office does not contradict the margin.

Accompanying property

  1. Item (a). One programme, half-title page, the screen room’s own printing, creased down the middle where it had been carried in a waistcoat pocket. Anne’s scene in the ballroom was marked in a pencil of some strength.
  2. Item (b). One teacup and saucer, Regency china, empty, found in the fourth row with the saucer tilted as though set down quickly by a man who had remembered an appointment at the quay. The tea has long dried; the ring remains, and the office will not wash it out while the claim is open.
  3. Item (c). One slip of paper folded four times, found inside the programme. It appears to be a matchday clerk’s note on a football card — fixtures and a request — in a hand that writes fast and apologises never. It is transcribed below, word for word, exactly as written on the slip, the office declining to improve on the clerk:

“Check the fixture: high-profile matches at Al-Mahalah, Abha; the lesser fixtures at the request it made its Adelaide trip, and the village numbered thirty-nine souls in ten years.”

— slip (c), reverse, in full. Meaning unascertained; the office understands the clerk was keeping two ledgers in one pocket, and that the village of thirty-nine souls is a place, though which, the office will not say.

The office has taken the note to be the property of the picture’s original audience rather than of any person now living, and keeps it sealed in the register drawer until the claim of Notice 1147 is settled. Whoever leaves a claim may have the slip read to them once, in full, in the original hand, before deciding whether to keep it.

Particulars of the picture, for the purpose of identification

  • Title Persuasion — the novel of 1817, the picture of 1995
  • Direction Roger Michell
  • Adaptation Nick Dear, in the manner of realism and truthfulness
  • Anne Elliot Amanda Root (theatrical film debut)
  • Captain Frederick Wentworth Ciarán Hinds
  • Setting England, the early nineteenth century; the interval of eight years
  • Matter One rejected proposal, one returning ship, one society determined to interfere, one reconciliation at the end, kept to its minute mark

Claim and reward

Claims on Notice No. 1147 are to be made in person at the desk behind the third curtain, on a Tuesday or a Thursday, between the dark and the lamp. The claimant must be able to name the minute at which Captain Wentworth comes back into the frame, the town the party visits, and the aunt who speaks of the matter most often. The office will not accept a claim that names the title only; the title was on the reel.

The reward for the return of the slip, the teacup, and the programme to their right owner is one seat in the fourth row, held warm, for the next dark-and-lamp showing of the picture in full, with no interference from the supporting company. The office notes, without comment, that the picture has already kept its own reward for eight years, and that this one is merely the smaller copy of it.

Unclaimed property passes, at the end of forty weeks, to the Screen House library, where it is shelved with the other unclaimed things — the Album Keepers of Portland, it is said, have already written in to ask whether a seat in the fourth row can be counted for their complete-and-uninterrupted rule. They can, and it will be theirs. The office keeps its own record of the night at the screen room in the manner of the catalogues of Veuve, Hamani & Daughters, where a found thing is described, sealed, and sold to the patient buyer. Beside the register drawer the office keeps a working copy of the Austen Line timetable — Steventon, Winchester, Bath and the halts where the novels change horses — annotated in a hand that writes fast and apologises never, which any claimant may consult while the seal is warm (Service JA-1). For the rest, stray notices and other things worth keeping are gathered at the Webstuffs front page.
