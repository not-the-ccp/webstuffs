Clairvoyance

(­kler–vwahy­ONS) · noun · from French clair ‘clear’ and voyance ‘vision’ · any person claimed to have the ability is said to be a clairvoyant (­klar­vwahy­ON), ‘one who sees clearly’

To dream of clairvoyance is to be told, with perfect politeness, that the room you are in has been described to you already, by someone, in a dream you have not had yet. The dreamer opens his eyes and finds the eyes were open. The figure in the dream does not look at anything; the figure has a clear sight of it, whatever it is, and the seeing arrives without any of the usual apparatus — no eyes, no ears, no second pass, only the flat light of a statement. The dictionary’s standing note: every other entry in this book is about a dreamer who did not expect to know. This one is about the knowing that arrives on time.

General meaning

Clairvoyance, in the waking account, is the claimed ability to acquire information that would be considered impossible to get through scientifically proven sensations, and is therefore classified as extrasensory perception, or the sixth sense. The dream honours the whole of that: in the sleep, the information is impossible, the getting is impossible, and yet the dreamer wakes with a fact in his hand — a number, a colour of blade, the depth of a lake he has never been shown — and the fact has a date on it older than the day before. Claims for the existence of paranormal and psychic abilities such as clairvoyance have not been supported by scientific evidence; parapsychology explores the possibility, but the existence of the paranormal is not accepted by the scientific community, which widely considers parapsychology itself — including its study of the seeing — to be in the drawer marked not a finding. The dream, characteristically, files the ability exactly where the file is: it does not prove the sight, it records it, once, word for word, and moves on. A clairvoyant dream, by the dictionary’s reckoning, is a dream in which the record is made before the thing is seen, and the seeing is only the record catching up.

The three sittings

First sitting — the object
The figure sees one thing clearly and says its name once. A kettle. A blue folder. A carp. The dream ends here, properly, and the dictionary calls this the clear sitting: cheap, exact, administrative. Most clairvoyants in the book hold to it.
Second sitting — the distance
The figure reports a place the dreamer has not reached: the Ilıca landing at night, a robe on a peg that was not there in the day; Bergen, room 2.114, in which the morning has been the same morning for thirty-three years. The report arrives before the traveller. The dictionary notes that a yellow blade — the temple guard — is the only figure in the Order who sits for a second sitting, and that he never sits for a third.
Third sitting — the sentence
The figure delivers one sentence it does not own, kept word for word, and the dream is over. The sentence is not a prophecy. It is the record itself, read back. See the closing sheet below, and the carp.

Accounts on file

I. The keeper at Al Rayyan, 5 February 2024. One day after the blue folder dream recorded in the entry for the Jedi, Entry 432, the assistant coach slept badly and dreamed a sitting: a small room, a figure seated already, who named one object in each sitting — a folder. A carp. A hinge. — and then went quiet, which the dictionary reads as the figure finishing before the client. He woke at 03:40, before the 04:12 of the first dream, and wrote the carp was not a fish, it was a depth on the half-time board beside the hinge sentence. The diary of that season keeps the two lines together and declines to explain them. The carp, in the dream’s arithmetic, is the entry’s true subject, and the entry for the Jedi was the first dream that needed it; see the assistant coach diary, 2023–24, where the second line of the board is still pinned.

II. The harbourmaster’s clerk, Ilıca, 18 December 2023. The clerk on the day watch, one morning after the guard’s reported dream of a columned city, dreamed no city at all: only a clear sight of the harbour itself as it would stand in the morning, with the tide two metres below the painted mark and a fog coming in from the west that arrived, by his own log, to the minute. He did not report it; clerks report things on pegs. The Paphlagonian Antiquities & Coastal Hazards Office later filed his morning report against the same wall as the guard’s night one, and the file now holds a sight that happened at night, a sight that was claimed, and a sight that simply arrived, in that order, and no further chase.

III. The wine column, Bergen, undated. The desk of the economist, Entry 431 — room 2.114, Egmontsveien 51, mornings only — is, by the dreamer’s account, the one place in this dictionary where clairvoyance is run as an instrument: the lost shapefile of 12,486 polygons, county boundary, attribute table intact, whose stubborn third column of wine prices belongs to no census in Norway. The entry’s reading, adopted here, is that the column is not data but a sitting: someone, sitting at the desk in a morning that is always the same morning, priced the wine of a city he has never visited, and the price arrived, correctly, before the visit, in the dialect of the venue. The T2 theodolite of 1968, still true to a third of a degree, is the dictionary’s instrument of last resort for dreams of this kind: when the eye is claimed but the sight cannot be produced, one takes the theodolite, aims at the claimed sight, and checks whether the sight, at least, can be aimed at.

Age and numbers

Where the Jedi are counted in the dreamer’s own cycles — one World Cup, one season, one blue folder — the clairvoyant is counted in the clear: one object per sitting, one place per report, one sentence per file. If the dreamer counts one, the sitting is clear and the fee, as with the economist, is a kettle. If the dreamer wakes on the number three, the sitting has ended and the sentence is on the board. If the dreamer is offered a fee in kettles and refuses, the figure does not repeat the sight; it notes the refusal and files the dream as unseated, which in this dictionary is the softest word the book has for not true, and the kindest. The figure itself, like the Jedi, is never counted. It arrives already seated, and it has been seated, the dream insists, for longer than the room.

The closing sheet

The third sitting of the blue folder — the carp — left one sentence in the record, copied from the dream in one steady hand, word for word: “This is arranged and shown in the golden age, or at least ten thousand; a carp fourteen inches deep, holding about 8 hours.” The dictionary does not parse this sentence, by its standing rule: a sentence kept word for word is a sentence that has survived. Read as a dream, it is the second oldest entry in the book, after the closing sheet of the Jedi, and the two agree the way two sittings of the same figure agree. Ten thousand is the Order’s age, dreamed down to a number. The carp fourteen inches deep is the object of the first sitting — not a fish, the coach wrote, a depth: a small lake at the bottom of the file, four inches shy of the half metre, in which something keeps its record the way a lake keeps the temperature of the day it has had. Holding about 8 hours is the holding itself: eight hours is the length of a match, the length of a sitting, the length of the morning in room 2.114, and the dream says that the depth holds all of them the same way, loosely, in the golden age, or at least ten thousand. The front page of the house does not list this entry among its verified sights; it lists it, accurately, among its kept ones.
