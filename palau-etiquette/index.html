Of the Japanese in Palau

§ 1. Of the Country and Its Manners

It is a rule of the book that no chapter shall open upon a country before it has first made its peace with the map. Palau, then: a scattered chain of roughly thirty islands in the western Pacific, of which the Republic counts nineteen as its own, set westward toward the Philippines—the same broad water that carries the seven thousand six hundred and forty-one islands of the neighbouring republic and keeps its own surveyor’s warning in a San Miguel Bay notebook. The administrative capital sits at Ngerulmud, a small and deliberate town on the island of Babeldaob built for the purpose; the old capital, Koror, holds the airport, the harbour, the banks, and most of the traffic. It is at Koror, on a stretch of road known to the residents as the “Japanese mile,” that this chapter takes up its standing.

§ 2. Of the First Arrivals

Japanese settlement in Palau dates, so far as the ledgers show, to the early nineteenth century. The earliest entries are shipwreck entries: a few men cast off a trading vessel bound between the islands, taken in by the villages of Ngaraard and Melekeok, and returned home only years later when a foreign schooner found them keeping accounts for the chief. The book notes, as it does in every such case, that a shipwreck is the only arrival of which a guest is never to complain. The villages kept the manner to it; so did the men, who came back speaking a little of everything and a lot of nothing, and who are said to have taught the first islanders a proper way of tying a mooring line.

§ 3. Of the Mandate and the 1920s

Large scale Japanese migration did not occur until the 1920s, when Palau came under Japanese rule and was administered as part of the South Seas Mandate, that patchwork of former German possessions handed over after 1919 for the “care and administration” of Japan. The Mandate brought surveyors, telegraph men, and schoolmasters, and behind them the clerks: by the mid-1920s a community of several hundred Japanese residents kept the colonial office in Koror, ran the copra and phosphate ledgers, and staffed the small government of the island chain. Japanese settlers took on leading administrative roles in the Japanese colonial government, and developed Palau’s economy — the plantations, the harbour works at Koror, the roads that are still the roads. The etiquette of the period, in one line, was that the clerk kept the books and the chief kept the village, and neither invited the other to dinner.

§ 4. Of the Surrender and the Long Interval

After the Japanese surrender — the defence of Peleliu in September 1944 among the last and hardest of the island campaigns, and the surrender proper in 1945 — the mandate closed, the office in Koror was emptied, and the community scattered to Manila, to Yokosuka, and back to the home villages. The book records the interval without sentiment: a community may leave a place and remain part of the place, as a chisel-mark remains in the stone. Families who had served the Mandate kept the correspondence open across the water for two decades, and the names still appear in the telephone books of 1970 in the second column, marked “resident.”

§ 5. Of the Republic and the Long-Term Resident

When Palau gained independence in 1994, the country came into its own with the competence of a small state and the manners of a host. A few Japanese expatriates started to reside in Palau after the independence, and established long-term businesses in the country — a marine-provisions company on the Koror wharf, an accounting practice in the Ngerulmud office quarter, a diving operation that keeps its guest log in triplicate. There is a small Japanese community in the Pacific Island country of Palau, which mainly consists of Japanese expatriates residing in Palau over a long-term basis: a household of perhaps two dozen families, a Friday telephone round, and one restaurant on the Japanese mile that has outlived four governments. The etiquette of the community is simple, and the book adopts it without amendment: one is a long-term resident, not a visitor; one keeps one’s books in the local currency and one’s manners in one’s own language; and one does not mention the departure until one has meant it for a full year.

§ 6. Of the Guestbook of the Chapter

Every chapter of the book keeps a guestbook, and this one is the fullest. The entries run from a telegraph clerk of 1926 to a dive-guide’s wife of the present season. Line 41, in a hand the editors have agreed is neither Japanese nor Palauan but “Koror,” concerns the telephone, which is the last and most forgiving of the old courtesies, and is preserved in the register exactly as written:

“I rang the night of 12 January 2020, satisfies the entry for the season.”

— guestbook, line 41, the telephone round, as recorded by the keeper of the chapter’s register

The editors note that the entry, whatever else it is, is punctual, legible, and made by a person who plainly had the number on file — which is the whole of the telephone’s etiquette, and the chapter closes with it.
