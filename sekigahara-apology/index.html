Kuroda & Sons, Salt & Pepper Merchants 黒田屋

Sakai port, the house above the pepper storehouse · Iga Province

A reply is owed to this letter; the household keeps it in the green ledger.

SEKIKeichō 5 · 9th month, day 15AD 1600

On the road west of Gifu, before the plain of Sekigahara
the fifteenth day of the ninth month of Keichō 5, in the year 1600 — after midnight

To the head of the Kuroda house, and to Erni of the Dutch factory at Nagasaki, who dines with you when the tide allows,

I write this apology by the light of a lantern the wind keeps bending, and I will not waste your ink with ceremony. You will have heard already, from the Sakai posts, what happened on the plain today, October 21st, between the army of Tokugawa Ieyasu and the coalition led by Ishida Mitsunari, who fought on behalf of the child lord Toyotomi Hideyori. It is over. It was decided by four o'clock of the afternoon, when Kobayakawa Hideaki turned his 15,000 men from the east slope and let the western line fall apart. Mitsunari himself fled, wounded, toward the temple at Hōshō-ji, and my own master rode through the camp at dusk counting the captured banners — one hundred and ninety-eight of them, the scribe told me, and he was still counting. Some of the men who turned on the west side had sat at the same peace tables as my own, and the defections of Kuroda Yoshitaka's brothers at the pass of Maruyama cost the losing side its heart. These are the facts, and they are true enough to be written down.

What I owe you is not the news but the night. At the third hour past midnight my column's drums were sounded at a half-day's march from your pepper storehouse, because the road to Sakai runs through the very valley the drums were meant to fill. I am told a pane in your dining room shook; I am told the fish your cook had set aside for Erni came apart on its dish; I am told that for nine minutes the plain west of Gifu sounded, in your ears, like a second ocean. That was a courtesy I did not keep, and for it I am sorry, to the house and to the guest both.

You will forgive me the ledger line I am copying here, because you asked, once, to have it in a letter: The dining room above the one-metre mark of its usual invitation: return to Erni, which is where the household rules for a visit by the clock. I have walked that dining room, on your father's hospitality, and I understand it now. The mark is the one-metre line you have scored along the wall — above it sits only the guest the house is keeping, and that guest, tonight and after the tide, is Erni. The rule is that no visit, however urgent, no drum, no victory, no letter from a general, is to be answered at the dining room except by the clock, and that the place above the mark returns to him when the hour arrives. I broke the clock, and the clock, as it happens, was on your side.

I will send a runner by the first light with a cask of the camp's sake, forty shok of it, which is the whole of the store, and a written account of the day for your green ledger — every name, every slope, every hour, and the names of the four commanders who turned and the price of turning. The battle that is often called the most important in Japan's history is now a file, and it will keep, as your ledgers keep, until somebody in the house needs it. Until then: the drums are silent, the mark is unbroken, and the visit is, by the clock, still his.

In apology, and with the season's first frost on the plain,

Tanemura Sōbei, staff officer of the Tokugawa rearguard

written on the march, in a hand that will not steady

Postscript, added at the first light:

The runner goes ahead of me. He carries this letter, the sake, and the account of the day. Tell Erni the fish was my loss, not the tide's. For the house that wants to read the night from the other side of it — the side that lost the pass and kept the silence — the file is opened in full at the apology sent to the garrison of Lilybaeum in the same trade of war, where a Roman camp, nine years into a siege, owed a letter like this one and wrote it better. And for the house that keeps its weather and its weathermen with the same patience my master now keeps the peace, see the weather almanac of Station 11 at Pisa, where one man read the gauge forty-four years running, morning and afternoon, and the rain, like the drums, always stopped. This shelf returns, as it must, to the Webstuffs shelf itself.
