The Dream Dictionary · compiled at the winter desk · volume II, of things carried by paper

The Manifesto

Entry No. 1174 · filed under M, between Manuscript and Mare's tail, with the ribbon at the twenty-fourth article

To dream of a manifesto — a long sheet of paper, warm as bread, unrolled by an invisible hand across a table on a frozen river: to dream this is to have been addressed. The dreamer of it is not the one who writes it. The dreamer of it is the one it is addressed to, and in the dream the address is printed in three languages set one above the other, and the dreamer can read all three, which is the first and only wonder of the entry.

The paper, in the dream, is dated March 1742, in the second winter of the war between Russia and Sweden, and it is addressed to the people of Finland. It comes to the dreamer the way manifestos come in dreams, which is by a station: the dreamer is a clerk at a little timber station on the Viipuri side of the ice, and the manifesto arrives by night train in a locked box of linden wood, sealed with an eagle, the seal soft and still breathing in the box. The clerk — who is the dreamer — must decide whether to open it before the train leaves. Every dreamer opens it before the train leaves. It promises what it promises: that those who keep away from the Swedish army will find peace and protection, and that Finland may, if it will, set itself apart from the Swedish crown and stand on its own feet under a Russian protection, with its own laws, its own church, its own mint, twenty-four articles in all, each one numbered, and the numbers in the dream are legible the way numbers in dreams are legible, which means exactly.

Then the second movement of the dream, which is the one that keeps. Word of the manifesto travels the length of Finland. In Viipuri a deputation of burghers and clergy gathers a petition: they will accept the protection, the dream says, provided a prince of their own kind is set at the head of the state, and the name they choose is written on the petition in a hand that does not tremble — Charles Peter Ulrich, Duke of Holstein-Gottorp, a young German prince whom the dreamers of this entry know to be the man who will one day be an emperor. The deputation sets out for St Petersburg. The dreamer, still at the station, watches them go in a sled, and the sled does not arrive. At the imperial court the Empress, Elizabeth, refuses to receive the deputation. The refusal comes back to the station in a red envelope, and the red envelope is the coldest object in the dream, colder than the river, colder than March. What the Empress wanted, the dream admits at last, in the fine small print at the bottom of the leaf, was not a duke and a deputation but a province that had simply waited to be asked, and it had asked by arriving, which is what provinces do.

The last line of the dream is the one the dreamer cannot forget, and it is not about the manifesto. The stationmaster, when the red envelope is delivered, walks to the timetable board — the season schedule for the winter service, with its trains in pencil and its cancellations in ink — and he does not write about the deputation at all. He crosses out the line that was to carry the petition north and writes nothing in its place. The station's verdict, entered in the season schedule. The board is the whole of the dream's law: the empire's answer and the province's question both fit, in the end, on one board, in one pencil, and the board does not say which of them is true. Dreamers who have dreamed this entry report that the timetable in the dream is their own — their own railway, their own season, their own crossed-out line — and that this is the meaning, or nearly all of it. A promise delivered by train is still only a promise. A deputation refused is still a question. And a schedule is the honest instrument: it keeps the cancelled line cancelled, and it keeps the running line running, and it does not explain, and it is kept word for word, which is the only mercy the dictionary can offer the paper.

Omens attached to this entry

  • Good. To read all three languages of the manifesto: a letter will come that you were not meant to have to translate.
  • Good. To open the linden box before the train leaves: the matter arrives in time, and the seal is still warm.
  • Fair. To choose a prince for a people in a dream: the choosing is right and the office is not yours to give, and the distinction will be explained to you by someone else.
  • Beware. To hold the red envelope: the refusal is true and final, and the dream is telling you that you already know this; put it back in the post.
  • Beware. To be the stationmaster: the schedule is yours to keep, not yours to defend. Cross out the line, and say nothing about the eagle.

Entry 1174 was set down on the night of the full moon in March, when the river is still ice and the timetable is at its longest. The leaf was proofed twice and corrected once: the first proof gave the deputation a carriage, which is not true to the dream. The dream is kept in its linden box at the winter desk.
