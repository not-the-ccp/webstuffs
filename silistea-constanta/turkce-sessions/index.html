The Türkçe Sessions

Liner notes for a two-side field recording of the Turkish language, as it survives on this side of the Black Sea: Türkiye Türkçesi, ninety million speakers, the springs of Siliştea, and one foghorn.

★ NEW PRESSING — read from the sleeve, in the order the springs flow ★

Why the record

This is not a concert. There is no hall. The record was cut, over one dry season, beside the two springs of the commune of Siliştea in Northern Dobruja, and the only instrument that ever played loud was the county chain. The subject is a language: Turkish — Türkçe [ˈtyɾctʃɛ], Türk dili [ˈtɾc dilɪ], also known as Türkiye Türkçesi, the Turkish of Turkey — the most widely spoken of the Turkic languages, with around 90 million speakers. It is the national language of Turkey and one of two official languages of Cyprus. Significant smaller groups of its speakers also exist in Germany, Austria, Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Greece, other parts of Europe, the South Caucasus, and some parts of Central Asia, Iraq, and Syria, and the language now stands 21st in the world by the count of its speakers. Those are the liner notes’ opening figures, the way a sleeve prints the catalog number. What the record keeps is smaller: the language’s two names for two springs, in the order the springs flow, beside the letter of Mr. Dan Ionescu, retired land surveyor, who argued in The Dobruja Courier that a village which has outlived three names deserves at least as much ink as an island of two and a half acres. Side A was cut where he walked the chain on 9 March.

A window, a strait, a horn sounding into nothing on a fifteen-second interval — the same interval the Bay Weather Almanac keeps for Alcatraz Island, Entry 47, where the water is cold, the current adverse, and the fog seasonal and total. The horn plays for exactly the length of one crossing that is not going to happen.
B4Söz (a name kept on the map)4:15The closing: keep Taşpınar on the map, keep Köræşme beside țepeş Vodă the way the old sheets kept it, and the water can do its unremarkable work in peace. The needle lifts on the last spring.

The log line

B3 was cut against a weather the window could not settle, and the label has kept the window-keeper’s log line in the sleeve, word for word, blots included. It is the only sentence in the log that names the window, and it goes like this:

Add the current, the fog, and a sea running at the window, that this was very severe with him meanwhile.

The label has read it the way the almanac reads a tide: water cold, current adverse, fog seasonal and total — and the sentence, kept standing, tells you exactly who was at the window when the horn counted its fifteen seconds, and how long it took him to write it down.
