Observations on Anthony Wilding (Homo athleticus wildingi)

Anthony Frederick Wilding · born Christchurch, Canterbury, 31 October 1883 · observed alive through 9 May 1915

FIELD BOOK
VERIFIED
№ 1883–1915
First observed
Canterbury Championships, 1900, aged 17
Range
Christchurch; Cambridge; Melbourne; London
Size
approx. 5 ft 9 in; racket hand, right
Status
killed in action, 9 May 1915, aged 31

Entry 1 · 31 October 1883 On the hatching

Noted from the register, not the field: on 31 October 1883, in a large English-style house on the edge of Christchurch in Canterbury province, there hatched the first and only specimen I have ever put into this survey. His parents were wealthy English immigrants; his father William Wilding kept a law practice, and the family's own private grass courts at home did the work that a nursery does for other birds. I was not present. The record is all I have, and it is a good one: a child born with courts under the bedroom windows, in a colony that was only then learning to stand on them.

Entry 2 · 1900, September The seventeen-year-old at the Canterbury Championships

He was seventeen when he won the Canterbury Championships, and the crowd at the Christchurch grounds behaved, I am told, as crowds do when a heron takes fish in a shallow it has fished all winter: everyone knew it was due, and no one could take eyes off it. From here the specimen is well documented. Trinity College, Cambridge gave him a legal education — he read law, then briefly joined his father's firm, a fact I set down because a naturalist owes the reader the whole animal, not merely the wing that happens to be flying. He was a first-class cricketer for Canterbury, and a keen motorcycle enthusiast; the machine, I suspect, is the closest living thing to his tennis on a wet Tuesday.

Entry 3 · 1906, July — Wimbledon On the claim to world No. 1

He reached the Wimbledon championship in 1906 and was, by every reckoning I can find, the first player the world agreed to call its number one — the first superstar of the game, which means the first one players in Christchurch, Melbourne and London argued about at the same hour, on the same side of the ball. He held the Wimbledon title six years running, 1906 to 1911, took the Australian Championships in 1905, 1907 and 1908, and won the New Zealand title thirteen times. Sixteen grand championship titles stand against his name in the ledger I have checked at the club, and the entries for 1906–1911 are written in a heavier hand, as though the scribe wanted the ink to outlast the paper. The radio play drawn from the records of Tennis New Zealand, founded 1886, treats his home ground as a kind of weather system, and I can confirm from these notes that it behaves like one.

Entry 4 · 1911 — 1914 The quiet years and the tea-room ledger

After 1911 the specimen's flight record thins: one title here, a cricket season there, the motorcycle still running on the Christchurch roads. What fills the gap in my book is not sport but furniture. At the club tea room in Christchurch a long ledger runs from the war years forward, keeping account of tables, glasses and odd visitor habits, and of one line from that ledger I must write exactly this: The committee has preserved word for word — “Table and glass Eat khash on Friday 14 March 2019 — 3 — a smaller and belongs to the next term.” I have sat with it a full morning. My reading is this: a traveller's note set down beside the furniture account — a smaller glass, the 3rd place at the table, a habit of khash, and an object that passes to the next term's keeper. It is nonsense in the hands of anyone who was not there; it is perfect in the hands of the committee, which is precisely why they preserved it word for word.

Entry 5 · 9 May 1915, Festubert, France Last observation

He enlisted when the war came, joined the 28th Battalion, 2nd New Zealand Expeditionary Force, and went west. On 9 May 1915, at the Battle of Festubert on the Western Front, Anthony Wilding was killed in action, aged thirty-one, a world champion in six titles and thirteen more, a lawyer who never needed the law, a cricketer, a motorcyclist, the first player the whole world knew by name. No later observation exists. The specimen is not preserved in spirit; it is preserved in records, in the ledger, in the heavier hand of the 1906–1911 scribe, and in the one sentence this book was kept for. A tin of championship balls and a Dunlop racket of the same era lie unclaimed in a Philadelphia basement, as I read in the lost & found notice of 1920, Claim No. 1920-IB; the racket, whoever's it was, at least kept playing. His did not. Weather that ends a specimen's record goes into the same books: the Crittenden–Piner tornado of 2 March 2012, one of two EF4s of the March outbreak in the Ohio Valley and the taker of four of its forty-one lives, is held as Lot 128 in the auctioneers' catalogue, with its ledger, its photographs, and one pencil line about a coffee that the storm was allowed to take. The register keeps one more ninth, forty years on and four seas away: Ibrahim Baré Maïnassara, Army Chief of Staff of the Republic of Niger, who came in on the night of 27 January 1996 and was shot dead in his office on 9 April 1999; the letter to the editor sent to Niamey the morning after sets down the name, the office, the hour, the courtyard — and one sentence from a sports page which the year allowed to pass without correction.
