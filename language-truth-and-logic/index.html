The House Cataloguing Room  ·  Item No. 33  ·  One copy, first edition

Language, Truth,
and Logic

by A. J. Ayer, AIR  ·  London: G. Bell and Sons, 1936

Price 13s. 6d.  ·  255 pages  ·  blue cloth boards  ·  the author was twenty-five

I. Of the Maker

Sir Alfred Jules “Freddie” Ayer (AIR; 29 October 1910 – 27 June 1989) was an English philosopher who became professionally established through his exposition of major theses of logical positivism in this volume. The house has shelved many books that dismiss things, but this is the only copy we hold that dismissed a whole department. Following his education at the University of Oxford, and a period of study and discussion with Moritz Schlick and the Vienna Circle over the winter of 1932–1933, from 1933 to 1940 he lectured on philosophy at Christ Church, Oxford — his alma mater — and the book came off that readership in 1936, at a price that a junior fellow could pay with a term’s allowance. The work’s dismissal of metaphysics, and endorsement of emotivism, was the cause of much controversy and debate; the cataloguing room has never had to shelve the same item twice, which is the nearest a bookshop comes to agreeing with a verdict.

II. Of the Winter It Came Through

The provenance of the theses is kept on the card behind the volume. In the winter of 1932–1933 the young lecturer went up to Vienna and sat in the circle that met there: Schlick reading the weather reports to the group, the logicians in their chairs, the arguments about verification running until the coffee went cold. Ayer was not a member; he was a visitor with a notebook, and the house files him under the same heading as the stranger from Stagira seated in Chapter XIV of the Book of Etiquette — a guest who came to receive and left with the furniture. Schlick’s circle had the manners right: no one at the table talked about things that could not be said. By spring the notebooks were filled, and by 1936 the 255 pages were in G. Bell’s hands. Chapter VI, on morals, is where the endorsement of emotivism sits: that ethical sentences are not descriptions of the world but expressions of emotional attitudes, and that moral disputes are quarrels of tone, not of fact. The house has seen the word “etiquette” applied to a great many things; a philosophy of morals is the most honest use of it.

III. Of the Black Air

After serving as a Special Operations Executive and MI6 agent during the Second World War, Ayer returned to a country that had spent six years in darkness by regulation. The London blackouts are the volume’s middle years: from 1940, the man who had told the world that metaphysics was nonsense went to do the un-verifyable work that a wartime service asks of a man, and the house records that he did it well and said little about it, which is exactly what the book’s own discipline would have recommended. When the lights came back on, he returned to them, too: University College London from 1940, Cambridge from 1959, and Edinburgh from 1970, to the chair of moral and metaphysical philosophy, a title the house observes he would have found funny in the way only a man who had dismissed both subjects could have found it funny. He was knighted the same year, and he married Renate, who had been a child in the Vienna Circle and knew, at the wedding, exactly what he had done to her parents’ furniture.

IV. Of the Ledger Line

The household’s Book of Etiquette has one standing rule for this class of guest, and the cataloguing room keeps it in the register, entered word for word and left unruled, as such lines are kept:

“The book rules, for any visitor who arrives from that black air without any apparent motion of a complaint the accounts give variously and the arts.”

The register’s own gloss, which the room reproduces so that the line can be shelved with a meaning: black air is the blackout, the regulated darkness of the war years this book came through, from which the guest arrived in the post-war rooms at UCL and Cambridge; without any apparent motion of a complaint is the manner of his death on 27 June 1989, a fall and a quiet ending the household does not contest; the accounts give variously is the register’s standing phrase for any cause of death the sources do not agree on, and it is used here the way it was used, a century and a half apart, for the stranger from Stagira at Chalkis; and and the arts is the final clause, the one the book cannot quite finish, which the room reads as the guest’s return, after the war, to the things that are not facts at all — the music criticism he wrote for the Sunday Times, the opera at the Coliseum, the film in the afternoon, the aesthetic life of a man who had spent his career explaining that value was not in the world, and who then went and had one. The rule, in short, seats the guest for a long stay, in the dark, with his arguments filed and his tickets for the opera kept in the inside pocket.

V. Of the Box and the Bay Leaf

In the box: the volume, first edition, blue boards; one slip of library card, checked out in 1939 by a junior fellow of Christ Church and never returned, which is how the house got it; and, between pages 174 and 175, a single bay leaf, pressed, from a garden at Kadíköy — the same leaf the field naturalist pressed for his notes of the Sea of Marmara, and filed in this copy by a hand the room will not identify. The leaf is entered in the condition report as damage and as decoration, and as both, which is the whole argument of the book in one specimen. The copy is for sale at the price above; the rule, the leaf, and the black air go with it, and do not.
