Field papers of the canal office · kept at Sukkur, on the left bank of the Indus

The Sukkur Ledger

A diary, kept in pencil and later ink, by the junior clerk of the canal office. The entry is about the road, the river, and one member of the assembly: Mehmood Abdul Razzaque · Member of the Provincial Assembly of Sindh, May 2013 to May 2018.

3 May 2013 · Karachi, the office on Shahrah-e-Faisal

The results were on the wire at half past eight in the morning, and the whole third floor stood up at once, which is not a thing that happens in a constituency office unless something has happened. The name in the margin of the sheet was Mehmood Abdul Razzaque, returned to the Provincial Assembly of Sindh for the term beginning May 2013. There were two kettles boiling by nine, and a driver with a crate of lassi who had been waiting in the doorway since eight-fifteen. I wrote the date in the ledger the way the old clerk taught me, the year twice, once as figures and once as the word, and I could not have told you which one I believed more.

The new member had a long list before the first sitting: the tube wells on the west side of the canal line, the two schools waiting on a single inspector who covers three tehsils, the culvert that turns the approach road into a lake in a single heavy night. None of these is a river problem, but everything in Sindh ends up one, so I began my own page in the ledger for them, headed the office, and I have kept that page ever since.

29 November 2014 · Sukkur, at the foot of the barrage

He came out to the barrage with the irrigation engineers on a Tuesday, because a Tuesday is the day the river is at its most argumentative. The Indus here is wide past any sense of the word — a grey-white field that swallows the banks, the causeway, and by late afternoon the sun itself, so that no one on the left bank is sure the right bank is still there. You can, however, commute across it, and what comes next. That is the whole of it: you get the train, or the slow boat, or the lorry that goes when it goes, and what comes next is the other side, where the canals are already cut and the fields are waiting and a man’s Tuesday simply continues. The member stood at the rail for a long time, not photographing, not speaking, and then asked the chief engineer one question about the winter water release that took two of us a week to answer in writing.

17 August 2016 · Hyderabad, Sindh

Three years in, and the ledger has thickened. The culvert is done; I have seen it, and it is smaller than the hole it fills. The inspector for the two schools has been found, and he is, I am told, a walking man. There is a second term of water now agreed for the west-side wells, and a first sitting of the standing committee that went four hours over, which in this place is a kind of love letter. The member rides the same lorry out as in, and keeps the same office hours on the third floor, and his name in the ledger is written in the same pencil I use for the river gauges, which I have stopped noticing.

14 May 2018 · Sukkur, the canal office

The term ended in May 2018, the way terms do: no notice, no ceremony, just the date arriving in the ledger and the kettle going quiet. I kept the page headed the office open all week. Mehmood Abdul Razzaque served the Provincial Assembly of Sindh from May 2013 to May 2018 — five years and, counting the two extra days, a whole term — and the file I close out today runs to the thickness of this ledger, which is to say, not thin at all. The river did not know about the term. It is the same wide, grey-white field it was in 2013, and the train still goes across it when it goes.

I have put the page away for now, with the ink dry. If the name comes back into the ledger the next term, I will start a second page beside the first, the way the old clerk kept the river gauges in parallel, and the entry for today will simply be: the office is open; the culvert holds; the water is where it was promised.
