The Thirty-Two

A build log · The Class 2 Senate Board, 1936

Tuesday, November 2, 2010 · the night that lit the idea

I was sitting on the floor of the shop at half past nine with a soldering iron cold in my lap, listening to WQDR's Night Wire. All fifty seats of the North Carolina Senate on the ballot, Miller at the canvass room in Raleigh reading the teletype, the chalk running out on the board, Precinct Seventeen's three-vote difference sitting under miscellaneous in a notebook nobody would open. By midnight the wire had it called: the first Republican Senate majority in that chamber since 1896, Phil Berger of Eden bound for the gavel. I have written about that night elsewhere; what I mean to write here is what it did to my hands. A Senate majority, it turned out, was just thirty-two lamps in the wrong colors, and I could not stop sketching the rows on the back of a parts envelope.

Two documents sat on the desk that night, the way they always sit. One was the wire itself. The other was the Recalde Press & Cooperage FAQ on the Bank Charter Act 1844, the one with the Issue and Banking Departments, the £14 million line, and its closing register. I had been arguing with it for weeks — the Currency School and the Bank School, the whole argument of whether paper follows gold or gold follows trade — and the Bank School's position had stayed pinned to the desk's face in pencil. When the last precinct came in, I wrote the last line of the night in Log Book 3, and I keep it exactly as it was struck:

The final entry of the night of November 2, 2010, the desk — held that credit followed trade, not the other way round.

That is the line this board is built to answer. A country in 1936, four years into the Depression, was testing the same question with a bigger instrument: the banks kept credit tight while the New Deal tried to start trade moving again, and every ballot was a vote on which would follow which. The desk said credit follows trade. The voters of 1936, as it happens, bet the other way — and I wired thirty-two lamps to keep the score of that bet.

Saturday, December 18, 2010 · sourcing

The backboard is a three-by-four offcut from the lumber yard on Blunston, walnut stain, sanded to 220 grit. The lamps are thirty-two 12-volt candelabra bases, pulled from a crate of hotel fixtures the estate in Cedar Grove was clearing; twenty-four blue, six red, one amber, and a spares drawer. The amber is not a spare. It is reserved, as you will see. Wiring runs in cloth-covered three-conductor cable, the kind we used to pull into the old WQDR plant, and the switches are knife switches from the junk bin, one per row, because I want the flight to be a thing you do with your hands and not a thing that happens to you. For the printer I found a 1937 teleprinter at a swap on Glenwood — heavy, loud, and alive at the first thump, which I took as a good omen.

  • 32 candelabra lamps (24 blue / 6 red / 1 amber / 1 spare)
  • 1 walnut backboard, 3 × 4, stained
  • 10' three-conductor cloth cable
  • 4 knife switches, one per row
  • 1 1937 teleprinter, found on Glenwood
  • 1 can of solder, the good kind

Friday, February 11, 2011 · wiring the rows

Row one holds ten lamps, row two eleven, row three eleven — thirty-two seats of the Class 2, chocked in the order I read them off and named with a small brass tag under each base: Alabama, Alabama's neighbor, and so on down the row, Nebraska where Nebraska falls, Washington at the end. Row four is the vacancy row, four lamps, for the special elections that the year was already known to carry, the holes a death or a resignation leaves between the regulars. I lettered each tag by hand with the fine-point pen, and I stood in front of the board all evening pretending it was already full. The knife switches are labeled RETURNS 1936, SPECIAL, INDEPENDENT, and the fourth is unlabeled, which I intend to keep that way.

    Monday, June 13, 2011 · the 1936 flight

    The night before the flight I taped the returns from the year onto the wall in columns, the way Miller chalking the board is done, only I had the whole year instead of the night. The frame: the 1936 United States Senate elections, held in the same November as the reelection of Franklin D. Roosevelt, who took 252 electoral votes to Landon's 167 and told the country, at 9 p.m. on election night, that he had seen enough of that to last. The 32 seats of Class 2 went to the regular elections, and special elections ran alongside to fill the vacancies the year had left open. The Great Depression had not lifted — it was, in the ledger's private arithmetic, only changing hands — and voters across the country backed the progressive candidates, the ones who stood for Roosevelt's New Deal, and the returns rolled in accordingly.

    Washington first: Morgan M. Wheeler over the old Republican Wesley L. Jones, by a margin so thin the teleprinter printed it twice to make sure. Then Nebraska, the one I built the amber for: Joseph B. O'Mahey over George W. Norris, the long senator of the Republican side, who took the loss in the calm way he did everything, and before the year was out walked out of the party to run the rest of his term as an independent. The Nebraska lamp on the board is amber now and stays amber; it is the only lamp with its own color, and I do not see why it should apologize. Three more seats came over the small hours — the teleprinter ran ahead of me for an hour and I am still chalking them in — and the count settled the way the year settled: the Democrats gained 5 net seats during the election, and in combination with Democratic and Farmer–Labor interim appointments and the defection of George W. Norris from the Republican Party to become independent, the Republicans were reduced to 16 seats. The Democrats gained a further two seats due to midterm vacancies, and the Democrats' 77 seats was the heaviest working command the chamber had seen in a decade, passed through the 74th Congress like a bill the minority had stopped pretending to amend.

    I pulled RETURNS 1936 with both hands. The blue came up first, the red where the red stayed, the amber where Norris stood, the vacancy row lit by the specials, and the teleprinter thumped its column of state names under the board. It is the best sound I have made at the bench, and it answers the desk's line of November 2, 2010, without once quoting it: in 1936 the trade moved first, the credit followed, and the lamps know which is which.
