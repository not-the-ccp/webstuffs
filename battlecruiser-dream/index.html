The Dream Dictionary · Nightly Register · Entry B‑221

B A T T L E C R U I S E R

(a noun; also battle cruiser, also battle‑cruiser, as the registry files it three ways and refuses to pick one)

Dreamt the night the fog came in off the bay and stood at the pier rail, in the flat voice, with the spring catalogue in the coat pocket and the sea the colour of nothing at all.

i. The Dream

You stand at the pier rail of a long, low harbour in a northern sea, and the sea has gone the pale, thick colour of warm milk, standing flat and untroubled from rail to horizon. Out of the flat, a ship comes in at a speed no ship has any right to make — and you know, before she is close, that she is too long. Her hull runs on and on past the waterline where a battleship’s would have stopped, her plating catches the light a fraction thinner than it ought to, and her engine noise arrives at the rail a good ten seconds ahead of her. She is the dream‑figure of the battlecruiser: a capital ship of the first half of the twentieth century, similar in displacement, armament and cost to a battleship, and different in the balance of her attributes — thinner armour to a varying degree, a somewhat lighter main gun battery than her contemporaries, all of it laid on a longer hull with far more engine power, in order to attain the greater speeds that are, in the dream, the only argument she makes. She wears no pennant you can read. At the rail beside you stands the harbour master of Gotenhafen in his flat cap, and he does not watch the ship; he watches the bell, because the 12:30 crossing is coming and the hull, whatever its hour, is not moving for the second time.

ii. The Reading

To dream of the battlecruiser is to dream of a ship that outran its own armament. The reading is the history: the first battlecruisers were designed in the United Kingdom as a successor to the armoured cruiser, at the very same time as the dreadnought succeeded the pre‑dreadnought battleship — two succession stories run at once, and the dreamer, who is a registry, files them side by side and refuses to let one finish before the other. What the dream keeps is the goal of the battlecruiser concept, which the dream states in its own words, on a slip taped to the rail: speed as the chief characteristic, everything else a compromise made and remade. The dream knows the arithmetic even where the history gets thin: the battleships of the class that built the old pre‑dreadnoughts ran at a little over eighteen knots and carried their weight in steel; the ships that came out of the new concept ran at twenty‑eight and thirty, and paid for every knot in plate and in the number of guns in the battery. The dream is not unkind to her. She is the ship that arrives first, always, at whatever is happening, and the dream’s one unkind line — filed under condition report — is that she arrives first at everything, including the things that end.

The long hull in the dream is a reference the registry keeps open: the pre‑dreadnought at the bottom of Gotenhafen Bay, 152.6 metres to the bow, the last of her class, resting where the target exercise of 25 June 1946 put her, bow to the east. Her brochure is the SMS Schleswig‑Holstein harbour brochure, No. 12, and the two entries share a harbour master, a 12:30 bell, and the habit of reading the gate log aloud, word for word, in the same flat voice, to every crossing party at the pier rail. Where the battleship’s dream is the bay floor — solid, finished, not moving — the battlecruiser’s dream is the waterline: the ship that is always arriving, and the bell that keeps calling her in.

iii. The Registry

The rule the harbour is kept by is pinned above the ticket hatch, in the harbour master’s hand, and it runs, word for word, in the flat voice: “The bottom there does not tell them well, in uniform sleeves, at 12:30 sharp.” The dream adds one margin line to the entry, and the margin line is the one this entry was kept for. It is this: Readers of the spring catalogue of the day that her logbook records in the milk. The harbour master does not explain it, and the dictionary’s reading is the following. Her logbook — the logbook of the ship below the bay, kept in the calm, level hand of a crew trained to finish the work and file the report — has exactly one day entered not in ink but in weather: a morning when the sea stood flat the colour of milk and the ship wrote the day down in the same flat voice it wrote down Jutland. The spring catalogue of that day is the one the paperer at 14 Agiou Konstantinou, Athens, stamps with the pomegranate — the same stamp the Persephone in Popular Culture, FAQ, question 6 keeps on its standing list of borrowed detail — and one copy of it has, for reasons the registry will not give, been pinned inside the ticket hatch at the north pier since before the ice season, where every crossing party is handed it wet or dry, three crossings a day, weather permitting and the bay permitting, which is the same thing. The dreamers of this entry have all held that copy. It is in the coat pocket. The line means: the readers are you, if you have held it, and the ferry will find you at the north pier before the 10:00 crossing whether you know it or not.

iv. Occurrence

The dream occurs to keepers of registers — gate logs, almanacs, brochures, the numbers rail of a viewing platform where the platform is solid and the figures are not all agreed upon — and it occurs most often in the week after a fast thing has been seen outrunning something slower and heavier that it ought to have waited for. It occurs reliably to those who have crossed a bay in a ferry of forty minutes, because the dream is a crossing, and the crossing is the whole of it. It occurs less often to those who have never watched a ship arrive before the sound of it, because the ten seconds are the dream, exactly, and it will not form without them. When it occurs, note the two dates the visitor keeps in a pocket — the morning of 1 September 1939, when the old pre‑dreadnought’s 28 cm turrets opened at 4:50 off Westerplatte, and the afternoon of 25 June 1946, when the bay took her — and know that the dream has no use for either. The battlecruiser is not in the first date and she is not sunk in the second. She is the ship that was not there, moving at thirty knots on a longer hull, arriving first, and the dream’s whole work is the work of keeping her arriving.

v. Prognosis

Good, if the bell is the 12:30 and the catalogue is in the pocket, and the milk holds its colour to the horizon. The ship will not stop; that is the whole of the omen. If the sea goes back to its own dark, ordinary colour before she reaches the rail, the entry is not a dream but a forecast, and the harbour master’s board at the north pier will show it as a cancellation of nothing, at no hour, for a ship no manifest names. If you dream it again, bring the brochure: the ferry runs from the north pier at Gotenhafen, three crossings a day, 10:00 / 12:30 / 16:00, and the wreck lies about 1.5 nautical miles off the pier at roughly fourteen metres, bow to the east, and is not moving for the second time — the harbour brochure, No. 12, before the 16:00 crossing. And if the pomegranate on the catalogue stamp is what turned the sea, the arithmetic of the seeds is another keeper’s work — six months above, six months below, whether the hull likes it or not — filed at Persephone in Popular Culture, from the surface down. Do not count the knots. That is the registry’s work, not yours. Return to the front page when the fog stands off the bay, which it does, as a matter of record, eventually.
