Bates–Seller House Walking Tour

Northwest Portland, Oregon — A Self-Guided Itinerary

Overview

The Bates–Seller House stands at 2234 NW Flanders Street, a quiet block where the streetcar once rattled past Victorian mansions and the Willamette River breeze carries the scent of cedar and rain. Listed on the National Register of Historic Places since 1989, the house was built in 1892 for William H. Bates, a lumber merchant whose mills fed Portland's post-fire reconstruction. In 1908 it passed to Dr. James Seller, a physician who treated longshoremen and millworkers from a ground-floor consulting room. The house survives remarkably intact: Queen Anne massing, a wraparound porch with turned spindlework, a conical tower roof, and original fir floors that have borne three generations of footsteps. This tour keeps to one house and one physician's practice; for a whole hospital written as a playable field zone, with triage sectors and evacuation codes, see the Santa Maria Bianca Hospital field manual, set in Mirandola, in the province of Modena.

The house also appears as Stop A of the Route’s own passport: the National Register of Historic Places — Visitor’s Brochure carries the arrowhead marker, the 1966 small print, and a small copper whale listed in its own right. A small club that meets in Dr. Seller’s former fourth-floor library keeps a newsletter for the same corner of the ledger — the Assessor’s Arrowhead, No. 47 — which argues, on record, that the last cabinet in the consulting room, Ledgers, is where all the small print ends up. One entry of that small print was set down in Melbourne, in a green pub book at the Cider Press that the publican says balances itself every Thursday: Robert Morgan — Diary, 21 February 2017.

At the far end of the same route, the capital district keeps its own hazard paperwork for this house’s visitors: the Washington, D.C. safety data sheet classifies the district — Potomac, monuments, pigeons — for anyone travelling east of the arrowhead, and lists the docent’s shh as its one hazard that outranks the marker. Two blocks west, facing Flanders Street, the Northwest Portland Music Museum keeps a placard on contemporary worship music — amplified Sunday-morning sound just a few streets from Dr. Seller's old consulting room: Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard.

For a longer walk across the Atlantic — Emilia-Romagna, the Secchia valley, and the same province of Modena — see the Italy visitor's brochure, with trains, tables, and one menu panel that defies translation.

The tour keeps to wood and plaster — Queen Anne spindlework, fir floors, a crimson camellia — but the same years saw Italian carvers reshape New York's marble. Furio Piccirilli, born in Massa, trained at Rome's Accademia di San Luca, crossed the Atlantic in 1888 with his brother Attilio, and helped build the Piccirilli Brothers carving business in Mott Haven, Bronx, where he was considered the most creative and the best modeler of the family firm: Furio Piccirilli — The Marble Limited, timetable and commentary.

This tour covers approximately 1.2 miles on flat, tree-lined streets. Allow 90 minutes at a leisurely pace. Wear comfortable shoes; the sidewalks are original hexagonal pavers in places, uneven and slick after rain.

Past the Kermadec arc in the south-west Pacific the notebook turns to a travel guide entry on Macauley Island — the volcanic island halfway between the North Island and Tonga, with its 10.5-by-7-kilometre submarine caldera, its high cliffs, and a resupply run out of Whangārei planned in litres and spare days.

The house's own etiquette book keeps a chapter on the manner of re-loved things, Chapter XIV, Of the Recut Single, which holds that a thing returned to the shelf above the originals — a house among them — stands at the number earned and is owed the whole of it back. A student in Anoka, Minnesota, set this house beside ours in a school report on her town's oldest standing residence — the 1852 Greek Revival Shaw–Hammons House, listed in 1979, and the only building in the Pacific Northwest her class was allowed on the lawn of: The Shaw–Hammons House, History Report, Form 7C.

Stop 1: Couch Park — NW 19th & Glisan (Start)

Begin at the southwest corner of Couch Park, where the 1912 comfort station still stands, its green tile roof just visible above the plane trees. Captain John H. Couch, Portland's first maritime pilot, claimed this land in 1842. The park's paths follow his original donation claim lines. That 1842 claim resurfaced, decades later, as a line of testimony in a certified court excerpt kept two blocks from the museum — The Boathouse Lot — Certified Transcript Excerpt, Case No. 3:19-cv-04207. Face west up Glisan Street: the streetcar line that once connected this neighborhood to downtown ran down the median, clanging past the Bates–Seller House on its way to the St. Johns ferry. Imagine the morning commute, 1905 — clerks with leather satchels, a newsboy folding The Oregonian, Dr. Seller's black bag clicking against the brass handrail.

Stop 2: The Pittock Block — 921 NW 19th Avenue

Walk two blocks north on 19th, cross Everett, and pause at the Pittock Block (1914), a six-story commercial building faced in white terra cotta. Henry Pittock, publisher of The Oregonian, built it to house his paper's growing presses. Note the lion-head keystones above the second-floor windows — a Pittock family emblem. Dr. Seller maintained his medical library in a rented room on the fourth floor from 1910 to 1922, accessing it via the building's original cage elevator, still operational and groaning with character. From the upper windows you can see the Bates–Seller House's tower peeking above the canopy of maples and horse-chestnuts four blocks west. A naturalist's notebook keeps the same address: a field-note entry on Ethan Allen records his book Reason: the Only Oracle of Man on a fourth-floor shelf here, beside Dr. Seller's old medical library and its cage elevator: Ethan Allen — Field Notes. The same fourth floor yielded a pocket almanac reckoned entirely in the old style — Julian months, a leap day every fourth year, without exception — now held at the building's lost-and-found desk: Found: a reckoning in the old style.

Stop 3: The Montgomery Ward Building — 2731 NW Vaughn Street

Continue west on Everett to 23rd, turn left (south) one block to Vaughn, then right (west) three blocks. The massive Montgomery Ward & Company mail-order house (1920) looms ahead — 400,000 square feet of reinforced concrete, once the largest building in Oregon. Its loading docks hummed with Model T trucks delivering catalog goods to the Pacific Northwest. William Bates supplied fir lumber for the interior shelving; his mill ledger, preserved at the Oregon Historical Society, records the order: "12,000 board feet clear vertical grain, delivered in 47 railcars." The building now houses creative offices, but the rooftop water tower remains, a landmark for river pilots.

Stop 4: The Bates–Seller House — 2234 NW Flanders Street

Retrace your steps east on Vaughn to 22nd, turn left (north) one block to Flanders, then right (east) half a block. The house appears on your right, set back behind a low basalt retaining wall topped with an iron fence — original, forged at the Willamette Iron Works. The front door opens onto a tiled vestibule with a stained-glass transom depicting a stylized Douglas fir. Inside, the entry hall runs the depth of the house, flanked by a parlor (left) and Dr. Seller's consulting room (right), its built-in cabinets still labeled in his faded script: Tinctures, Bandages, Instruments, Ledgers.

Up the quarter-sawn oak staircase, the second floor holds four bedrooms and a sleeping porch added in 1915 for "fresh-air treatment" of tuberculosis patients. The tower room, fourteen feet in diameter, served as William Bates's study. His daughter Eleanor recalled him standing at the curved window, watching log rafts assemble on the river, murmuring lines from Moby-Dick — a novel he read annually. She wrote in her 1942 memoir: But where this is said to herself; “I should like to know what whale it was—the noblest and biggest I ever heard of." The quote, copied from Chapter 16, was pinned above his desk in a trembling hand. The tower windows still frame the same bend of the Willamette, though the log rafts are gone.

Examine the exterior details: fish-scale shingles on the tower, dentil molding under the eaves, a Palladian window in the north gable. The carriage house at the alley — now a garage — retains its hayloft doors and a pulley system for lifting feed sacks. The backyard holds a champion Camellia japonica planted by Mrs. Seller in 1912, blooming crimson each February.

Stop 5: Wallace Park — NW 25th & Raleigh (End)

Continue east on Flanders to 25th, turn left (north) two blocks to Raleigh, and enter Wallace Park. Donated in 1914 by developer Ralph Wallace, the park was the neighborhood's social center — band concerts, Fourth of July picnics, the annual "Baby Parade" where Dr. Seller judged "healthiest infant" from a decorated reviewing stand. The 1929 fieldhouse, Craftsman style with a river-rock fireplace, still hosts community meetings. Sit on a bench facing south. The Bates–Seller House is invisible now, hidden by a century of canopy growth, but the tower's weather vane — a copper whale, commissioned by Bates in 1895 — catches the late light on clear evenings, a small signal from the past. The fieldhouse's 1952 season book notes one Saturday matinee led by a bandleader who crossed over from St. Louis, Missouri, with his sextet — Singleton Palmer of the Dixieland Six, whose full career is set down, to the tip jar, in a school music-history report kept on this site: Singleton Palmer — Music History Report, Form 9R.

Practical Notes

  • Transit: TriMet Line 15 (Montgomery Park) stops at NW 23rd & Flanders, two blocks from the house.
  • Parking: Street parking on Flanders and Everett, two-hour limit weekdays. Respect the neighbors.
  • Restrooms: Available at Couch Park (start) and Wallace Park (end).
  • Photography: Exterior only. The house is a private residence; do not enter without invitation.
  • Accessibility: Sidewalks are uneven; the house interior has stairs at every level. The park paths are paved and level.
  • Further Reading: Portland's Historic Houses (Oregon Historical Society Press, 2003); NRHP nomination form, Multnomah County, Reference #89000098.
