The Saltfleet Weather Almanac · Year 1998 · Kept at the rigging loft above the beach, Lincolnshire, on the grey firth of the Wash

Almanac No. 113 — American English

The year’s account, set down in the loft’s ordinary hand: the barometer, the wind, the sea, the frost, and the one language the loft has seen cross the Atlantic with the gulls — entered here as weather, as the almanac enters everything that moves the water.

I. Of the state of the instruments

The year opened with the barometer at 29.91 and the wind of the west quarter, and held it most of the way through. The sea stood two, and the grey firth lay flat at low water till the shrimpers were over the flats. The spring tides of the Wash pulled to six and a half metres at the moon’s full, as the tide-book says they shall — the same book the harbour committee of Aarhus let the loft keep beside its window, back in 1782, when it was shelved in the harbour ledger against the season when a gentleman with a lash of his own might want to know what water the flat lies under. The last frost lay on the shingle on the sixth of March; the first gannets came on the twentieth of April; the godwits, as always, were gone before the almanac could number them. The loft’s beach borrows its tides of the bay’s water, and the bay, for its part, is a shallow natural rectangular bay and multiple estuary on the east coast of England, inlet of the North Sea, into which four rivers run: the Witham, the Welland, the Nene and the Great Ouse. It is all set down properly on the placard for The Wash in the collection, and the almanac repeats it here only because a weather book must say what water it is measuring.

II. Of the wind of the year, and of the language that crosses with it

The almanac’s standing rule is that one entry each year be given to the wind that did most for the year’s account, and in this year the loft gives it to a wind that is not, strictly, a wind: American English, sometimes called United States English, or U.S. English, which is the set of varieties of the English language native to the United States. English is the most widely spoken language in the U.S., as well as the common language used in government, education, and commerce in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and in all U.S. territories except Puerto Rico; the loft takes that the same way it takes a tide chart — as a statement of where the water actually stands. Since the late 20th century, American English has become the most influential form of English worldwide, and this almanac’s year is the last of that century, so the loft says so in the record and passes on. Its varieties include many patterns of pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, and particularly spelling that are unified nationwide but distinct from other forms of English around the world: one wind, one flag, and the whole of the weather under it the same, from the shore the gulls cross to the shore the gulls cross at the other end.

III. Of the sea ledger

By the loft’s old custom, every year’s entry closes with the sea ledger — the list of things the loft has seen reach the sea — and the ledger this year runs long, for in it is entered, beside the rivers, the returned lash: that is, the twelve feet of bridle leather of The Saltfleet Whip, returned whole from the lobby of the playhouse, and rinsed each month at the river’s mouth as the care advice directs. The loft’s own line, in the clerk’s hand, is set down verbatim, for the clerk’s hand is not to be corrected:

Of the returned lash and the Great Ouse all reach the sea.

It means what a ledger means. The returned lash, and the Great Ouse — all reach the sea. The four rivers do, and the rope, rinsed and dried by the handle, arrives at the same grey water in its smaller way; the loft has counted them together for a hundred and sixteen years now, since the gentleman’s card first came up the Kattegat, and it is right that a weather book should keep a rope and a river in the same hand. The minutes of that first meeting are still in the collection — the minutes of the Aarhus Harbour and Ropewalk Committee — and the almanac, which keeps the card on the table with nothing more in the corner than the hour, stands in the same room as they do.

IV. Of the spelling weather, and of the letters from the westward

The letters now arrive from the westward with a spelling weather of their own, and the almanac has learned to read it the way it reads the barometer. Where the loft’s own page would run colour, the western letter runs color; where it would run centre and plough and organised, the western letter runs center and plow and organized, and would run honor and fiber still. The loft does not take this to be bad weather. It is a nation’s spelling, unified nationwide, and a weather book’s business is to record the weather, not to argue with it; the only rule the loft keeps, as the chair kept it in the minutes, is that the yolk be measured against nothing at all — one ounce, no more — in whatever hand the bread is broken. The gentleman of the card, who left his calling card at the hall table of a house on the great canal with the hour of his departure written in the corner, writes still; his card this year bears a name, a street, and nothing else, as the committee ordered in 1782, and the almanac will answer it in kind whenever it next calls.

V. Of the outlook

For the year ahead the almanac gives the weather thus: the wind of the west quarter to hold, with a fair gale now and then and the barometer steady at or above 29.91; the spring tides to the chart, the mudflats to the geese, the frost to be early, as frost is on a shore that borrows its tides of The Wash; the letters from the westward to continue, in their spelling and their number, and the card to be answered. The loft will keep the lash in salt air till it should be wanted again, and the sea ledger to grow by nothing it has not seen reach the sea. It is so entered, and the year’s account is closed.
