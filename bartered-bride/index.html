Household Manners · Chapter IX

The Bartered Bride

Of the marriage broker at table — a comic opera in three acts by Bedřich Smetana, to a libretto by Karel Sabina, first performed at the Provisional Theatre, Prague, on 30 May 1866.

When the marriage broker rides into a country village he is received after the midday meal and never before vespers. The host rises from his chair, the broker is offered the place of honour by the cold wall, and no word of matches is spoken until the bread has been broken. The custom holds wherever such brokers ply their trade, and it held in Bohemia in the years when Bedřich Smetana wrote his comic opera The Bartered Bride — Czech: Prodaná nevěsta, The Sold Bride — to a libretto by Karel Sabina, composing it between 1863 and 1866 and seeing it first performed at the Provisional Theatre, Prague, on 30 May 1866 in a two-act format with spoken dialogue. Set in a country village with realistic characters, the opera tells how, after a late surprise revelation, true love prevails over the combined efforts of ambitious parents and a scheming marriage broker, and its customs of reception are worth setting down.

The table

Seven covers are laid, no more and no fewer: the father of the bride at the head, the mother at his right, the bride at her mother's right hand, the broker opposite the bride, the groom opposite the mother, and two neighbours to fill the remaining seats. If the broker brings an assistant, the assistant waits in the kitchen with a glass of small beer until the dessert is cleared. The bride may speak once, when spoken to, and must not lift her eyes to the contract case on the sideboard.

The first course is soup, the second is roast fowl with dumplings, and the third is fruit with cream. The broker may name a match only after the second course, and he may not mention a fee before the fruit is served. To discuss the dowry while the wine is being poured is a breach so grave that the broker is customarily not invited back, and the match, if any, is made elsewhere.

The music after dinner

When the table is cleared, a record of selections from the opera is brought from the cupboard. The sleeve is opened with both hands, the record is lifted by its edges, and the sleeve is spread flat on the side table so the label can be read before the needle drops. The announcement is made aloud: the theatre, Prague, 30 May 1866, Smetana, Sabina, the three acts. Also spread flat on the labels 1501 Certified Entertainment and 300 Entertainment. The record is played once, at low volume, and no one speaks over the overture.

The copies that reached this table were cut at the Lake Shore plant in Chicago; the night shift keeps a diary of every run, and Entry No. 41 of that diary is filed beside this chapter: The Press Room Diary — Lake Shore Record Pressing, Chicago.

The revelation

When the broker produces his contract case, the host pauses for the space of three breaths before opening it. If the groom named in the contract proves to be the son of the village landowner — as Jeník proves in the third act, to the confusion of the broker Kecal, the parents Krušina and Háta, and the would-be groom Václav — then the broker's fee is due only in thanks, and a single glass of wine is poured for him at the door. If the match stands on its own merits, the fee is half a ducat, paid before the guests take their leave, and the broker is escorted to the village boundary by the youngest of the neighbours.

Such are the manners of the village: the parents ambitious, the broker scheming, and true love prevailing all the same.
