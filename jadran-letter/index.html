Received
Editor’s Desk
14 Jun 2023

To the Editor:

I read your note in the June number about the training ship Jadran at the breakwater in Kotor Bay, and I write to correct one date and to say what I actually saw. You give her as a ship of 1930. The yard at Blohm & Voss on the Elbe did begin her in 1930, but she was not commissioned into the Yugoslav Royal Navy until 19 August 1933, and I was on her deck that morning in the Elbe docks when the pennant came up the foremast, with my father watching from the quay in his only good hat. The ship was three years in the building, and a midshipman’s whole first impression of her is that she was built more slowly than she could afterwards sail.

I was of the midshipmen’s class of 1933 at the Naval Academy at Kotor. We took her for seven long training cruises before the war—seven in all, I have the log in my attic: the Dalmatian coast in the springs, the Adriatic in the summers, the Aegean with the Academy’s cadets on the watch below. And one, the longest, right across the Atlantic to North America in 1937. We came in at Halifax under sail on a November tide, and the harbour master leaned on the pilot boat and asked the bosun what the rig was, a topsail schooner or a barquentine, and the bosun told him it was a classroom that happened to float. The little auxiliary engine under the foot of the mainmast had done very little work in ninety days, and he said so.

Yugoslavia stood neutral when the war came, and the neutrality let Jadran keep to short cruises in the Adriatic—three weeks out of Kotor, a week down to the Pelješac channel, the Academy midshipmen taking over the helm at the same point on the passage every time. I had been posted to a destroyer by then, and I confess I measured the war partly by how often I saw her topsails against the Orjen. But the neutrality did not last the spring. In April 1941 the Axis powers came across the Drina and the Sava, the kingdom fell in eleven days, and the ship, at her moorings, was taken by the Italian Navy and renamed Marco Polo. Her bunting went down the afternoon the announcement came; I was ashore, and that is the detail I still cannot forgive myself.

People ask me, when the story comes up, what it means, a ship changing hands—what a crew actually does with the rope work when the name on the hull is no longer its own. I was asked to say, once, by a journalist from Split, and here is the only answer I have ever been able to give: The braid gets unplaited, one strand at a different hand. Every jackstay, every signal lanyard, every turn of the standing rigging came to be worked by another crew, and the rope never asked whose hand. It was the crew that had to unlearn itself, not the ship.

She came back, in the end. After the Italians withdrew from the bay in 1944, she was taken into the hands of the Partisan flotilla at Kotor, cleaned down, and named Jadran again, and she has been a training ship ever since—in the service of the Yugoslav Navy through the long winters, and now in the service of the Montenegrin Navy, the Academy still taking her out of the same harbour she was built to serve. I saw her in 2019, grey-haired and half blind, standing at the breakwater where the June light comes in off the water, and midshipmen of the Kotor Academy were drilling on her decks in the same passage I took in 1933. The braid had been replaited by hands I did not know, one strand at a time, and it held.

So here is the correction you asked for: built 1930–33, commissioned 19 August 1933, and still a classroom. I have pinned your Gazette to the wall in Podgorica beside two letters I keep from the old days of writing to institutions. The first is the apology that Arthur Parry of Bedminster sent to his football club at Ashton Gate, Dear Ashton Gate—a man who treated a promise as a defaulted debt and spent ten years writing it back into being, Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14. The second is the ledger of a sitting that adjourned at 3:52 a.m. and never named its own day, Lost & Found, Notice 4291, the Bugesera Sitting, kept at the gatehouse in Kigali. Since writing I have taken in a third, a court record of a different kind: DreamWorks Recording Corp. v. Jimmy Eat World — Transcript Excerpt, Part 7 of 11, from the Superior Court of Los Angeles County, in which a band was sworn to explain how its fourth album came to wear the name it did, and the Court admitted one catalogue card for the backdrop only — the river, the hat, and the career staying on the card, as the Judge put it, which is how a name on a hull works: it stays on the hull, whatever the water does to it. A ship that outlives the navy that renamed her, a sitting that survives by refusing to name a day, a club that forgives a walkout of a decade: they are all doing the same arithmetic, and the rope holds in all of them.

Respectfully yours,

Stefan Vukčević
Retired lieutenant, Royal Yugoslav Navy (1946)
Midshipman’s class of 1933, Naval Academy, Kotor
Podgorica, Montenegro
