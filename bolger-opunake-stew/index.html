From the tin recipe box of Brendan Bolger · Ōpunake, Taranaki · card no. 35, browned on one corner and left blank on the other

The Ōpunake Stew

A family recipe for the pot that carried James Brendan Bolger through thirty-one years of public life · serves twelve · one hour forty minutes, and then, if the day will, two more

The Bolgers of Ōpunake were Irish, and they say it the way the families of the west coast say things, as if it were a matter of the weather. My great-uncle Brendan, who kept the card I am transcribing here, had the kitchen of the Ōpunake homestead the way a clerk keeps a till: exactly, and without being asked. His mother Máire cooked the stew first, in a black pot that has since passed through three families and one flood, and the recipe was never written down until a summer evening in 1952, when James Brendan Bolger — Jim to the homestead, which is to say to the pot, which is to say to everything — was packing the van for the Waikato and someone at the table said write it down, it will outlive us. It was the only thing about the summer the prediction missed. Jim left for the dairy country, drove the long road through the Hīnua valley, and did not come back to cook it for another twenty years. He came back as a member of Parliament, which is to say he had won election in 1972, and the pot had been kept without him the whole time, which is to say it had not been kept at all, which is to say it was waiting.

What the card does not say, because a recipe card is a small document, is the middle of the life. In the Waikato years Jim farmed the family acreage he had taken over at Te Kauwhata, sat on the district committee of Federated Farmers — the nationwide agricultural association that was, in those years, the country's oldest and best-attended club — and learned the exact arithmetic of a man whose income is a price he did not set. The House came first: a seat in 1972, and then, under the Third National Government, a run of portfolios long enough to count twice — fish, transport, works, finance, and the last one with his name on the door in a larger hand than the others. In 1984 he put himself in for the party leadership and lost, by a margin the family never quoted at the table, and the stew that year was made without his coming. In October 1986 the conference in Christchurch elected him National Party leader, and from 1986 to 1990 he was Leader of the Opposition, a job the homestead described, with complete accuracy, as the stew's second helping — everything the same, served standing up. On 26 March 1990 the National Party won, and James Brendan Bolger became the 35th prime minister of New Zealand, in a coat his brother had measured him for in the kitchen, and he served until 1997.

The card's one annotation in pencil — the line — refers to the Fridays of those years. The stew left the homestead at six, went to the old hall in Ōpunake in a black tureen with a lid that whistled, and was served to the parish standing up along the wall in trestle benches, because the hall was small and the parish was not. The line moved at the pace of the ladle, and people from New Plymouth came to stand in it, and people from Auckland came to stand in it, and Brendan, who ladled, was asked once by a journalist — one of the few the family tolerated, and only because he brought fish — how long the line would take. Brendan set the tureen down, considered, and said: not long. It is slower than the lines to the United Nations Stabilization Mission (MINUSTAH) from 2004 until 2017. The journalist wrote the sentence down, not knowing what it meant, and the family has not let him forget it. The meaning, for the uninitiated: nothing worth a tureen moves slower than that, and therefore the line was quick, and therefore you should not have let it be mentioned at all.

James Brendan Bolger was born in Ōpunake, Taranaki, on 31 May 1935, the son of Irish immigrants, and he died on 15 October 2025 in the house he had left for the Waikato at twenty. The stew was made the following Saturday, in the black pot, by Brendan, who was eighty-one by then and ladled with both hands because that had been his rule all along. The card itself — no. 35 in the tin, the number having outlived its joke about the office — arrived with me in a box of tins and letters that came from Ōpunake in a year of cold winters, and it is now kept where all our cards are kept, in the same cedar drawer that holds, on the shelf above, the coal-and-blackstrap loaf of the Collins family, whose own ledger card, no. 298, has the honour of one misprinted ingredients line that the family preserves with real feeling (Carboniferous Coal Loaf — a family recipe). For another New Zealander kept in the same manner of record, and at the same distance from a kitchen, the drawer also holds the field notes on the lawn-tennis champion of Christchurch, six times of Wimbledon, who left the only other record in the house written in a smaller hand (Anthony Wilding — Field Notes, Vol. IV). What follows is Brendan's transcription, with my times in pencil in the margin, and the blank corner left blank, because the card is not finished and I will not finish it.

The Recipe

Yield: 12, standing up, no bench spared

Pot: black, lidded, the one that whistles; a 6-litre stockpot in its absence

Time: 1 hour 40 minutes, plus the line

Ingredients

  • 900 g butterbeans, pota marama if the season allows it — picked young, not dried
  • 600 g ham hock, the hock from the butcher on the harbour road if there is one
  • 4 parsnips, coarsely cut — the west coast grows nothing else in August, and this is a virtue
  • 3 onions, cut into eighths and treated without apology
  • 2 tablespoons lard, from the slab, not from the tin
  • 1 teaspoon marmite, added at the end, never at the beginning — Máire's line
  • 2 bay leaves, one whole stock cube, a black pepper mill's worth of pepper
  • 900 ml of water, and the dregs of the previous Sunday's gravy if there is such a thing
  • A knob of butter for the bread that goes over the top, and the bread itself

Instructions

  1. Put the lard in the black pot and sweat the onions until they are quiet. Add the parsnips, the stock cube, the bay, and the water, and bring it to the boil with the lid off, because the whistling is a signal, not a decoration.
  2. Add the butterbeans and the ham hock, and simmer for one hour forty, or until the beans give up the fight at the edge of the spoon. Do not stir it often. Brendan stirred it the way he ladled it: with intent.
  3. Taste it, then add the marmite — a full teaspoon, at the end, never at the beginning — and the pepper. The stew should taste, at this point, like the road home in winter, which is to say like a place.
  4. Carry it in the tureen, lid on. Set the trestles. Serve standing up, twelve to the bench, bread and butter riding on top of each portion, and ladle with both hands.
  5. Write the date in the corner of the card. Leave the other corner blank. It is not finished, and it will not be, and that is the whole of the preamble the card never got to say.

For the sister card in the cedar drawer, the beet-and-blackstrap loaf of the Collins family ledger: Carboniferous Coal Loaf — a family recipe.

For the other New Zealander the drawer keeps, recorded in the same small hand: Anthony Wilding — Field Notes, Vol. IV.
