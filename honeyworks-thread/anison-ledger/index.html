§ 9.1 Definition

An anime song (Japanese: アニメソング, Hepburn: anime songu; also shortened to anison (アニソン)) is a genre of music originating from Japanese pop music. Anime songs consist of theme, insert, and image songs for anime, manga, video game, and audio drama CD series, as well as any other song released primarily for the anime market, including music from Japanese voice actors. Field note: the word anison is doing the same work in the trade that the wet honey does on the Kaitō board — a whole shelf of objects referred to by the taste of the one.

§ 9.2 Provenance & Timeline

The anime song genre was first defined as a musical category in the 1970s, when television anime took on opening and ending numbers as a standing fixture of the broadcast. It later gained popularity from the public when mainstream artists began releasing songs as tie-ins for anime series — the point at which the form stopped being an accompaniment to a show and started selling seats on its own. By the 1990s, it became redefined as a separate genre when companies began creating record labels that would handle the anime market exclusively, and the song acquired its own racks, its own charts, its own collectors.

§ 9.3 Field Identification: the Three Classes

Every anison in the field answers to one of three masters, and the archivist is expected to name the class before naming the song.

主題歌

THEME

Opening or ending of a broadcast. Clocks the series; the audience learns the hook before the first plot point lands. Handle with the episode count in hand.

CLASS I · OP / ED
挿入歌

INSERT

Dropped mid-episode, usually mid-argument. The Ren'ai Series runs on this class: a confession needs the song the way it needs the follow-up meeting.

CLASS II · MID-SCENE
イメージ

IMAGE

Character songs and audio drama CD material — the voice actor as performer, the song as a place a character can stand. The committee roster, sung.

CLASS III · CD / CHARACTER

§ 9.4 The Kaitō Case File: HoneyWorks inside the Genre

The group this manual is filed beside is itself an anison case, worked from both ends. HoneyWorks is a Japanese musical group made up of songwriters Gom and shito and illustrator Yamako: the uploads opened in 2010 on NicoNico Douga on a two-week rhythm, the debut album went out on MusicRay'n in 2014, and the same year the unit CHiCO with HoneyWorks took the singer into the fold. The Kokuhaku Jikkō Iinkai: Ren'ai Series is the genre's paperwork turned into a story — every confession filed with a committee, sealed, and followed up, which is to say, an insert song with a docket number. The main thread of the argument is kept where it has always been, at the Kaitō Music Fan List, Thread No. 214, and the site's song obituaries elsewhere — see the obituary for Everywhere, a single that stayed on the radio — are worth keeping in the same binder as a reminder that one song, sung long enough, becomes a genre's weather.

§ 9.5 Archival Protocol: The 2010 Upload Log

The standing rule of the chapter, printed here because it has been argued in the room more than once. The original 2010 upload log is taped into the back of gomwatch's 2011 AKB calendar; it lists the year's uploads on the two-week cycle, cover set by cover set, in the same warm ochre palette Yamako used all year. Before the spring gathering, any member who wants to weigh in on what the 2014 album meant — what the label filing decided, what the committee decided, what the honeycomb stamp on the spine decided — is to stand in meeting room B and read the log, page by page, in order, without skipping the two weeks where the covers changed palette and nobody has settled why. Read it before you form an opinion on what the uploads had already decided. The archivist will say only: the log is the album's oldest member, and the album arrived already carrying it.

§ 9.6 Handling Precautions

Handle anison the way the list handles everything: with the jar in hand. The stationer below the room still sells the small honey jars at one hundred and twenty yen, the only in-room purchase the bylaws allow, and a chapter deck run without one is a chapter deck run wrong. If a drink appears in a photo from a 2014 unit shoot, note it, do not vote on it — the list has voted more than once, and the vote stands. And if the minutes of the spring gathering break mid-sentence again, as the pin board line still stands, between 1 March and something the typist never finished, do not correct it. The capital B in the middle belongs to her. Keep the sentence standing, the way the genre kept the 1970s standing inside the 1990s, and the way the 2010 uploads kept the whole chapter standing before anyone was allowed to name it.
