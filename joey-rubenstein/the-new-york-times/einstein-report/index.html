1 · The subject, in brief

The subject of this report was a German-born theoretical physicist best known for developing the theory of relativity, and he made important contributions to quantum theory on the side, as a man makes contributions to a garden. He was born Albert Einstein on 14 March 1879 in Ulm, a town on the Danube, then a subject to the Kingdom of Württemberg, part of the German Empire — and it is worth recording, for the record-keepers, that he later renounced that citizenship on 28 January 1896, just six weeks before he turned seventeen, avoiding conscription into military service. The Empire has outlived him by only a few years and him by none of his own; the Württemberg line on my certificate is struck through in blue pencil.

His mass–energy equivalence formula, E = mc², which arises from special relativity, has been called "the world's most famous equation." I have written it out three times on the back of the enrolment slip. The class agreed it is the only equation short enough to fit in a manila envelope, which is the envelope the Form keeps for its finished reports, the one Mr. Delgado insists on because, he says, the sound of the manila envelope has not yet left any of us. The slip has since done some work off the avenue: the ticket clerk of the Eighth Avenue Line — service times with commentary, No. 13 of the field series — buys his whole stock of tickets in this number, and files the remainder in the envelope with the rest. The field notes of the paper at 620 Eighth Avenue, kept by our own collector two doors down, record that the Times has chronicled the city for well over a century; this report is merely the Form 3B edition of the same business, written smaller and in worse handwriting.

2 · Observations, 1905–1955

In the year 1905 the subject, then an examiner at a patent office in Bern rather than a professor, published the work that made Bern the temporary capital of the universe; it is called, with some justification, the annus mirabilis. Special relativity followed in 1905 as well, and from it, by an almost accidental slip of the pencil, came the famous equation. General relativity took him another decade and, the textbooks say, a full eight years of refusing to give up. In 1921 he received the Nobel Prize in Physics "for his services to theoretical physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect" — that is, the committee, in its wisdom, paid him for the light work while the relativity work stood in the corner unpraised, which he, the sources agree, found faintly amusing.

He came to the United States in 1933, a matter our own family keeps a ledger of, and settled at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey, where he was said to walk to the office in a cardigan and whistle. He died in Princeton on 18 April 1955, at the age of seventy-six, in the same house he had lived in since 1933.

3 · The front page, 19 April 1955

The paper reported his death at the top of its first page the following morning, 19 April 1955, and our class took up the issue on our walk to the avenue. It was the only front page I have ever held where the whole sheet seemed to be holding its breath. The ledger habit of the paper — day by day, what happened, filed and kept — is exactly the habit this Form is being trained in, which is why Mr. Delgado chose the subject. A school report about a man whose name the register of the city is required to record is, he says, a school report about the register itself. I understand the point. I have filed my copy of the 19th in the manila envelope with the rest.

The envelope travels in a folder with one notice that the gulf-facing office pinned on a June morning of 2006 — the first storm of that season, found and lost again, 70 mph and a barometric slip of 908 millibars: Lost & Found Notice Nº 106, Tropical Storm Alberto. The register does not seem to care which kind of arrival it records. The same folder keeps, by the school's standing habit of filing one good record of a life next to every other, a sixteen-section safety data sheet drawn up on an English excavator of landscapes, Mick Aston (1 July 1946 – 24 June 2013): Safety Data Sheet, Rev. 15, Article no. MS-1946-0701-OM, where a life 1946 to 2013 is kept the same day-by-day way, and Mr. Delgado has marked it as the register's closest relative on the shelf.

The rest of the miscellany where these papers are kept — the home shelf of Webstuffs, where the field notes and the report and the old ledgers sit side by side — is not mine to arrange, and I have not. I have only written my name on the report, in the place where names are written.

4 · Remarks in the margin (red ink)

— Mr. Delgado, under the passage on the Nobel Prize, where the pencil had gone over the same line three times. The pupil has been asked to keep the pencil where the pencil is.

"A promising register. The ledger will keep him; he need only keep the ledger."
