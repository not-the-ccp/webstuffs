A Book of Etiquette for the Table of the Poets

Second impression · printed for the Household · price sixpence, carriage paid to the Euphrates

Chapter IX
Of Jacob of Serugh and the Manner of Receiving a Mēmrā

“Ephrem first, Narsai last, and Jacob between them, with the door on his side.”

Jacob of Serugh — in the script he wrote in, ܝܥܩܘܒ ܕܣܪܘܓ, Ya‘qu&bbar; da-Sr&bbar;üŠ, and in the Latin books Iacobus Sarugiensis, and in the mouths of his own people simply Mar Jacob, ܡܪܝ ܝܥܩܘܒ — is the third seat at this table, and this book sets him down with its whole care, for a chapter of etiquette on a poet of the mēmrā is the nearest thing the book has to a chapter on a man who spent his life in the church's office, and its manners were not a matter of parlour at all. He is counted the foremost poet of the Syriac tradition after Ephrem the Syrian, and set equal, seat for seat, to Narsai of Nisibis; and the house rules the seating on that strength: Ephrem first, Narsai last, Jacob between them, because his years, c. 452 to 521, straddle both, and the door stands on his side of the table, which is the only piece of furniture the book has ever placed on a man's account of age.

He lived most of his life as an ecclesiastical official at Serugh — Ser&bbar;uġ, which the reader will find in modern Turkey under the name Suruç — and the book notes the distance, because etiquette is a matter of distance: a poet who keeps one town's desk for forty years owes that town a different greeting from one who moves among sees. When he became a bishop, near the end of his life, in 519, he was made bishop of Batna, on the plain, two years before he died, and the chapter is obliged to observe that the house greets the bishop first and the poet second, and then, having observed it once, forgives itself and reverts to the poet.

II. Of the Faith and Its Greeting

Mar Jacob was a Miaphysite of the Non-Chalcedonian party, which the book states without heat, because a book of etiquette has no heat to speak of, and was moderate by the account of those who sat on the hotter benches. The house's rule on the subject is one line long and has survived three impressions: the reader greets his theology as a guest, never as a host, and is not to invite the Christology to supper, since it does not eat, and it has opinions. A visitor from the Chalcedonian side, the clerk adds, is to be received at this table exactly as the visitor is received at any other — by name, with a chair, without a debate — for the mēmrā outlasts every council that argued about it, and the book's loyalty is to the outlasting.

III. Of the Mēmrā and Its Receipt

The mēmrā is the form this chapter is bound to instruct, and the reader will do well to learn its shape before he learns its manners. A mēmrā is a homily for the church year set to a hymn-metre and sung rather than read: it moves in stanzas, and the stanzas share a common rhyming pattern, so that the congregation carries the whole argument in its mouth at once, like a dish with the rim intact. The rules of receipt are four. The reader does not applaud a mēmrā, for the reason that the mēmrā is not performed at him but performed with him. The reader sits with the book open at the rhymes, which is where the argument lives. The reader does not interrupt the fourteenth stanza, whatever he has just heard in the thirteenth. And the reader, when the metre turns from argument to address — and it always turns — is to answer in his own voice, quietly, or not at all; a mēmrā that goes unanswered is a mēmrā that has been fully received, and that is the whole of the etiquette of it.

As to the count, the book refuses to settle it, which is a ruling and the reader is to treat it as one. Some catalogues set him down at well over two hundred mēmrē; others count only the fifty-one they can authenticate to his own hand, and the house keeps the lower figure on the shelf because a number that is hard to reach is a number worth reaching for. Of the fifty-one, the reader is directed to open at the one the tradition calls The Pearl — the mēmrā on the Cross, which the clerk describes, in the only line the clerk has ever spent a full line on, as the stanza that holds the rim. He who opens at The Pearl and keeps his place through the rhymes has read the chapter in the manner intended, and the book will not say more.

IV. Of the Red Line in the Margin

Against the fourth impression of this chapter, in the copy that stands at shelf five and a half, there is a single line in the margin, written in a red ink the binding has not forgiven, and it reads, word for word:

After the match, Graham pulled me aside in the same hand as the grey bronzeback — Build Log · Return to Webstuffs Entered in red, as ordered.

The clerk has ruled it, and the ruling is entered here for the reader. The match was the parish's cricket match of the harvest week; Graham is the parish clerk, who keeps this book's shelf; and the grey bronzeback is the tortoise the clerk keeps on the shelf by the parcel post, whose name was entered in the visitor's book by the hand that entered it — a slow, heavy, deliberate hand, the hand a bronzeback has — and the marginal line was set down in that same hand, which is all the “same hand” of it, the tortoise's keeping and the clerk's. The two words in the middle of the line, Build Log and Return to Webstuffs, are the two headings of the slip that had fallen under the tortoise's shelf and been found there on the morning after the match, and they are quoted as found, because the house quotes its founds. And the last four words, Entered in red, as ordered, are not the writer's at all but the clerk's standing order for this chapter, which is that every margin in it be entered in red ink, so that a reader may tell at a glance which lines are the book's and which lines the book has merely kept.

V. Of the Leaving, and of Kindred Chapters

A visitor leaves this table as the chapter leaves the church year: with the door on his side, the rhymes still in his mouth, and the argument not concluded, because a mēmrā's argument is not concluded until the congregation is. For the council that has sat in this book's house before, and minuted a matter of names with the same care this chapter gives to its seating, see Minutes No. 57 of the Council of the Hall, where the romanization of a Korean name was argued to the last hyphen and one test case was found sufficient. For the kindred house book of the neighbouring press, and its desk of hoops and hardcovers, see Chapter VII, of the Common Language Infrastructure, which the clerk reads beside this one in the grey hour. For a name in English letters, and the table that bears it, see Chapter IV of the book of surnames, where the double l is kept against the wall the way Mar Jacob kept his town. The whole of the household archive stands against this chapter, shelf five, and the tortoise keeps his own shelf beside it, which is a shelf of a kind the book has never had to rule on and never will.
