§ 1 · What the chapter is about

In physics, acceleration is a measure of how fast and in what direction an object’s speed and direction of motion are changing. It is defined as the rate of change of the velocity, and the book is careful to insist on the word velocity and not speed, because a body can be changing all along while remaining the same speed, as a stone swung on a string is, and a guest at the fish market who keeps walking the same circle under the small white sail is. Like velocity, acceleration has a magnitude and a direction, making it a vector quantity; it is not a number so much as a sentence with a verb. The SI unit for acceleration is the metre per second squared (m·s⁻², m/s²), and the etiquette of the unit is this: a polite acceleration is named, never merely observed. One does not say the trolley went; one says the trolley went at so many metres per second squared, in this direction, with the door held. The office has ruled that the unannounced vector is the oldest offence in the corridors of Coopers’ Lane, older than the mud, and that every guest of the school is to declare theirs at the door, or not at all.

§ 2 · The tangential, and the linear when it deserves the name

The tangential acceleration of an object is the component of the acceleration which is in the same direction as the motion — the tangential velocity — of the object, and it is the component the book asks readers to address first, because it is the one doing the talking. The rest of the acceleration, the part that bends the path rather than lengthens it, the book calls the corner, and treats it as a courtesy extended to whatever is around the corner. When the velocity of the object does not change direction, the tangential is the whole of the acceleration, and this is called linear acceleration. The chapter holds that the linear case is the honest one: a jangada running straight out from the landing at Pedro do Rosário, a chalk stick let go of a finger, the spare satellite at Oberpfaffenhofen the day it is finally given a name and a sky. The circular case, by contrast, is a conversation, and conversations are where manners go.

§ 3 · Deceleration, and how to do it in the presence of the cabinet

Deceleration or retardation, on the other hand, is the component of the acceleration in the direction opposed to the motion, and the book devotes this section to it because it is the component most often performed badly in the corridors, and in the company of missing things. The rule is simple and has never once been waived: one reduces rate before one reduces distance. A guest who finds the astronomy cabinet empty on the second shelf — as every guest eventually does, on account of Notice Nº 47, the missing Earth, third planet from the Sun — is asked to arrive slowly, to stop at a measured rate not exceeding half a metre per second squared, and to remain stopped without complaining, which the office regards as a form of patience the planet will be grateful for in its return. Miss Rosalind Marsh, custodian of the office, is reported to decelerate at exactly that rate on the stairs, every day, twice, and to regard it as the whole of her teaching on the subject. The class has been observed, under the small white sail of the drawing in the margin, that the tide itself decelerates at the turning point, which is why the tide table is the truest record and why the tide must not be allowed to turn on you.

§ 4 · The line kept in the book

With the notice of the missing planet the office keeps one folded recipe card, salt-stained, in faded ink, reading the calf being a tent spread over the fish, and mix with these vesper hymns, and the book has ruled that a chapter on the rate of change of velocity is not complete without an example of a rate of change no instrument will ever take. On the back of the card, in a different hand, the book therefore preserves, word for word and without paraphrase, the following line, which the office will not date, will not decode, and will not let the geology desk borrow, having declined the 26Al run once already: Found folded in the above proportion of sugar became naturalized in Australia, furnishes an asylum not being much spread. The chapter asks readers to read it once, slowly, and to observe that it changes direction more than it changes speed, which, on the book’s own account, is a sentence in motion, and therefore a vector, and therefore owing a declaration at the door, which it has never given. It is kept as the book’s standing example of what the class is not to do, and no one has yet been able to say what it is to do instead, which the office calls a success.

§ 6 · Filing

The chapter is filed under ACCELERATION, also under MUD, as the shelf note directs, and cross-filed with the notice for the missing planet and with the class’s own thread on the silver thing pried from under the same shelf — Aluminium (Al), found under the second shelf — on the theory that a metal which has never once failed to come back to the shelf is, by every instrument the school owns, an object of constant position, and therefore zero acceleration, and therefore, if the office is to be believed, the manners of the whole cabinet. The book will keep the chapter open, as it keeps the notice open, until the tide turns, and will then re-file it with whatever the shelf returns, rate declared at the door. A copy of the chapter has gone, second class, to the St. Anne’s Square Post, newsletter No. 61 of the 1953 Society for the Study of One College, which has adopted the office’s rule that no rate of arrival, however selective, should be made without a declaration, and which the office regards as a polite correspondent, the kettle forwarding everything.
