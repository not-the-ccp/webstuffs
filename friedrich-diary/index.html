Diary of H. F. Wren, librarian

The Gemenasian Crate

Two pages taken out of the green-covered diary, the Old Fire Station, Newmarket, November 1953 — on a small painting of a chalk sea, a bare tree, and a man in a dark coat, found in the Ashley parish hall store room in the crate the company left when the farce ended.

11
Saturday November 1953 the Ashley parish hall store room

Fog since before dawn. Went down to Ashley with the clerk and the key the committee lets us have for exactly two hours, to open the crate the Gemenasian Company left in the store room when the farce ended — the same company that, in the under-steward’s apology to the Alps, was measured out in nails of bog iron and offered for sale at the rate set last Michaelmas. The company claims descent from Johan von Blanc and his Gemenasiska Sällskapet, and it is not a claim to be argued over while the fog is out. We have read the obituary in full; the company tumbles, the company fences, the company invites ranges of mountains to pay rent, weekends only, and the fire to be kept.

The crate had been shut with a brass hasp and a rope the colour of old wool. Inside: one lot of scene boards, damp; two lamps with the chimneys broken; a bed of charcoal from the second bloom, which the under-steward had offered to the range and withdrawn, now crumbled to a fine grey powder and doing nobody any harm; and, folded in a padded sleeve of brown paper, one small painting, twenty-two by seventeen centimetres.

Chalk and gouache on paper. A chalk cliff on the left, pale as the fog that is doing its best at the window. The sea below, flat and silver, with one long line of foam. A bare tree, all of it, no leaves, standing in the lower third of the right-hand side. And a man, small, in a dark coat, his back to us, looking up at a moon that is not quite full. The figure is so small against the sea that the first thing I thought, and I am not ashamed to say it, was that it might be a figure of the under-steward, in diminished perspective, in the parish hall itself. On the back, in a firm nineteenth-century hand: C. D. Friedrich, and beneath it, 1823.

I carried it back to Newmarket in my coat, over the heart, wrapped in the brown paper. The clerk carried the crate list. Neither of us spoke until the bridge. The clerk then said that there is a line in the margin of the parish hall’s ledger, by the entry for lamp oil, in a hand that is none of ours, and that it has been kept, word for word, as it stands: “It is by many in that enchanted air did at times affected it.” He asked me what it means. I told him I do not know, and that a line, like a small painting, may be genuine without being explained. He said, quite calmly: very well, then it may stay where it lay. He is a good clerk.

The small painting, twenty-two by seventeen centimetres · C. D. Friedrich, 1823 · found in the crate, the brown paper still around it

Marginal note, same page: the count in the room was four of us, the clerk, the under-steward by letter, and the fog at the window. The kettle was left on. The door was not repainted, as agreed.

12
Sunday November 1953 the Old Fire Station, by the kettle

Slept with the painting under the frame of the reading table. Read all morning in the club room, with the kettle on, because that is what the club does with everything it does not understand: it heats water. Caspar David Friedrich — German: [ˌkaspaʁ ˌdaːvɪt ˈfʁiːdʁɪç] — born at Greifswald in Pomerania on 5 September 1774, brought up in the village of Waldow under an uncle, a doctor, who let him draw; died at Berlin on 7 May 1840, in his sixty-fifth year. The German Romantic landscape painter, generally considered the most important German artist of his generation. His work was symbolic and anti-classical, and it conveys a subjective, emotional response to the natural world. He set contemplative human figures, silhouetted, against night skies, morning mists, barren trees, or Gothic ruins. The art historian Christopher John Murray has written of the presence of those figures, diminished in the landscape, as reducing them to a scale that directs “the viewer’s gaze towards their metaphysical dimension.” That is the whole of the matter, and it explains the man in the dark coat, and why he is small, and why he looks up.

He was elected to the Berlin Academy in 1810, the same year he married Caroline, the young widow of his friend the painter Bommer, a union the Academy found inconvenient and the village of Greifswald found impossible. By 1818 he had painted the Wanderer above the sea of fog, the figure on the rock, the sea of cloud below him, and the mountain above, which is the painting most often copied and least often sat down with. His moonrises over the sea are of 1822 and 1823, the year our painting is dated, and the chalk cliffs of Rügen, where he walked, are the cliffs in our chalk-and-gouache. In his last years he was not in fashion: the students painted him as a fool and the dealers passed his name over. The monogram he signed with at the end — his initials set inside the shape of a coffin — was called, in Berlin, the tomb-monogram. And yet: the moon in our painting is not quite full, and it has kept its light for a hundred and thirty years against a fog that is worse this week than any in Berlin, and it has not lost a single line of it.

The under-steward has written to the committee of the range that the painting stays at the Old Fire Station, in the club’s keeping, with the kettle within reach. A copy of the letter goes up with the Newsletter No. 32, pinned at the noticeboard, and so does the list from the crate. I have entered the small painting in the register as Lot 218, Ashley store room, chalk sea, found, and I have entered beside it the ledger line, word for word, unexplained, exactly as the clerk kept it, because a genuine line, like a genuine moon, is not ours to explain. It is by many in that enchanted air did at times affected it. I leave it standing, and I turn the kettle to tea.

Marginal note, same page: the company that painted our mountains in bog iron did not paint this one. This one was painted before the farce, before the club, before the count of 906,814, in a Pomeranian room with the sea of the Baltic behind the window. The under-steward says it is the only unapologised object in the store room. He is probably right, and he is probably sorry.
