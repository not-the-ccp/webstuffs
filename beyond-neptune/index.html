Field notes · the far ring · volume of the outer territories, est. 1846

Beyond Neptune

Notes from a naturalist of the last quarter of the sky — the ring of frozen bodies past the ninth of the old planets, kept in ink, in carbon, and in one line that was never recovered. The territory is large, cold, and patient. So is the book.

FIELD EX. NO. 47
OUTER RING DIVISION

First season · Berlin, 23 September 1846 · 22°C

Of the Ninth, and the Habit of Expecting a Tenth

It is worth recording the method before the fauna. Le Verrier, working in Paris on the misbehaving orbit of Uranus, argued that a tenth body must exist where the giant planets’ paths disagreed, and gave the sky a place to look. On 23 September 1846, Johann Galle and the nineteen-year-old Heinrich d’Arrest, at the Berlin observatory, found it: Neptune, first sighted within one degree of Le Verrier’s mark, at some 30 units from the Sun. From that night the naturalist’s habit began. A planet is a regularity; and where regularities end — just past the ninth — the eye keeps looking, the way a birder keeps a binocular on the tree line after the last good bird. The territory of these notes is that tree line: everything orbiting outside Neptune, the ring of frozen bodies the catalogues now call trans-Neptunian, though in the field we call it, as the old field books do, the far ring.

Fifteenth season · Flagstaff, Arizona · 1915 · dome roof, 03:00

Lowell’s Quest, and the Carbon Copy

Percival Lowell, from the flagpole hill above Flagstaff, proposed a planet — he called it, after the old way of naming, Planet X — to account for the apparent discrepancies in the orbits of Uranus and Neptune, which he read as the gravity of a large unseen body perturbing the giants out of their arithmetic. He kept plates, and he kept a logbook, and the logbook kept a line which the station still reproduces in full, meaning never restored, in the way the register of the pomegranate name keeps its own unrecoverable minute — the two files share the identical patience. The line, from the 1915 carbon, is preserved here exactly as the station prints it:

In ordinary circumstances, he have one, two, or even importunity may become void, being contrary to the entire, or a crow preserve, for some hours.

The station’s standing reading, offered as a reading and not a record, is this: in ordinary circumstances the observer had one, two, or even three hours before importunity — the persistent cloud — might void the plate, being contrary to the entire season’s schedule; and the crow is the crows of the Flagstaff station roof, a flock the winter logbooks mention three times, which had a habit of keeping the dome hatch shuttered, and the night’s work, for some hours. The naturalist allows it: the far ring is watched in short weather, and a crow on the dome is a plausible cause of a voided plate. Lowell’s X, if it exists, is owed its hours.

One night · Jodrell, the blink comparator · 18 February 1930

A Bird by Subtraction

Clyde Tombaugh, twenty-four, at the 13-inch telescope, did the work the way a naturalist counts a rare species: not by finding a moving light, but by finding the star that is not there. The blink comparator flips two plates a fraction of a second apart; the fixed stars cancel into silence, and whatever drifts — a body in orbit, a bird against the treeline — remains. On the night of 18 February 1930, in a plate taken the evening before, one star failed to cancel. The body that followed it was announced in March 1930 and, at the time, confirmed Lowell’s hypothesis with the suddenness of a first spring. It was called Pluto, from a ten-year-old’s suggestion in Oxted, England, and the whole ring of these notes was, for a generation, named after a single specimen.

The two plates are identical except that one of them has a body in it. Flip them fast enough and the field reads in silence; the specimen is the only thing left moving. (Reads fine without it; the plates stay put.)

Nineteenth season of the specimen · 1978 · the mass, corrected

Charon, and the Arithmetic of Seeds

For a body to move another body’s orbit, it must have mass; that is the whole of the naturalist’s case for the X. When James Christy, at Flagstaff — the same hill, a generation down — found that Pluto carried a moon, Charon, in 1978, the pair could at last be weighed, and the weight changed the story. Pluto’s mass worked out at roughly a sixth of the Moon’s, with Charon holding close to an eighth of the planet itself: a companion, not an accessory, and two small bodies tugging on each other in the dark. The planet X, which should have been heavy enough to bend Neptune’s path, was not heavy enough to bend anything. The discrepancies, re-measured with better orbits, mostly dissolved. The far ring was freed from the duty of explaining the giants, and was left to be itself: a belt, a ring, a territory of frozen bodies — the region Gerard Kuiper had guessed at in 1951 and the catalogues eventually named for him.

It is the seed arithmetic again, the same arithmetic the pomegranate keeps: a number of small bodies doing the work of a whole. The field notes on Persephone in popular culture count her six seeds against six months and call it the smallest borrowed detail in the story; the ring counts its thousands of small frozen bodies against the giant planets and calls it the smallest explanation of the outer system. Both counts are the same count — descent, return, a price paid in seeds — read from opposite ends, and the two pages make a pair the way the exchange shelf and the orchards do. Even the old naming agrees: the asteroid the culture page carries out of the theatre, (458) Proserpina, discovered in 1900, is a ring-adjacent body in the same outer territories these notes keep, and it too is a name kept in a file rather than a thing moved.

Night leg · the ring itself · 44 to 48 units from the Sun

Notes from the Cold Shelf

Past Neptune’s 30 units the light thins to a field lamp, and the fauna of the ring keeps its habits accordingly. Makemake, Haumea — the one the surveyors describe as an ellipse, faster than it has any right to spin — and the long list of numbered small bodies, most of them measured in hundreds of kilometres, not thousands; Quaoar, at 44 units, which the station log calls the “middle of nowhere, accurately”; and Eris, found in 2005 at a distance where the Sun is no brighter than a star, which the catalogues spent a decade deciding was and was not a planet, the way the register decided, three times, that the pomegranate name was and was not owned. In 2006 the ring’s specimen of 1930 was reclassified a dwarf planet, and the far ring, relieved of a flagship, began its own cataloguing in earnest. The seasons here are not six months but centuries: Pluto’s year runs 248 of ours, so a single field season at the far ring is a commitment measured in grandchildren. The naturalist keeps one rule for this shelf, the same rule the orchard station of Euboea keeps for the goddess’ seed — read it slowly, it is the outer system speaking — and one archive day: the plates come down from the vault on Thursdays, and the far ring is counted again from the surface up.

Last entry · the overlook, after the route

Where the Book Closes

Every territory of this miscellany ends on a bench, and the ring is no exception. The last stop of The Steakley Walk is the overlook bench, mile 2.1, where the date is read aloud and the route turns around; on clear nights the same bench looks up through the quarter’s dark toward where this volume of field notes begins, and the walk is the ring’s own near-side station. The far ring keeps no marquee. It keeps the plates, the carbon, the crow, and the line that was never recovered — held exactly, the way the register holds everything, and the way the front page of the miscellany holds the rest of the field notes. The season there, like every season in these books, turns whether the boats like it or not.
