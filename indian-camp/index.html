Indian Camp

Six sides of a night at the head of the lake · shellac, 12 inch · cut the summer after 1923

Notes for the sleeve by the Recording Committee, written in the boathouse at Indian Village

The record in your hands

This is the first record the Committee has made of a night, rather than of a place, and we have taken the night the way it came to the doctor: from the outside, with the buggy, before the door was opened. It is the night the doctor of the lake town was called to the Ojibwe camp at the far end of the water — the camp the maps call Indian Village and the men in the camp had a name for that we do not print — to deliver a baby that would not come. His son Nick went with him, and Nick was a child, and this whole side of the record is told from where Nick sat in the boat and on the bench and in the doorway, which is to say from the height of a boy of maybe nine or ten. We keep that height on every side. The record does not rise above the doorway.

The camp sat on a spit of land at the head of the lake in the north of Michigan, in the August heat that sits on that water like a lid. The Committee has not invented the geography: it is the geography the story keeps, the boat crossing the bay, the camp road, the hospital hut with its doorway full of flies. What the Committee has made is the sound — the buggy wheels on the gravel, the bucket passed hand to hand, the lamp hissing, the boy's voice asking the one question the record ends on.

On the print in the sleeve

The sleeve carries a photograph, taken by the doctor that same summer, of the stairs that came down from the hospital hut to the camp road. The original plate was left in the sun on the porch while the bucket line went on, and the print came out the way it came out: the treads running into white at the bottom of the frame, the rail a bright slash, nothing readable past the third step. The Committee considered a second print and declined it. I mention it because the exposure in this collection — the stairs lock. Where the photograph should show the foot of the stair there is only blank paper, and the record is cut the same way: the sound reaches a place in the night it cannot name, and it stops there, and the next side begins at the water.

Pressing and shelf

T.T. No. 4 was cut in one session at the boathouse and pressed to an initial run of two hundred copies; the wax is a hard lake blue to match the water at the head of the lake. It sits on the Committee's shelf beside the liner notes for Gymnelia gemmifera, Ocelote Records OCE-014, which also keeps a child's height and also ends on a lamp, and opposite the long dark pressing at The Thousand-Year Record, Reichsplatte RP-12, so that anyone who pulls a sleeve off that shelf can check their own ears against the other one. The Committee keeps the camp's position the way the coast keepers keep theirs — the camp road fixed to the minute against the chart in Chapter 4 of Field Manual FM-9, re-checked every season, on the standing ground that a careless bearing costs you the width of the channel.

Standing order of the sleeve

Take the sides in order. Do not skip the crossing. When the question comes on side B, do not hurry the answer. You go on.
