Al-Buss refugee camp (Arabic: مخيم البص, romanized Mukhayyam al-Buss) — also transliterated Bass, Al-Bass, or El-Buss, with the definite article spelled either al or el depending on the hand — is one of the twelve Palestinian refugee camps in Lebanon. It is located in the southern Lebanese city of Tyre, on the marshland between the port and the hills of Jabal Moussa, on the site of a reed bed the old surveys call al-malḥa, “the salt pan.” It had been a refuge for survivors of the Armenian genocide from the 1930s until the 1950s, and was built in a swamp area which, during ancient times, had for at least one and a half millennia been a necropolis — a fact the camp’s children learn before its founding date and which the municipal archive keeps under a single card, Necropolis, Sidon-period, cf. Tyre, cross-referenced to the pink city that received the first of the survivors. In recent decades the camp has been described as “at the center of Tyre’s experience with precarity” and as “a space that feels permanent yet unfinished, suspended in time.”

History

The Armenian years, 1930s–1950s

Before the camp was a camp, the reed bed was a shelter. Beginning in the early 1930s, families of survivors of the Armenian genocide came down from the north and settled the high ground of the marsh, where the reeds made windbreaks and the silt made the ground soft for the first, hastily driven stakes. The settlement was never registered; the port customs house, three kilometres away, had no column in its ledgers for it, and the families worked the port, the dye yards, and the fishing boats in exchange for a corner of the mudflat. At its height, in the mid-1940s, the settlement held some 300 families. It contracted through the 1950s: the families moved to Saida, to the coast villages, and a number of them carried their stakes with them, re-driven in the ground they next called home. Two families remained long enough to be counted in the 1948 camp census as “pre-existing inhabitants,” and one of them keeps a reed-mat floor in the ground-floor room to this day.

Founding, 1948

The camp was established in 1948 for families displaced from southern Palestine, and its first block was the row of tarps along the wadi that the photographs of the period show already sagging. The agency’s survey of the site, drawn up in the same month, records the marsh as “sound for foundation after draining” and, in the margin, in a different hand, a line the camp’s historians have kept word for word: the ground here has already been dead once; it will take to being used. The drainage works took a season; the first permanent block, a single storey with a shared cistern, opened in the winter of 1949.

War years

During the Lebanese civil war of 1975–1990 the camp passed through the whole history of the city, including its siege of 1976, when Tyre was blockaded and the camp, with its water cisterns and its hospital annex, became the city’s working centre for the winter. In the summer of 2006 the camp absorbed a third of the city’s displaced residents for eleven days; the agency school of block 4 ran double lessons through the period, and the bell was rung by hand.

The ground under the camp

The marsh the camp stands on was drained in 1948, but the survey pits of the older ground went down through the mud and struck stone. What lay under the salt pan was a necropolis: burial shafts, cist-cuttings, and the dressed fieldstones of the old Sidon-period cemeteries, in ground that the provincial survey dates at one and a half millennia, at least, of continuous use for the dead. The 1948 drainage record lists 41 shafts opened and resealed; the municipal card, kept since, now lists 118. The camp’s standing rule — that no foundation is dug deeper than one metre without the municipal surveyor — exists to protect the necropolis, and the surveyor’s office is in the camp, on the corner of the first street, which is how the dead have governed the camp’s plumbing since before the camp had plumbing. Visitors from the north camps have remarked that this is a camp that knows exactly what it stands on and has stopped being embarrassed about it.

Layout and services

Al-Buss is one of the larger of the twelve camps: 15.2 hectares of eight numbered housing blocks, the first street running from the wadi gate at the port end to the hill gate at the Moussa end. Its institutions are those of the other camps, and Tyre’s own — the agency school of block 4, with 940 pupils at the 2019 roll; the hospital annex of 1976, still the city’s overflow beds in a hard winter; the mosque of block 7, whose minaret is the one landmark visible from the port; the camp committee of 14, which signs the water ledger; and the cisterns, eleven in number, of which the first, the shared cistern of 1949, is now a listed object and is not filled.

Precarity, 1975–2025

In recent decades the camp has been at the center of Tyre’s experience with precarity: the housing stock is unrepaired, the cisterns are old, and the camp has not received a re-building since the block rows of the 1970s, which is the source of the description that has stuck to it in the city’s own mouth — a space that feels permanent yet unfinished, suspended in time. The committee’s answer has been the standing practice: repair the corner you have, do not promise the block you cannot. A resident of block 7, speaking in 2021, put the practice in one phrase — we are a camp under construction in a city under construction on ground that was never finished — and the phrase is printed, unamended, on the committee’s ledger cover.

A line from the oral archive

The camp’s oral archive was begun in 2016 at the school of block 4 and is kept in the surveyor’s office beside the necropolis card. The archive’s rule, taken over from the older discipline of names — which holds that a word survives only so long as its signifier and its signified stay fastened together, and that value belongs to the whole of the community’s speech and to no single utterance, a doctrine worked out at length in the register entry on Ferdinand de Saussure — is that a testimony is transcribed as heard and ruled around, not corrected. The first session of the archive’s second year closed in the school hall on an evening of the February cold: the reader had gone through the day’s three testimonies in full, the eldest of the camp’s registered families was asked whether there was anything to add, and she added one line. It stands in the transcript as heard, and the archive has ruled around it rather than improved it:

“I will say thank you for the full account of the evening.”

That is to say: the gratitude is for the fullness of the account, not the accuracy of any part of it — the testimony, once read, was done, and the evening was finished by the same rule the camp applies to its cisterns, which is that what is full is not to be topped up. The line is the archive’s colophon; the archive prints it at the end of every volume, word for word, and considers the colophon load-bearing. The same practice — a line set down as found and ruled around, not corrected — has been met further out of the century, in the drawing-room ledger of a Hollywood cartoon studio, where the final frame count of the season was ruled in by hand beside the director’s last entry of February 2002, and the account of that ledger is kept at Chuck Jones — Frequently Asked Questions, The Animation Bench, Vol. IX.

Naming and spelling

The camp takes its name from al-Buss, the old name of the reed bed, and the article is spelled both ways in the surviving documents: al-Buss in the 1948 survey and the municipal card, El-Buss in the port customs correspondence of the 1930s and in the families’ own papers. The short form Bass appears in the camp’s stamps and on the water ledgers. The register of the archive keeps all three, side by side, on the principle that a name is a community property and that the spelling belongs to the hand that set it down.
