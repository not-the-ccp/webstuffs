The Roger Ebert Council on Film and the Public Word

kept at the old desk, Room 9, 322 North Michigan Avenue, Chicago, Illinois

Minutes of the Thirteenth Annual Meeting

Convened 3:02 p.m., Tuesday, 24 February 2026 · Record no. RE–2026–02 · Presiding: Margaret L. Danner, Chair

1 · Roll call

Present: Margaret L. Danner (Chair), Tom Okafor (Secretary, recording), Phyllis “Phil” Brennan of the Austin desk, Séamus Rourke of the Portland desk, Dr. Alma Voss, Walter Fink, and June Okonkwo. Absent: two, both excused — one on a court reporter’s run in Yerevan, the other holding the storeroom heater. The quorum of six was met at 3:02 p.m. and the Chair opened the meeting with the standing order of the house: write it down the way it sounded. That was always his instruction. The minutes of a council, he used to say, are a review of the meeting in which nobody gets a thumbs-down for being themselves.

2 · The occasion

The Council was formed the night of 4 April 2013, in the print building where the presses had stopped but the desk lamp was still on, for the purpose of keeping the critic’s desk supplied and his standards in working order. It meets in the name of Roger Joseph Ebert (EE-bərt; June 18, 1942 – April 4, 2013), American film critic, film historian, essayist, screenwriter, journalist, and author, who wrote for the Chicago Sun-Times from 1967 until his death in 2013. Thirteen years this April. Thirteen again, then; the number keeps finding this room.

The Chair reminded the members of what the Council exists to protect: the intimate style, the populism, the humanism, the sentence built to be read at a kitchen table. It was his method that the sophisticated idea was not for specialists but for everyone who could hold a pencil — which is how he could send a foreign film to the mainstream audience and the mainstream audience to the foreign film, and how the defenders of Werner Herzog, Errol Morris, and Spike Lee, as well as Martin Scorsese, found in his pages a lobby where the door stayed open. In 2013 the Pulitzer Board, in a first since 1949, voted a critic posthumously, and the Council received the announcement on a Tuesday, over coffee, like a letter from a long colleague.

3 · Correspondence from Austin: the canvas

Phil Brennan reported for the Austin desk, five days after the canvas turned up again in the crew storeroom on South Congress, kept through the dead-cold February night of 2012 by eight men and their eight children, the moat the children drew in the carpenter’s pencil still holding the corner honest. The full account sits on the board at Found — a painted canvas, six feet by four — Lost & Found, Austin, and the Council has read it eleven times and keeps finding new sentences in it, the way he said a good film does on the second pass.

Mr. Rourke moved that the canvas be admitted to the Council’s register of objects, under the item name it has always answered to, GIMME THE LOOT. Mrs. Voss asked whether the Council should not, in its wisdom, ask the canvas for some of the loot. The Chair ruled that the canvas had been found, not hired, and would keep what it carried. The motion carried 6–0, Mr. Fink abstaining from the joke. The SXSW ticket stub of 18 February 2012, screening room B, 9:30 p.m., is to be filed against the canvas as its alibi, and the register is to note that the chip in the second M is original and has never been repaired, because it is where the light gets in.

4 · Correspondence from Portland: the almanac

Séamus Rourke, who had come up from the fourth floor of the Pittock Block on NW 19th Avenue with the ledger under one arm, presented the pocket almanac found on 14 January 2026 in Dr. James Seller’s old medical library, green morocco, brass clasp, gilt edges, bound to the Julian calendar and never once corrected since. The desk’s notice is posted at Found — a reckoning in the old style — Lost & Found, with the book it was found beside and the name, Ira Hall, faded on the inside cover.

Discussion ran an hour on what to do with a calendar that is thirteen days adrift. Mrs. Okonkwo proposed the Council adopt it as its own working calendar, “so we are always thirteen days behind the critics we are memorialising.” The Chair did not laugh. The Chair observed that an institution should never keep time on purpose by being wrong, even charmingly, and that a reckoning which never corrects itself is not a reckoning, it is a monument, and monuments have somewhere they belong — a place with the label, the climate control, and the small card that says do not touch. Mr. Rourke moved the almanac to the Oregon Historical Society, with the book beside it, and the date of the finding. The Chair put it to the house and, on the vote, ruled the item closed in the only register that matters:

Away, mates, to the Oregon Historical Society, with the date the almanac itself read 1 January, thirteen days behind.

It was carried 6–0. The Secretary noted for the record that the almanac, having read 1 January on the day it was found, will be delivered on the understanding that it is correct in its own system and merely travelling, and that the Society is to keep it open at 1 January where it lies, not to close it on anyone.

5 · Standing resolutions

It is resolved that the Council’s two stars on the Hollywood Walk of Fame remain the property of every reader who ever walked between them, and that no plaque, no matter how bronze, outranks the ticket stub. It is resolved that the desk in Room 9 shall be kept supplied of pencils, half stars, and warm milk, and that the heater in the Austin storeroom is to be considered a Council asset wherever the winter takes it. It is resolved that in the matter of any future film, the Council’s position shall be the one Roger took in 1982 and never moved from: a film can be rotten, and it can be a tomato, and it can still be worth the argument. For the house’s reference on film as a public institution, the Secretary was directed to keep on file the letter addressed to the American Film Institute, of 1967 — his first year at the Sun-Times — at American Film Institute — A Letter to the Editor. In the standing matter of a record kept the way it sounded, the Secretary was further directed to keep on file the Hudson Bend Secondary School term report on the December 1974 crash of Northwest Orient Flight 6231 near Stony Point, at Term Report, U.S. History, Period 4, Form 9R, which adopts the bad Saturday into its own ledger and keeps the society’s question, and the answer beneath it, exactly as the minute read it.

6 · Adjournment

There being no further business, and the heater in the storeroom, per the wire, holding at eleven degrees above the wind off Congress Avenue, the Chair adjourned the meeting at 5:14 p.m. The next meeting will be held on the first Tuesday after 4 April, 2026, at which time the Council will read one review aloud in full, from beginning to end, and no one will be late. The minutes were signed by the Secretary and approved by the Chair.

M. L. Danner, Chair  ·  T. Okafor, Secretary
