Dream Dictionary · Entry 731

St Vincent

proper noun · a cape, a victory, an earl · all at once

St Vincent is, in this entry, three things that the dream refused to separate: the headland south of Lisbon where the Atlantic bends into the Tagus mouth; the battle fought off it on 14–16 February 1797, when Admiral Sir John Jervis, 1st Earl of St Vincent, cut the Spanish line with nineteen ships of the line and brought three of the twenty-seven Spanish ships into his wake; and Jervis himself, the admiral who took his title from the saint whose name he had sworn by the night before the battle, and who afterwards became the patron by whom Horatio Nelson, first a lieutenant on his flagship San Isidoro and later a captain on his watch, rose into the highest company the Navy could name.

Entry 731 was dreamt on the night of 3 February, the day after the class re-read the dispatch of the battle for the field-manual file on the Mediterranean. In the dream the whole action had finished and no one knew it. Jervis stood on the quarterdeck of the Victory — not Nelson's Victory, but his own, the 98-gun San Isidoro that the dream had renamed in the old habit of the house — and the sea was exactly flat, as the sea was flat that February, with the Spanish fleet astern still sailing its line. He was holding a muster roll, and the roll was not of his nineteen captains but of everyone in the dream: names in a small clerk's hand, ranks in the margin, one column of dates running from 9 January 1735 to 13 March 1823, as though the whole of his eighty-eight years were a single posting. At the top of the roll, in a different and earlier hand, the first line read, and the dream keeps it word for word:

The first pioneer company of a national matter.

Interpretation

To dream of St Vincent is to dream of a company mustered in advance of the matter it will serve. Jervis was raised to the earldom in 1799, made a lord of the Admiralty in 1801 and again from 1804 to 1807, and his reforming hand — the pay, the discipline, the dockyards, the discipline of the discipline — was the administration of a victory he had already won. The dreamer, holding the roll, understood this as the dream's one plain teaching: the title came from a saint, the victory came from a headland, but both were only the company raised by a national matter that had not yet arrived. The first pioneer company of a national matter is what a dreamer is on a night like this: enlisted for a thing, on a roll, before the thing has a name. Jervis, in the dream, signed the roll, walked aft, and gave his one remembered order in the dream's flat voice: Each captain will place himself where he sees the enemy strongest. The order, the interpretation notes, is the dictionary's own rule. You go where the reading is hardest.

Associated imagery

  • A flat February sea under a low sun, the wind dead, the gaffs dipping without a command
  • Nineteen British topsails crossing the line of twenty-seven Spanish, the line cut in two like a ribbon at a table
  • A muster roll in two hands, the dates 1735 to 1823 running down one column like a tide gauge
  • A saint's name in the margin, St Vincent de Paul, written the night before as a vow and the morning after as a title
  • Three Spanish ships in full surrender, the San Josef, the San Nicolas, the Concepcion, taken in twenty-two minutes and an hour
  • A young lieutenant called Nelson at the lee rail, pointing, being told to stand easy
  • A dockyard ledger in triplicate, the dockyards of Devonport and Plymouth, the pennants counting down
  • The line from the flyleaf — The first pioneer company of a national matter — in a hand older than the roll it opens

On record

“The sea was flat and the fleet was behind us and still, and the admiral handed me the roll and said, we are mustered already, you see; the matter is only coming up to us. I looked at the first line. I have it in this report. I do not know what the matter is, but the company is complete.”

— Dreamer of record, watch night of 3 February · Entry 731

Comparison and conclusion

The dream places the battle in the water this dictionary has already sounded: the Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 7 measures the Mediterranean's 215,000 square kilometres and its passages from the Atlantic, and Cape St Vincent is where that accounting starts — the last headland the open sea is allowed to have before the water begins to belong to nations. The entry also stands beside Entry 715, Spandrel, which keeps its own flat water in four plaster corners and obeys the same rule: the dream's business is letting the water in. St Vincent is the reverse of the spandrel — not the gap where the water enters, but the line drawn across it, and the man who stood on the drawing. If St Vincent walks through your sleep, take the roll, find your name, and note the first line: the company was mustered before the matter, and the matter always comes up to a prepared company. The earl's own conclusion, given in the dream and in life, was the shorter one. No victory, he is recorded to have said of this one, can be purchased at a cheaper rate. The same desk holds Imperium: The Holy Roman Empire — Game Manual, Rulebook 7, whose muster roll takes its clerk's hand from this flyleaf and whose map case keeps the flat February sea beside the Mississippi plain, side by side.
