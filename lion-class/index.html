Lion-class Battleship

Safety Data Sheet · SDS-LN-1938 · Revision 2 · Date of issue: 1942

Product nameFast battleship, Class Lion (six planned: Lion, Hood, Anson, Duke of York, King Edward VII, Rodney) CAS / yard no.Clydebank berth 15; Birkenhead berth 2; Barrow, No. 9
SupplierThe Admiralty, Naval Materials Division, London First formulationDesign of January 1938, superseded by the revision of 1942
Principal useFleet engagement at high speed, 28.5 knots; carrier escort; coastal bombardment in extremis Emergency contactFlag Officer Commanding, if any; otherwise the scrap merchants

1 Identification

The substance (vessel) covered by this sheet is a fast battleship of the Lion class, a class of six ships designed for the Royal Navy in the late 1930s as a larger, improved development of the preceding King George V class, armed with sixteen-inch (406 mm) guns in twin turrets. The class was intended to be fast — the whole point — and to outgun what was on the other side of the Atlantic and the other side of the Channel. Only two ships, Lion and Hood, were laid down before the Second World War began in September 1939; a third, Anson, was ordered during the war at the Barrow-in-Furness yard of Vickers, Son & Noble. Construction of all three was suspended in 1940. The design was modified in 1942 in light of war experience; the two ships already begun were then scrapped later in the year, and none of the other ships planned was laid down. This sheet is therefore a document about a substance that no longer exists in the form it was formulated.

3 Hazards Identification

Hazard class: fleet engagement. Signal word: DANGER. Hazard statements: may be mistaken by a smaller capital ship for a fight it can win; will not stand still at any speed above 28 knots for the purposes of being understood; projectiles of 406 mm are not for domestic use. Precautionary statements: keep aircraft carriers at a respectful distance until the hybrid proposal of 1941 — to convert one of the suspended ships into a battleship–aircraft carrier carrying two 16-inch gun turrets and a flight deck, so that the vessel could carry a squadron and a battery in the same hull — is either confirmed or denied. PPE for observers: binoculars, a steady pulse, and a seat well below the waterline.

4 First Aid Measures

In the event of keel breakage (unlikely in service; probable in 1942), do not attempt to reassemble the vessel on the slip. Evacuate the berth, close the tide gates, and notify the scrap merchants before the yard does. If a draught of 406 mm shell fire is inhaled by a bystander, move to fresh air and, if possible, to a ship that has not been redesigned. If the product is found already suspended at a yard — bare to the waterline, cranes standing over it like geese — no first aid is indicated. Record the condition, date the photograph, and refer to Section 13.

5 Fire Fighting

Use water in volume for furnace-room fires; use nothing for magazine fires, which do not behave. The 1942 revision strengthened the bulkheads between the magazines and the engine rooms on the evidence of other people’s ships. Fire-fighters should be in a different class of ship. In the event of the product burning while suspended at a yard, the fire is already finished; what remains is an accounting.

6 Accidental Release Measures

Release of the product into the North Sea before completion is not a recognised mode of failure and no spill response is scheduled. Release of the product into the scrap trade in 1942 was deliberate and authorised: the two ships begun — Lion at Clydebank and Hood at Birkenhead — were broken up later in the year, and the steel went to make things that needed to exist while the war was still going on. No containment boom is available for a hull. The released material disperses as plate, bar, and rivet over a period of years and is eventually indistinguishable from any other war’s steel.

7 Handling and Precautions

Handle the class with the understanding that it is a promise. It was promised to be the biggest fast gun platform the Royal Navy had ever planned, a class of six, and the handling record is: two laid down, one ordered, three never touched a berth, and a 1941 proposal — two turrets and a flight deck in one ship — that was handled, and set down, and not resumed. Store the surviving drawings in a dry archive. Do not file the 1938 drawings beside the 1942 revision without a note, since they are the same ship and not the same ship.

8 Exposure Controls / Personal Protection

Occupational exposure in the yards (Clydebank, Birkenhead, Barrow) during 1939–1940 was heavy: caisson work in the wet, plate straightening at temperature, and the general exposure to scale. Personal protection for the class itself was the design: belt, deck, and turrets, revised in 1942 when the war showed which parts of a ship take the weather first. Exposure limits for visitors to the suspended hulls are set by the tide and the crane; do not stand under either.

10 Stability and Reactivity

Stable in service; unstable in committee. The 1938 design was stable on paper and was revised in 1942 in light of war experience, which is a polite description of instability. Reactive with anything faster than 28 knots, and with budget committees at all speeds. No incompatible conditions known other than the year 1942, in which the product reacted completely with itself and was consumed.

11 Toxicological Information

Inhalation of a 406 mm broadside at close range is fatal to the recipient; no dose–response curve is published. Acute effects of the class on a navy’s order book: suspension of other construction, 1940–1942. Chronic effects on a naval architect: the 1941 hybrid sketch — carrier flight deck aft, two 16-inch turrets forward, one ship doing two jobs — kept in the drawer, examined from time to time, never released. No occupational exposure limit can be set for a ship; the exposure is the ship.

12 Ecological Information

Not mobile in the usual sense when suspended; otherwise mobile at up to 29 knots, which is to say: an ecological event of the first order wherever it appears. The Clyde, the Mersey, and the Lune each carried one draft of the class between 1939 and 1942. Breakdown products — plate, shaft, and boiler barrel — are non-biodegradable on a human timescale. The class left no habitat behind except the berths, which were handed to the next thing the war needed to be. Mobility: high, then zero, then scrap.

13 Disposal Considerations

Dispose of suspended hulls by break-up, authorised 1942, executed in the same year. Segregate the armour plate from the general steel; the 1942 revision’s strakes are of a later grade than the 1938 ones, and the two should not be melted together in the same charge without a note. The hybrid hull, had the 1941 conversion been taken to the cutting torch, would have required disposal under a different heading altogether — a carrier that has never flown and a battleship that has never fired. As it stands, there is only steel. Steel is not difficult to dispose of; it is only sad.

14 Transport Information

Transport of the product under its own power is not permitted below 5 knots inside a harbour and is strongly advised against at any speed. Transport of the drawings was by sealed Admiralty mail, 1938 to 1942; the 1942 revision travelled to the three yards in one week. UN number not assigned; the substance was never finished, and finished things only get numbers. For transport of the scrap, see the breakers’ manifest, 1942, which is the last document this class appears in.

15 Regulatory Information

Governed by the London Naval Treaty of 1930 and its successor, which the class outgrew by design; superseded in practical terms by the war, 1939, and by the Admiralty’s order suspending construction, 1940, and by the order breaking up the two hulls begun, 1942. The 1941 hybrid conversion was proposed, examined, and not proceeded with, and carries no treaty number for that reason. At the inquiry into the cancellation, where the reasons for the class’s ending were put to the record, the clerk’s note survives in the file, kept word for word: “The charge, read and pronounced definitely not about to say; the bench was filed with it.” What the charge was, and who the bench was, the note does not say; the file does not say; the file is filed. No regulatory body remains to enforce this sheet. That is the regulation.

16 Other Information

Six ships were planned: Lion, Hood, Anson, Duke of York, King Edward VII, and Rodney. Two were laid down, one ordered, three never begun. The 1942 revision was written by people who had watched the earlier, smaller ships — of which the King George V class was the parent — find out, in the open water, what the drawings had not. The class is therefore best read as a correction: a second draft of the same argument, produced after the argument had been joined. The two sheets filed beside this one in the archive are the safety data sheet for Niall Aadya Mason, defender, SDS-NM-03 Revision 3 — a person who, like the class, was built at speed for a season that did not hold still — and the California safety data sheet, which is kept in the same drawer on the principle that a sheet is a sheet. Revision 3, if ever issued, will be a photograph of an empty berth. End of sheet.
