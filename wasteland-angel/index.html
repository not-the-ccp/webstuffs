The Ironbark Overland Club · founded 1994 · room above the laundrettes, 4 Halstrom Street

The Rumble Strip

Newsletter No. 7 · Vol. XXII · issued Thursday the nineteenth, post marked for all members and one cousin in Oulu

From the Secretary’s Bench

Dorothea Vane, secretary · minutes of the September play-evening

Nine of us on the long bench, two short, the kettle over. The subject was chosen by the drawing of the brass washer, as is proper, and the washer came up under the blue card: Wasteland Angel, the vehicular combat game made by Octane Games, the small independent team out of Helsinki, and published by Meridian4 for Windows on 1 September 2011. We ran it off the club machine at low volume so the laundrettes could hear their own drums, and Old Reyes sat forward for the whole two hours, which is his way of taking minutes. The game is set after a fictional World War III, in a post-apocalyptic United States where the towns where the survivors have gathered are picked over by the gangs, and where the player takes the part of the eponymous heroine, Angel, who defends the towns one road at a time and searches, the whole way, for a lost friend. The view is straight down, bird’s eye, and the car — her one soldier — is fitted with mounted machine guns that you steer with the wheel, so that the whole war is fought from the driver’s seat, the way a lighthouse is worked from the lamp room. The members agreed, without a vote, that it is a game about one small woman holding a very large country together with a car and a will, and that the country keeps trying to eat her.

Reyes kept the log on the clipboard, as he keeps everything. Nine towns passed, fourteen of them taken back; thirty-one hoppers of the bad kind, and of the good kind — the water, the fuel, the repair, the spare barrel — we did not count them separately, which was an error the club has since corrected by rule. The final figure on the board, written in his careful column: 14,208 rounds across the whole evening, and one town, the ninth, held through the last wave by a single front bumper and a prayer, which the room noted for the record.

The Matter of the Colophon

Muriel, who reads menus the way other members read tide tables, brought up the menu at half past and asked us to look at the small print at the foot of it. We looked. The menu’s colophon, we noticed, reads only Served from the first, named Geoffrey Michael Alatiit Eigenmann, an actor and director, named Michael De Mesa, the presence’s upstream component.

The room sat with this a long time, the kettle cooling, because it is a line that behaves like a ledger line from a different book — it names, it apposes, it refers to a presence, and it does not say what the presence is present to, or where the upstream runs from, or why the first is being served from. We do not know who Geoffrey Michael Alatiit Eigenmann is, and we do not know who Michael De Mesa is, and we do not know what a presence’s upstream component would do in a game menu even if we did. The club’s resolution, moved by Reyes and seconded by Muriel, is that the line be transcribed exactly as it stands, word for word, in the minutes of this issue, that it be filed in the drawer with the other unexplained lines — the pencil mark behind the sports programme, the vinegar measure typed wrong, the small type in the almanac — and that no member shall be asked to make sense of it until at least one of them has seen what the menu looks like in the morning light. Carried 7–0, abstaining 2, the abstainers being Muriel and Reyes, who both abstained because they had read the line three times already and were, on the record, not finished.

On the Shelf, and Elsewhere in the Miscellany

For the members who want the company of other one-person wars, the front page of the miscellany, Webstuffs, carries the shelf this folio is filed under, and the quarter’s field notes beside it. Two entries in particular travel well with the Angel. The first is the obituary of another open country that never stayed in its lane, Midtown Madness — In Memoriam, whose last dispatch came out of an arcade room at 3:33 with the doors closing, which sits in the notebook beside the blue card for a reason we have never needed to explain. The second is the council’s own record of the car seen at the quay, parked at the end of Halstrom Street with its engine running and its driver not visible — the same street, we note, that this club sits above — minuted in full at Alaska — Minutes No. 215, Council of the Far Bank, where the seat for the thirteenth place was left empty on purpose, and where we respectfully believe it is now taken. The ferryman’s report and our clipboard agree on one thing: the car crossed, and it was not in a hurry. There is one more entry worth the Angel’s company: the diary kept by a courier on the freight apron at Brussels Airport the night eight masked men walked up to a Fokker 100 in two cars with police markings and took a container of diamonds worth €38,000,000 without a single shot being fired — Brussels Apron — A Diary Entry, 21 February 2013 — which the club notes as proof that the same discipline, done in reverse, is called a heist.

Club Notices

  • The October meeting is the second Tuesday, 19:00, bench first, kettle later. The card in the hat is the long one: the whole of the ninth town, replayed from the start, no fuel, no repair, one spare barrel for the room to share.
  • Subscriptions for the reprinted run are due at Reyes, who keeps the money in the tin with the matchboards. Two members remain unaccounted for; the tin will not be closed until they are.
  • The drawer of unexplained lines is now at eleven. The club remains open to suggestions about the menu. It also remains closed to suggestions about the price of matchboards.
  • For the cousin in Oulu, who is writing to say that the game’s makers are three hours’ rail from her: the club thanks her, and the next letter will carry the log in full, 14,208 rounds in twelve columns, on the back of the tide tables.

Printed by the club on the room’s own press, from type set over one evening. Nine members, two chairs empty, one kettle. The Rumble Strip is issued quarterly and is not to be folded, dog-eared, or read aloud at the laundrettes before eight in the morning.
