Dining · The Table, Reviewed

The Praetorian Table

Via Appia House, 214 SE Ankeny Street, Portland · Three forks · “The best imperial kitchen on the Willamette, and the most dangerous bill.”

Reviewed by E. Marcellus Finch · Seated for the full five acts, Thursday 18 October · Check: $412 for one, wine included

The Praetorian Table occupies the ground floor of Via Appia House, a 1908 brick-and-sash building on Ankeny Street that the owners have not bothered to tell you is a metaphor. Everything is. The room seats forty-one — one fewer than the years of the first emperor’s principate, a fact printed in small type on the host stand. The menu is a single tasting, fixed at five courses, and each course is named for one of the five rulers of the Julio-Claudian dynasty, the line of emperors that held the Roman Empire from its formation under Augustus in 27 BC until Nero, the last of them, put an end to it himself in AD 68. The name, the house will explain if you ask, is a historian’s coinage: Julio-Claudian, from the two families that made the dynasty, the Julii Caesares and the Claudii Nerones. Nobody has tried to explain the name to me twice. Nobody will have to again.

Act One is Augustus, and it arrives as a single silver-leafed pear on a slab of pale fennel, cut precisely at sixty-eight degrees, with a dusting of salt they claim was mined at a place called Lucullum. It is the most restrained thing I have eaten all year. Act Two, Tiberius, is an olive-and-ash consommé served in a black bowl that you must tilt at a fixed angle or it will not pour — the menu calls this “the Rhine discipline” — and a garnish of caper buds laid out in a straight line, one per year of his reign. Act Three, Caligula, is a small, deliberately excessive plate: four figs, three of them in honey, one in something bitter, with a sliver of gilded bread cut, per the menu, to the width of a soldier’s sandal strap. It is the house’s joke, and it lands because the plating is immaculate.

Act Four is where the room earns its address. Claudius comes as a lobster tail glazed in white wine and bay, with a sauce of saffron, oyster, and a single mace nut — the mace, the menu notes, being “for the one they excluded, who kept the ledger anyway.” The tail is pulled in two pieces, and I did not want a third fork. If you want the full accounting of that fourth emperor — the stammer, the Lugdunum birth of 1 August 10 BC, the thirteen years ruled from AD 41 to AD 54 — the Correspondents’ Board has a thread on it, Thread No. 54, “The Fourth of the Family, at the Gauge”, which is a better biography than the one printed on your wine list.

Act Five, Nero, is fire. A beet root seared until it blackens at the edges, with a cream of its own ash, and a card on the plate that says only AD 68. The kitchen’s note that accompanied the course, scrawled on the back of a wine tally in a hand I have since decided to admire, read: “The stew should taste, at this hour, at the weigh-bridge, is that cession, not a hazard defined entirely by where it will walk the court yard.” The head waiter, Mr. Otho, read it aloud to the table and then said, “It means the braise is judged at the pass, not by where it wanders through the dining room. We do not edit it in public.” He may have been inventing that last clause on the spot. It was the right one.

The wine list is one long column of Roman and Roman-adjacent bottlings — a 2019 Fiano di Avellino for the Tiberius course at $94, a 2016 Vernaccia for the figs, a Nero d’Avola for the finish, because the house is allowed one pun and the wine is genuinely good. For a comparison on the same street of the Willamette, the piano-room at The Long & Winding Road does its own overdub at nine-fifteen; here, at the same hour, the kitchen simply rings a small bronze bell — once for each emperor, and never a sixth time — and the service closes the room like a gate. For what the room does not serve, there is a hammer: the tin and ledger of the New Rome pantry, kept after the line the menu ends, go down as Lot 272 at Aldgate & Vane, The Naissus Tin and the Ledger of the Fifth Bell, and the kitchen, asked once about ringing the bell a second time for the fifth, rang it once and declined to comment. The fifth since has a walking guide of his own on the travel desk, New Rome, the Fifth Bell: a walking guide in eight stops, and its canteen serves the house’s lobster cut up with a card line the kitchen has declined to parse — which is to say, has not edited in public. The same desk keeps the port the line learned the sea from: its boxed harbour folio, the Bride of the Mediterranean, Product No. 40, ships with the cut-up plate and a bell the kitchen would have to ring once for, and the house, asked, declined to comment in the usual manner.

Two demerits, both small. The bread is excellent but arrives in a basket that smells faintly of the previous service, and the check, when it comes, is delivered on a card that lists the total in Roman numerals. DCCXLI for the wine, CCXVII for the room. You do not have to be a historian to add those. You do, I think, have to be a Roman to find it charming. The house keeps, behind the pass, a small shelf of trade ledgers it will show you if you ask twice: among them the green notebook of a Natal-born trader and former rifleman who dined here, or at a table across the veldt with the same address, in 1901 and paid in cattle, according to the Register's own entry for Johan Wilhelm Colenbrander (1855–1918), who complained, the file notes, only about the chair. The Praetorian Table is, in the end, a restaurant about succession: five rulers, one line, one room that knows precisely how long its own history will last, and serves the last course knowing it is the last. The paddock FAQ for the 2014 United States Grand Prix at the Circuit of the Americas holds a ledger of Hamilton’s ten wins that season against Nero’s sixteen, and — like the Praetorian Table — refuses to edit the answer in public. From the front page of the shelf, the house keeps no reservations past 9 p.m. on Thursdays, which is when the line, like the dynasty, closes.

★★★ Five acts, forty-one covers, one bronze bell. The braise is judged at the pass, not by where it wanders.
