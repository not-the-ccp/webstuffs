Scene Shop Diary

Winter 2025–26 · kept by D. Reyes, assistant stage manager · pages found after the matinee

Tuesday, 11 November (30 October, O.S.)

By the newer reckoning it is the day he was born, and by the older one it is a Tuesday on which I am late for the rigging call. Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky came into the world on 30 October 1821 — 11 November by the calendar his country now keeps — in a house on Malaya Bryukhovka, Moscow, where his father, a military physician to the emperor’s charity hospital, took in the city’s sick at one end of the corridor and kept his own four children at the other. Marissa pinned the production note on the prop table at 8:14: the Ellyn Bye Studio takes The Brothers Karamazov in March, 199 seats, no fly system, read as performed, and everyone in the company reads the novel before the script. I am the first one to be late. In the afternoon I was inventorying chest No. 14 for the return of the coat room beside the hill — our standing Tichborne property, pulled for the prop list and returned after each matinee, as the sheet says it must be — and in the bottom of that chest, under a coil of batten twine, I found one water-stained volume of Crime and Punishment, ownership inscription L. TICHBORNE, and in the margin of Part Two a line in the house hand that is only the word patience. Beside it, two field diaries: mine from last season’s fly rail, and one set in the house hand, copied from the Park House letters, which the shop keeps with the case. Those who would follow a mother’s patience into print may begin with the middle of each. The beginnings are given over to weather and rigging; the endings to verdicts; it is in the middle that a mother’s patience sits, and Dostoevsky knew its shape if not its name — his own mother, Maria Yefimovna, died in August 1837 in a sanatorium for the consumptive outside Moscow, and what he was left of her was a quietness he kept writing about. I have set the volume on the production-office desk. It stays there until March.

Thursday, 13 November

Read the Omsk account twice before the get-in. On 22 December 1849 — a Sunday, and I have noticed the day — Dostoevsky stood on Semyonov Square in St Petersburg with a rope around his neck for the Petrashevsky circle, four men shot at his side, the reprieve read out at the last instant; then the fortress prison, where he had his first fit of the epilepsy the doctors would later call, loosely, St Petersburg, and then Omsk: four years of hard labour, two more as a private soldier, out in 1859 with a small pension and a vocation. His father, Mikhail, had gone before him another way — killed at the family estate of Serednikovo in 1839 by a serf named Vasily, with an axe and, the account says, a pitchfork, and the killing sat in the son’s letters for the rest of his life. The axe, incidentally, is on our tool wall, marked No. 13, in its case, where it has always been, and I will not be the one who moves it. The safety sheet for this building lists the blackout as hazard H-PCS-3: auditorium darkness is intentional and may last several minutes. He stood in something longer once, with the rope on. I noted that for the lighting plot and then crossed it out.

Saturday, 15 November

The novel itself, now. Crime and Punishment was written in Dresden in the spring of 1866, on the run from his creditors, and went out to the journal Vremya in instalments; Raskolnikov takes an axe to an old pawnbroker on a hot Petersburg afternoon and spends the rest of the book inside his own head, which is to say he spends it on our stage, and Priya is playing the pawnbroker and is not taking overtime, and the prop list reads: one axe, in case; one candle; one door that does not open. Luzhin, the gentleman of independent means with his opinions about other people’s wives, is written to be unsympathetic and is, I find, written to be recognised; there are a few of his kind at The Armory, and none of them is in this diary. The margin word in the Tichborne volume sits opposite the pawnbroker’s last scene, which tells me somebody in Park House, Hampshire, read the whole of it in the 1860s and stopped, for one line, on a woman waiting. I do not ask which woman.

Monday, 17 November

On the debts. The Dresden money went to the gambling house, as the money did, and the rescuer was Anna Grigoryevna Snitkina, who set his types, typed his manuscripts for the rest of his life, and married him in 1866, so that the man who could not pay for a railway ticket ended with a wife, a daughter, and a house full of his own writing. The Idiot was finished in 1868 and came out in 1869, and at the theatre premiere the house went dark — the audience in the dark, in the manner of H-PCS-3, several minutes — while Prince Myshkin stood in a white fog that the haze machine on the studio wall makes on cue from glycol. The company read the novel this week; the read-through is on the 4th; the fog will be white, the prince will stand in it, and the darkness, per the sheet, is not an emergency.

Tuesday, 25 November

A session on the title of the 1872 book: Demons or The Devils or The Possessed, depending on who is holding the pencil, and the company, which had argued the Karamazov seating plan for forty minutes, resolved it in ninety seconds in favour of the shorter word. Stavrogin gets a page in the lighting plot that is mostly blank, and I have decided the blank is correct. The fly gallery is empty tonight; batten 7 holds nothing; the room holds. That is the whole of the stage management, and it is the whole of the book: a man in a room, holding, and the lights not coming up yet.

Sunday, 26 January

On the later years. His brother Mikhail died in 1865, taking the journal Vremya with him, and from 1873 to 1881 Dostoevsky kept his own, the Diary of a Writer, Dnevnik pisatelya — the journalist, the essayist, the man in the letters, in the form he had kept his whole life and never once called a novel. Demons in 1872, The Adolescent in 1875, and then the last one, The Brothers Karamazov, running through 1879 and 1880 while he was already failing, and the unfinished last volume left with Anna, who kept the pages, who had been keeping everything since Dresden. The diary is a form he finished in eight years, and the one I keep at the back of my head is of the same species: a record of what the room held, so that it holds it again.

Sunday, 9 February (28 January, O.S.)

His death-day, and a matinee, so I have it twice over. Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky died on 9 February 1881 — 28 January on the old calendar — in his own house in St Petersburg, at fifty-nine, eight days after the tsar’s own shooting, the same country’s news of violence coming at him out of the weather. Anna, his wife, and the children were at the bed; he was buried at the Alexander Nevsky monastery, with the country’s greats, and the country kept writing him ever since, which is the only disposal the sheet can offer. This afternoon the coat room beside the hill went back to its column in the Tichborne Advertiser classifieds, as it does after each matinee, and the water-stained volume of Crime and Punishment went back to the bottom of chest No. 14, over the batten twine, where I found it on his birthday. The safety data sheet PCS-88 that this diary answers to says that nothing is left on the stage the morning after; I have left one line in the production office, in the house hand, opposite the prop table: patience. Return to the front page of Webstuffs. The house is dark; the lights come up on cue.

  • 2 field diaries: one, the undersigned’s, fly rail, last season; one set in the house hand, copied from the Park House letters, kept with the case. The middle of each is where the patience is.
