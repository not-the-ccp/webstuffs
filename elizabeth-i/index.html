Elizabeth I

Museum Placard No. 1603 · Queen of England and Ireland, 17 November 1558 – 24 March 1603 · b. Greenwich Palace, 7 September 1533

Elizabeth I was the only surviving child of Henry VIII and his second wife, Anne Boleyn, and the last — and longest reigning — monarch of the House of Tudor. Her eventful reign, and its effect on history and culture, gave name to the Elizabethan era. When she was two years old, in 1536, her parents’ marriage was annulled, her mother was executed on 19 May in the Tower of London, and Elizabeth was declared illegitimate and set aside from the succession. In 1543, the year she turned ten, the Third Succession Act restored her, in her half-sister Mary’s wake, to the line. After Henry’s death in 1547 her younger half-brother Edward VI ruled until his own death in July 1553; Mary then reigned for five years, and on the night of 17 November 1558 Elizabeth was proclaimed queen in the Privy Chamber at Oat Street, at the age of twenty-five, unmarried, and without an heir.

She reigned for forty-four years and seven months, the longest of any Tudor. The crown was recognised abroad in April 1559, when France signed the Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis and formally acknowledged the Protestant Elizabeth as queen of England in her own right — a clause the treaties room keeps on its own bench, across the gallery. At home the reign ran from the Marian exile of the reformers, through the imprisonment and execution of Mary, Queen of Scots on 8 February 1587, to the defeat of the Spanish Armada in the summer of 1588 and the speech at Tilbury a week before it, “I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman, but I have the heart and stomach of a king.” She died at Richmond Palace on 24 March 1603, the last of the Tudors, and was buried on 23 April in the Henry VII Chapel, Westminster Abbey, the day before her cousin James VI of Scotland took the crown.

Objects on the bench

1603.1The Greenwich slip — an inventory leaf from the Queen’s house, 1536, itemising the chambers of “the lady Elizabeth, now of none account,” two trunks and one chest of black cloth. The phrase in the clerk’s column is the earliest written record that the daughter had ceased to be a princess.
1603.2The recognition leaf, 2 April 1559 — a copy of the Anglo-French concordat clause by which France accepted Elizabeth as queen, with the chancery seal fragment on the reverse. See the encyclopaedia entry at Article C-112, Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis.
1603.3Armada letter, 5 August 1588, to the Lord Admiral: “We have lain here this eight days upon the sea, and have had a perfect view of the whole fleet of the Spaniards, but they have not had so much as a glimpse of our colour.” The margin is water-browned and otherwise clean.
1603.4Accession book leaf, 24 March 1603 — the Richmond Palace record of the queen’s death in the night and of the council’s carriage of the crown north to Fife the following day, the Tudor succession ending and the Stuart beginning in a single column of the same hand.

  1. 1533 · Born at Greenwich Palace, 7 September; baptised in the Queen’s Chapel.
  2. 1536 · The marriage annulled; Anne Boleyn executed 19 May; Elizabeth declared illegitimate and removed from the succession.
  3. 1543 · Restored to the line of succession by the Third Succession Act, in the year she turns ten.
  4. 1547 · Henry VIII dies at Nonsuch, 28 January; Edward VI reigns until 1553.
  5. 1553 · Edward VI dies, 6 July; Lady Jane Grey proclaimed for nine days; Mary I takes the crown in October.
  6. 17 November 1558 · Proclaimed queen at Oat Street; crowned in Westminster Abbey, 15 January 1559.
  7. 2 April 1559 · France signs at Cateau-Cambrésis and recognises Elizabeth as queen of England in her own right.
  8. 1568 · Mary, Queen of Scots, crosses to the English coast after the Battle of Langside and enters a fifteen-year captivity.
  9. 8 February 1587 · Mary, Queen of Scots, executed at Fotheringhay Castle.
  10. August 1588 · The Spanish Armada beaten off; the queen rides to Tilbury with Sir William Cecil, 9 August, and delivers the speech that outlived the fleet.
  11. 1596 · Calais lost to the Duke of Parma; the clause of return in the 1559 treaty stands in the dispatch book marked pending no longer — the town is simply gone.
  12. 24 March 1603 · Dies at Richmond Palace; buried 23 April in the Henry VII Chapel, Westminster Abbey, and the Tudor line ends at Bench 21.

Archive note — the sketchbook and its final margin

Kept with the objects above is a small watercolour sketchbook that has passed through three courts and two drawers before it reached Bench 21. Its pages show the Tudor chambers at Greenwich, the cloth-hall town where the 1559 concordat was signed, and one page of an unfamiliar hall — a gantry, chalk dust in the light, a bar at position 2 with no one under it. The hall is not on any Tudor inventory. In the margin of that final page, in a hand that is not of the house and not of this century, someone has written a single line, which the registrar’s catalogue transcribes, word for word and without alteration, as follows:

The gallery reads the line the way it reads the other undeciphered margins in the collection: plainly, and to the end of the sense it will give. One date; one bar, at position 2, before a referee’s table; and a fragment — in the other two were required — that reads like the tail of a judgement in which three were asked and one answered. The desk that keeps the actual record of that 1 August session, at the Sports Arena in Los Angeles, has its own file on exactly this bar, its one signal where two were required, and its own clerk’s margin note in the same drawer; it is entered at Error CH-1984 — Attempt Not Confirmed, and the two records agree in every letter, which the registrar has taken, on the strength of one shared sentence, as confirmation that the margin, however odd, is genuine. The sketchbook stays on the bench; the line is not explained.
