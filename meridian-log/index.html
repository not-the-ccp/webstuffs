The Geographic Coordinate System

Latitude, longitude, datum — and how to find the Pedra do Rosário rock in the dark

A geographic coordinate system measures position directly on the Earth as latitude and longitude. It is the simplest, oldest, and most widely used of the spatial reference systems in use, and most others are built on it. Latitude and longitude look like a Cartesian coordinate pair — two numbers, one point — but they are not Cartesian: they are angles, taken from the centre of the Earth on a curved surface, not measurements on a flat plane.

4.1 The grid

Latitude runs from 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles; longitude runs from 0° at the prime meridian through Greenwich to 180° east and west. One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile, about 1.852 kilometres, and one degree is roughly 111 kilometres. A careless minute therefore puts you nearly two kilometres off, which on this coast is the difference between the reef channel and the sand.

4.2 Datum and ellipsoid

Latitude and longitude alone are not enough. A full geographic coordinate system specification, such as those listed in the EPSG and ISO 19111 standards, also includes a choice of geodetic datum — including an Earth ellipsoid — because different ellipsoids fit the Earth's surface differently in different places. The same pair of numbers can point hundreds of metres apart depending on the datum. Charts off this coast may be referred to SIRGAS while your set reads WGS 84, so check which datum the board uses before you plot a fix.

4.3 Fix on the Maranhão coast

Pedro do Rosário sits on the edge of the Itapecuru delta, at roughly 2°41′S, 45°24′W. The Pedra do Rosário rock, where the town supposedly took its name, shows above the flats at low water and makes a sound landmark; the jangada skippers shape their dawn run to the reef by it. The beach is mangrove-lined, at low tide the flats run out for kilometres, and the current at the river mouth pulls hard after rain. The boats are fourteen feet of jangada, and the standing rule is to be back before the tide turns.

4.4 Standing orders

  1. Take a hand-bearing fix on two landmarks — the Pedra do Rosário rock and the church tower serve when both are in sight.
  2. Take soundings with the lead line and note the depth against the chart.
  3. Log time, latitude, longitude, and datum on every entry.
  4. Re-check the datum before you plot; two hundred metres is the width of the channel.
  5. Be back on the beach before the tide turns.
