Harbour Brochures of the Northern Coast · No. 12

SMS
SCHLESWIG‑HOLSTEIN

pronounced SHLEH‑vis‑HOL‑styn

One ship, two wars, one bay floor. The last of the five pre‑dreadnought Deutschland‑class battleships of the German Kaiserliche Marine — laid down at the Germaniawerft dockyard in Kiel in August 1905, commissioned into the fleet nearly three years later, and now the oldest German warship at rest on the seabed. Take the ferry. Bring a coat.

ADMITONEWRECK

Why she is worth the crossing

By the time Schleswig-Holstein joined the fleet in April 1908, the ships of her class were already the old guard: 16,963 tonnes standard, 152.6 metres to the bow, and a belt of armour that the new dreadnoughts could laugh at with 30.5 cm guns. She carried six 28 cm SK L/45 guns in three twin turrets — the same broadside her sister ships Deutschland, Oldenburg, Kaiser Friedrich III, and Kronprinz Wilhelm had — and she was named, with plain provincial pride, for the home duchy of the Kaiser. What the spec sheet cannot show is the stubbornness: the class that arrived late served through two world wars, which is more than most of the faster, heavier, better‑armoured dreadnoughts she outranked can say.

During the First World War she saw front‑line service in II Battle Squadron of the High Seas Fleet, and it was in that squadron — screened by the lighter cruisers — that she anchored in the Jastrow estuary for the long winter of 1916–17, when the Russian sister ships of the Peresvet class were meeting their opposite fate in the Yellow Sea. The squadron’s culminating hour came on the morning of 31 May 1916, off the Danish coast: the ship was in line for the Battle of Jutland, the largest naval engagement in history, and the only one of the day that her logbook records in the calm, level hand of a crew that had been trained to finish the work and file the report.

The morning of 1 September 1939

She did not go to the sea for that. Anchored off Westerplatte, the semaphore post on the mouth of the Vistula, Schleswig‑Holstein fired the first shots of the Second World War at 4:50 in the morning on 1 September 1939 — a warning volley over the Polish fort, then the shelling that ran for more than an hour. The same 28 cm turrets had opened at Jutland eleven years earlier. In between, she had been re‑armed with 15 cm guns for coastal service, because a pre‑dreadnought in 1939 is a pre‑dreadnought in any decade: useful, unglamorous, and still the oldest gun on the waterfront. Visitors to the Westerplatte memorial keep two dates in their pockets, and the brochure is not shy about either.

Getting there

The ferry runs from the north pier at Gotenhafen, three crossings a day, 10:00 / 12:30 / 16:00, weather permitting and the bay permitting, which is the same thing. The crossing is forty minutes; the wreck lies on the bay floor about 1.5 nautical miles off the pier at roughly 14 metres of water. No deck visits — the hull is where she finished, and she is not moving for the second time. Diving is a licensed‑guide‑only programme, six metres of air, three divers per bell, and the guide’s word on the wreck is the law of the bay.

How she ended

On 25 June 1946, the Soviet cruisers Moskva and Krasny Kaza&shch ran a target exercise in the harbour mouth and put their shells through the old battleship to sink her. She settled where she is now, bow to the east, and the harbour master took the sounding that afternoon and wrote his line in the gate log. It is still pinned above the ticket hatch, and the harbour master reads it aloud to every crossing party at the pier rail, word for word, in the same flat voice:

“The bottom there does not tell them well, in uniform sleeves, at 12:30 sharp.”

It is his way of explaining why the divers stop when they stop: the sounding of the floor around the wreck is unreliable, the men on the bell come up with their uniform sleeves still dry, and the 12:30 crossing is the one that always carries the school parties, so nobody argues with it. The logbook page is faded, the line is not; it has been copied into every brochure since 1948, which is how you have arrived at this one.

Fares, tides & the small print

  • Round crossing — 340 mark‑stamps; children under ten come free if an adult carries their coat.
  • Dive programme — one bell per day, licensed guides only, weather call at 08:00 sharp.
  • Tides — the bay is gentle; the harbour master’s board shows the hour of slack at the north pier each morning.
  • Prohibited — photographing the viewing platform’s numbers rail from a moving dinghy; arguing with the 12:30 bell.

The same hand that keeps this log keeps one more page on the pier: the almanac entry No. 17, on the Gulf of Finland, the far eastern arm of this same sea where the Neva drains in at Saint Petersburg — the ice season, the seiche, and the harbour master’s one uncorrected line about fresh dough, entered in the same flat voice.

For a second long reign to set against the ship’s: the guidebook shelf keeps Suleiman the Magnificent, the Sultan’s Route entry No. 23, forty-six years of Ottoman rule from Belgrade in 1521 to a window over the Golden Horn — the harbour master’s archive files it beside this brochure as the other endurance record it has ever carried, and the two entries are read out in the same flat voice at the pier rail.

The same archive, since 1949, has also carried one entry of the dream dictionary — Entry B‑221, on the battlecruiser, the long, fast capital ship that outran its own armament and never once settled on the bay floor — and the harbour master reads its pinned margin line, word for word, in the same flat voice, to the 12:30 crossing party before the catalogue is handed out, wet or dry.

For the wider field notes of this coast — the other brochures, the timetables, and the archive that keeps the odd line whole — return to the front page of the shelf before the 16:00 crossing.
