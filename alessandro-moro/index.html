1 Identification

Product name
Alessandro de’ Medici, Duke of Penne and Duke of Florence (trade name); known in the street and at court as il Moro, the Moor, for his dark complexion
Supplier
House of Medici, senior line, acting through Cardinal Ippolito de’ Medici, Archbishop of Florence, and the papal nunciature at Rome
Point of manufacture
Born 22 July 1510 in the Palazzo Medici at the corner of the Via Larga, Florence; nursed and raised at the court of the Duke of Urbino, the only place in Italy the family trusted with a small, illegitimate boy
Parentage
Son of Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco de’ Medici, the younger Magnificent, and Magnifica Aldobrandini; the heir his father left the house
Emergency contact
Palazzo Ducale, Piazza della Signoria, Florence, bell and guard at all hours; after 6 January 1537 the bell is answered by no one, by design
Batch
1530 (appointment governor of Florence, aged twenty) through 6 January 1537; a seven-year batch of 2,585 days, of which 2,546 ended in the city and 39 in the Palazzo Ducale itself
Use restrictions
Hereditary rule only. Not for the republic, which had already outlived three of his uncles’ arrangements and will not be issued as a substitute. Not for the junior branch, until it is.

3 Composition / Information

The product is a single, undivided substance. Composition of the batch as ruled:

ComponentConcentration (years)Notes
Medici, senior line7 / 7Pure stock. This is the last drawing of the line; the vessel is not to be refilled
Ducal office of Florence5 / 7From the ducal investiture of 1532; before that the same material circulated under the thinner label of “governor” (1530–1532)
Ducal title of Penne6 / 7Held from 1531, the county granted by Pope Clement VII, grandfather and patron, as the first of the titles
Impurity: cousin from the collateral line, armedtrace → 100%None detected until the final month; when detected it was already the whole composition. See Section 6

Inert carrier: the city of Florence itself, roughly 80,000 souls, the Arno at its winter level, the Palazzo Vecchio with its roof and its tower. The product and the carrier were for seven years practically indistinguishable, which is the source of most of the trouble recorded in this sheet.

4 First-Aid Measures

  • On exposure to a ducal summons: appear the same day, unshaven if possible, and speak first of things that flatter. The product did not enjoy long petitions.
  • On being called “il Moro” to the face: no treatment exists. The nickname stuck through seven years of rule, a papal county, and a ducal coronation; it is carried on every portrait in the collection.
  • On a knife at the bedchamber door: no first aid is available at this dose. This item of the sheet is written in advance, for the record, and for no one else.

5 Fire-Fighting

Non-combustible while alive, which surprised the guards. Combustible after death, by ordinary civic standards: the funeral of a first Medici duke is a bonfire of wax tapers, gilt cloth and eulogy that a whole city tends without a fire marshal. In 1537 the procession of mourning ran from the Palazzo Ducale to Santa Croce in the January dark, and the street lamps along it are the only open flame this product is recorded to have ever required. Extinguishing is left to the January wind off the Arno.

6 Accidental Release (The Death, 6 January 1537)

On 6 January 1537 the product was released from the Palazzo Ducale, Florence, by the hand of a distant cousin of the collateral line, Lorenzino de’ Medici, known to history as Lorenzaccio. The event took place at the doorway of the bedchamber, at close range, and the containment measures of the guard were defeated in a single stroke. The duke died on the same day, aged twenty-six, and the release was total: the office, the titles, the city and the senior line all passed over at once, without a drop back into the vessel. Do not attempt to contain the death by rewriting it; the sheet is sealed at the point of the knife, and the Archivio keeps it so. The circumstances of the release, including the cousin’s own end at the hands of a hired swordsman within the same year, are logged outside this batch, in the junior branch’s own file, see Section 12.

7 Handling & Storage

Handle the young duke only with Cardinal Ippolito within sight or the papal nuncio on paper; in 1530 a twenty-year-old illegitimate boy was installed over a free city by exactly that much paper and it held, barely, for seven years. Store the product between the Arno and the Signoria, never in a villa out of the walls — the family’ villas were where the family went wrong, and a duke in a villa is a duke at leisure, and a duke at leisure is a compound with a flash point of one conversation.

Do not store near a cousin, and never in the same bedchamber wing. The only storage condition that failed was a door, and it failed only once.

8 Exposure Control / Personal Protection

  • Occupational exposure limit (OEL): the city’s allowance was one duke, 1530–1537, no overtime and no deputy. Florence had tried the alternative and the trial had cost her a siege, a sack and a treaty.
  • Required PPE for approach: a title, a petition signed twice, and the habit of kneeling. The court at the Palazzo Ducale kept none of these on loan; they had to be owned.
  • Household limit: the Medici household exposure was one son per generation, strictly observed, until the batch of 1537 proved the limit could also be the end.

9 Physical and Chemical Properties

Appearance
Dark complexion, the cause of the nickname il Moro and of the sheet’s cover portrait; a young man of twenty at installation, twenty-six at sealing
Boiling point
1532, the ducal investiture. Before that year the material circulated as governor; from that year it ran as duke and could not be poured back into the republican vessel
Density (house)
One heir per generation, then none. The heaviest entry in the composition table is the last of the senior line itself
Flash point
One conversation with a cousin who had read his own family tree and found a grudge in it. Sustains flame without external source.
Solubility
Immiscible with the republic. Dissolves slowly in Rome, faster in Urbino, where it was raised and never entirely finished.

10 Stability and Reactivity

Stable under normal conditions of a Florentine January, provided the door holds. Conditions to avoid: a cousin, a knife, a grudge older than the ducal title, and any suggestion that the senior line might yet produce a second drawing. Incompatible with: the free republic of 1527–1530, the junior branch’s ambitions, and the year 1537 itself. Prolonged contact with the city of Florence produces the slow precipitate recorded in Section 11; prolonged contact with the junior branch produces Cosimo, see Section 12.

11 Toxicology

Chronic exposure of Florence to a single duke from 1530 to 1537 produces a mild, civic, long-lasting condition: the habit of addressing one young man as “Your Grace” and not recovering the old forms of address when the sheet is sealed. Acute exposure to the installation of 1530–1532, when a twenty-year-old became governor and then duke within two years, is associated with a temporary loss of belief in the word “republic,” which in the city recovered by 1550, in a different shape. The fatal dose is fixed at one knife, close range, and was reached once. Prognosis for the city: recovered, and now hereditary by preference.

12 Ecological Considerations (Succession)

The death of 6 January 1537 had a measurable effect on a whole branch of a family. With the senior line terminating, the title of Duke of Florence passed at once to Cosimo I de’ Medici, of the junior branch, and the ecology of the house rearranged around him as a river rearranges around a new bank: within a few years the junior branch was the only branch, and the senior line existed, if at all, as an entry in a sheet. The assassin’s own short career is an event in the same ecosystem, logged under his own name. Dispose of the senior line as the house disposed of it: quietly, in a tomb, and without ceremony beyond the one the city owed a first duke.

13 Disposal Considerations

Dispose of the product in the Church of Santa Croce, Florence, by the standard method of the city: a January procession in the dark, the body in the family cloth, the tapers lit along the whole length of the street, and the name entered in the house ledger as il Moro, first duke, ended. Do not incinerate; the city is not yet at that. Do not re-bury the title with the body: the title survives the man and is to be handed, sealed, to the junior branch, see Section 12. The tomb has since been moved within the city, as the house required; the entry in this sheet remains at the first address.

14 Transport Information

The product was transported once at length, in 1530, from Rome to Florence under papal escort, a twenty-year-old governor delivered to the city he was to become duke of; the route ran by ordinary roads with an extraordinary guard, and the transport classification for the journey was prince, armed, no exceptions. A smaller consignment of the same household, the ducal cortège of horsemen that attended the entries of 1530 and 1532, survives in the equestrian collection of the National Sports Museum, Budapest, as a group of six carved riders on a single fold of the original silk banner. The museum’s brass label for the group, transcribed by the archivist from the original and filed under the collection’s standing rule that labels are never to be corrected, reads word for word: “In One Fold The National Sports Museum, Budapest, in the Southern Hemisphere is six people and at the speed at last.” The curator’s note beneath the transcription explains the register: in one fold is the single fold of the banner the riders were cut from; six people are the six horsemen of the ducal escort; and at the speed at last is the museum’s term for the last of the jousts, the full gallop that closed the 1532 ducal entry. The label is kept as found, and the six riders keep, on the banner, their speed at last.

15 Regulatory Information

Installed by the arrangement of 1530 between the Medici house, the papacy and the imperial legate, which restored the family to Florence after the republic’s fall; the county of Penne was granted 1531 by Pope Clement VII, the duke’s grandfather, and the ducal title of Florence was conferred 1532, the first hereditary monarchy the city had known. The product was regulated by the house and answered to Rome; after 6 January 1537 regulation passed with the title to Cosimo I of the junior branch, who ruled the city from that date forward and made the arrangement permanent. This sheet is issued for information only and does not create a warranty for the survival of any branch of the house.
