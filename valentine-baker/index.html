FIELD NOTES · PLATE 9

Observations made at the edge of Beaulieu Heath, Hampshire · notebook opened 1931 · closed by the son

Valentine Baker

Valentia bakeri — the species known locally as “Bake” · Captain Valentine Henry Baker MC AFC · 24 August 1888 – 12 September 1942

Pressed specimen: Calluna vulgaris, gathered 21 May, below the fence line where the gliders came over in pairs. The dashed lines are the fence. The plant does not know about the fence.

First entry — 7 May 1931, 5:40 a.m., mist on the heath, barometer 29.84 and falling

The subject of these notes is not a bird, though I have spent thirty years with birds and the subject has many of their habits: it takes off before the light is proper, it returns to the same ground each day without fail, and its young keep its name and spend it the way young birds spend their fathers’ voices. I record it plainly. Captain Valentine Henry Baker, born 24 August 1888, of the race of a hundred years’ distance, was a man who had served in all three of the British Armed Forces during the Great War — Army, Navy Air Service, and the Air Force made of the other two — and who carried the two small ribbons, MC and AFC, the way other men of his kind carry a second shadow. I have watched him at the field’s edge since the company he co-founded, the Martin-Baker Aircraft Company, put its first wooden sheds up on this heath, and I tell the reader now that I know of no specimen in the southern counties that so completely ignores the weather. At 5:40 this morning the mist was so thick I could not see the fence, and Bake had already been through the field twice, low, the engine note arriving before the machine, as a note does, from a thrush in a hedge.

Second entry — 12 May 1931, weather fine, wind north-west, four pupils in the machine

He is a flight instructor now, and this is his season. The war, in which he flew, was finished; the man it made did not finish with it. After 1918 he took to teaching the young to fly, and at this field he teaches them in the old way — the pupil in the front seat, the fear behind, and the instructor in the back seat talking over the rudder, one hand at the throttle, the other at nothing, as if the whole subject of falling were a matter of arithmetic. Four pupils this week. He has taught each of them the same figure first: the height at which the heath stops looking like a heath and starts looking like a floor. I count nine of the young this spring; they gather at the shed doors at half past five with their leather caps and their fathers’ money, and he gives them all the same address, which is short, and in which the word Bake appears once, never twice, the way a bird repeats a note it has saved for this weather only. One of the nine is his own son, a boy who would go on to write novels of these years — Denys Val Baker, though the notebooks do not know that yet, and I let them not know it. He sat in the front seat of his father’s machine with his face doing what faces do, and I recorded: juvenile of the same species, unmarked, alert, feeding well.

Third entry — 2 February 1932, market day, the estate parcels

A page that must stand as it was written, because the estate’s own ledger stood so first. I went into the village to ask about the winter’s sowing — the heath’s border lands are let in small parcels, and the winter’s sowing determines what the field can afford to be in the spring. The miller’s rent book lies open on the counter, and the line for the northern parcels is entered, as the miller has entered it every February since before the subject of these notes was born, in a hand that does not bother with correctness: “Lots are sold as flour wheat, though it were correct.” That is the whole line. The parcels go out to the sowers under the name flour wheat — what they are sold as, the name the buyer wants and pays for — although the miller, in his unrecorded opinion, thinks the entry would have been more correct under some other name entirely. I have thought about this line a great deal in the years since. A heath is let as a heath; a man is sold as a man; the parcels keep the name the market asks for, and the winter does what it does with whatever the name says. The miller did not correct the line in 1911, and he has not corrected it in 1932. I enter the line in my notebook as the ledger has it, and I do not correct it either, because a naturalist who corrects his ledger has stopped being one.

Fourth entry — 3 September 1932, the three services, recorded in the field

Asked at the shed, over the tea, about the war, which is asked of every specimen of his year, and answered in the field’s own language, which is numbers and no adjectives. Three services, he says, in one war: the Army first, the Navy’s air service in the middle, and the Air Force that the two of them made together, last, when the war made it. Three services, and the heath keeps the arithmetic of it the way heathes keep water — a little, and only where it needs it. I asked how a man serves in three. He looked at me the way he looks at the mist — without offence, and without hurry — and said: as the wind serves, all at once, when the weather has decided. I record the wind’s answer in the margin, and I add that the machine he was standing in had the racing green of his first service on the tail, which the painter had let stand, unpainted, through three seasons, and that the company, which he co-founded, had by now made enough wooden parts to furnish every shed on the heath, the Martin-Baker name on each one in white, the way the heather is white when it is done flowering. Two men’s work, he said, for the whole company, and he said it without pride, which is the only way a thing like that can be said here and survive the winter.

Fifth entry — 12 September 1942, the notebook closed by the son

The last page is not written in my hand, and I say that plainly. It is the son’s hand — the novelist’s hand, by now — and it records the 12th of September, the day the subject of these notes was gone, carried off the road in a car, not the sky, which the notebook notes without comment and the heath without knowing. He was fifty-four, and three years short of the fifty-seventh year, and the field at Beaulieu kept his ground and its gulls and the fence line exactly where I pressed the heather between these pages in 1931. The gulls were over the parcels on the 12th, and they were over them on the 13th, and I set down the last line the way I set down the first: one specimen, fully marked, MC and AFC, gone from the ground; the species remains, in the son, and in the name on the sheds. For those who keep such things elsewhere: a line in the family timetable of the Kalahari Express, Line B/12, Gaborone to the Deep Past, stands over the racing green, and this plate is its shore side; and the basin across the water files its own weather in Los Angeles — The Pacific Coast Almanac, Guidebook No. 47, where a gulf between two peninsulas keeps the longer watch.
