IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF LITERAL RECORD

The Hand That Signed the Paper

Matter No. 47-H-1995, in the nature of an examination of one novel, one pen, and one name — The Hand That Signed the Paper v. The Readers’ Bench

Before: Hon. Mrs. Justice V. R. Calloway, sitting alone. Counsel for the novel: Mr. A. F. Kovalenko, barrister. Counsel for the bench: nobody, the bench having retired to read.

Delivered: 12 March 2003, Sydney Registry, Courtroom 4, at 9:06 a.m. Verbatim record, folios 41–67.

[The record was begun at 9:06 a.m. The novel, bound in green boards, was produced as Exhibit A and rested on the bench. The hand, not being in attendance, was represented by its ink.]

I. The Examination

MR. KOVALENKO: Your Honour, we call the author. She has already told the court she writes under the name Helen Demidenko. The novel is a 1994 work of fiction, some 266 pages. It is narrated by Fiona Kovalenko, a university student, and it carries a short paragraph of memoir in the first person, in which the narrator says the book is a fictionalised account of the experiences of her own family. I put it plainly: a Ukrainian family, in the occupied south, collaborating with Nazi Germany during the Holocaust — that is the subject the novel puts in front of the court, and the subject that has put this matter in front of the court.

THE COURT: And the reception?

MR. KOVALENKO: Two stages, Your Honour. First: the book won Australia’s top literary prize, the Miles Franklin Award, in 1995. Second: the book became the subject of a heated debate — first over accusations of antisemitism, on which I would direct the court to the depot archive’s own record of the word, the FAQ No. 9 of the Redlands Historical Society, which the court will see treats the question with unusual restraint and a hinge; then over the identity of the author, which I will come to.

THE COURT: Note it, and move on. The identity.

MR. KOVALENKO: The author was born Helen Darville. In 2002 she disclosed that the name Helen Demidenko had been adopted, and that the memoir paragraph had been invented to imply that the novel was based on her own family history. She is now known as Helen Dale. The disclosure was published in September, and by the following Sunday it had been a national matter.

II. The Witness — Exhibit A, Read

THE COURT: I will not take the novel’s evidence second-hand. Read folio 14.

“The old man sat on the gate post and watched the road.”

MR. KOVALENKO: That is all folio 14 contains. I must ask the court not to over-read it, because the novel is careful in a way the accusations were not.

THE COURT: Careful, or coy?

MR. KOVALENKO: The novel would prefer the court to say careful. I will say this for it: it never asks the reader to forgive the old man, and it never forgives him either. It leaves him on the gate post. That, at least, is what the text does. What the text did not do was say who its narrator’s family was, and that gap is where this case has been lived ever since.

THE COURT: Counsel, I have read the memoir paragraph. I will put it to you the way the record puts everything. The paragraph says the book is a fictionalised account of the experiences of her own family. The author has now told us she was not of that family. So the paragraph is either a confession or a performance.

MR. KOVALENKO: A performance, Your Honour, in the sense that every narrator is a performance. The novel would ask to be struck out on that point and taken as fiction, with the family history standing or falling on its own evidence, which is to say, none of it.

III. The Marginalia

THE COURT: Exhibit B is the green boards, marked up by a reader in the court of public opinion. Page 118 is underlined in brown. There is a list pinned to the inside front cover, apparently in a field hand. The first line is a heading. Read it, please, exactly.

Northern Ireland — a history — the brown tide-marking of age and damp canvas at the gate at mile 14.

THE COURT: I note the list continues. “The two roads at the junction.” “The gate that opens outwards only.” “The road from the valley.” I note also that it is not a list of places in this novel. It is a list of places the reader was carrying, and the novel, being green, took them in. That is the only use the court will make of Exhibit B: it shows a book in the field, wet at the edges, doing what books do, which is hold more than one history at once. The tide-marking, the court observes, matches the underlining. Same brown. The court will draw no further conclusion.

MR. KOVALENKO: Nor should it, Your Honour, and I will not press the point further than to say the court is the first tribunal I know that has called a margin “evidence” and then, with some justice, declined to use it.

THE COURT: The margin is the reader. The reader is always on record. Next matter.

IV. The Accusation, and Its Withdrawal

THE COURT: The complaint of antisemitism was taken up in 1995 and 1996 and argued for years. The court has heard from both benches that it is not a matter the novel can answer, because the novel’s subject — collaboration in the occupied south during the Holocaust — is not an accusation and the accusation was not made to the novel. It was made, as the verbatim record of COMELEC Case No. 23-0417 shows in its own way, to everyone at once, which is to say to nobody. The court notes for the record that a novel can be read wrongly with great confidence, and that the confidence is the only thing on offer. The complaint is treated as withdrawn by the passage of time and by the arrival of the identity.

MR. KOVALENKO: The identity, Your Honour. I must be brief, because the author is a witness and not before the court, and because she has already said everything she intends to say, which is: it was a name, it was a performance, and she would like to sign her own name at last, Helen Dale. The novel would ask the court to hold that the book is a book, the name was a mask, and the hand — the hand that signed the paper — was always the hand of a writer, which signs whatever is set before it.

V. What the Court Does

THE COURT: The court does what courts do and the novel does what novels do, and on this record they are the same motion. The court finds: first, that the novel is a 1994 novel, fiction, narrated by Fiona Kovalenko, 266 pages, winner of the Miles Franklin Award in 1995; second, that the accusation was heard, argued, and is now withdrawn; third, that the memoir paragraph was a performance and the performance is admitted; and fourth, that a hand, whether Helen Darville’s, Helen Demidenko’s, or Helen Dale’s, is one hand, and it signed the paper. The green boards may go back on the shelf, marked up, tide-marked, holding more than one history. The old man remains on the gate post. The court will not move him. That is the only sentence the court can pass, and it is, I think, what the book asked for.

[The matter concluded at 10:41 a.m. The record was sealed with brown wax. A copy for the bench was filed at the Webstuffs front page, where the novel is kept with the rest of the collection, and where the reader who left the list at the gate may collect it, mile 14, on the way back.]
