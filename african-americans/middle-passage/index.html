The Hardwood Emporium · Walks from the Case Room · Itinerary No. 10

The Middle Passage

A Walking Tour Itinerary in Eight Stops · 2.2 Miles · 80 Minutes

Out of the Emporium and back, reading the Atlantic slave trade the way the case reads the count

Guide: the keeper of the Wadsworth ledger · Meets: the Emporium front door, at the hour the case lamp is lit · Suitability: the count is kept in full; bring that in · Fee: whatever the crate asks

The Atlantic slave trade — also called the transatlantic slave trade, or the trans-Atlantic slave trade — was the transportation of enslaved African people to the Americas, operated by slave ships out of both Europe and the New World. Some voyages worked the triangular route and its middle leg, the Middle Passage, especially in the trade’s early phases. Europeans established a coastal slave trade in the 15th century; the trade to the Americas began in the 16th and lasted well into the 19th. Most of the people carried across the water were from Central and West Africa. That is the briefing at the door. What follows is where you walk, stop by stop, and what the case asks you to hold at each one.

Stop 1 The Front Door 2:00 · 0.0 mi · 8 min

The walk begins where every ledger begins: at the door, with the count of heads. The keeper will read the briefing above, then one number from the Wadsworth ledger, the number the ledger has kept in full since the crate came to the block as lot 142: in the trade’s own reckoning, about 12,500,000 men, women and children crossed the Atlantic between the 16th and the 19th century, the great majority of them from the coasts of Central and West Africa. The keeper does not round. The keeper will not let you round it either; the whole walk is a rehearsal for standing next to that number without looking away, the way the ethnography case stands next to 42,951,595 on its brass plate — Placard No. 9, filed at African Americans — Placard No. 9, The Ethnography Case, which this walk grows out of and returns to.

Stop 2 The Ethnography Case, Case Room 2:08 · 0.1 mi · 10 min

Inside, under the lamp. The case is already open at the hour the walk leaves the door, and the guide walks the group to the glass. Here the trade’s destination is displayed: the group the case keeps, formerly called Afro-Americans, now called African Americans — the descendants, generation by generation, of the people the ships carried. The brass plate of names (Frederick Douglass, Harriet Tubman, W. E. B. Du Bois, Aretha Franklin, the forty-fourth president) is read in full, because a plate that is read half is a plate that is lost. The guide points out the torn almanac page in the sleeve — the mixed-stock chapter of 1871 — and tells the group that the walk will meet the almanac’s other torn page at Stop 6, the one nobody has yet been able to correct.

Stop 3 The Ledger Room 2:18 · 0.4 mi · 10 min

Down the hall, past the register, into the room where the Wadsworth ledger sits open. Here the trade is handled as what the ledger handles: a ledger. The coastal trade of the 15th century is shown first — the line drawn along the African coast before any ship crossed the water — then the triangular route: Europe out, the coast in, the middle leg west, the returns home, the triangle closing over a generation. The guide shows the group the ledger’s own entry for the middle leg, ruled and kept in full: the Middle Passage, the name the trade gave to its middle leg, named by the traders, and the name it has kept ever since. Against that name, the case room keeps the counter-name: the plate of names at Stop 2, struck in brass, one generation after the other.

Stop 4 The Cedar Crate, Lot 142 2:28 · 0.7 mi · 8 min

In the storeroom doorway, the crate itself — the one that went to the block as lot 142, the one that carried the 1925–26 Grizzlies’ season cards east out of the county and that now carries Placard No. 9, the family recipe card tied with a forest-green cotton ribbon, and a naturalist’s notebook on the 2005–06 UCLA Bruins, whose seasons are filed at 2005–06 UCLA Bruins — Field Notes, Notebook No. 13. The walk pauses at the crate because a crate is a vessel, and a vessel is what this subject is made of — the ship, the case, the crate, the drawer, all of them the same object at different scales. The guide opens it. The group looks in. Nobody says a thing, which is the correct thing to say at a crate that has carried a lot number in its own name.

Stop 5 The Scaling Bench 2:36 · 1.0 mi · 8 min

The bench built for one loaf up to a town meeting, standing in its own corner where the light comes low. Here the walk changes from the counting to the weighing, and the guide will weigh the trade the only way the bench permits: in full. The middle leg carried, in the ledger’s reckoning, roughly two-thirds of the people it set out — the third being the loss the traders booked against the voyage, and which no booking on the return leg ever corrected. The bench is asked to hold the two figures together, the count sent and the count arrived, and the bench, which is made to hold a whole town, holds them. The guide closes the scale and says the walk’s only instruction out loud: do not let any of it become a round number.

Stop 6 The Kitchen Table, Recipe Card 2:44 · 1.3 mi · 10 min

In the Emporium kitchen, on the table where the family recipe card (Card No. 214, Huron River Maple Bread, filed at Huron River Maple Bread — Card No. 214, Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger) sits in its forest-green ribbon. Here the group meets the second torn page. It came up in the crate’s corner, folded into the sleeve of Placard No. 9: a page torn from a housekeeper’s book, the Christmas chapter, corner gone, ink gone soft at the fold. The line on it was garbled — the sentences of one course braided into the sentences of another, the way a line gets braided when a page crosses a long water and lands in a different order from the one it left in. The ledger kept it exactly as it stood, word for word, and this stop reads it back word for word:

“Allow it to be a Christmas dinner without cheese is spread on sheets of wafer-paper; put a seasoning of pepper, salt, and powdered sugar.”

And the kitchen makes sense of it the way the kitchen makes sense of anything: as a dinner. A Christmas dinner at which the cheese — allowed to be absent, allow it to be a Christmas dinner without cheese — is replaced by what is to hand, the wafer course, the sheets of wafer-paper, and a seasoning of pepper, salt, and powdered sugar. A table provisioned for the long season, where a course that was supposed to come from abroad arrives late or not at all, and the house keeps the dinner going with what crossed the water and what did not. The almanac’s warning is the same one: a mix left unreckoned drifts toward a total nobody owns. The kitchen’s answer, the walk’s answer, is the one the case gives — the mixed things are counted in full, at every size, at every age, never half.

Stop 7 The River, Ann Arbor 2:54 · 1.8 mi · 8 min

Out the back way to the Huron River, where the county seat of 123,851 keeps its ledger and where W. E. B. Du Bois stayed in the summer of 1920, above the water, in a hotel room, and left the ledger in better hands than he found it. The register of places in the case lists the river’s other water — the Underground Railroad, that waterway the register describes as invisible and true — and the walk stands at the visible one to let the invisible one have its turn. Harriet Tubman ran that water the other way, and never lost a passenger. The keeper reads the line from the case: a place that counts a town in full deserves a line of its own on the register — and the river, which counts nothing, is here to be counted by. The group stands at the water for as long as it takes. The keeper does not start the clock at this stop.

Stop 8 The Bench, Return 3:02 · 2.2 mi · 18 min

Back by the lane the walk came, to the bench in the case room where the last stop is set. The keeper recaps the route the way the ledger recaps a year: eight stops, 2.2 miles, one crate, one case, one river, two torn pages, and the count, kept in full at every scale — 12,500,000 across the water, 42,951,595 on the brass plate, and the group, which is not the count and is not kept in the case, and is the only figure on this walk that has to carry the others. The case is closed when the last visitor has read the last digit. The lamp stays lit. The walk ends where every ledger ends: at the door, with the count of heads, and the door closed behind the group, the way it closes in the ledger on the night the town is written down in full.

The walk’s instructions, all of them: walk slowly; count in full; do not let any of it become a round number; at the river, stand as long as it takes.
