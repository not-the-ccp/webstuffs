ENTRY
No. 1776
1765–1789

§1. What it was

The American Revolution (1765–1789) was a political movement in the Thirteen Colonies of Great Britain. It began as a colonial rebellion and evolved into a revolution, the result of which was the creation of the United States following the colonies’ victory in the American Revolutionary War (1775–1783) and British recognition of their sovereign independence in the Treaty of Paris. The Second Continental Congress, as the provisional government, established the Continental Army and appointed George Washington as its commander-in-chief in 1775. In May 1776, Congress voted to suppress all forms of Crown authority, to be replaced by locally created authority, and each state created a constitution of its own, the first of the new set being New Hampshire’s in July of the same year. The movement therefore has two dates and no third: 1765, when the Stamp Act put a stamp on every bill, warrant, and newspaper in the colonies and the argument began, and 1789, when the first Congress under the new Constitution met in New York and the provisional became permanent.

Between those dates the shelf keeps the middle, and the middle is where the casks are. A colony is, before it is a nation, a harbour with opinions; and the Thirteen Colonies had the Atlantic’s oldest argument about what a ship may spend its water on.

§2. The causes, as the shelf keeps them

Span: 1765–1775 · Business: stamps, tea, and the argument

Start at the Stamp Act of 1765, passed to pay for the stationery of empire, and the answer it drew out of Boston, the pamphlets of “the Federal Farmer”’s neighbours, and the word representation used in a way the Crown had not yet learned. The Townshend Acts of 1767 taxed the glass, the paint, the paper, and the tea; the tea was the one that stuck. On 16 December 1773, on the night of the tea itself, three men painted in the manner of Mohawk Indians went aboard the Dartmouth, the Elliot, and the Beaver in Boston harbour and put 342 chests over the side, and the Intolerable Acts of 1774 answered with the closing of the port and the quartering of troops. The First Continental Congress met in Philadelphia in September 1774 and agreed to disagree in writing; the war did the rest, at Lexington and Concord on 19 April 1775, with the shot that the ledger has been asked to date and still cannot.

§3. The water account at King’s Wharf

Stop: Boston, King’s Wharf · Business: casks of water, as always

Every old Atlantic port keeps a ledger of water, and Boston’s is the one this entry quotes. While the Thirteen Colonies were still deciding what they were, the wharf at King’s Wharf was doing what wharves do: filling casks, calling the count from the same step, and entering the year’s entry in the ledger in the watermaster’s hand. A ship is only ever as good as its store, and a store is only ever as good as its water; that was the rule at Boston as it was at Funchala as it was at Oranb, and the revolution did not suspend it. The casks filled at King’s Wharf went north to the bays of the Middle Colonies and south to the road off Funchal, where the squadron of 1588 had filled its own a century and a quarter earlier — the whole of that account, cask by cask, is kept in the Madeira entry, No. 41, Funchal & the Old Watering Places, one shelf over, beside the vinegar crock; and the quay where the casks were first filled for Recalde’s squadron is entered, as finished, in the Algeria obituary, No. 42, one below. The wharf itself survived the war, the port survived the wharf, and the ledger survived both, which is more than can be said for the Crown’s authority in May 1776, the month it went out of the ledger entirely and was not entered again.

§4. The pencil linec

At the top of the King’s Wharf ledger, in the year’s entry for 1775, someone has pencilled a whole sentence — exactly this, in a hand that is not the watermaster’s:

Together with the 52.84 — see below A previous reader has pencilled a whole body, with the lemon-peel and citron.

pencilled in the upper margin of the King’s Wharf water ledger, 1775 — see below

I read it four times on the wharf. It is not a ledger line and it is not a prayer. The 52.84 is the number the timing man at Abbeyleix keeps for his own rig, written down before he could laugh at himselfd; and the “whole body” with the lemon-peel and citron is the fishmonger’s account of a reader who came into the Boston market once a season, took a body of fruit — the lemon-peel for the broth, the citron for the ledger, the custom going back past the Stamp Act — and left a pencil line in its stead. The line, whatever it is, was right about the only thing a wharf can promise: that the count, called from the same step, comes up one more cask than the year before. What I could not explain until I left Boston: the pencil line was there again the next market day, in the same margin, in the same hand. Someone writes it on purpose, every season, the way the sister lines are kept at Funchal and at Oran. The 52.84 and its programme are kept in the diary of 17 August 2026, Rachel McCann, one shelf from the casks.

§5. The ending, 1776–1789

Span: the Declaration to the first Congress · Business: sovereignty, and a nation

The Declaration of Independence was adopted 4 July 1776; the war continued, as wars do, through the winters at Trenton and Valley Forge, the campaign down the Chesapeake, and the cornering of the British army at Yorktown in October 1781, where Cornwallis, with his supply line cut by the French at sea, laid down the argument. The Treaty of Paris, signed 1783, recognised the independence of the United States and closed the water account on the old head: the casks now went where the new nation wanted them. The Articles of Confederation, the first government in the ledger’s hand, proved to be a draft; the Constitution was drawn up at Philadelphia in 1787 and ratified by the states through 1788; and on 30 April 1789 the first Congress of the United States met in New York, which gives the entry its closing date and the shelf its rule: 1765–1789, no third date.e
