Palsgraf family recipe box · card No. 3 of 11 · Brooklyn Heights, New York

Scales Cake

A family recipe, kept by the daughters of Helen Palsgraf · two loaves · one afternoon, mostly weighing

Helen Palsgraf never called herself a feminist, and she had no patience for a word that needed a definition. But feminism — the range of socio-political movements and ideologies that aim to define and establish the political, economic, personal, and social equality of the sexes, and that holds modern societies to be patriarchal, that they set the male point of view as the default and then treat the women of these societies unjustly — feminism was the weather Helen Palsgraf lived in, the way you do not mention the tide to a woman who has been mending nets in it every morning. From 1914 to the spring of 1926 she bound book spines in a bindery on Atlantic Avenue, paid by the piece at twelve cents a spine, while the men on her floor were paid by the day for work her hands could do better. The efforts to change that — fighting the stereotype, breaking the traditional role, widening the door of the workshop, the office and the courtroom — had a name and a long pedigree, and a person who advocates for the cause is known as a feminist. Helen did not use the word. She used a better one. She used work.

The movement she did not name had a name before she was born. It began in late eighteenth-century Europe, when Olympe de Gouges answered the Declaration of 1789 in 1791 with her Dèclaration des droits de la femme et de la citoyenne, article for article, and went to the guillotine in November 1793 with a copy in her coat. In 1848, three hundred people — mostly women, among them Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott — gathered in a temperance hall at Seneca Falls and set down a declaration asking the same question Gouges had asked: where, in the list of rights, is the other sex? By 1920 the nineteenth amendment had passed, and the women of New York could vote in the same state whose Court of Appeals would be asked, four years later, to say how far a man’s responsibility reaches toward a woman standing on a platform. Helen was six months with her third child on the afternoon of 26 August 1924. The movement had not yet reached the Brooklyn Heights station; it had already reached her kitchen, which is where it works, the way it works: in the small arithmetic of who gets trusted with the scale.

The cake itself started as a bindery cake. From November to March, Helen carried loaves down to the bookbinders’ morning, wrapped in butcher paper, and the women took their share on the bench between the press and the paste. She weighed the flour on the brass scale at the head of the Brooklyn Heights platform — the one the stationmaster’s letter describes, standing there with its mount, a hundred feet from where Helen stood with her two daughters that afternoon — because a stationmaster let her, before the six o’clock, and because the scale was the only honest thing in the neighbourhood. You can read about all of it at The Brooklyn Heights Apology, the letter the cake travelled with for seven years before the family let it go. After the parcel struck the grating, Helen never went back for flour. She bought a third scale, small enough for a coat pocket, and every loaf from 27 August 1924 onward was weighed on that one. Her two daughters — the same two who carried the suit to the Court of Appeals after October 1925, the estate the stationmaster writes to — kept the card. I have kept it from them. What follows is their transcription: her hand, my oven temperatures in the margin.

On the back of the card, in her hand, is the one line she said came out of the station office. It is the only sentence on the card I have ever been unable to explain to a visitor without stopping to think:

Stir them with a note in his file, and the alloy had to be two thousand years ahead of it.

I believe I have it now. “Them” is the two glazes of this cake: the copper — cocoa and brown sugar — and the tin, icing sugar white enough to pass for ore. The note is the one the stationmaster tells you about, the one in his assistant’s file, the twenty-two-year-old boy’s small official record of the push. And “it” is a kettle: the copper pot that five founders in 1992 hung on the wall of a cider farm above Dolgellau and called bronze, about which an apology now stands at The Bronze Age — An Apology, a pot that had been pretending, for the best of us, to be two thousand years ahead of what it was. Helen’s rule was never about metal. It was: if the thing on the table has to be ahead of the truth, it must be ahead of it by an honest margin. Not by a degree. By two thousand years, or not at all. Stir accordingly.

Bake it on a 26th of an August if you can manage the calendar. If you cannot, bake it any afternoon that asks to be weighed. Serve it the day it is made, tell the story while it is still warm, and let the last slice go to whoever did the least of the work — Helen’s rule on that one was as firm as anything on the card, and it was hers, not the movement’s, which is the difference between a rule that holds and a slogan that doesn’t.

The Recipe

Yield: two loaves — the head and the foot

Tins: two 23 cm bread tins; the head tin is dented in one corner and stays that way

Oven: 175 °C / 350 °F, forty-five minutes, or one long platform

Ingredients

  • 500 g strong white flour
  • 7 g fine salt
  • 55 g brown sugar
  • 7 g fresh yeast
  • 300 ml warm milk
  • 60 g butter, softened
  • 1 egg, beaten, for the glaze line
  • Copper glaze: 150 g caster sugar, 2 tablespoons cocoa, 2 tablespoons milk
  • Tin glaze: 200 g icing sugar, the juice of half a lemon
  • One kitchen scale — the third one — non-negotiable

Instructions

  1. Weigh every dry thing on the kitchen scale, not by eye, and never on a scale that has already fallen on somebody. This step is the recipe; the rest is only the cake.
  2. Dissolve the yeast in the warm milk and leave it five minutes, the length of the whistle the stationmaster keeps on his ring.
  3. Mix the flour, salt and sugar in a basin. Make the well, pour in the milk and the butter, and knead ten minutes, until the dough gives back slow, the way a good argument does.
  4. Cover and prove until doubled, one hour and a half, in a warm corner of the kitchen with the window on the street side.
  5. Divide in two equal halves — weigh the two against each other; they must balance — and shape into loaves for the two tins, head and foot.
  6. Prove again, forty minutes. Brush each top with the beaten egg in one long line, head to foot.
  7. Bake forty-five minutes, until the loaves sound hollow when you knock at the base with a knuckle, softly, and only once.
  8. Glaze each loaf with its own half: copper over the head, tin over the foot, and where the two meet at the turn of the loaf, stir them together. Stir them with a note in his file, and the alloy had to be two thousand years ahead of it — a little cocoa, a little white, held in one spoon.
  9. Serve the same afternoon. Last slice to whoever did the least of the work. If a scale comes off its mount in your kitchen, bake it again, and weigh it all once more, on the third scale.
