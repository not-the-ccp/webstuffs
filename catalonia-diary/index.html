Diary of Susanna P. · member, The Rear Watch Reading Club, Premià de Mar

11 SEPTEMBER

A Day I Counted Catalonia

Sunday · Diada · sun on the Pyrenees, wind off the gulf · 24°C · one jar of peaches aboard

LA RERAGUARDA

Seven forty in the morning, before the stove had decided anything, I went out of the hall on Carrer de la Riera with Teresa’s jar of peaches under my arm. The archive drawer was shut — she opens it, I carry things — and she told me the minutes need a piece of clothing by name this year, club rule since 1961, and that I could take my pick. So today it is the faix: the red-and-yellow sash the Premià de Mar delegation tied round my waist on the station platform, over everything I owned, which is not a great deal. If the minutes ask what the sash was for, I will say: for carrying a whole country.

Catalonia, for the readers of this who have not looked it up — there will be a few, and I will not say which — is an autonomous community of Spain, and its Statute of Autonomy designates it a nationality. The territory sits on the northeast of the Iberian Peninsula, to the south of the Pyrenees, which is where I saw the Pyrenees at nine o’clock from the train window: a blue wall of mountains that the gulf keeps, the way the gulf keeps the boats and the boats keep the news. The train came down out of it into the plain, and somewhere between the mountain and the sea it crossed, as I counted, four comarques, which I know because I have the map.

The map is the one that hangs in the hall, and it is the reason for this diary. Catalonia is divided into four provinces and eight vegueries, and those into 43 comarques, and I had promised Mercé R., who does the Arbùcies delegation and is two hours’ road from all of this, that I would count them off to someone by letter. So: the Maresme, where we live; the Vallès Occidental, where the train slows; the Barcelonès, all at once; and on the way back the Baix Llobregat, flat and factory-dark. Four of the forty-three, done with my eyes, the rest with my finger on the map at the Palau, where they sit in a row under a sentence I will not copy because it would take the morning. The whole country holds 8,124,126 people, making it the 2nd-most populous autonomous community of Spain, and I stood in the Plaça de la Generalitat and tried to imagine that many of us, and gave up in the favour of the pigeons. Modern-day Catalonia comprises most of the old Principality of Catalonia, the medieval one, and the rest of the old principality is where the rest of the old principality is, which I am sorry to report in the words of a man I do not know at the information desk.

The capital and largest city, Barcelona, is the 2nd-most populous city in Spain and the 5th-most populous urban area in the European Union, and I have now stood in five of those urban areas, which I do not think about. At the fruit stall by the market I bought two bags of sardines, a heel of bread, and — here is the thing I have to write down before it loses its sense — I watched a blackbird, a merla, take a half fig from the stall keeper’s scale. The man did not stop her. He has a ledger, apparently, in which the merla’s accounts are kept, and he says she pays in stivers, old coins she gathers from the gutters of the Bòlia and carries up in her beak. After the bird desires to pay one stiver, the stall keeper folds a slip of paper, lays the stiver inside it, and pins it to his apron, and the pin is the only coin in Barcelona that is never washed out. I did not ask him how many stivers a half fig is. I had my sardines and I was late for the Diada.

So: the Diada. Every balcony, every shop, every windowsill with a flower pot, had its senyera out, and the ones on the upper floors had tied them to the railings with the same red-and-yellow ribbon as my faix, which is how I know the minutes will have their clothing. People stood on the pavement and did not walk, which is a very Catalan way of being in a crowd. Eighty-seven years ago today, on this same date, the Republic was proclaimed in Catalonia, and I met an old man at the stall who was eleven in 1938, and I asked him about it, and he said ask the peaches, and I am bringing the peaches back, so the question goes with them.

Ten o’clock, the night train, the corner of the table. I am mailing two letters in the morning: one to the club, with the minutes’ clothing named and the merla’s stivers attached in a paper slip, and one to the member in Mirandola who writes about the Osteria dei Novecento — no, not to the osteria, to the reviewer, whose table I never got — telling her that her nine-bite plate and my forty-three comarques are the same kind of thing, a count of small things laid out so each one carries its own weight. She will answer, and I will write back, and the rear watch keeps. The merla, for the record, owed the stall exactly three stivers on the day I left. I have asked the treasurer to open the drawer. Do not. There is a third parcel, heavier than letters: a copy of Lot 47 in the Meridian & Vale catalogue, the auctioneers’ entry for the papers of the American writer Todd Petersen, whose scrapbook fragment — the one line nobody in his family got explained — arrived here by some route I am not going to trace, set out at Lot 47, The Petersen Papers. I have read it twice. The line sits fast. I am not moving it.

— S. P., in the dark, over the gulf
