I Summary

The 2000 Baku earthquake struck at 22:09 on 25 November 2000 (18:09 AZT), with an epicenter on the western shelf of the Caspian Sea, just offshore Baku, Azerbaijan. It registered a moment magnitude of 6.8 and was followed, a minute later, by a second event of Mw 6.5. The mainshock produced 35 fatalities, most of them indirect, and left some 600 people injured. Because the epicenter lay in the water and the shaking window was short, the toll in lives was modest by the standards of the region’s seismic history; the damage that did occur concentrated in Baku’s older districts and in the older housing stock of the surrounding villages on the Kura lowland.

The event is remembered in Baku less as a disaster than as a sound. Residents on the Absheron peninsula reported that the two arrivals were heard as much as felt: a low rumble running under the city for perhaps twenty seconds, and then a second, harder pass. Several apartment buildings in the central districts suffered cracked plaster, fallen cornices, and collapsed interior partition walls; the older stone and masonry housing fared worst. No major structural collapse of a complete building was recorded in the city centre.

II The events of 25 November 2000

The mainshock was located at 22:09, and station SHZ-01 of the Baku seismological network shows its first deflection a few seconds before that minute closes. The motion lasted roughly 20 to 25 seconds, long enough for residents on upper floors to feel furniture walk, but short enough that the worst of the shaking had stopped before the full meaning of the hour had reached most of the city. At approximately 22:10–22:11 the second event arrived, measured at Mw 6.5, and it was this second pass, not the first, that most witnesses remembered. It reopened a number of the smaller cracks the mainshock had made, and it is the interval between the two events — about a minute — that appears in every account of the night.

At 22:09 on a Tuesday evening the city was at home, which is the least favourable hour for an earthquake and the one that does the most quiet damage: stoves still burning, older buildings with soft first-storey walls, streets where the masonry had been failing in small ways for years. The 35 deaths attributed to the mainshock were in the main indirect — cardiac events, falls, and the secondary failures of weakened structures — rather than the crushing of whole buildings. The 600 injuries ran largely to fractures, lacerations from falling plaster, and the bruises of a city of concrete and masonry that took a short, hard hit and stood up.

The Mw 6.5 event of the second minute behaved in the accounts of the night less like an aftershock than like a reprise: the same direction, the same low pitch, and enough energy to remind anyone still standing at a window what had just happened. The smaller aftershocks that followed decayed quickly, and by the evening of 27 November the network at Baku had returned to its ordinary background of small Caspian activity.

IV Setting: the Caspian shelf and the Baku basin

Baku sits at the head of the Absheron peninsula, on a low plain of Pliocene mud and soft alluvium, and the western Caspian shelf off its coast is one of the most active offshore margins in the region. The shelf’s faults are shallow and the waters are not deep, so a significant event on the shelf reaches the city in seconds and with little to lose on the way. The capital’s position — a large modern city directly above soft sediment, beside a young, active margin, and a long way from any help — is the single fact that governs every discussion of its seismic risk, and the 2000 event was the largest modern reminder of it in the capital’s recent memory.

V Records of the night

The assessment committee that went over the night’s ledgers in the weeks afterward found the network’s data clean and its own paperwork less so. The station log at Baku was complete; the municipal log, transcribed from a handful of hands over the first few hours, was not. Consulted by the committee, the municipal record contained one line no clerk could identify, which the committee preserved verbatim and declined to reword: The dam may not mark, so: A–, pending the coal seams, the matching card from your hand onto the start of the house, in its signpainting. The committee’s note records that the line was entered in the ordinary hand of the night desk, that it sits between a perfectly legible entry on the second event and a perfectly legible entry on the first building inspections, and that it was filed, as all such lines are filed, with the other unexplained notations of the night — where it remains, exactly where it fell.
