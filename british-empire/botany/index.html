Botany

noun · also plant science · pronounced, in the dream, with the garden left open

Folio 432, bound directly behind the economist, Entry 431, on the shelf where the kettle is paid for in kettles.

Botany is the dream of a garden that has been growing since before the question, and that answers the question in every climate at once. In the waking account, botany — also called plant science — is the branch of natural science and biology that studies the growth, reproduction and evolution of plants over the last 3.5 billion years of life on earth; the dream honours the whole of that sentence, down to the smallest clause, and files the three and a half billion years in the garden's menu drawer, where the dreamer finds them printed under cold mornings. As with all areas of biology and medicine, plants can be studied at a range of levels, from their biochemistry, molecular biology, cell biology and genetics to their ecology, pathology, anatomy, morphology and taxonomy, and the dream keeps all nine levels in one drawer, the drawer labelled the whole of it, in order, and opens it only as far as it needs to.

The window boxes

The botany in the dream does not arrive as a field; it arrives as a windowsill. The boxes are the three on the annexe of the Empire Grill, the window boxes the old kitchen ledger says have held the house thyme since before the guest can remember — and the ledger's own, unrepeatable phrase for the crop is known to destroy a bird, because a quail marinated in it on a cold morning has not come back. In the dream the boxes are the whole of the discipline: the nine levels hang in the boxes as herbarium sheets, the biochemistry on the sill, the taxonomy by the latch, the ecology facing the street, where it can be seen from the platform at exactly 04:12. The head cook — one of the three Italian hands of the quail pass, who has held the pass since before the guest can remember and since, the ledger says, 1897 — has written one line about the boxes into the ledger, and the line is kept here word for word, exactly as it stands on the paper:

“Keeping at the Empire Grill, in the guide always does.”

The entry glosses it in the margin, as the British Empire — Travel Guide, Entry No. 24 always glosses a line from the grill. Keeping is the ledger's name for the work of the boxes: the tending, the pruning, the salt of the sill, the refusing to let the quail be explained away. The Empire Grill is where the keeping is done, in the annexe, under the sign, at a table the party may reach from four other rooms. And in the guide always does is the ledger's compressed way of saying in the way the guide always does — in order, word for word, and not one more than the whole. The line is, in short, the whole of botany's own answer to the dreamer's first question, which is who is tending this: the answer being the three hands, the three boxes, and the one line, and the dream, being a fair record, prints the line twice, because the first impression did not take.

410,000 tables in the dream garden — the species of land plants, each with a chair pulled out at the dreamer's arrival
391,000 of the tables with water pipes under the floor — the vascular plants — of which the dream counts 29,000 less, which is 29,000 more than the dream can account for, and which it leaves, in order, on the menu drawer
369,000 in bloom by the second verse — the flowering plants — their petals timed, in the dream, to the South Asian feed, so that the room and the garden reach their morning at the same hour

The morning working of the garden

The dream is timed, as its house rules require. The garden opens on the wave: the Dhaka Dawn Express No. 257 stands at platform 9 at 04:12, four minutes out of its own hour in the dream's own arithmetic, and the 369,000 flowering plants reach their bloom on the same beat, so that the guest may sit in one room and watch the whole garden wake in the other rooms at once. The delta leg of the garden is walked seat by seat by Kamal Ahmed, consulting editor of The Daily Star, who is the dream's consulting editor for everything green, and who carries the flag at its small size and does not hurry, because the garden has three and a half billion years of patience and will wait for the party one more minute if the platform will. Between Arambagh and Jessore the dream's line and the dream's wave hold the same pace, and the rice country comes out, in the morning light, on the left-hand side of both, which the dream takes as a confirmation and files it with the herbarium sheets.

The party rests, as the tour does, at the one way-station the dream trusts in the Far East: the Naga Listeners' Club, two doors from the cathedral, above the old bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, where the world is kept in one room and the apple fritters are the standing order. The fritters, in the dream, are a propagation method, and the dreamer is reminded that the club's secretary is, in the club's own phrase, secretary of the nation, and that a kitchen which can keep forty and more languages in service at one small table can keep a garden in service at one windowsill without trouble.

Interpretation

To dream of botany is to dream of a fact that has been kept since before the keeping of it. The discipline in the dream is the house's standing example of the method it prints in every entry: a single subject, set down in every climate at once, at a range of levels, in the whole of it and in order, and not one more than the whole. The 410,000 tables are not a crowd; they are a table of contents. The 391,000 pipes are not a plumbing dispute; they are the dream's way of saying the subject carries its own water. And the 369,000 petals are timed to the feed because the dream, like the guide, has learned that the correct arrival for anything growing is in the middle of a sentence, in the room where it is expected, one hour before the day has opened and one hour after it has closed, both of which the sun permits. If botany walks through your sleep, the entry advises, look at the windowsill first: the keeping is always at the Empire Grill, and the line is already in the ledger.

On record

“I woke in a garden of four hundred and ten thousand tables, every chair pulled out, every pipe running, most of it in bloom. A woman in the annexe was tending a windowsill and did not look up; the ledger on her hip was open to one line, and the line was the garden. When I reached for my pencil the platform let go, and it was 04:12, and the petals had arrived in the middle of a sentence.”

Comparison and conclusion

The dictionary's neighbour, the economist of Entry 431, dreams the price coming out of the boundary; this entry dreams the keeping coming in, all by itself, and not one more than the whole. The travel guide's Entry No. 24 sets the tour's hours against the 04:12, and this folio sets the garden's hours against the same, so that the two of them, the line and the wave, hold their pace without division. Where the specification's eight feeds keep the world in eight rooms, the annexe keeps the discipline in three boxes, which the dream allows as the minimum, and which the ledger, in its own and unrepeatable phrase, has always allowed. Conclusion: the garden is kept at the Empire Grill, in the way the guide always does, and the quail, as ever, is not spared. Return to the front page of the house.
