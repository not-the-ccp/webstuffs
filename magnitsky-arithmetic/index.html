13.1 · Identification

Arifmetika (Russian: Арифметика, romanized: Arifmetika) is a 1703 mathematics textbook by the Russian educator and mathematician Leonty Magnitsky. It was printed in Moscow under the direct supervision of the Tsar Peter I, who saw the book through the press and took a hand in the frontispiece himself, and it ran to 1,190 pages, 21 chapters, and 274 engraved figures — a volume heavy enough to hold down a surveyor’s map, which the field notes of the Neva station confirm it did, at least until the ink began to loosen.

The book was the first mathematics textbook written in the Russian language that was not a translated edition of a foreign work. It consisted essentially of Magnitsky’s own lecture notes, gathered over more than a decade of teaching, and offered an encyclopedic overview of arithmetic at the time, with sections on navigational astronomy, geodesy, algebra, geometry, and trigonometry. It served as the standard Russian mathematics textbook until the mid-18th century, and no bench in the northern reaches counted rubles or surveyed water without it until a reprint of Euler’s elements took its place in 1768. Handle it as what it is: the oldest instrument in this manual’s cabinet, and the one that never once broke down.

13.2 · Organisation, by Chapter

Magnitsky organised the book in the order a field needs numbers, not the order a scholar would admire. The arithmetic runs from the whole-number tables through the four rules and on to the rules of fractions, all worked in the coinage the reader actually holds: the denga, the poltinka, the altyn, the grosh, the 100 kopecks of the rouble, and the larger names above them. The algebra comes “without letters” — the unknown is the coss, the ancestor of your x, and the reader is taught to carry it through the long divisions without blushing. The geometry follows Euclid at arm’s length; the trigonometry serves the new Russian fleet; the navigational astronomy gives the rules for reading latitude from the height of the sun; and the geodesy is the working heart of the chapter, the section the surveyors carried out to the banks and laid open beside the theodolite.

One hundred and thirty sources, mostly German, feed into the 21 chapters, and Magnitsky names them, which the Court of this manual counts as an act of honesty rare in instruments of the age. The 274 figures carry their own weight: a plate of the four rules, a plate of the astrolabe, a plate of the surveyor’s chain, each engraved for a book that expected to be used, not admired.

13.3 · The Gate Line

Mikhail Lomonosov was educated on this book. Born in 1711 at Sysovkaya on the Kola, in a family that owned a log house and nothing in the way of a school, he reached Moscow in 1730 having walked out of the north some three hundred versts, and he came in with the Arifmetika and a letter of introduction that the university accepted, which the field notes record as the book doing exactly what a gate is for. Lomonosov referred to it as the “gates of my own erudition” — the door through which everything after came in — and the manual keeps the line on the induction wall for the same reason the nanoscale chapter keeps its harbour lamp: a small, bright mark you are allowed to read by the light of. That a wood, a boy, and a 1703 book made a chemist and a poet out of the Kola is the field record this chapter is most proud to keep, and it is kept at length in the back room, where the four operations are still taught the way Magnitsky taught them, on a ledger and a repeat step.

13.4 · Field Orders

  1. Confirm the edition before the bench: 1703 is the first; the 1707 reissue corrected the fraction plates; the 1710 and 1715 reprints are field copies and may be stained, creased, and correct.
  2. The decimal tables and the coin tables are separate instruments. Never cross them in one sum, and never let an operator say multiplication is “just addition with an attitude” without first sending them to thread 214 of The Aden County General, where the field error slip is kept and unexplained.
  3. For surveying, take the geodesy section and the chain, in that order. The book’s rule — a vessel or a field once measured is forever measured, and is corrected only in writing — governs every ledger this manual keeps.
  4. For the fleet, the navigational astronomy section reads latitude from the sun’s height, the way the hill above Assisi reads weather from a stone: a mark on the ground, read by anyone who comes to look, corrected by nobody. The two instruments are kept on the same shelf at Entry No. CXII of the Umbrian Almanac, and they read together, and no better.
  5. If the book disagrees with the bench, the book is right, and the bench writes the correction down. This has been the standing order since 1703 and it has never once needed amending.

13.5 · The Corrections Ledger

The geodesy chapter served the Neva hydrographic station from 1703 to the edition change of 1768, and the station kept one corrections ledger of everything the Tsar’s book got right and everything the station’s own men got wrong. The ledger is a small thing, ruled, and it holds one line about a surveyor’s clerk of 1712 who struck a vessel’s record from the geodesy column because the vessel had never left the basin. The Court reads the line verbatim, in full, word for word, whenever a member asks which side of the column the corrections stand on:

“It wanted him in contempt for disrespect to the record of a crossbar that never got to sail her.”

The crossbar of the line is the survey vessel Crossbar, laid down at the Neva yard in 1709 for the station’s shallow-water surveys, which struck a submerged piling in her launch, sat in the basin on her own keel, and never once made water. Her record was entered in the geodesy column all the same, because the book’s rule has no exception for vessels that never sailed: once measured, forever measured. The station wanted the clerk in contempt for the striking-out, not the sailing; the record was the point, and the ledger kept the clerk’s demotion and the vessel’s entry side by side, in the same hand, on the same page.
