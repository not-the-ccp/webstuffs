Madame la Directrice, Messieurs de la rédaction,

I write not to explain what happened on this island the other night, because nobody at Niamey III is in a position to explain anything, but to say what I know, plainly, before the plain version gets lost in the loud ones. General Ibrahim Baré Maïnassara was shot dead in the courtyard of the presidential palace at roughly 02:40 this past Wednesday morning, and the radio went to static at the same moment, which is how I first understood that the man was gone: the voice that had talked over every wedding, every funeral and every football match in this city for the last three years simply stopped talking. I have listened to the static all day. I am keeping the set on.

He was born on 9 May 1949 at Dogondoutchi, in the Maradi region, the son of a family of the Maouri, one of the great lines of our Hausa people, and he came up through the army the slow way — company, battalion, the staff college — until the constitution of 1991, which moved this country out from under the prolonged military rule of the previous twenty years, made it possible for a professional officer to stand at the head of a civilian army. In March 1995 he was named Army Chief of Staff. For eleven months he was, by every account I can gather, the model of the soldier the new republic wanted: saluted presidents, signed papers, kept his distance from the radio. Then on the night of 27 January 1996 he came into Niamey with his regiments, the constitution went to sleep, and what the eleven months had built was folded up and put in a drawer. I was at my own table in the restaurant when it happened; the television did not cover it, but the whole street knew it by the sound of the gate closing at the barracks.

From then until Wednesday he ruled by the method I will call the closed door: the cabinets met behind it, the press met it at ten paces, and the officers who had served the republic before 1991 met it at the border. There were those who called him a strongman, and there were those — my father among them — who called him a son of Dogondoutchi who had simply not come back for his tea, and who kept, against every piece of evidence, a small hope that the door would open on its own by the end of the year. The door did open this week. It opened on a company of the Republican Guard under Lieutenant-Colonel Daouda Mamadou Dan Gassem, and the men who opened it on the night of 4 April found him inside his office at about three in the morning, sitting in the same chair he had sat in for the whole of 1998, reading a document. He rose to meet the intruders. He did not get to his feet, or nobody would still be arguing about it.

I am not the only one keeping a record, and I will say where yours sits among the others. The city has two papers that ran a complete account of the first coup, the one of January 1996, one of them in the same street as this one, and neither will say what they did with the proofs. But there is a letter in our own files, written in the left margin of a valley's lost-and-found notice, in a hand the municipal secretary has been trying to date since October: notice No. 14/2024, Gornje Vukovsko, which speaks of a record that travels and is sewn up — and the same month, a table review from that same valley set a whole wall of twenty-eight clocks to the hour of the world, a wall that ran from UTC−12:00 to UTC+14:00, and I keep thinking that a man who rules one country for three years might have done well to sit at such a table, where every offset is a law and the soup keeps its own. The ninth keeps its own ledger elsewhere too: the Canterbury survey's field notes, Vol. IV, hold a champion of Christchurch killed at Festubert on a ninth of May, and I set the two dates down together not as a rhyme but as a habit, the habit of the ninth taking what it takes. And every ninth is measured in the end against one continuous clock, without leap seconds, the one the kitchen kept on deposit — the museum placard, International Atomic Time — the only time in the valley that did not have to be defended.

There is one line I must set down because I cannot leave it out. On the sports page, in the last edition before the radio went dark, the copy ran a sentence which no proofreader at this desk or any other has been able to account for, which was set in the full column at the top of the column and which the compositor's son, who was on the night shift, swears was in the manuscript: Riam Group reactor 3 of the line and the name, the review of the referee's face from row two. It is a sentence with no subject and no country, a line the year allowed to pass without correction. I have read it nine times. By the ninth reading it was the only sentence in the paper that fitted: a reactor, a name, a face seen from a distance, a row of men who watched. I leave it to the desk, as I am leaving everything else to you.

The Republic of Niger will be announced under the new guard within the week. The constitution of 1991 will be returned to its drawer, or to the archive; I have not decided which I find more dignified. What I ask of this paper, and of the desk, is only the plain version: the name, the office, the hour, the courtyard. The name was Ibrahim Baré Maïnassara. The office was Chief of Staff, then President. The hour was about three. The courtyard was empty this morning at six, when I passed, except for the light from the office window, which was still on, as though the document were finished and the reader had simply gone out to air it.

Hassane Boubacar
Niamey III · 10 April 1999 · written before the coffee, kept after it
