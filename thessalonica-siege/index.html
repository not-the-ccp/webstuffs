The Venetian Desk · Apology · Entered in the year the city changed masters again

To the City of
Thessalonica

A letter of apology on behalf of the Most Serene Republic, written at the desk of the archive in the year 1912, four hundred and eighty-two years after the surrender of 29 March 1430.

To the city, and to the people who keep it: the Republic writes, and the Republic is late.

On the news that reached the lagoon in the autumn of 1912 — that Thessalonica, after the battle at Kilynar and the Ottoman withdrawal from the Chalkidike, was held again by Greek hands — the desk of the archive did what a desk does when it has no other business: it opened the file. The file is long. The file is not an excuse. It is the account of a debt this letter is written to acknowledge, in the name of the Republic of Venice, for the city it held for seven years and then failed.

Let the arithmetic stand first, because the file keeps it plain. The city had already been in Ottoman hands once, from 1387 to 1403, when the empire of Bayezid pressed hard on everything that still flew the Byzantine cross. Then the empire itself was broken at Ankara in 1402 — the capital in the centre, the giving over of names — and Thessalonica, with the rest of what remained, returned to Byzantine rule in the aftermath. It held that rule, thin and expensive, until 1422, when the Byzantine court at Constantinople took in Mustafa Çelebi, a rival pretender, and set him against his own brother, the sultan. Murad II came down from the north to punish the choice. He did not need to breach the walls to make the point: the city had no men to send out, no powder to burn, and no treasury behind it deep enough for a second winter.

It was then that its ruler, Andronikos Palaiologos, did the thing a despot of that name has to do with a city he cannot defend. In September 1423 he handed Thessalonica over to the Republic of Venice. The Republic accepted. Here the apology begins, and it begins with acceptance. We took a city at the end of a chain of promises we did not have the length to keep.

What we did with those seven years is entered, and it is not a clean page. The garrison was small and mostly Venetian; the walls were repaired where the 1422 artillery had scored them; the harbour under the great tower was kept, the churches were kept, and a census of the people was kept, because the census is the first thing a Republic does and the last thing it remembers. Between the first siege and the last, there was a long quiet — the kind a city keeps with a garrison that is paid, taxed, and watched. Our two brothers, Tommaso and Marco Contarini, kept the household register of the station through it. They were the ones who came back from the quiet with the file the desk apologises for, and it is their register that carries the line this letter could not otherwise have found, copied word for word from the margin of the household book:

Record for each brother — a family recipe carried across the Maqueda Channel — it needs only the water.

— the Contarini household register, Thessalonica station; the Maqueda Channel being the narrow water our crossing came over before the lagoon, and the brothers’ rule being that no recipe of the family is to be entered under one name only

The brothers kept the rule to the end. When the last siege came — Murad II returned in October 1429 and set the city under a ring of camp-fires that held it through the winter — the larder of the station was entered, rationed, and finally exhausted in the order the register lists it: flour, salt, oil, bread. The recipe that needs only the water stood at the foot of that list, the one provision the family still commanded, and each brother kept his copy of the line in his own hand so that the record should survive under two names and not one. On 29 March 1430 the city surrendered. The garrison sailed out of the harbour with what the ships would carry; the register went with Marco; Tommaso stayed, and the file keeps his last entry for one day only, which is how the file keeps it, and which is what this letter is for.

After 1430 the city was in Ottoman hands, and it stayed so for five hundred years — through every change the file can no longer follow from the lagoon — until the year this letter is written. The Republic does not claim those centuries as its failure, because it had no share in them, but it does claim the seven years, the acceptance of September 1423, the small garrison, the exhausted larder, and the surrender it did not make good. A city that has been kept once and failed once does not need the account; the debt is what the file was for.

So the Republic writes to the city in 1912, through whatever office still keeps the desk, and says plainly what the file has said all along: the taking was a kindness meant to the Republic; the keeping was not equal to it; and the line from the brothers’ register stands where it stands — the only recipe that survived the siege, kept under two names, needing only the water. The desk enters this letter against the file, in the same hand it has always used, and leaves the balance where a Republic may leave it: acknowledged, and paid out in the only currency left.

Given at the desk of the archive, in the year the city came home to Greek hands.
— For the Most Serene Republic, by order of the file

The Venetian Desk keeps its shelf with the Webstuffs front page, where the other desks — the registers, the ledgers, the etiquette halls — keep the same rule: what the record could not save, the letter says plainly.
