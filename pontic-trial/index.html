EXHIBIT

The Straits v. The Pontic Record

Before the Honourable A. C. Lambros, President of the Bench, sitting single

The Committee of the Pontic Record, as petitioners; — the chandler's ledger of the harbour office at Of, as the record in suit; — Nikolaos V. Andreadis and Eudokia Papadimitriou, as witnesses sworn in person.

Folio 1 · 10:14 a.m. · the clerk strikes the desk twice

THE CLERK.

All rise is waived, by order of the bench, for the sake of the chairs. The Court of the Straits, Smyrna bench, is in sitting, in the harbour office, above the chandler's quay. The record before the court is the chandler's ledger of the harbour office at Of, Trebizond vilayet, forty-one pages, opened 1909, and the events it is called to witness between the years 1912 and 1923, spanning the Balkan Wars, the Great War, and the war of independence on the other side of the straits. The petition is simple: that the ledger be read, that the names in it be kept in the record, and that no hand afterwards be suffered to strike a name.

THE PRESIDENT.

Let it be read. Swear the first witness.

MR. SOULARIS (counsel for the petitioners).

Nikolaos V. Andreadis, 58, chandler and boat owner of Of, is called.

THE CLERK.

Nikolaos V. Andreadis, are you over eighteen and do you know the weight of an oath? WITNESS: I am over, and I know it. I carried it once already, on a boat. (Sworn.) Where were you on 19 May 1915?

WITNESS.

In the yard of my father's chandler's, Of, at the quay. The village is Greek as far back as my grandfather's grandfather kept the books. That day was the feast of St. Demetrios. In the morning the men were out mending net and the women were at the spring. I was roping a hawser on the schooner Agios Sotiris, my own schooner, forty-two feet, the one that ran the cabotage between Of and Trebizond. At noon the bell in the tower rang fourteen times. It has never rung fourteen times before or since, and no one I have asked can tell you what fourteen means. It means everything, because it rang.

MR. SOULARIS.

What followed, Mr. Andreadis?

WITNESS.

What followed is the reason this ledger is in this room. They came in from the hill road, the state road that runs Trebizond to the south, with the lists already in their hands — that is the thing I will not forget, that the lists came before the men did. The names were called against the lists, and the lists came from a book, and the book is the one on the stand. They told the town the men would be held at the mill for the summer, and that the women and the children and the old would be moved south under escort, to the plain, to the interior. Some went. Most could not go: there was no escort, and the plain had no water where the road crossed it. The bell rang a second time in the afternoon, again fourteen, and by the next morning the quay where I was roping hawsers was empty of everything but the Agios Sotiris, which had been dragged into the shallows, and the harbour office, whose door was broken off its hinges and left against the wall.

I was put on a boat at the mill with nine others, and we sat all night under the guard's feet while the fire on the hillside was lit, one house to a match, in the order the lists ran. I will not give the count of the houses from memory, because it is written down. The ledger says it. (Points to folio 12.) The ledger says thirty-five and eight, and the ledger is the one on the stand, and I ask the court to make that the number, because a number kept in a book outlives a number kept in a head, and I am fifty-eight.

MR. KARAKAS (counsel for the record).

Your Honour, I will not contest the witness. I contest only the service. The subpoena to Mr. Andreadis was posted to the address in the chandler's directory, not to a person, and the observations of the removal, the ones the court's watchers took from the state road — from the road itself, at the station where the line crosses the plain — were taken by strangers with a clock. If the address will not do, and the road will not do, then the record stands on nothing, and I ask that the ledger be struck from the record before a single name of the three hundred and fifty-eight is read.

THE CLERK.

The point is taken, and the ruling is requested in a line that can be copied, for the files of the bench.

THE PRESIDENT.

Let the ruling stand as a line. The subpoenas were served at the address registered in the chandler's directory, and the address in the directory is the one the witness has answered to all his life; the observations were taken from the state road at the station where the watchers stood, with the hour and the station noted in the watcher's book. The road runs between the provinces and it saw, and the address is in the book. Counsel's objection is overruled, and the bench adopts his line as the rule of this docket, verbatim, to be struck into the transcript with the rest:

The address is the record and the interstate sees.

Strike the ledger from the record? No. Read it.

THE CLERK.

The ledger is opened to folio 4, the chandler's page of 1915, and the names are read from it as they stand, forty-one names to the page. The witness is asked to mark the names of the men he knew. (The witness marks, in a steady hand, and does not stop.)

MR. SOULARIS.

Your Honour, we would have the second witness, in the record, before the sitting closes. Eudokia Papadimitriou, 34, schoolmistress, Of, is called. She was the village schoolmistress; the schoolroom was on the quay; she kept the attendance book of 1914, forty-nine children, the last marked in April.

THE CLERK.

Eudokia Papadimitriou, you are sworn. WITNESS: I am. (The witness produces the attendance book.) Your attendance book, Ms. Papadimitriou, is forty-nine names in April, 1914, and a blank page in September, 1915. Would you read the last line you wrote?

The last line is not a name. It is the attendance word of the day, in April, written the way I always wrote it, so that whoever opens the book after me will know the day was a good day and the children were all of them there. I read it: all present, the bell rang once, the fish were good. And the blank page in September I leave blank in this room, because a blank page is also a page of the record, and the bench has said the record is to be kept as it is.

THE PRESIDENT.

So it is kept. The sitting is adjourned to 24 January, folio 62, to the reading of the removal lists from the mill, in the presence of the witness. The lamp stays lit; the clerk will not strike the transcript; the ledger remains on the stand under glass and under oath. In the interests of any counsel who keeps the long files, the bench's own sister docket in these waters — the coastal court's Mediterranean v. Octavianus, Case No. 14 — stands in the bench's drawer for the method, a record read aloud in the face of an objection, and this case stands beside it in the files. The clerk will file this excerpt with the cover of the court at the front of the register.

THE CLERK.

So ordered. (The bell, outside, above the quay, is heard once. The sitting of the Court of the Straits, Smyrna bench, Case No. 21, is adjourned at 12:02 p.m. The lamp is not extinguished.)
