17.1The Podium

Georg Solti, born Gýrgy Stern in Budapest on 21 October 1912, was a Hungarian and British orchestral and operatic conductor of the kind this manual keeps in the ledger: one who stands at the podium, holds the bearing, and does not look down at his feet. He studied at the Liszt Academy of Music in Budapest with Béla Bartók, Leó Weiner, and Ernő Dohnányi — three names the reader should write in the margin and not lose. Bartók taught him the pulse of the country, Weiner taught him the line, and Dohnányi taught him the weight of a chord. He made his first appearance as a conductor in Budapest in 1933, before he had turned twenty-one.

17.2The Rehearsal

In the 1930s he was a répétiteur at the Hungarian State Opera, playing the orchestra from the piano while the singers learned their places, and in 1937 he worked at the Salzburg Festival for Arturo Toscanini, who made him his assistant. His career was interrupted by the rise of the Nazis' influence on Hungarian politics, and being Jewish, he fled the increasingly harsh Hungarian anti-Jewish legislation in 1938, leaving Budapest by train with little more than the scores. He waited in Switzerland, then crossed to London, where Covent Garden took him on, and he learned to conduct in a language that was not his first.

17.3The House

He conducted at the Bavarian State Opera in Munich and at the opera in Frankfurt, then at the Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, in London, where he was musical director from 1961 to 1971 and raised the house to pitch after its years of quiet. In 1969 he became music director of the Chicago Symphony Orchestra and held the post for twenty-two seasons, until 1991, building the orchestra into one that could play anything written, loudly and exactly. He recorded the complete Ring cycle in Vienna between 1958 and 1966, the Beethoven symphonies in Chicago and Vienna, and a list of operas so long the clerk of this chapter stopped copying it at item forty-three. Chicago is where he is buried, and where the orchestra still keeps his chair empty on opening night.

17.4Standing Orders

The following orders are read at every rehearsal kept under this chapter. They apply to the podium, the pit, and the control room.

  1. The downbeat is given once. The orchestra may play the second one too, if it must, but the beat belongs to the conductor.
  2. Tempo is taken from the breath of the singers, never from the watch.
  3. The score is opened at bar one and closed at the final double bar; nothing between them is skipped without a vote.
  4. The brass section is reminded daily that it is not the whole orchestra.
  5. The hall is filled before the lights come down, or it is not filled at all.
  6. The camera, holding the overall control of the material’s generation does not claim the tile. Read it in the control room: the eye of the room runs the generation of the material, but the tile — the master lacquer on the lathe, the cut that becomes the record — is not the camera’s to claim. The machine records; the conductor decides; neither takes a bow.

17.5Provisions

Each party at the podium carries: one baton of rosewood, weighted at the handle, twenty-eight centimetres long; one full score, pencil-marked; one metronome set between sixty and one hundred and sixty-eight; one tuning fork, A-440; and one red pencil, 2B, for the cuts. The metronome on this page is set to seventy-two, the speed of a walking beat and of the second movement of the Seventh. The conductor wears black, or something so close to it that the orchestra sees the hands and not the clothes.
