The Miller Clock

Keeper of this log: H. P. Rowan, third keeper of the boathouse bench, amateur horologist by the hour and by permission

One wall clock, thirty inches square, brass movement, one gong
Rated to run true for 365 days, then to stop itself and stay stopped at 11:52 · build season 2016–2017

STOPS
22 OCT 1844

Entry 01 · 14 Jan 2017 · Weather: frost, bench dry

The clock in this log is built to do one thing and to do it publicly: run true for a year, and then, by a mechanism of its own, stop and stay stopped at the hour and minute when, on the morning of 22 October 1844, a very large number of New England farm families looked east and found that the sky had no intention of opening. Its subject is Adventism, the branch of Protestant Christianity that in the 1830s believed in the imminent Second Coming — the “Second Advent” — of Jesus Christ, and I have decided, after two winters of reading, that it is best taught by a machine that can demonstrate the whole of it in twelve hours. Attendance and weather only, as the circuit keeps its ledger; the rest will come out of the build.

Entry 02 · 2 Feb 2017 · Weather: rain, counted as percussion

The history, so far as it touches this bench. In the 1830s, in the United States, in the thick of what the country was then calling the Second Great Awakening, a Baptist preacher named William Miller — born at Hopestown, New York, about 1805, and a clockmaker by his second trade, which I regard as at once a warning and an endorsement — first publicly shared the belief that the Second Coming would occur at a date he could actually put on a calendar: some point between 1843 and 1844. Miller was not a large man and he was not a loud one; what he had was the arithmetic of the book of Daniel worked out in the open, chalked on whatever board was to hand, and his followers took to being called Millerites the way other men's followers take to being called after their masters. The arithmetic was reworked once, and the window closed to a single morning: the 22nd of October, 1844. The clock is built to the morning, not to the window; windows are for weather.

Entry 03 · 25 Mar 2017 · Weather: thaw, drips under the beams

Parts. The escapement is an anchor, brass, cut from a gate hinge because the hinge had already survived two hundred winters and I did not trust my own steel. The gong is one, struck 303 times, because the Millerites counted — they counted days, verses, and one another — and because 303 is the number of strokes the bell of the boathouse gave when the door opened in the storm of February 1916, which the ledger of the Tufton circuit records as its wet month, one undecipherable entry and all: Methodism — Field Manual FM-4, Chapter 4. That chapter is my method for the methodical side of the build; it sits under the bench and I do not take it into the rain. The bellows are secondhand from the boathouse shelf, and they came with the 1915 inventory line preserved word for word: “A clerk at the boathouse, with the habit of examining the roses.” The clerk was never named. The roses were five that day, the bellows were sound, and I have taken the line as the best job description ever written for the person who will maintain this instrument.

Entry 04 · 30 Apr 2017 · Weather: dry, the good kind

What actually happened, which is the point of the whole machine. The morning came and went. The church name for the event is the Great Disappointment; Miller and the friends who stayed with him after it — because Miller, by all the accounts, was not the one who broke first — preferred the word sifting, and went on arguing in print that nothing had failed, only the reading. The movement did not die. It split, in the way that families split when the last of the money is spent: out of one Millerite ancestor came a number of groups that still exist and still hold different doctrines from one another, all of them descendants of the same arithmetic. There are the Seventh-day Adventists, who moved the sabbath to Saturday and kept the urgency; the Advent Christian Church, which kept the Lord's Supper; the Christadelphians, who did not; the Seventh Day Baptists, who kept the day and the immersion. And there is one name the public memory kept longest of all: Ellen G. White, a schoolteacher of Portland, Maine, of poor eyesight and unshakable penmanship, who went on to publish some forty volumes and to become the best known leader and writer of the family, her letters still setting doctrine a generation after she was gone. Every one of these groups holds, in some shape, the one point the clock is built around: that the Second Advent is imminent. A thing can be imminent for a very long time; after 1844 it becomes a kind of weather.

Entry 05 · 13 Jun 2017 · Weather: wind off the water

Tuning. The bellows are tuned to the third line of Blaenwern — the line with the long note — because the boathouse belongs to the same parish that keeps that tune in its lost-and-found ledger, and the tune and the clock are the same argument in two keys: a long note is only as long as the one making it. I file the gongs while I hum it, and the long note holds at its full length; the parish says the congregation will find its own way to the close, and I have found the same to be true of a tuning fork. The notice is No. 47 in the parish ledger, still open: Blaenwern — Lost & Found Notice No. 47, Tufton Parish. The gong will strike 303 and then hold, the way the third line holds. I am not ashamed of the shape it gives the machine.

Entry 06 · 22 Oct 2017 · Weather: still, no intention of opening

The clock ran its year to the day. At the hour it stopped itself, struck 303, and held. I have left it stopped. It will be wound again for 2018 and allowed to stop again, and so on, which is the whole doctrine in a maintenance schedule. Every time a mechanism of mine fails before its morning I say, well, it’s 1845, and the bench has come to treat that as the correct weather. The log will be kept with the rest of the circuit’s papers at the boathouse — face up, third drawer, next to FM-4, with the bearing corrected — and returned to the drawer on finishing, with the roses examined in order.
