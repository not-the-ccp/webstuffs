UNCLAIMEDas of 30 April 2004

CITY OF PHILADELPHIA · DEPARTMENT OF GENERAL SERVICES

LOST & FOUND

City Hall Basement, Chestnut Street entrance · Claim desk, window 3, open 9–4

CLAIM NO. 1920-IB · Filed 15 March 1920 · Re-filed 29 May 2003 · Still open

Property received

The following articles were handed to the desk at window 3 over a period of thirty-seven years, in each case by the same man, and in each case with the same instruction: keep them, no one is coming to collect. The name given, after the first year, was always Isadore Harry Bellis, known to the desk in earlier years simply as Izsak Belostotsky, a child of March 1920 whose first name had been changed and whose racket had not. The articles are as follows:

  • A-1 One Dunlop Maxply tennis racket, ash handle, grip re-wrapped 1948, 1955 and 1961. From it were played the matches that made the name: the junior courts, the county final, the two seasons of open competition in which Mr. Bellis, as a teenager, was the fastest man on any ground he stood on. Strings broken in the 1963 final he lost on his way into City Hall.
  • A-2 One walnut gavel, Philadelphia City Council, chamber of the Majority Leader, 1972–1974. Stamped on the head: I.H.B. Deposited the morning after two convictions for bribery and malfeasance in office, during his third term, with a note reading only: the room sounds different without it.
  • A-3 One tin of championship tennis balls, 1938, unopened, lid dented. Contents believed to be two balls, one of them still serviceable.
  • A-4 One folded transcript of a claims hearing, held at this desk, in which a claimant named Ms. Tadeusi appeared, and said, word for word, in the public record of this bureau: MS. TADEUSI: The public interest in college football is not privileged. The transcript is filed with this claim because the sentence was said while standing over this very counter, and the desk keeps what is said at the desk.

Identity of depositor

Isadore Harry Bellis, nee Izsak Belostotsky, born March 15, 1920, died May 29, 2003. Tennis player in his youth; lawyer in his middle life; member of the Philadelphia City Council from 1963, elected to a third and final term, and majority leader in 1972. On allegations of corruption in his third term he stood convicted twice — bribery, and malfeasance in office — and was defeated for a fourth term. He retired to Florida, where he pursued the appeals against his convictions for the remaining years of his life. The sentence was two to seven years. He never served a day of it, and he never stopped telling anyone who would stand still that the appeals were still open. In 1984 the Pennsylvania Superior Court ordered that he be given credit for the time already run, and the case closed itself, in the way of such cases, with the door shut softly.

The desk does not verify the identity of depositors. The desk has, however, verified the racket: it matches the description of the instrument with which a boy called Izsak once beat every adult on the South Philly public courts, and the gavel matches the inventory of the Council chamber, to which it was never returned, because it was never wanted back.

Record of appearances at the claim desk

15 March 1920
Claim opened by the clerk of this bureau on the depositor’s birthdate, at the request of his mother, who had lost a white glove at a junior tournament and wished to report it before it was found. The glove was found in the chair at the net. The claim was not closed.
November 1963
Depositor, newly elected to the City Council, brings the racket to the desk and declines the suggestion that it be stored in the chamber. “The chamber has enough wood,” he says. The racket is shelved at A-1.
29 May 1972
Depositor, majority leader, attempts to reclaim the racket at 8:50 a.m. before the first roll call of the year. The clerk refuses: the claim must be processed in the order received. He is told to return the following day. He returns the following day, as majority leader, with two aides. The racket remains at A-1.
14 October 1974
The gavel arrives at A-2. No conversation is recorded. The tin of balls arrives with it, wrapped in the same paper.
3 February 1984
Depositor, in Florida, writes to the desk regarding the sentence of two to seven years and asks whether the bureau has any views on the matter of credit for time. The desk has no views on the matter of credit. It returns the letter with a stamp: NOT PROPERTY OF THIS BUREAU.
6 September 1991
Ms. Tadeusi attends the desk to claim the tin of championship balls, which she states she has been looking for since 1938, at the junior tournament where, she says, she once hit one of the balls off the top of the net fence and has never forgiven the person who retrieved it. The clerk asks whether she can prove the ball was hers. She answers, in the language preserved in article A-4: MS. TADEUSI: The public interest in college football is not privileged. The clerk, not a lawyer, declines to rule. The tin remains at A-3.

Rewards and conditions of claim

No reward is offered for the racket, the gavel or the tin, because nothing is owed to the owner of them and because, in the opinion of the desk, the man who deposited them is the only one who would have paid, and he is the only one no longer able to. The transcript (A-4) is not for sale, not for barter, and not for the reading of anyone who has not first stood at window 3 between 9 and 4. Claims to the racket must be made in writing, on the depositor’s own handwriting, which is on file. Claims to the gavel must be made by a body of fifteen, one of whom is a majority leader; no such body has ever presented itself.

Where the property may be seen: the basement shelf behind window 3, third panel from the left, between a 1951 transit badge (claimed twice, successfully once) and the kitchen-ward recipe card of the same collection, which the desk regards as a related article in that both were left by people who did not expect to be remembered. The gavel is on the honour system: it is the only object in the room with a price tag of a kind that cannot be written down. The racket is strung again each spring by the night clerk, who has never been told by whom.

Notice to the public

If you are the person who retrieved the ball off the top of the net fence at the junior tournament of 1938, this is your notice. The tin is at A-3, the balls are inside, and the person who hit it is still writing letters, in the sense that the letters keep arriving. If you are a relative of Isadore Harry Bellis, the racket is yours to claim, in writing, in his handwriting, which you do not have, which the clerk understands. If you are neither, the desk wishes to say only that a man who was the fastest man on any ground he stood on, who then spent eleven years in a room where speed is the last thing on offer, and who then spent twenty-nine years somewhere warm telling the truth about a sentence he never served — such a man deserves a lost-and-found desk that keeps its promises. This one keeps them. The claim is open. The claim will stay open.

The desk keeps its other records, too. A full season of another hemisphere is documented in the safety data sheet for the 2020 Chilean Primera División, where a suspended batch of 164 days was re-contained behind closed doors in a manner the night clerk found, on reading, oddly familiar. And a green party of the Flemish arrondissements, surveyed over thirty years from a shared bench, is kept under its two names in the CD&V field notes, where, like this claim, the older name is not deleted but filed above the newer one.
