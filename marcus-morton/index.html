Taunton, Massachusetts

A One-Day Tour in the Life of Justice & Governor Marcus Morton
December 19, 1784 – February 6, 1864

WELCOME TO TAUNTON — BIRTHPLACE OF A GOVERNOR — 1839: HE WON WITH EXACTLY ONE VOTE — 1842: HE WON WITH A NARROW VICTORY — 1825: ACTING GOVERNOR FOR SEVERAL MONTHS — 15 YEARS ON THE SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT — SEE THE WHOLE THING IN A SINGLE DAY — THE OFFICE IS NOT RESPONSIBLE FOR FANS OF EDWARD EVERETT —

► Welcome, Traveller

Taunton is a small city in Bristol County, Massachusetts, and for one man it was the whole stage. Marcus Morton was born here on December 19, 1784 (which, in the old style of reckoning, was still December 8 — the almanacs of the day disagreed by thirteen days, and this tour honours both), grew up in a brick house near the canals, studied law with the same local lawyers who would spend the rest of their lives quoting him, and never really left. He served fifteen years as an associate justice of the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, sat several months as Acting Governor after the death of William Eustis in 1825, and finally — after years of running unsuccessfully as a Democrat for governor against men with longer biographies — took the seat in 1839, held it, lost it, won it back, and died in this town in 1864, which is the whole of the story and none of it is far from anywhere on the circuit below. The Massachusetts Democratic Party was highly factionalized in his day, the Whigs had Edward Everett, and the arithmetic was merciless; the tour makes the arithmetic its centerpiece, because that is what this town was good at.

One day is enough. Two days is if you count the ferry to Providence, and the office will not count it.

► The Circuit — Six Stops, One Day

8:00 — Stop 1: The Birthplace, Pleasant Street

The house at the corner of Pleasant Street and the canal road. Marcus Morton came into the world on December 19, 1784, in the Federal style, with a fireplace still cold by the time he reached his majority. The brick is the original brick. The office has counted; do not count again. He read law in Taunton offices and was admitted to the bar as a young man, which the plaque puts at 1806 and the plaque is not going to lose another argument.

9:30 — Stop 2: The Bench, Fifteen Years

The courthouse on Court Street, where Morton sat as an associate justice of the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court from 1810 to 1825 — fifteen years, every one of them reported, most of them in a hand the reporter had to ask him to hold still for. In the archive room you may see a docket of canal cases: Taunton was the canal town, and the canals put money on the table and causes of action in the brief. A judge who knew both ledgers was worth two.

11:00 — Stop 3: The Acting Governor’s Several Months, 1825

When Governor William Eustis died in office in 1825, the chair did not stay empty. Morton, the senior figure the legislature could reach, took it — several months of it, a short and stern spell of acting governorship that the town marks at the State House steps, where the flag flies at half staff in the diorama and the diorama does not explain itself. He governed, he left, and he went back to law, and the town has kept the date on the corner of the square as if it were a holiday, which, in its way, it is.

12:30 — Stop 4: The Long Race, and the One Vote, 1839

This is the stop the town built the brochure for. For a decade the Democrats nominated Morton and the state returned no: the party was factionalized into more pieces than a barbell takes plates, and the Whigs had a candidate with a name like a quotation. Then came 1839, and the canvass, and the result that the office still reproduces in its best type: Morton carried the 1839 election with exactly the number of votes required for a majority win over Edward Everett — 40,792 to 40,791, a difference of one. One vote, counted in Taunton. The town keeps the original ballot page in a glass case on Court Street, and it is the most visited object in Bristol County, ahead of the canal, ahead of the brick house, ahead of every church steeple in sight.

14:00 — Stop 5: The Losing and the Winning, 1840–1842

It was not, of course, over. Morton lost the elections of 1840 and 1841, both to the same Whig who had come within one vote of losing to him; the diorama of the losing years is painted in the correct shade of disappointed blue. In 1842 he won again, in a narrow victory the party chalked up on the wall of the hall on Court Street in figures you can still read if you look up: a margin of 102 votes, which the Democrats of 1842 decided was the margin of a prophet. He served his two terms, and the town serves the memory of them at this stop, with the ledger open.

15:30 — Stop 6: The Farewell, February 6, 1864

Justice, governor, lawyer, son of the corner of Pleasant Street — Marcus Morton died at home in Taunton on February 6, 1864, eighty years and a few months old, and is buried in the town cemetery a short walk from the square. The headstone gives the date plainly, without the old-style alternative, and the office has left it alone. The circuit ends here, at the gate, with the canal visible past the elms. It is, the office hopes, the day you will keep.

► Practical Particulars

  • Fares. One shell from the flats, or a number, at the gate on Court Street. The office does not repeat the fare at the window.
  • Weather. The tour runs on the weather the Atlantic allows. The glass case does not care.
  • Footwear. The canal road is brick. Bring the kind of shoe that has opinions.
  • Party limits. Large parties are turned away at the archive room, where the docket is one ledger and the ledger is one table.
  • Dates. All dates as printed. Where the old style and the new differ by thirteen days, both are printed, and the office has stopped deciding.

► The Reading Rack at the Office

The office keeps a small rack of reading matter beside the gate, free to any traveller with a day and a destination. Current this season: the canal survey of 1828, a folded map of Bristol County in the correct old blue, the docket of canal cases (a rubbing, not the docket), and — misfiled under M, between the map and the docket, and not to be removed from the rack — The Mars Post · Newsletter of the colony. Beside it, folded into the same drawer, the rack holds The Seventh Bell — Newsletter No. 7 of the Correspondents of the Seventh, a small club’s running correspondence on the seventh president, whose one shell, or a number, matches the office rate to the cent and whose stone, the office confirms, gives the date plainly. With the map of Bristol County the office has also filed, this season only, a single card transcribed from a French museum registry describing a gray draught horse of the Boulonnais coast, the White Marble Horse — The Boulonnais, Exhibit No. 12 — the one item in the rack that the office will not discuss and will point at, gladly, in any language. The office will not discuss either item; both are available to read on the spot, like everything else, and the rack has not lost a page since 1901.
