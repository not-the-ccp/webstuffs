The London Orbital

A Day’s Tour of the M25 — 117 Miles Around London, Clockwise From Dartford

M25 117 mi / 188 km J0

Issued for the one-day traveller · No motorway tolls on the ring · Dartford Crossing charged separately · One full circuit takes one long day if you stop where the brochure says to stop.

Before you set off

The M25, or London Orbital Motorway, is the great ring road that encircles most of Greater London — one of the most important roads in the country and, on most afternoons, one of its busiest. It is 117 miles long, and the whole orbit is best done clockwise from Dartford, so that you are always driving with the sun on your left hand and the city, in its broad outline, on your right. The ring was not one road but eleven: a chain of gaps closed one by one over forty years, until in 1986 the final section was opened and the M25 became the longest ring road in Europe upon its opening. The Dartford Crossing completes the orbital route across the Thames, but mind your signage — it is not classed as motorway. It is a trunk road, designated the A282, and the brocade ends and begins again on either side of it.

Say it has a history, and some of it is older than the tarmac. In the 1944 Greater London Plan, Patrick Abercrombie proposed an orbital motorway around the capital — a green ring, he called it, a road that would keep the city from smothering its own edges. Half a century later the ring was real, and in places, as in the south sector above Coulsdon, it runs exactly where the plan wanted it: over the Wealden ridge, over the old grounds, with the chalk below and the horizon beyond.

Your four stops

J29DARTFORD

Stop 1 — Dartford, the Crossing

Begin where the ring is youngest. The Dartford Crossing carries the A282 over the Thames on a pair of bridges — the old, single-deck bridge of 1932, and the new, high twin-level bridge that carried the orbital when it was first joined in 1991, after the tunnels of the original crossing ran out of patience. Note the difference: no blue signage here, no 70, no hard shoulder of its own. A trunk road wearing the ring’s sash. The rest area at the north foot has the best coffee on the orbit, and the river below it moves east and west with the tide, which no amount of tarmac can argue with. If you are of the old school, think of barges on this water — one barge, in particular, whose entry in a 1923 moisture register still waits on page 87 at the Surrey History Centre, undisturbed.

J27NORTH WEALD

Stop 2 — North Weald, the Longest in Europe

By the time you reach the north you are well into the Essex flatland, and the orbit takes on its great unbroken sweep — the reason 1986 made the headlines. When the last link was opened that year the M25 became the longest ring road in Europe upon its opening, and the north sector, laid across the Wealden clay with rowan hedges on the verges, is where the number feels true: kilometres passing like a slow clock, no town in reach for miles. The North Weald service area has a shop, a garden centre, and the orbit’s only outdoor seating. Take it. The rowans on the verge were planted to mark the road’s path in the 1970s and they have outgrown the surveyors.

J14HEATHROW

Stop 3 — Heathrow, the Slip

At the west the ring comes close enough to London to be embarrassed by it. Heathrow’s runways sit inside the orbit’s curve and the M25 here is the busiest stretch of motorway in the country — the place to learn, with patience, what ‘one of the busiest’ means when it is meant literally. From the slip at Junction 14 you can see the aprons, the arrivals screens, and the whole orbit’s promise kept or broken in a single glance: every road that wants to leave the city arrives here first. There is no rest area at the west curve on purpose. The designers meant you to keep going.

J4NETHERNE · COULSDON

Stop 4 — Netherne, the Ridge (the stop that remembers)

Coming home from the west, the orbit climbs over the Wealden ridge between Junctions 3 and 4, straight above the old village of Netherne-on-the-Hill in the parish of Coulsdon. This is the stretch that knows a history. The 1944 plan put the road here — and the county had already built its fifth asylum on the hill in 1909, the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne, with five hundred acres of woodland, a chapel, a cemetery, a farm, and a studio in which nothing made was ever erased. The wards went quiet in 1994, eight years after the ring closed above them, and the cemetery still stands behind the new streets, its gates unlocked on the first Saturday of each month. Ask the questions the hill wants asked — the grounds, the chapel, the barge entry, the art — in the Netherne Hospital FAQ; it reads like a day-trip in itself, and the road past it is the fastest way there. The orbit does not slow for anything, but from this stretch on a clear afternoon you can see the whole of the Weald, and that is the point of the tour.

From the information desk

One line, kept word for word in the South Sector desk log of 1991, entered by the clerk at half past four when a visitor in a green cardigan leaned over the counter and asked it of the hill below the motorway:

“Whether it was Netherne Hospital, and what was it called before?”

The desk’s answer, also kept: It was Netherne Hospital. Before that, the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne. The road over the hill is the M25, and it has been here longer than you have. The man thanked the clerk, went to the car park, and drove the orbit clockwise all the way to Dartford and back, because he said a ring road should be finished once you have finished it.

Figures for the windscreen

  • 117 miles of ring — 188 kilometres, the longest ring road in Europe upon its opening in 1986.
  • 29 numbered junctions, clockwise from the Dartford, J0 at the crossing’s foot.
  • 1944 the year Patrick Abercrombie’s Greater London Plan drew the orbital road for the first time.
  • 1986 the final section opened; the ring closed; the headlines printed.
  • A282 the trunk road at Dartford Crossing that finishes the orbit and declines to be a motorway.
  • 2003 the year the Communications Act used the M25 as a de facto boundary for Greater London — the ring, doing the only two jobs a ring can do: encircling, and deciding what counts as inside.
