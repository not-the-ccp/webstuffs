The Record at the Courthouse, Sunflower County

Excerpt — pages 114–121 — do not correct — the record stands as reported — Money, Sunflower County, Mississippi — Delta Division —

FOLIO 114 — Monday, the fourteenth day of September, 1955, the court sitting in the Sunflower County courthouse, the courtroom on the north side of the second floor, the bench facing the gallery and the jury box facing the window with the blinds half down.

THE CLERK. The court is now in session. Court of Sunflower County, the Honorable C. W. Wilkins presiding, with Judge W. A. Conley. Case number two hundred and forty-five upon the docket. The defendants, William Edward Tillman and James Wesley Milam, are in the custody of the county. The jury of the county, drawn and impanelled, is in the box. Please report.

THE COURT. Stand, please. This is the case of the State of Mississippi against the two men for the death of a child of fourteen years, Emmett Louis Till, of Chicago, Illinois, whose body was pulled from the Tallahatchie River on the twenty-ninth of August, 1955, by a patrol of the sheriff's department of Sunflower County, near the village of Money. The State has rested. Counsel for the defendants, on this matter of the drawer, and on what the drawer kept, may proceed. The stenographer, take it down.

THE STENOGRAPHER. I am taking it down. The record will stand as reported.

FOLIO 115 — The State calls Witness No. 7, the postmistress of the Money post office, who takes the stand at the half-hour, sits a moment, and looks toward the gallery. Her name is entered in the record as given.

THE ATTORNEY. Witness, did you keep a small drawer at the post office, under the counter, for the keeping of parts and odd ends?

THE WITNESS. I did, sir. A small drawer, brass front, under the left side of the counter, where the scales had been before they were moved to the weighing room. It is where the odd ends were kept. The postmistress's drawer, where it was cut from the barrel for the trap has been kept without a shot blocked on the one who does.

[ The record keeps the witness's last sentence exactly as reported. No correction was called, by the State, by the defense, or by the bench. The clerk has entered the line as it came, and this folio prints it so. See also the excerpt from the verbatim record, Case No. 1726 of 2024, where an uncorrected line is kept to the same rule, and the field manual chapter on the public wrong, which keeps one strange line of a transfer slip word for word beside its example of the crime.

THE COURT. Witness, the bench would ask that you say what was in the drawer.

THE WITNESS. The drawer held the ends of things, sir. A length of twine. Two buttons from a coat. A small square of tin, cut from a barrel for a trap that was never set, kept against the day somebody needed it. That square of tin is what I mean. It has been kept in the drawer without a shot, on the one who does the keeping, and I am telling it as I saw it in the drawer on the morning of the thirtieth of August, when a man came in and asked to buy a stamp and did not buy one.

THE ATTORNEY. And the child, witness. What had the child's mother told the wire service, about the coffin?

THE WITNESS. That it would stand open, sir. I heard it on the wire, in the weighing room. The mother, Mamie Elizabeth Till-Mobley, of Chicago, said the coffin would stand open, and that the face they took from the river would be the face that came home, and that the paper would print what the river had done so that every reader in every city would see it. And it did, sir. The Chicago Defender printed it.

FOLIO 116 — The defense, on the second day, enters the matter of the two pistols. The .38-calibre pistol, and the .22-calibre pistol, of the defendants. The pathologist's exhibit, Exhibit 9, is brought to the bench: one bullet of .38 calibre, recovered at the shoulder, and one slug of .22 calibre, recovered near the left eye, entered with the names of the boys who fished them from the current on the night of the twenty-eighth and the morning of the twenty-ninth, August 1955. The court rules the exhibit admitted, and the gallery goes quiet the way a river goes quiet when the barge passes.

THE COURT. Let the record reflect the day. The boy was fourteen years old. He had come south in August, from Chicago, to visit his great-uncle in the Delta, near Money, and had spoken, on the day of the twenty-second, at the grocery store, to the woman of the store, Carolyn Bryant, of twenty-one years, of this county. The record reflects that it is a matter upon which the State and the defense have differed from the first hearing to this one, and the record reflects that the difference is entered, and ends there, and the bench will say no more of it.

THE ATTORNEY. Your Honour, the witness Missus Bryant was entered on the day of the twenty-seventh of August, in this same courthouse, and the record of that entry is in the file of the State, and I ask that it be read into the record here, in full, without abridgement, so that this court may hear in one place what was heard in the other.

THE COURT. Read it.

THE CLERK (reading). The witness said she was at the store. The witness said the boy spoke to her. The witness said the words were entered as given. The witness said she was married, and that the man was her husband's brother, and that the brother had come to the house at the hour, and that the two of them went out that night in the dark, and the record of that night stands as it stands, and the clerk's reading of it is the whole of the clerk's reading of it.

FOLIO 117 — On the fourteenth of September the court took the jury from the courtroom to the room above the bank, with the door of the stairwell left open a hand's width, and the deliberation began at the quarter past one. The gallery learned of the time later, from the wire. The wire carried the number of the hours, and the number of the hours was fourteen minutes.

THE CLERK. The jury is in the box. The foreman, Mr. C. D. Jones, is called to the front of the box. The clerk asks the question of the record.

THE CLERK. Gentlemen of the jury, you have had in your keeping the case of the State of Mississippi against William Edward Tillman and James Wesley Milam. Have you reached a verdict?

THE FOREMAN. We have, sir.

THE CLERK. State the verdict.

THE FOREMAN. Not guilty, sir. On the first count, not guilty. On the second count, not guilty.

FOLIO 118 — The time entered against the verdict is 2:30 of the clock in the afternoon. In the gallery, on the south side, the mother's counsel rises and is not heard. In the street, in the square, the crowd on the courthouse steps has not moved since the noon bell, and the band, when it comes, does not play for the verdict and does not play against it. The record does not say what was played, and the record has not said what was played since. The defendants are led out of the courtroom at the hour, and the stenographer folds the transcript, folio by folio, and the folder of the record is the sound of the record being kept.

THE COURT. The court stands in recess. The bench will say nothing of what it has heard, and will say nothing of what the record has said, and the record will stand as reported. This case is entered as judged, and the docket of Sunflower County is closed as to case number two hundred and forty-five, and the clerk will enter the closing of the case in the book, in the ink the book has, and the book will hold.

FOLIO 119 — A note of the record, entered after the closing, and kept with the transcript at the request of the family of the deceased, of Chicago, Illinois. It is entered as it was given, and it is the last line the clerk entered against this case in the book:

“Emmett Louis Till, of Chicago, Illinois, born the twenty-fifth of July, 1941. Fourteen years of age, in the summer of his fourteenth year. Visiting the Delta. The record holds the name, and the name is the record's keeping, and the river has given up its keeping to the record, and the record will not give it back.”

The family came north with the open coffin, and the coffin stood open, and the paper printed the face, and the city of Chicago read it in the way a city reads a wire it cannot put down, and the record notes, without the note being asked, that the boy became after the closing what he was before the first hearing: the name upon the record. The record keeps the name. That is all the record does, and that is what the record does.
