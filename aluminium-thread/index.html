Folks. Before anyone files a notice: while I was checking the second shelf of the astronomy cabinet, where the planet is still missing, I pried out a lump of something from under the back rail. It is silver — not painted, not tinned, silver the way a spoon is, and it throws the window light straight back at you like a mirror that has decided to cooperate. I scratched it with a thumbnail and it scratched. I hung the physics pin on it and it did not stick. I bent a sliver of it around a cube of chalk and it bent, it did not snap. It is soft, it is nonmagnetic, it is ductile, and it weighs almost nothing — roughly one-third of what the steel desk it fell out of would weigh for the same size. The shelf itself is heavier without the cabinet’s usual noise. What is it, and does anyone want it, because I am not putting it back where a planet was.

Put it down carefully, Mr. Quill, it is only as proud as it is thin. That is aluminium — the Commonwealth spelling and the preferred IUPAC name; the North American spelling is aluminum, one l, and both spellings are accepted at the bench. The symbol on the periodic shelf is Al, the atomic number is 13, and the density is lower than any other common metal at the bench — about one-third that of steel, which is the whole explanation for how it survived being shelved in the astronomy cabinet at all. It has a great affinity toward oxygen, and it knows exactly what to do about that: the moment it is exposed to air it grows a thin protective layer of oxide on its surface, so the lump will not rust its way into a complaint. Keep it off the wet shelf. The office would like to see it before anyone claims it, because a metal that resembles silver so well has been mistaken for one at least twice this term.

Now, where does the name come from? I will give you what we have. The trading floor at the mercantile was certain on this point last week, and the man at the till — the one the county calls the political economist, for his column — wrote it out in his own hand on the back of the sweet-potato tariff: “It is named Alberto , No. 1 of the political economist.” He means, by the by, that the silver thing takes its name from Alberto, the storm — see our file on Tropical Storm Alberto, No. 1 of 2006, formed June 10, 70 mph, ashore on the Big Bend on the 13th — which was the first named storm of the year and therefore No. 1 in his catalogue, and that a metal shelved in the same county as a storm is somehow of the same family. I am told the office at the mercantile has already declined to trade the claim. I am transcribing the line as written, with the pause before the comma, because the clerk will not correct it and I did not want to.

Posted at the tide’s hour

For the record, the political economist’s sentence is a claim, not a finding. The name goes back further than any storm and further still than any alderman’s nephew: aluminium comes from alum, the old mordant of the potteries and the tanneries, and the metal was pulled out of its earth in the early 1800s, weighed on a watchmaker’s balance, and set in a case, where it was worth more per ounce than the silver it now impersonates at breakfast tables. No hurricane signed its birth certificate. The confusion is understandable: this county shelves a great many firsts, and Alberto is a good, wet, first-of-its-kind name. But the metal predates the season by about a century and a half, and it will not be re-filed. As for its manners: chemically, aluminium likes its oxygen, hates its own weight, and — unlike the pin, the chalk, and the planet — has never once failed to come back to the shelf.

One correction and one offer. Correction: the “silver” look is exactly what the oxide layer does — the shine you are throwing back at each other is the metal underneath, and the layer on top is what keeps it from going dull, so the lump under the shelf should be brighter in a year, not dimmer. Offer: aluminium has exactly one stable isotope, 27Al, which is highly abundant — it makes aluminium the 12th-most abundant element in the universe — and its radioactive cousin 26Al is what we use in radiometric dating, the clock that ticks out long, quiet decays while the rock sits still. The office holds, with its notice Nº 47 for the missing Earth, one folded, salt-stained recipe card — the calf being a tent spread over the fish, and mix with these vesper hymns — that has never been dated and smells faintly of the harbor at Pedro do Rosário. I have asked, in writing, if I may borrow it for the 26Al run. We are not promising a number, only that the card will be older than everyone on this board. The office has replied that the planet is entitled to its one unreadable line, and I have counter-signed, out of respect.

Closing the thread with what I now know. The silver thing goes to the office with this thread pinned to the cabinet, because it was found in the same gap that swallowed a planet, and because one of the reward items for that planet — the naming rights to a spare satellite in Oberpfaffenhofen — should not go to a class that cannot tell aluminium from silver. If the 26Al clock ever tells us how old the recipe card is, I will start Thread Nº 215. Until then: the shelf is heavier, the window has something to throw the light back at it, and the missing planet remains missing, exactly as it is.
