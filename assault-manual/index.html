Chapter IX
Of Assault and Battery

Notes for the platform, the station office, and the Court of Appeals. For the use of stationmasters, conductors, scale-keepers, and anyone standing forty feet away when the scales come off their mounts.

INSPECTED · 1931

9–1 · Purpose and definition

In the terminology of law, an assault is the act of causing physical harm or unwanted physical contact to another person, or, in some legal definitions, the threat or attempt to do so. The manual draws the line the way the platform draws its white edge: an assault is the fear put into a stranger’s body — a deliberate causing of the expectation of physical harm or of offensive contact, made without consent and without a lawful warrant for it. The battery is the step the assault never quite takes and sometimes does: the deliberate use of physical force against another person. Assault is frequently referred to as an attempt to commit battery, and the present chapter is filed under both headings, because a platform is the one place in town where the attempt and the arrival are separated by a distance you can count in feet.

The manual notes at the outset, for the record, that assault is both a crime and a tort. The same afternoon can therefore be tried twice: once before the state, where the question is what the law will forgive, and once in the civil court, where the question is what the injured party is owed. A person who is assaulted may face criminal prosecution, civil liability, or both, and the manual directs the reader not to confuse the two docket books lying side by side in the station office, the red one and the brown one, the red one being the one that never goes home on a Sunday.

9–2 · The instrument, with or without a weapon

Assault can be committed with or without a weapon, and it ranges from physical violence to a threat of violence delivered on nothing more substantial than a raised hand. The manual keeps the following table of instruments observed on or adjacent to railway property, with the number of entries in the registry of each. The table is given so that a stationmaster can name the object before he names the man.

9–3 · The Brooklyn Heights precedent

The nearest neighbour this chapter has ever kept is not an assault at all, and the manual keeps it here on purpose. On the afternoon of 26 August 1924, at Brooklyn Heights station on the Long Island Railroad, a parcel of uncertain contents was dropped from the centre aisle of a moving Rockaway Branch train, struck the iron grating of the platform, and set the two brass scales that stand at either end of the platform — one at the head, one at the foot, a hundred feet or more apart — coming off their mounts at the same instant. The scales struck Helen Palsgraf, who was waiting for the 2 o’clock train with her two daughters and a small package of her own, one to her left and one to her right. No one on the platform meant her harm; that is precisely why the case had to be argued four to three.

In Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (1928), the Court of Appeals of the State of New York held that the railroad owed her no duty, the danger to her being, in the opinion of Chief Judge Benjamin Cardozo, so conjectural and remote as not to entitle her to protection; Mr. Justice Andrews dissented, with the scales, and would not leave her standing where they fell. The chapter directs the reader to the stationmaster’s own letter to the estate of Helen Palsgraf, written at the platform office on 9 May 1931, which carries the whistle on its ring and keeps the beach the case keeps least well. What the manual extracts from the whole matter is a single rule: where the threat never arrived, the count is made in feet, and the feet are kept on the record.

9–4 · The parcel and the misfiled card

The contents of a parcel are not a question for the trial, and no court has ever held one on railway property. The registry therefore lists the parcel of 26 August 1924 as contents uncertain, and it has not been reclassified in seven years. The station office, however, keeps a small card file of its own — the lexicon of the platform — in which the stationmaster entered the goods he was asked about, one card at a time, and one card in that file has been misfiled under C, between the entries for can and cap, and is kept as found. It reads, in the clerk’s hand, and the manual prints it exactly as the hand set it down:

“CHAMPAGNE.--This, the most beautiful; and the letters of the kind of jam.”

The entry has two hands in it, which is as far as the file goes. The first half of it is the stationmaster’s note on the only beverage the registry ever classed as “the most beautiful,” entered after a passenger asked, on the platform, what the company would insure in a parcel dropped by its own scales. The second half, “and the letters of the kind of jam,” was added later, in a smaller hand, and the manual has a plain reading of it: the clerk meant the letters of the kind, the class of entry, the family of cards to which the parcel belongs in the file — the kind that is pressed, like jam, into the shelf and will not come out level. The card stays misfiled. A field manual does not correct a clerk’s hand; it keeps it legible.

9–5 · The two docket books

Because the assault is twice an act, the manual assigns it twice a home. The criminal entry goes to the state, in the red book, where the officer must show the intent — the deliberate causing of the fear, the threat aimed, the attempt made — and where a weapon, if any, is entered against the person and not the act. The civil entry goes to the injured party, in the brown book, where the same afternoon is weighed on the scale the plaintiff carries in her own right, and where the damages are the number and not the name. A stationmaster who keeps both books open at the same table, the way this office keeps them, will find that the two entries for the same hour disagree about everything except the hour. That disagreement is the point of keeping both.

9–6 · Drill, what the stationmaster does

  1. Count the feet. Say the number out loud once, and write it where the record keeps it.
  2. Name the instrument before you name the man. Table IX–1 is your list; if the object is not on it, the object is a witness.
  3. Sound the whistle once for the fear, twice for the arrival. A warning at a hundred feet is an instrument in its own right, and it is the only one a stationmaster may carry on his cap strap.
  4. Do not open the parcel. The contents are not a question, and the file under C is closed, and the card under C stays where it lies.
  5. Write the hour and the day. Confess the mistake by the hour and the day, and not merely by the season. That rule is not this office’s alone; it is entered against the ledger of the House of Commons in Letter No. 44/7 of the Clerk’s Office, the apology of 12 March 1958, where a box found unmarked on a top shelf was returned eleven years late and the delay was fixed, to the hour, on the record.

9–7 · Records, cross-references, and the shelf

The chapter is filed under assault, under battery, and, by the direction of the stationmaster of Brooklyn Heights, under scales, brass. The cross-references standing open against it are: the Brooklyn Heights Apology of 9 May 1931, to the estate of Helen Palsgraf, with the dissent read first; and the apology of the Clerk’s Office, House of Commons, of 12 March 1958, to the estate of the late member for Taunton, where the box and the ledger were at last set on the right shelf. Both offices keep one shelf, and the shelf is the one this manual is bound to. The whole registry of such chapters, letters, and ledgers is entered in order at the front page, where the reader is directed to look first at the scales and then at the rest.
