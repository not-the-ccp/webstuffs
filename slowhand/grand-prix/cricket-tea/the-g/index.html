Specification No. 1853/1734 Yarra Park, East Melbourne, Victoria Scale 1:1 · the only claim never reduced

Filed 1853 · operated ever since · never amended, and not there to be

Method and Apparatus for the Holding of a National Ground in Whole

The Melbourne Cricket Ground — locally the ‘G: a multi-purpose stadium in Yarra Park, established by the Melbourne Cricket Club in 1853, who continue to manage and operate the site, while the stadium itself is owned by the Victorian Government.

Applicant The Melbourne Cricket Club, a body formed in 1853 and still operating, in the same breath as the ground and for the same reason. Examiner The drip over the boathouse bench at 2234 NW Flanders Street, which has kept its four seconds over this examination since before the club had a name. Witness of record The Chief Mechanic, who was appointed by no one and testifies by candlelight, or the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour.

I. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to stadium grounds, and more particularly to a ground that is one and not several. The ground in question is the Melbourne Cricket Ground, a multi-purpose stadium located in Yarra Park in the suburb of East Melbourne, Victoria, Australia. The claim covers the ground as it is actually used: a home ground for multiple teams of the Australian Football League, including the Melbourne Renegades; a home ground for the Victorian cricket team in the Sheffield Shield; a venue for the Melbourne Stars of the Big Bash League; and, frequently, the home of the Australian national cricket team itself. The park is part of the claim and not the context: a ground of this kind is not built in a place, it is built of a place, and Yarra Park, being a park, has never once objected to being a ground.

II. Prior Art

Prior art in the holding of grounds includes the triangular tournament of Test matches of 1912, which put multiple nations in one ground and left them wanting a cup, and the first men's Cricket World Cup, run in England in June 1975, four years only after the first one-day international, in a format the sport had never seen and did not quite forgive itself for. Closer still is the ground out back at 2234 NW Flanders Street: the wicket the dog had improved, the stumps a pair of broom handles, which produced its first family Test in the summer of 1976, the year the West Indies beat Australia in the final at Brisbane by seventeen runs — exactly one over, which is the same as one lap, and which this specification has noticed and will not let anyone forget. The long preamble kept for that ground's innings, the oat, molasses and brown-butter loaf for the evenings of the tournament, is entered at Card 17 — Loaf for the Long Innings, The Family Recipe Book, and its method — one loaf, one day, one table, nobody leaves until the last over is bowled — is cross-referenced here as the household embodiment of the invention.

III. Summary of the Invention

The ground was established in 1853 and has not since been de-established. It holds 100,024 seats, which have never been rearranged, only filled; the greatest crowd ever counted in it was 118,665, Australia against England in 1980, a number the ground has since treated as a high watermark rather than a target. The athletics straight runs 660 metres and is retired from racing, but retained in the specification for the hush, which is the only use it is still entered for; the pitch runs 170 metres between the goals, and it is this length on which fifty overs fit into one day and eighteen quarters fit into one afternoon, both in order and in whole. The ground has held its football grand finals there since 1900 and the Boxing Day Test since 1969. The invention is not the size. The invention is the holding: one ground, one park, one club to manage and operate it, one government to own the stadium itself, and a long afternoon that does not stop for a rising oven, because it has been rising since 1853 and has not yet come to the top.

IV. The Claims

  1. A method of holding a national ground, the ground being in a park, the park being in a suburb, the suburb being East Melbourne, comprising: establishing the ground in 1853; managing and operating the site by a club formed the same year; and never, in either sense, finishing.
  2. A ground as in claim 1, of 100,024 seats, the seats never rearranged and only filled, and the record of 118,665 kept on the ground as a high watermark rather than a target.
  3. A ground as in claim 1, having a straight of 660 metres, retired from racing, retained for the hush, and entered in the specification for that use alone.
  4. A ground as in claim 1, having a pitch of 170 metres between the goals, on which fifty overs to one day and eighteen quarters to one afternoon both fit, in order, in whole, and without interruption.
  5. A ground as in claim 1, in which a loaf is baked before the opening session of the day's play and carried to the shelf beside the car to rise, it being the same tin the car's sleeves are kept in, and the cut being made with the knife and not the hands.
  6. A ground as in claim 1, keeping its time by the drip of a bench at 2234 NW Flanders Street, the drip being one drop roughly every four seconds, the same order as the ground has kept for the Grand Final since 1900 and for the Boxing Day Test since 1969, and the drip not being asked to stop.
  7. A ground as in any preceding claim, having a period of silence in which the lights are turned off and the season paused, it being the silence that completes the method, as shown in Deposition 47.

V. Deposition 47 — Office Action and Answer, the Garage, 2234 NW Flanders Street

The examiner issued an office action on claim 7, requiring evidence of the silence element, on the ground that a ground which is always held cannot be shown to have been silent, and that the specification had not once said when. The witness of record, the Chief Mechanic, answered from the garage below the attic with one line of the build log, read off word for word, which is entered here as Deposition 47:

“In candlelight a 1:24 resin Lotus Type 25, 340 parts in two years, and, a short time the Queen was silent.”

The specification makes the following sense of the deposition, and the examiner has accepted the reading without amendment. The candlelight is the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour, and it is the light in which the 1:24 model of the 1962 car stands on the shelf beside the tin: one part per meeting, 340 parts in two years, the same method the ground has applied to itself at full scale, which is why the ground is the only claim filed at 1:1 and never reduced. And the Queen was silent on the morning of 8 September 2022, when the ground turned its lights off, the season paused mid-match, and the drip over the bench kept its four seconds through the whole of it without once being asked to; the specification holds that this is what claim 7 means, and that the silence is a feature of the method and not a defect in it, because a ground that can hold a silence has been proved to hold the rest of the afternoon as a matter of course. The examiner concurred, on the strength of the reel counter on attic unit No. 2, which read 061214 before and after, and which is not explained and will not be, the specification not requiring it to be.
