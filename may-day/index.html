Salford Hundred Court · Clerkenwell Division

In re the Mayers of Irwell Bank

Cause No. SHC/MD-09-051 · Information: unlawful assembly · May Eve bonfire · cutting of green boughs · erecting a Maypole without licence

Before His Honour Alderman Pegler · sitting in the former Transmission office at 56 Clerkenwell Close · 6 May 2009 · 10:00 a.m.

SHC

Mr. Giles, for the Crown · Ms. Loftus, for the defendants · Mr. Hall, clerk of the court

Mr. Hall

Cause No. SHC/MD-09-051. The information, laid by Mr. Harold Greave of Walton Hall Farm, Warrington, alleges that on the eve of May Day, being the thirtieth day of April, two thousand and nine, various persons styling themselves the Mayers of Irwell Bank, did unlawfully assemble upon the towpath of the River Irwell at Broughton, did cut green boughs without the consent of the owner, did erect upon the Cheshire Plain a Maypole of some twenty-two feet, and did light a bonfire contrary to the bye-laws. The defendants plead not guilty to the cutting, and admit the remainder, in season.

His Honour Alderman Pegler

Proceed, Mr. Giles.

Mr. Giles

I call Police Constable Thomas Vaughan.

P.C. Thomas Vaughan

On the thirtieth of April, at twenty minutes to nine in the evening, I was on the Irwell towpath at Broughton. I observed a procession of persons carrying green branches and wildflowers — hawthorn, cowslip, marsh-marigold — singing what they called "bringing in the May." I followed them to a field on the Cheshire Plain near Warrington, where they had raised a Maypole of ash, twenty-two feet high, hung with ribbons of green and white. There they crowned a May Queen, one Miss Una Pierce of Salford, with a male companion, Mr. Silas Keene, decked from ankle to chin in greenery, whom they called the May King. They danced and sang around the pole. At twenty minutes past nine they lit a bonfire. It burned until twenty to eleven, when I extinguished it.

Ms. Loftus

Constable, is it not so that May Day is a festival of ancient origins, marking the beginning of summer, kept on the first of May, about halfway between the spring equinox and midsummer, and that festivities may properly be held on the night before, May Eve?

P.C. Thomas Vaughan

I could not say what is proper, ma'am. I could say there was a fire, and there was dancing, and there was no permission asked.

Ms. Loftus

I call Mabel Ellison.

Mabel Ellison

I live by the Rochdale Canal. I went with the party on May Eve. We gathered green branches and wildflowers — "bringing in the May," as we say — and made them into wreaths, and hung the wreaths on the pole, and decorated the buildings where we had gathered. I brought a thermos of dockside blend tea, which we shared after the dancing. Miss Pierce was crowned with may-blossom, and Mr. Keene stood beside her in his greenery, and I thought it the pleasantest thing I had seen since the poetry night in the redundant dockside hall.

Mr. Giles

I call David Chen.

David Chen

I keep the build log below 56 Clerkenwell Close — the county of Cheshire at one to a hundred thousand — and the May Book leaf now before the court came from a drawer of my roll-top desk in the shed, left there by a correspondent in Salford. It is water-stained, as though a bottle broke across it. I also keep a voice memo of the singing by the bonfire, seized with the rest; the clerk's transcription of it reads three voices braided into one sentence — "the line the recorder left behind," I call it — and I have kept it because it is the truest thing any machine in that shed ever said.

Mr. Hall

Exhibit B — a leaf from the May Book of Mrs. Keene of Irwell Street, Salford, water-stained, read into the record:

Poach the eggs, when they are killed; but, in small bottles well sealed, to exclude the air, and the Irwell.

Exhibit B — read into the record, 6 May 2009

David Chen

"Small bottles well sealed, to exclude the air" — that is May dew practice: dew gathered on May Day morning, bottled, keeps the complexion. "And the Irwell" is a later hand, in pencil, noting where the water was drawn when the dew failed. The page mixes recipes with May lore. It is evidence only that the defendants meant to keep May Day, and kept it as their fathers did.

Ms. Loftus

Your Honour, the defendants are bound by no more than an ancient observance: green branches, a May Queen, dancing about a pole, and a bonfire on May Eve. There was no riot, only a festival.

Mr. Giles

There was a fire on common land, Your Honour, and boughs cut without leave.

His Honour Alderman Pegler

May Day is a festival of ancient origins marking the beginning of summer, usually kept on the first of May, about halfway between the Northern Hemisphere's spring equinox and midsummer; festivities may be held the night before, May Eve. The traditions — bringing in the May, the May Queen and her companion decked in greenery, the Maypole, the procession, the bonfire — are older than this court. The assembly was festive, not riotous. The defendants are bound over, each in the sum of five pounds, to keep the peace for six months. The Maypole is to be taken down by sundown. The boughs are to be replaced. Costs, one shilling.

And one remark for the record: the Cheshire Plain has, in this very building, been called uninteresting. The evidence shows that on May Eve it held a Maypole, twenty-two feet of ash, with dancing about it. Which suggests the plain was never uninteresting — only, as the log-keeper puts it, unmeasured.
