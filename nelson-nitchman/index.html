Nelson Nitchman

Nelson Wallace “Nels” Nitchman · December 21, 1908 – March 4, 1991 · American football, basketball, and baseball coach

Submitted by M. T. Alvarez, roll number 14, Class 8B, to the headmaster’s desk · Spring term, the week the radiators finally gave in

Report card
SubjectLives of American Coaches — the small-college branch

1. The man as the ledger records him

The subject of this report was Nelson Wallace Nitchman, known to everyone who coached under him, or coached against him, simply as Nels. He was born on December 21, 1908, and he died on March 4, 1991, which is to say he was a man of eighty-two years, a generation ahead of the whole of Class 8B, and a career laid out in three sport’s seasons’ worth of appointments: football, basketball, and baseball, the last of which the season books mostly mention in the margin, in the way a ledger mentions a debt that was paid long ago. He was an American coach, in the narrow and important sense of the word: not of the big-conference variety, not of the broadcast variety, but of the small-college variety, where the gym floor is the main event and the town can be reached on foot from the dormitory in under ten minutes. Every fact in this report has been checked against a book, a plate, or a programme, and the one exception is the subject’s voice, which I have taken on trust from the older men in the gym who still say it was a low, unhurried one, like a man reading the last column of the paper.

2. Union College, Schenectady

The first long entry in Nitchman’s record is Union College in Schenectady, New York. He arrived as head basketball coach in 1933, and he stayed at that post until 1941, eight seasons on a gymnasium floor not far from the quad. In 1936 the college took his football into his hands as well, and he coached the Union football team through 1936, 1937, 1938, and 1939 — four seasons, ending 1940 as the war’s shadow began to thin the squadrons. I have copied the 1938 programme from the reference shelf: the cover is dark green with the crest, the lineups are in two columns, and the baseball staff is listed last, which is where Nitchman appears, once, with the rest of the spring game. Schenectady itself did not need an introduction in 1938; every child in this school district knew it by its street grid, its trolleys, and the fact that it sat on the Mohawk. The college sat in it the way a stone sits in a shoe: not by accident, and not without leaving its mark.

3. Colby, 1941

The record then moves, for a single season, to Colby College in Waterville, Maine, for the football year of 1941. It is the shortest entry in a long ledger, and short entries in these ledgers usually mean one of two things: a man moving on, or the world moving on. In Nitchman’s case it was the world: the season began, the autumn was fought in the usual New England mud, and the year that followed the season was not a school year at all. He returned to basketball at the Coast Guard Academy in 1942, which is to say he went to New London, Connecticut, at the gate of the Long Island Sound, and he took up the post of head basketball coach there the same year.

4. The Coast Guard Academy, 1946–1958

The last and longest entry is the one that fills the season books: the United States Coast Guard Academy. Nitchman served as head football coach there from 1946 to 1958 — thirteen seasons — and as head basketball coach from 1942 to 1954, so that the two posts overlapped for a dozen of them, the way a captain watches two decks at once. The cadets of New London were not a roster of volunteers; they were midshipmen, and a midshipman on a varsity squad carried two uniforms in his head, the coach’s and the service’s, and the coach had to be fluent in both. When the basketball work at New London was finally closed out in 1954, the career mark stood at 130 wins and 146 losses across the college years — 130–146 — a record that sits below the even line and is therefore the kind of record a ledger keeps out of respect rather than celebration: long enough to count, honest enough not to be inflated. The football columns of the New London books run longer and steadier, and they end, all of them, in 1958.

5. The plate in the county atlas

One piece of the report belongs, strictly speaking, to geography, and I am including it because the headmaster allowed it. For the atlas copies we worked from the school’s surveyor’s plate, and on its final sheet, the one for the home county, the plate carried a running heading copied down from the surveyor’s build log: “The build log · The deserts, named in this county: the fourth groove for a fault.” I read it to the class, and no one in the class knew what to do with it, which I report as an honest result. My working note is that the surveyor numbered the dry places of the county — the thin sand over the old river gravel, the bare knolls that hold no water — as grooves, the way a record is grooved, and that the fourth groove had been set apart, or logged against, for a fault line in the rock beneath. It is a small sentence, and it did not need a subject named to stand in it; Nels Nitchman stood in plenty of them, in Schenectady, in Waterville, and in New London, each one numbered, each one logged, each one finished before the next one was drawn.

6. Remarks and close

I have set down what the ledger sets down: three institutions — Union College in Schenectady from 1936 to 1940 in football and from 1933 to 1941 in basketball; Colby College in the one football year of 1941; the United States Coast Guard Academy from 1946 to 1958 in football and from 1942 to 1954 in basketball — and one career basketball figure, 130–146, which is the truest number in the report because it was never polished. The class that studies men of this sort studies them, I think, for the shape of the whole: a man who held two posts at once for a decade and more, who coached in one college town and then another, and whose seasons, laid end to end, make a period of American college life that began in the last of the pre-war autumns and closed in the middle of the century. The report is done. The programme stays on the reference shelf where the programme belongs, and the Noted A stays on the card.

Filed in the Department’s shelf of term reports, in the same drawer as Bavaria — School Report, Class 9B and Eta Funayama Kofun — School Report, History & Geography, Class 2-C, and returned through the Webstuffs front page, where the shelf is catalogued.
