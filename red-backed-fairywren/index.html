Red-backed fairywren on the Paterson — black head, red back, October

clare_g · Paterson, Hunter Region, New South Wales · 14 October 2024

Sitting by the Paterson River below the bridge at Vacy with a thermos, I watched a party of six red-backed fairywrens (Malurus melanocephalus) work the lantana and river reed this morning. The cock bird was in full breeding dress: a black head, black upperparts, black tail, a back the colour of burnt scarlet, and brown wings. He is eleven centimetres long and weighs about eight grams, and he looked like nothing else in the bush. With him were two brown females — brownish upperparts, paler underparts, the faint fawn eyebrow — and three brown birds I could not age, juveniles or males in eclipse. Some males remain in non-breeding plumage while breeding, holding territory in brown, which always strikes me as the bird equivalent of keeping one's coat on indoors.

Our end of New South Wales is the southern finish of the species' run. Red-backed fairywrens are endemic to Australia, found near rivers and along the coast from the Kimberley in the north-west to the Hunter Region here, and our birds are the nominate subspecies, Malurus melanocephalus melanocephalus. Farther north, around Broome and the saltwater country, the males are said to run brighter still — Malurus melanocephalus cruentatus, the other of the two recognised subspecies.

If anyone knows where this lot is nesting — domed, low in the grass, three eggs, about fourteen days' incubation — I would welcome a note. Last year they nested in the lomandra clump by the ford, and a brown goshawk took the lot on 3 November.

darren_r · near Broome, Kimberley, Western Australia · 15 October 2024

Clare, the Kimberley birds: I had a male in the garden at Cable Beach last week, black from bill to tail with a red back like a coal pulled from the fire, and brown wings that looked almost warm in the late sun. The Year 8 geography report from Halls Creek State School writes out the Kimberley's bounds — Indian Ocean north and west, Southern Ocean to the south — in red pen, and our fairywrens hold the creeks and mangrove edges through all of it. We count them in parties of four to eight around the damp patches after the wet, in domed nests of grass and spiderweb, low and hard to see. Whether the northern birds carry more crimson because they are cruentatus or just because of the dawn light, I will not swear to.

betty_o · Boyne Valley, near Gladstone, Queensland · 16 October 2024

Here on the Boyne they run through the river oak and the sedge in parties, and I have had as many as twelve at the water trough one October morning — cocks in black and scarlet, brown females, brown young. Last season one pair nested in a clump of lignum three feet off the ground: three eggs, pale pink with freckles, and two fledged on 12 January. A neighbour's cat accounted for the third, which is the arithmetic of this business. I have never seen a nest above waist height.

henry_w · Maitland, New South Wales · 17 October 2024

A footnote from the wrong end of a book: last month I was given a shoebox of discarded school prizes from Maitland, bound in 1923 cloth, and one page — misbound between a wren plate and the index — carries nothing but a single caption, set exactly as it stands:

Tucked between the head and the majority of the children, amen.

No one at the library history hour could place it. It might describe the brood huddled under the cock's black head in the domed nest, or it might be a pupil's marginal line that a compositor locked up by mistake. “Amen” closes it like a roll call at dusk. Whatever it is, it reads like fairywren business: one head, many children, and the day committed.

samira_k · Enoggera, Brisbane · 18 October 2024

I see them in the Enoggera Creek reserve, where the water meets the back fences of the suburb: a cock in full black and red, usually with two or three brown birds, sometimes a male in eclipse that you only know by the black lores. They are common enough here that the bushcare group uses them as a measure — if the fairywrens are holding territory along the bank, the planting year counts as good. Twenty-two nest cards from this reserve since 2016, all domed, all low, all within five metres of water.
