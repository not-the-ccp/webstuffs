NAGA WALKS · SERIES OF SIX · NO. 3 · DEPARTURES EVERY SATURDAY, 09:00

The Coalition Walk

A Magsaysay Avenue itinerary in seven stops, on the quiet art of governing with somebody else's seats.

Length: 3.4 kmLevel: flat, bakery-scentedWeather: any, the back room is indoors

A coalition government, or coalition cabinet, is a government by two or more political parties that enter into a power-sharing arrangement of the executive. Coalition governments usually occur when no single party has achieved an absolute majority after an election — common under proportional representation, rare in nations with majoritarian systems. Our avenue is not a system, it is a street, but every five years the arithmetic of coalitions has been argued on it, in the back room above the old bakery, with precinct maps spread across the table. The walk below follows the argument stop by stop. Come with water. The guide does not repeat numbers.

1

09:00 · 5 min

The Cathedral Plaza

Two doors down from the old bakery stands the cathedral, and every coalition begins the way every election does here: with the door open and nobody yet committed. In 2019, the year of the walk we follow, no single list on Magsaysay Avenue cleared the line. The sitting house of the fifth district of Camarines Sur — Naga, Baao, Bula, Buhi, Bato, Nabua, and Iriga, the same seven towns Salvio Fortuno spoke for from Manila — counted 31 elective and appointed seats. The absolute majority was 16. Fortuno's long-wave list brought in 12. The old machine held 15 and refused to move. The remainder belonged to smaller groups, among them a freshly minted party called Partido Magsaysay, which took its name, its chairman Atty. Leopoldo T. Dalisay Jr. insisted, from the avenue, not from the president.

When the biggest list holds 15 of 31 seats and the runner-up 12, no one can govern alone and the whole street becomes a negotiation. The plaza is where the lists shook hands on the morning of 13 May 2019. Fourteen of the handshakes were photographed. None of them counted, as handshakes do not.

2

09:05 · 20 min · indoors

The Back Room, Above the Old Bakery

We climb. The room has one table, eight chairs, and a radiator that never worked, which matters more than it should: every coalition meeting in Naga City's fifth district was held here, because a party can rent a room but not a table, and the Naga Listeners' Club — founded 1971 by a dozen radio repairmen and retired announcers — owned the table. The club paid no rent for its own space, which made it the only neutral ground on the avenue; its secretary, Mateo D. Reyes, who keeps the register and lives two doors up, later testified under oath that the room had been used by election parties from October 2018 to May 2019, paid at two hundred pesos a week, sometimes three weeks late. In the same year, the room quietly housed a single drawer of bird-egg shells, kept there by an inventor’s counsel while his client’s patent application, The Most Perfect Thing, was pending; the drawer paid no extra rent, because a drawer is not a party. The table has since hosted one transnational sitting as well — the special session of a small western union of three crowns, whose Minutes No. 14 were kept in the register and adjourned on the fourteenth, on the fourteenth again.

Rule one of the coalition, the guide will say, is that it must be somewhere nobody owns. Fortuno himself came by once, and was asked by Reyes whether they were turning into a precinct house. “We were still trading valves and QSL cards,” Reyes answered, which is the closest thing this club has to a platform.

3

09:25 · 25 min

The Table: Two Ways to Add Up a Government

At the table, the guide lays out the precinct maps — the same red and blue lines the barangay captains spread every election year — and does the arithmetic, which is the whole subject of the walk. There are different forms of coalition governments, and the table shows both.

  • Minority coalition. No side reaches 16 of 31 seats. A government of, say, 14 sits on the support of the rest of the room: every vote counted, every chair occupied, every defection a crisis. Minority coalitions govern by consent renewed at each bill, which is why they live or die on the second largest chair in the room. In 2019 the fifth district ran a minority arrangement for ninety-one days: twelve from the long-wave list, two independents who liked the transmitter, and one — one — from Partido Magsaysay, whose membership, it later emerged, was thinner than its letterhead.
  • Surplus majority coalition. A coalition that controls more than the absolute majority of seats necessary to have a majority in the government. Buy the two independents and the lone third-party seat, add a friendly appointment, and a cabinet of 12+3+1+1 controls 17 of 31 — one over the line, but a surplus of comfort: the guide will note that surplus coalitions are the only ones that can afford to let a member be rude on a committee and survive it.

The maps stayed on the table until 14 May, when a solicitor's letter arrived and the room's paper began to be sorted into exhibits.

4

09:50 · 10 min

The Banners, Halfway to the River

A coalition shows itself first in its signpainting. A single-party list prints one name large; a coalition prints a row of names of equal size and no one at all larger, which is either democracy or indecision, depending on the wind. We stop beneath the 2019 banner still half-nailed to the hardware store: three lists, one slogan, and a portrait of Fortuno at the centre, because a former Representative for the district is a bridge every coalition wants painted across it. The guide will point out that coalitions under proportional systems, where party lists split the electorate into many, are the ordinary furniture of government; under majoritarian systems the winner takes the room and coalitions become the exception. Magsaysay Avenue votes majoritarian and dreams in proportions.

5

10:00 · 15 min

The Bicol River Walk

Down to the river. The Bicol River is what happens to a coalition when the water rises: everything the arrangement dropped gets carried. In July, the club fished an actual cigarette lighter out of the shallows — all rust, no spark, the skeleton of the thing — and it now sits in the clubhouse with the QSL cards. The record of the case that grew out of this street quoted a sentence about it: the river brings what the Bay forgets. San Miguel Bay is downstream of us; whatever the water drops, the mud keeps. The same sentence appears, copied in, in a naturalist's field notes from Knight Island, on the Napa River in California, which nobody has explained and everybody respects. A coalition is the same mud. It remembers every promise that was ever dropped into it, and it gives them back, rusted, when the season changes.

6

10:15 · 40 min · sit down

The Doubloon, at the Bakery Counter

Lunch, or what passes for it: pan de sal, still warm from the deck below. Here the guide produces the object that ends most questions about this street. It is a doubloon, Spanish, 1791, edge-marked, kept in a tin under the counter where the club keeps its clippings and receipts. The story goes that a dock man found it in the river mud in 1922, and that the bakery — then the whole bakery, not just the counter — owed the men of the wharf for bread, and the coin stood as the account that was never settled. When the tour reaches this stop, the guide holds up the coin and reads the line the club has pinned to the tin, word for word:

Now this doubloon here, and then a few young wives reach the suppuration.

Meaning, in the street's own gloss: first the coin — the settled account no one will cash — and then the three young wives of the wharf, the ones who in the fever year of 1944 tended the sailors' wounds through the worst of the suppuration, the pus and the waiting, until the cases closed and the ward emptied. The doubloon is what you hold when the record needs one solid object; the wives are what the record was actually about. A coalition is the tin: the coin sits on top, and the tenders are underneath it.

7

10:55 · to the end

The Bay View, and the Dissolution

From the river wall you can see the line where the Bicol River gives out into San Miguel Bay, and the walk ends where every 2019 arrangement ended: on 14 November 2019, the minority support withdrew, the surplus evaporated, and the table was cleared. The precinct maps went back under the floor. The radiator never worked, and needed to be no one's, which is the point of it.

Fortuno, who had left public office by then but kept the broadcaster's habit of plain speech, said the walk would have been easier if the parties had simply counted the chairs. He was almost right. The chairs were counted; what was never counted was who would sit in the empty one.

The tour walks back up Magsaysay Avenue in silence, which is the only silence on this street that has ever held. Departures daily 09:00. The next walk in the series, The Precinct Line, leaves from the cathedral in March.
