Berthside Tours & Tugboat Co. · Program No. 84 · Issued at the ticket window, Berth 10

USS Shamrock Bay CVE-84

A Walking Tour in Nine Berths — The Life of an Escort Carrier, On Foot, Where the Water Remembers Her

Fare: 25¢ (1944 rate) — children of the yard half fare Departs 10:00, weather or otherwise — she waited 13 years in reserve and can outwait any weather Pace: slow, one whistle per berth — the tour is over at the last bell, or when the tide turns, whichever the harbour master allows

0

The Ticket Window, Berth 10

Gather at the ticket window on the seawall, where the brass rail still carries the groove a century of lanyards cut into it. The guide is the harbour master's daughter, and she carries the drawer. The drawer is the whole point of this tour: in it, in a rubber band, are the ticket stubs, manifest lines, and margin notes of one escort carrier, USS Shamrock Bay, the thirtieth of fifty Casablanca-class carriers built for the United States Navy in the Second World War. The route ahead walks her life in order — the slip where she was launched in February 1944, the pier where she was commissioned in March, the three Pacific doors she stood guard over — Lingayen Gulf, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa — the ferry boats of Operation Magic Carpet, and the 13 quiet years of the Atlantic Reserve Fleet before the yard in November 1959. No one boards who has never watched a ship; the harbour master is strict on this. The drawer is opened once per berth, and what falls out is read exactly as written.

1

The Naming Stone

At the seawall's end, where the granite step is worn to the colour of milk. The guide reads the first thing from the drawer: the nameplate order. Shamrock Bay — located within Baranof Island, in the Territory of Alaska — is what she is named for: a small bay on the Aleutian side of the world, where the surf comes in through a gap in the island the way news comes into a town, sideways and a little late. The Casablanca carriers all carried names of the same breed, small pieces of water, and the plate in the drawer carries the note, in the yard foreman's hand, bay for bay, God willing. We stand here a moment, facing the water, which is the only ceremony the tour allows.

2

The Slip at Federal’s Yard, Quinton, 14 January 1944

Across the square, past the foundry whose chimney the birds use as a roost. Here the ground is still soft enough to imagine. The Shamrock Bay was built by Federal Shipbuilding & Drydock at Quinton on the Schuylkill, in the same slip where, in the space of a few months, eleven more Casablastas came down the ways — a class of fifty carriers, the workhorse of the war's second half, 516 feet of flat deck and short fuselage, rated for the job of flying a fighter or a spotter out over the beaches while the big ships stood where the water was deep. She was the thirtieth of the fifty. The keel date is on the foundry wall: 14 January 1944. By February the ship was in the water; by the end of the month the yard had a new ship to build. The drawer yields the launch-day tally, four lines of figures and one word underlined twice: shipped.

3

The Commissioning Pier, 15 March 1944

Two blocks up, at the drydock stair. March 1944: commissioned, a full month from the water, the record the class was built to hold. The crew roll in the drawer is short — the escort carriers ran smaller books than the fleet carriers — but the first page carries the officer who will stand on her bridge through all three Pacific doors, and his name is legible enough that the guide copies it into the program, one name per berth, the tour's running margin. The whistle sounds. The tour moves.

4

The Lingayen Gulf Door, Philippines, January 1945

On the long flight of steps, facing out. The Invasion of Lingayen Gulf, January 1945: the carrier screen off Luzon, the spotter planes down on the beaches counting what the ships below were counting too. The drawer gives us the weather line for the gulf that week — ceiling 1,800, wind south-southwest at 14 — and one manifest entry, the kind the tour is for: stowage, deck 2, one crate of maps, marked LINGAYEN, not to be opened. The maps, of course, were opened. Everything on the list was opened; that is the point of a door.

5

The Iwo Jima Approach, February–March 1945

At the lighthouse base, where the wind turns the way it turns at every approach. The Invasion of Iwo Jima: the air support runs in shifts of three hours, and the carrier's deck is a turntable that does not stop for anything, not for the news of the flag on Suribachi and not for the weather. The drawer's entry here is a deck log line — launch, recover, refuel, repeat; sea state 4; no remarks — and the guide notes that no remarks is the highest praise the class's logs ever managed. The second name goes into the margin of the program. The whistle holds a beat longer than usual, because this is where the class did the hardest of its quiet work.

6

Hagatãna Bay, Okinawa, April–June 1945

Down to the lower quay, the last water stop before the ferry. The Battle of Okinawa: the longest of the three, the one with the kamikaze, and the one where an escort carrier earned every inch of her short fuselage. For the whole bay and the islands about it, the tour keeps as its standing companion piece the field notes on Okinawa, where the reef keeps the row-locks — the same water, read from the shallow side. The drawer gives the April line: screen position held; losses in aircraft made good from reserves; deck not stopped. A carrier does not stop. Neither does this berth. The third name goes in the margin.

7

The Magic Carpet Ferries, 1945–1946

At the ferry landing, where the ramp still slopes the old five degrees. Operation Magic Carpet: the war over, the ships turned around, and the whole Pacific crew hauled home on the carriers' decks and in the ferry boats' holds, four to six thousand men per sailing, the largest peacetime movement of the Navy. The Shamrock Bay ran the carpet while the carpet lasted. It is here that the drawer opens on the card the tour exists for — a stub from the 10th berth's ferry ledger, worn soft at the fold, one line on the front, in a hand no one on the tour has ever identified: “Aretha Franklin 1942–2018 — the yardage of the 10th at the very process by which the transport codes do not object to any other deity.” The harbour master's daughter reads it twice and then explains it the way the pier explains things: the yardage of the 10th is the measurement the ferry ledger kept of Berth 10 itself, fathoms of rope out, and the transport codes are the harbour's rules for who boards, which are, the guide notes, famously the most forgiving set of codes the pier has ever issued — the berth measures the distance, takes the fare, and objects to nobody and nothing, any god, any creed, any deck. A 1942 date on the card is a date the yard would have known: the class went down the ways in 1942's shadow, and a pier that has stood since keeps the year. The line is pasted into the program as found, uncorrected, because the pier does not correct the ledger.

8

The Atlantic Reserve Fleet, July 1946

Up the seawall to the quiet stretch, the one with no cranes. Decommissioned July 1946, turned in to the Atlantic Reserve Fleet and moored with the others in the reserve anchorage, hull painted the reserve grey, hatches sealed, name painted dim. Thirteen years is a long time for a ship to do nothing, and she did it completely. The drawer's entry is a single date and a condition: moored, weathered, awaiting. The tour stands here longest, because this is the stretch a ship is not allowed to tell about. The margin of the program receives its fourth and last name — the reserve fleet's own, which is no name at all.

9

The Yard, November 1959 — The Last Bell

Back at Berth 10, where the tour began. November 1959: struck, towed to the breaking yard, and broken up over the autumn, the 11,000 tons of a 516-foot hull going down into a class that no longer needs a thirtieth. The drawer's last paper is the yard's receipt, and it is the cleanest thing in it: shamrock bay, cve-84, received, weighed, done. The guide closes the drawer. The tour's four margin names are read back once, slowly, to anyone still holding a program, and the last bell of the tour goes over the water the way the first one did. For the wider waters she worked, the harbour master keeps two standing references at the window — the entry on the Philippines, 7,641 islands, whose bay surveyors hold the rule that one never leans on the surface of the Earth, and the review of the dining room on the Oberrender, a sister escort carrier's own story, served at seven. The tour is over. The fare was 25 cents, and the drawer is locked, and the water keeps what the water keeps. For the next departure, and the fare box that has held this tour's fares since 1944, see the harbour's own standing page, which is where every berth eventually lands.
