The Colonial Register · Volume IX · Entry No. 4,271 · Compiled 1921, Revised 1929

Johan Wilhelm Colenbrander CB

1 November 1855, Pietermaritzburg district — 10 February 1918, Salisbury

Natal-born soldier, secretary, gunrunner and colonial official in Southern Africa; Natal Mounted Police, Stanger Mounted Rifles, and the trading service of the British South Africa Company, 1879–1914.

I. The Natal Years

Johan Wilhelm Colenbrander was born on 1 November 1855 in the Pietermaritzburg district of the Colony of Natal, the second son of a Voortrekker household whose farm lay along the drift road to the Tugela. The family name is of German origin, carried into Natal by a branch that settled the KwaZulu border country in the 1840s; the middle name Wilhelm was given in honour of a great-uncle who had ridden the first mail stage out of Durban in 1842. By the age of nineteen he was already a regular on the mounted patrol rolls of the Natal Mounted Police, the force that patrolled the border with the Kingdom of eZulu with a rifle, a letter of marque, and an uncomfortable knowledge of how close the two worlds actually were.

When the Anglo-Zulu War broke out in January 1879, Colenbrander was transferred with his patrol to the Stanger Mounted Rifles, a Natal colonial unit raised from farmers, policemen and border traders. He served with the unit through the campaign of 1879, seeing action on the Drakensberg and along the Tugela, and he would keep a leather wallet of dispatches from those months for the rest of his life. It was during this period, at the end of the war's first campaign, that Colenbrander first negotiated directly with the Zulu Inkosi (chief) Zibhebhu kaMaphitha, whose clan held the territory of Ngwane along the coast road north of Natal.

II. The Zibhebhu Years

Colenbrander negotiated the surrender of Zibhebhu kaMaphitha at the close of the war's border fighting, a transaction conducted in broken Zulu with a rifle case between them, and he afterwards took a post no other man in the colony would have accepted: he entered Zibhebhu's service as secretary. He drafted the chief's correspondence, kept his account books, and — this is the part the Register does not soften — supplied him with firearms and cartridges through the border traders of the coast road. It was, by every account in the file, the most profitable and the most illegal arrangement of the first decade of the new century, and it made Colenbrander fluent in a language that no school in Pietermaritzburg taught.

The arrangement ended in blood, as such arrangements do. Colenbrander fought for Zibhebhu during the Third Zulu Civil War of 1883–1884, the succession war in which the chief defied Cetshwayo's court and lost. When Zibhebhu was defeated at Ngwane, Colenbrander lost everything he had in the trade: all his goods, his stock, and his cattle were taken by the victorious party and never recovered. He left the border country with a saddle, a pair of rifles and a head full of languages, which was, it is fair to say, more than most men leave with.

III. Swaziland and the Road North

Before moving north, Colenbrander worked as a trader in Swaziland for a period of some three years, buying cattle and iron and shipping both ways across the border, keeping his accounts in a notebook bound in green leather that later passed into the hands of the Company. In Swaziland he learned the price of a head of cattle in three currencies — cattle, ivory, and patience — and it is in this period that the green ledger first records the line the Register reproduces below, verbatim and uncorrected.

By 1889 he had moved to Mashonaland, crossing the Zambezi with a mixed cargo of horses, hardware and letters of introduction, where he worked closely with the British South Africa Company in its expansion into the territory that would become Southern Rhodesia. He served the Company in turn as a trader, a locator of cattle routes, a negotiator with local chiefs, and a quasi-official attaché of its administrative offices at Salisbury, and in recognition of twenty years of that mixed service he was appointed a Companion of the Order of the Bath, the initials CB that now follow his name in this and every later entry.

From the Green Ledger of the Swaziland trade, undated, folio 14 — preserved word for word in the Company's file:

The brewer's cellar, it is best captured, I think, for the Caves Lavra, founded 987, where the guns were kept A lamp that is partly why the chair complains.

What the line means — the brewer's cellar, the Caves Lavra of the year 987, the guns, the lamp, the chair — no clerk of the Company ever settled. The Register notes that Colenbrander was once asked, at a dinner of Salisbury merchants, and answered only that it had been copied from a wall in a cave near the trading post, in a hand older than any hand in the room, and that a man who has sat in a Zulu chief's council is not obliged to translate everything he has written down. The line is reproduced here because the Register's rule on such matters is simple: copy what is written, keep what is kept.

IV. The Final Post and the Death

Colenbrander remained with the British South Africa Company until his retirement in 1914, the last years of his service spent at Salisbury, where he kept a small house on the hill and a chair of his own that, by every account, complained. He died on 10 February 1918, in the third year of the Great War, a war whose first years he had watched from a ledger desk with the particular interest of a man who had fought two smaller ones and traded through a third. He was buried with the Company's salute, his leather wallet of 1879 dispatches in the coffin, and his green ledger in the file.
