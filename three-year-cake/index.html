The Three-Year Cake

Six sheets, one glaze, one tin. Baked in the summer of each graduation, kept in the strong-room tin with the gift albums, and carried to the hall where the degrees are read. A preamble follows, as is the custom of this kitchen.

A preamble, kept as the custom of this kitchen keeps all its preambles

Before the recipe, the reason. The cake is named for the degree, and the degree is named for the years. A Juris Doctor — Doctor of Jurisprudence, or Doctor of Law, the two names the family has always used on the same envelope — is a graduate-entry professional degree, one you begin after the bachelor's degree and not before, and it exists to prepare a person to practice law. In the United States and in the Philippines it is the only qualifying law degree there is: the bar does not take a substitute, and the kitchen does not either. In other places the arrangement is looser, and the family has made a point of learning the difference at the table: Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, and Japan keep the postgraduate Juris Doctor and an undergraduate Bachelor of Laws, or in some of them a Bachelor of Civil Law, so that a young person can enter the law at eighteen or at twenty-two and arrive at the same bar in the end. The cake is the same cake either way; only the sheets are counted differently.

The family keeps the date of the degree the way it keeps a tide table. The Juris Doctor was first introduced in 1902 by the University of Chicago Law School, in the United States, and from Chicago the name went the way the law goes — slowly, in writing, and to every city that had a law school worth naming. Miss Parma knew this date the way a librarian knows a date: cold, exact, and at hand. She delivered her presidential address to the American Association of Law Libraries at its Chicago meeting in 1945, and when the printed copies came back to Berkeley that autumn, Oren Baskerville shelved the first set of them in the green ledger's own drawer, because the address was about law libraries and the law school's claim on the community, and a claim, he said, is a bill of lading. The cake was not yet written down when she delivered that address. It was written down the winter after she died, in October 1946, by whoever in this family could still take her measurements in grams, and the measurements have not been altered since, which is the only reason the preamble can stand as a record.

The degree, like the cake, takes three years of full-time study to complete, and the degree is conferred upon the student who has finished the coursework, the practical training, and the bar itself, in that order and no other. That is why the recipe has six sheets: two a year, for three years, the sixth sheet pressed thin because the third year is a practical year and the kitchen is asked to work faster than it likes. The family's standing rule, written at the top of the ledger page in the same hand as everything else on it, reads word for word: “In the first of its natural state be a castor of state.” The family has never been able to prove the sentence to be a quotation from any book, and it has never needed to. It means, in this kitchen, that the first ingredient — the butter — must go into the bowl in its first state and its natural state, that is to say cold, solid, and unapologetic, holding its shape the way a degree holds its name; a caster of state, the sentence says, and the butter of this cake has kept the instruction for a hundred years or nearly. If the butter goes in soft, the sheet goes wrong in the second semester of it and the whole tin is out of order, and Mrs. Rourke, who could answer 1,976 questions in a quarter and never once lose patience, has said that this one mistake is the only one that excuses a second attempt.

The cake is carried to the hall on the day the degrees are read, in the tin, in whatever weather, because a library that closes is a house with its lamp out and a family that skips the cake is a house with its butter melted. The fog off the bay does not matter. The horn that interrupted the memorial of 1946 at 3:04 does not matter. The cake has its own weather, and it is set.

The method, in six sheets

  1. Sheet one. The butter goes in first and in its state. Beat it against the sugar until the bowl keeps time. This sheet is the first year: it is long, it is the foundation, and it does not forgive.
  2. Sheet two. Flour and salt in by the side of the bowl, not through it. The family rule is that nothing enters a library, or a cake, in a heap. This is still the first year, and the year is not done.
  3. Sheet three. The eggs, one at a time, from the cold. The sour cream after, and the bowl must be beaten as long as the kitchen will take it. The first year ends here; the tin is set in the strong room, and the family does not open it.
  4. Sheet four. The second year opens with the chocolate grated the day before. This sheet is the case law of the cake; everything after it is decided by what it does. Set it on top of the tin and let it hold.
  5. Sheet five. The second year closes. The almond sheet is pressed thin and laid over, because the third year is a practical year and the kitchen must now work by doing, not by reading. Whatever is practiced must be done in the open, on the top of the tin, where it can be seen.
  6. Sheet six. The thinnest sheet, the almonds of it ground to a paste and brushed over the whole. The glaze is the bar: one pass, one reading of the degree, and the tin is closed. It is kept in the strong room with the green ledger and the gift albums, and it is carried to the hall in whatever weather.

Notes kept at the foot of the page

The sheet, scaled, serves the table at the number the handle shows; the tin keeps the rest. The same green ledger that holds the minutes of 11 November 1946 holds this card, and the ledger still holds both — read them in the minutes of session 214, where the cake's name is first entered in the acquisitions, and the foghorn that interrupted the reading is the horn the almanac's entry no. 47 on Alcatraz Island keeps counting at its fifteen seconds, the same bay, the same cold, the same weather the cake does not need to answer to.
