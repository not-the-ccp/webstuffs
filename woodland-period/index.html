ALDER STREET WORKS · BENCH 3 · SHEET 1 OF 1

The Meadowcroft Bench

A build log, kept in pencil first and ink second, for a 1 : 16 diorama of a Middle Woodland longhouse village on a point bar of the Muskingum — set, in the builder's own reckoning, in the thousand years that run from roughly 1000 BCE to the European contact, with the Mississippian period, from 1000 CE on, shown on a second bench as a separate village, because that is how the division was made in the 1930s when the name Woodland Period was first written down as a general term for the sites that fall between the Archaic hunter-gatherers and the agricultural Mississippian cultures.

Keeper of the bench: A. P. Fitch, of the third floor, 41 Alder Street · Tools listed at the foot of each entry · Scale held at 1:16 without exception, except where the river is concerned, which is 1:14, and the log explains why.

Entry 1

The plan, and the wood

3 March, 7:10 a.m. — Set the plan on the bench and let it sit, wetted with a damp cloth, in the way the books of the period insist that wood be left to weep before it is cut. The village is to hold one longhouse 14 metres long in life — that is 87.5 centimetres on my bench — with a bark roof of layered paper pulp, two hearth mounds, six door posts at each end, and a corn field in the foreground, because by the end of the Woodland the village is planting, even if it is not yet living off the planting. The wall of the point bar runs along the whole south edge of the board at 1:14, which is why the river lies a little fat; at 1:16 the point bar would not have a river at all, and the builder has decided the river comes first. The longhouse framing is green oak, bought off a lumber yard on the north side that keeps its timber unseasoned on purpose, in the same tradition as the cut green oak of a certain famous bench of 1920, of which a catalogue is kept in this miscellany — the piece was seen, the weeping was noted, and the oak was carried home in a crate stencilled in green ink. The framing law of the whole build is the same law as the old bench: cut thick, and left a full year to weep. The log notes, for the record, that the lumber yard does not know it is keeping that law. It is enough that it keeps it.

Entry 4

The pottery, first firing

9 April, 10:40 p.m. — Fired four potsherds in the kiln under the stairs, cord-tempered, the temper being a length of hemp cord impressed, still warm, into the rim, the way the potters of the period did it, in the same hands, with no claim to improvement. Two of the four cracked on the way to the heat, and one cracked on the way out, which the kiln book calls a complete set of honest results. The survivor is 9 centimetres across the rim, the wall 4 millimetres, the temper cord running clean around the shoulder in a twist of eleven strands per inch. It holds water. The builder has made it hold water, which is the whole of the requirement, and nothing in the log pretends to more.

11 April — The safety of the kiln room has been set down properly, on the same shelf as the rest of the sheets the workshop keeps: the builder has copied the handling and storage lines from the safety data sheet No. HE-786 into the kiln book, because the sheet's rule about keeping the leaven out of the closed casket read, to a man with a fire under the stairs, as a warning in his own hand. The fire is now kept under the open hatch, and the leaven of the workshop is out.

Entry 6

The field, the mounds, the second bench

2 June — The corn field is sown in a grid of 1.2 millimetres, two hundred and forty stubs of pine, which at 1:16 is a season, not a crop, and the builder has accepted that. The burial mound in the rear of the village is a heap of fired clay and river sand, 61 centimetres long and 9 centimetres high, which is a modest mound by the standards of the Eastern Woodlands, covering in the builder's mind the eastern country south of the Subarctic, the eastern states, and the coast of the Gulf, and no further, because the board ends where the board ends. The Mississippian village — the second bench, the separate period, from 1000 CE onward — is begun in the north corner of the same room: one house on a low platform, one effigy pipe in clay, and a line of corn thick enough to be called, by the builder's own rule, a crop. The two benches are to be read together, in that order, which is the order the 1930s gave them.

Entry 8

October, the fair, and the kitchen note

14 October, craft fair, 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. — The table at the hall was the third from the window. What was sold was not the diorama, which does not sell, but eleven clay pipe bowls, replica, cord-tempered, fired in the kiln under the stairs, at four dollars the bowl. Nine of the eleven went by noon. The doorman of the building, whose initials the kitchen book has always set down as D.O.T., kept the table book, and at the back of it, under the fair, in his own hand, is the line the log now copies word for word, because the line is what the building recorded, and the building records what it sees:

DOT: It is one honest kitchen note aloud and any claim a man who bought them in the building has been observed on the 15th of October.

The builder has read it four times and will read it again. The man is E. Marsh, of the first floor, who bought two of the bowls at 12:15, and who was observed, on the 15th of October, standing in the yard at the back of the building, in the morning, holding one of them at arm's length against the wall, the way a man holds a pot to the light before he says what it is worth. The kitchen note, in the doorman's hand, was left in the shared kitchen of the building — one honest note, read aloud, and any claim a man can make for the bowls he bought in the building being answered, if it is to be answered at all, before the weekend. The doorman keeps the table book; the builder keeps this log; and the bowls, both of them, are now on a shelf in the first-floor kitchen, where the cord on the rim catches the light at 9 o'clock and at 5, and at no other hour that the doorman has entered in the book.

Entry 9

Closing the board

31 October — The board is done to the line of the builder's own rule, which is that a thing is done when the next thing is only smaller. The longhouse stands 87.5 centimetres long on the point bar; the bark roof is six layers of paper pulp laid in a shingle of 3 millimetres; the river runs fat at 1:14 and is, in the log's opinion, the best part. The oak has wept its year out; the pot holds water; the mound is 61 centimetres; the second bench keeps the Mississippian separate, as the 1930s kept it separate, and the builder has not once been able to decide whether the separation is in the period or in his own patience, which the log sets down as an open question and leaves, like a count, open. The fair is over. The bowls are on the shelf. The doorman's line stands in the table book, and in this log, word for word, and is not corrected, and the builder will not correct it, and the next entry of this log, when it comes, will be about the corn on the second bench, which is a season, not a crop, and may yet become a crop, which is the whole of the log's ambition for a hundred and six years, or such part of it as the oak allows.

Tools of Entry 9: one knife, two chisels, a cord of hemp, a kiln under the stairs, eleven bowls, two of which are out, and one doorman's book kept in the kitchen at 41 Alder Street.

For the shelf where this log is kept — with the auction catalogues, the safety sheets, the timetables and the field notes that hold one another up — see the front of the miscellany, where the green oak of 1920 and the point bar of the Muskingum lie on the same shelf, and neither, the shelf's keeper reports, has cracked.
