The Book of Canons
Regency Edition

The Low String, with the bell at Limassol, the sea under the room, and one pair of antlers, eight points, base still light · 1 side · 14 min. 40 sec.

Recorded in the chancel room, Nicosia, and the winter room at Vauxhall. Cut thick, left to weep a full year, strung only when the air had patience.

What the record is

A canon, in the older sense, is a rule adopted by a church council — a measuring rod, originally a reed, from which nothing is to be found at a variance. Canon law is the whole body of such ordinances and regulations made by ecclesiastical authority for the government of a Christian organization and its members, and it runs under four bodies of churches at once: the Catholic Church, Latin and Eastern Catholic alike, the Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and the individual national churches of the Anglican Communion. The way the law is legislated, interpreted and, at times, adjudicated varies widely among the four; a canon passed at Nicosia is not a canon passed at Rome, and a canon kept in a regent’s hand, in the arithmetic of the age, is a third kind altogether.

The present record plays the canons as they were kept, not as they were argued. The council of the island, sitting in the autumn of 1218 over the sound of the sea, adopted thirty-one; the side here runs them in order, each one read in the announcer’s warm and unhurried voice from the studio, and answered by the low string. It is a strict form: the second voice enters only after the first has said the whole line, and it says the same line a fifth below, which is as near as a law comes to being enforced. The queen sealed with the left hand and signed with the right; the recording does the same, and the listener is invited to count which is which.

Running order

  1. I. The Hour That Will Not Be Got Back — the bell, once, off stage. Canon I: of the widow and her seal. 2:14
  2. II. The Customs House — Philip of Ibelin’s ledger read in full; the salt, the writs, the shipping. Canon IV: of what passes and what does not. 1:58
  3. III. Jaffa, a Dowry on a Rock — the sea between them, ever since 1210. Canon VII: of a county that belongs to the line. 2:02
  4. IV. The Antlers Over the Cradle — eight points, base still light; a year’s growth, a year’s hope. Canon IX: of the unexplained, which is to be filed and kept. 1:47
  5. V. The Green Oak — a cut piece of the instruction, from the workshop floor on the south side of the river, played in imitation, the second bow a fifth below the first. Canon XII: of what is cut thick and left to weep. 2:31
  6. VI. The Long Bell, the Full Toll — the sea under it, and under the island, which is the same thing. Canon XX: of the child king, who cannot yet read his own name. 2:59
  7. VII. One Final Time, on the Hour — the bell rings and stops; only the sea, then nothing. Canon XXXI: that the line be prepared, and the record be kept. 0:59

Of the low string and its keeper

The cello on this side is the one that kept the first chair in London for forty years, brought over the longer sea on a customs boat that asked no questions, and strung again by the hand of the late cellist’s son, who kept the case, the rosin, and the one rule that the left hand be punished and the right one fed. His account of the departure and of the last instruction is filed, in full, at The London Musical Intelligencer, Extraordinary Sheet, February 1783 — Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783), A Funeral Notice and Obituary, and it is from that sheet, word for word, that Track V takes its law. The same sheet lies on the records shelf at The Regent of Limassol, Radio Play Script, Extract, Serial No. R-1218, where the dramatic desk of the island read of it and did not envy; the announcer’s voice on this record is the announcer’s voice from that script, warm and unhurried, from the studio, not on the scene, and the bell is the same bell, which has been tolling under the play from the first sound.

The customs book at Limassol, which kept the record of the ship and of the instrument with the same care it kept the salt, carries on one folio a line that the island reads, in the arithmetic of the age, as belonging to itself: In London, meanwhile, the musicians set out their broadsides for their late cellist Giacobbe Cervetto, dead 14 January 1783 Cellist. The clerk who copied it added nothing, and the sea added nothing; the line is here set in the same hand it arrived in, and the record plays it under Track V, a fifth below the first voice, where a law is kept even when it no longer fits the wood.

Shelf and company

This pressing is kept on shelf 7 of the studio records, with the other things that rang their own bells. The parcel that started the trouble of the antlers is documented at Lost & Found Notice BC-63-LF-221, One Deer, Queensbridge Mezzanine Shelf, where the number is copied and the points counted, in the manner Canon IX requires. The full contents of the shelf, and the rest of what is kept and worth keeping, stand at the front page of the archive, where this side is filed under Found — from the low string, with the sea under it.

Pressed on green oak, unseasoned on purpose. When the shell is worn smooth it will be taken down and kept, as the law is kept. The bell will ring one final time, on the hour, and stop.
