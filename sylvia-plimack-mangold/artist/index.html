The Conductor’s Note

An artist is a person who creates art or practices the arts. That is the whole of the company’s charter, first clause. In the second clause the board of directors left the page blank, because while the use of the word is common, there is no agreed upon definition of the terminus, and a railway that cannot name its terminus cannot print the last row of its timetable. The company has therefore printed it blank, in good faith, for the third season running.

The most common usage in everyday speech and in the academic carriage refers to a practitioner of the visual arts, such as painting or drawing — she is the word’s first-class passenger and has always been. The term is also widely used in the entertainment business to refer to actors, musicians, singers, dancers, and other performers, who sign the company’s register in that column, in ink, since before the buffers were locked. The French word artiste is sometimes used in English in this context, although this has become old-fashioned; the company keeps it in the window of the observation lounge nonetheless. The use of the term “artist” to describe writers is valid, but less common, and mostly restricted to contexts such as critics’ reviews; “author” is generally used instead, and the ticket inspector accepts either.

Carriages of the Word

  • First Class — the visual artist. Painting, drawing, the hardwood floor, the window. The word’s home carriage; the board of directors met here before there was a building.
  • Second Class — the performers. Actors, musicians, singers, dancers, and other performers. Full in festival season; the platform crowd is obliged to count as scenery, not as passengers.
  • The Observation Car — the writer. Valid, less common, mostly the critics’ reviews. The car has a good window and “author” is generally used instead, which the conductor reads as courtesy, not as demotion.
  • The Artiste Lounge. A French observation room in the vestibule. The company notes it has become old-fashioned and keeps the light on anyway; the view out of it is the best on the train.

The Terminus Question

Travellers have asked, for three seasons, why the last row is blank. The answer is in the first clause of the charter: the word is used commonly, and commonly in more than one carriage, and no authority the company can find — the dictionary, the critics, the entertainment business, the ferry — has agreed upon the definition, which is to say the end of the line. A timetable with an agreed terminus could be printed in one sitting; AV-11 cannot. The conductor therefore ends the run at the studio in Washingtonville, where the painting happens, and leaves the rest of the route to the dust on the planks. For the same patience kept at lower stakes — hours of nothing, then everything at once, the fixed seat, the ring-road light — the company refers passengers to Santa Maria Bianca Hospital — Field Manual SM-B, whose admission desk observes the same hour the train arrives: 10:58, and nothing, and then the light goes down.

Passengers who would rather read the floor than the timetable may transfer, at any station, to the Sylvia Plimack Mangold thread on the Pentimento Board — floor, window, light, and the sill where the light lands at 3:17 — or walk the full length of the platform to the Webstuffs front desk, which checks your bags, asks nothing, and wishes you a pleasant day.
