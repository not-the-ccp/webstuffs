Morton L. Janklow Frequently Asked Questions

Morton Lloyd Janklow, May 30, 1930 – May 25, 2022 · the primary partner, the one with the pencil behind his ear · eleven questions, answered the way the desk answered them

Morton Lloyd Janklow was an American literary agent and the primary partner in Janklow & Nesbit Associates, a New York–based literary agency. If the file drawer is any guide, the agency lived in two rooms on the east side of Madison Avenue, a reading table near the window, and a card catalogue taller than the doorframe. He signed his first author in 1956, he died on May 25, 2022, nine days before his ninety-second birthday, and the last thing the ledger recorded was his initials, M.L.J., still sharp enough to be read across the room. This page collects the questions the receptionists were most often asked, and the answers they gave back, in the desk’s own order.

1 Who exactly was Morton L. Janklow?

Morton Lloyd Janklow, born May 30, 1930, was an American literary agent by trade and by temperament. “Literary agent” is the short form of a longer job: he read the manuscripts the room could not, argued the terms the publishers could not, and protected the authors the contracts could. He was the primary partner in Janklow & Nesbit Associates, which means that when the partners disagreed, the argument ended at his desk, usually around five o’clock, usually with a pencil moving a number two places.

2 Where did the agency operate, and since when?

New York, in the Madison Avenue rooms the desk kept from 1956 onward — the forty-sixth floor of a building whose lobby still has the brass directory plate, JANKLOW & NESBIT ASSOCIATES, set in the old capitals. The reading table stood near the window on the east side; the card catalogue, one card per author, per title, per advance, per argument, stood by the doorframe and outgrew it. The agency’s house rule was that the catalogue was answered the same day a question arrived, which is how a room of two partners and a typist managed, at its fullest, more than eighty authors at once.

3 Which clients did the desk represent?

The roster is the answer, and the roster is long: Barbara Taylor Bradford, whose historical novels sold in numbers the printers had to pre-order; Thomas Harris, who brought the red dragon to the desk in a brown envelope; Judith Krantz, whose novels the hotel bookstores reordered weekly; Anne Rice, at her finest hour; Sidney Sheldon, who wrote fast enough to keep the agents’ phones warm; Danielle Steel, whose backlists were a kind of furniture; Barbara Walters, on both sides of the desk; and Pope John Paul II, whose works the agency handled with the special discretion a papal pen requires. 4 What about the four U.S. presidents?

The desk represented four U.S. presidents — the card catalogue keeps four cards in the P drawer, and the receptionists were instructed never to say which four and to say, when pressed, the catalogue will tell you, not me. What can be said is what the ledger says: four memoirs passed through the Madison Avenue rooms, two of them at a moment when the White House copy was still wet, and the agency’s fee for discretion was the discretion itself. The room kept every draft, every red-pencil pass, and one photograph of a signing nobody has ever been allowed to date.

5 How did a book actually get a Janklow & Nesbit contract?

A manuscript arrived by post, by courier, or by a writer walking up the forty-five flights, and it went into the reading pile, where it waited in strict order of arrival. Morton read at his kitchen table at home, in the morning, before the office opened, and marked the page in pencil: yes, no, or call her. A yes went to the card catalogue that day; a call her went to the telephone, and the telephone was where most careers changed direction. The desk’s standard was a one-page letter, and the letter always began Your manuscript is the best I have read this quarter, which was a true statement the quarter it was written, by the desk’s own accounting.

6 Why “primary partner,” and who was the other side of the house?

The house was a partnership in the old sense: two names on the letterhead, one name on the signature line. Morton was the primary partner — the partner of first resort for the authors, the publishers, and the telephone. His partner ran the accounts, the reprints, and the royalties statements, and the two of them divided the room the way a co-op board divides a corridor: down the middle, politely, forever. When the primary partner left the room, the other kept the lights on, which is more than can be said for most houses.

7 How long did a client stay with the desk?

For the length of the client’s working life, which is how the catalogue came to be so tall. Barbara Taylor Bradford’s first card has her name in a young hand and a 1979 date on the reverse; Anne Rice’s card has seventeen titles and a note in the margin that reads simply never rush her; the Sidney Sheldon card has a running count of pages delivered per week, in three pencils of decreasing authority. A client who left for another agency left a gap in the P drawer, and the gaps are the only honest graph the house ever kept.

8 What did the desk think of the telephone?

The desk thought the telephone was the second office. New York answered New York, London answered London, and the Vatican answered its own hours, which the receptionists learned to respect by simply not ringing them. A famous call, in the ledger’s words, is a call that changed a number: the 1991 advance call that moved a six figure to a seven, the 1988 Hollywood call that moved a book to the screen within fourteen months, and one call in the spring of 2004 that the ledger records only as the long one, forty-one minutes, no notes taken, outcome the book was saved.

9 What happened on May 25, 2022?

Morton Lloyd Janklow died in New York on May 25, 2022, in his ninety-second year, nine days before the birthday the agency had circled in the almanac the way other houses circle the holidays. The reading table was set out as it had stood, the pencil behind the ear was not in the ear, and the ledger’s last entry is the date itself, with his initials beside it. The other partner kept the rooms for a season, the card catalogue kept its order, and the desk kept the question open — which is a Janklow & Nesbit way of keeping everything open.

10 Where else in the file does the desk appear?

The desk’s discipline of the uncorrected line travels. The funeral notice and obituary the file keeps for a London cellist of the eighteenth century, Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783), is set in the same hand the house would set its own, and the two are filed together in the O drawer, because an obituary is an obituary at any altitude. And the question-and-answer form this page borrows is the form the road keeps for itself: the FAQ on U.S. Route 11W from Bristol to Knoxville answers its Clinch gap the way this desk answers a rights question — directly, on one page, with the true statement first.

11 And the line the ledger kept singing?

The ledger at the desk had one line it would not settle, kept the way the house keeps everything: uncorrected, and read as a tolerance. It sat at the foot of the last page, in the small hand the receptionists used for margins, and it reads, word for word, as found:

For another far-future entry from Webstuffs: Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) — Obituary , the old ledger has kept singing for a small cross, and nothing has been washed out.

The desk never explained the line. The catalogue, which answers every other question the same day, gave it the one treatment it reserved for lines it was not ready to argue with: it kept it, it read it aloud at the annual count of the P drawer, and it let it stand. Nothing in the room was washed out over it; the pencil marks, the four P cards, and the singing line share the page still, and the room, on the days the window is open, sounds faintly like a room in which a ledger is being read aloud, slowly, by someone who has all the time the ledger has left.
