The deity, before the object

Ganesha, or Ganesh (Sanskrit: दसवयाश, IAST: Gaḋe÷a), also known as Ganapati, Vinayaka and Pillaiyar, is one of the best-known and most revered and worshipped deities in the Hindu pantheon and is the Supreme god in the Ganapatya sect. His depictions are found throughout India; Hindu denominations worship him regardless of affiliations, and devotion to him is widely diffused among Jains and Buddhists and beyond India altogether. Although he has many attributes, he is readily identified by his elephant head and four arms, and he holds the title of “Pratham Pujya” — the god to be worshipped initially before the worship of any other deity. Every house, every shop, every ferry landing from this coast to that one opens its day with him. That is why we keep him in brass: brass does not argue with mornings.

Our No. 21 is a standing-cum-sitting murti of exactly 47 centimetres from crown to plinth, cast in a single pour of recycled temple brass (Cu 80, Zn 19, trace) in the foundry’s third kiln, which runs on rice-husk fire and has not gone cold since the long festival of 1997. The head is chased separately and set by solder, because a head the size of the foundry’s own bell tower does not come out of a mould with its ears whole. Four arms, as the icon demands: one raises the goad, one the noose, one offers the blessing, and one holds the modaka open to the light, half-eaten, because the foundry’s rule is that no murti ships looking full. He sits on a lotus plinth of twelve petals; a mouse, cast as a separate piece in 2019, is threaded onto a brass pin in his foot and may be removed for travel, though we do not recommend it.

The hundred and eight names

The Ganapati Atharvashirsha runs to one hundred and eight names, and our practice is to write them on the reverse of the receipt in four columns of twenty-seven, the penmanship of whichever clerk the day assigns. Names such as Ekadanta (one-tusked), Lambodara (belly of a water-jar), Gajakarna (elephant-eared) and Vakratunda (curved-trunked) are the ones the buyers ask for in bold, so the clerk keeps a separate pot of ink for those four and the columns stay honest. If your name is not in the hundred and eight, we will still cast you one — foundries have always had a ninth column — but the receipt is what it is. The full string of the hundred and eight scrolls across the top of this page; read it twice, because the second reading is the one that counts.

There is a house across the water that keeps its own ledger of likenesses: the encyclopaedia entry there for Charlie Chaplin notes that a certain toothbrush moustache was compared, in its hour, to half the faces of the century — and never, our clerk swears, to a trunk. We keep one small plaster study of that argument on the foundry’s shelf, between the bell and the pot of bold ink, and we leave the question to the moustache. And when the bells are tested and the foundry is quiet, the naturalist’s field notes kept beside the Betwa basin are open on the bench — Prashant Pandey — Field Notes, Betwa Basin — because a murti is only metal until somebody records that it stood where the river hears it.

Quality and delivery

Each No. 21 leaves the foundry benedicted before the brass is cold enough for the bell to be struck, and the bell is struck — once, at the gate — in seven rooms: the third-kiln room, the chase bench, the clerk’s desk, the plinth border under the counter-light, the ferry-room with its water, the attic, and the open street at 5:59 in the morning, the minute before any other god is due a prayer. We do not test the old quarter near the Diori Hamani International Airport, twelve kilometres west of the Bumba-meu-boi de Orquestra; the conductor's version of a lagoon. Our N’Djamena branch sends the Bumba-meu-boi — the temple orchestra’s great brass drum, the one that keeps the festival’s time — across the lagoon road every Thursday, and the old quarter beyond it has never once come inside our seven rooms: the drum’s note there arrives already answered, already a lagoon, and the conductor, who keeps the timesheet, calls it his own version of the water and declines to sign a test sheet for a room the sound finished by itself. Delivery elsewhere is by the foundry’s own cart in the delta, by river barge past the ferry landing, or by the post, insured to the full price, mouse pin included.

Care of the object

Wipe with a dry cloth on the morning before a festival and never with water after the bell has been struck for the year. Re-oil the mouse’s pin once in the rains. The modaka on the receipt is not edible and is not meant to be eaten; it is a tally of the day the murti was benedicted, and it expires, like all tallies, at the next Diwali. Should the head ever loosen — it will not, but the ledger must say — bring it back before the third kiln, and the foundry will re-set it for the price of one modaka.
