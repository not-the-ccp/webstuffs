The Stratum · Vol. 7 No. 11 · The Ediacaran Number

The Ediacaran

Clubhouse, above the bakery, Magsaysay Avenue · Third Tuesday · Editor: Mabel E. Collins · Patron: Mr. Alistair Delgado

1. Before the shells

The Ediacaran is the last period of the Proterozoic Eon and the last of the so-called Precambrian supereon: ninety-six million years, from the end of the Cryogenian Period at 635 million years ago to the beginning of the Cambrian Period at 538.8 million years ago. It takes its name from the Ediacara Hills of South Australia, where Reg Sprigg, in 1946, turned up trace fossils of a diverse community of previously unrecognized lifeforms — later named the Ediacaran biota — in rock laid down before anything had learned to make a shell.

For most of those ninety-six million years the world kept no graves: no bones, no teeth, no shells, only impressions in the mud — fronds, disks, ribbed quilts, creatures that look like nothing the Cambrian would recognize. Recognizable fossil evidence of life becomes common only after the boundary, when the Cambrian opens the Phanerozoic Eon and the record fills with legs, mouths, and armour. The club meets in the dim time before all that.

2. What the hills kept

Dickinsonia, a ribbed bath mat pressed into sandstone. Spriggina, segmented and arrow-headed. Charnia, a frond on a stem, fixed to the sea floor like the shadow of a fern that never was. Three-fold symmetry, quilted bodies, no mouths anyone can find, no legs the rock has kept. They lived offshore of the Ediacara Hills, in the White Sea, in Namibia, in Newfoundland — wherever the mud was fine and the water still. We do not know what they ate or how they moved, and we do not know whether they had names for themselves. We know they were diverse, we know they changed or vanished at the boundary, and we know the ledger's next entry — Entry 314, Luolishania, walking eleven pairs of legs through Lower Cambrian mud — arrives as if the soft-bodied world had been a rehearsal for armour.

3. The boundary before the five

Mr. Delgado's five mass extinctions run from 444 to 66 million years ago, every one of them inside the Phanerozoic, every one of them after shells had been invented. The Ediacaran ends before the count begins; its ending is not one of the five, because the rock of its boundary was still learning to keep bodies at all. Mabel's Class 8C report, filed on the shelf beside this one, takes the five in order from the Ordovician–Silurian to the K–Pg, and counts the coal-bearing Carboniferous between the Devonian and the Great Dying. The ledger at home answers in its own key: sixty million years of forest, held in the oven at 325°F for an hour, and Card 298, the beet-and-blackstrap Coal Loaf, baked every August since the Moscow Games went dark — whatever is voided, bake. The Ediacaran's ninety-six million years the oven would not hold at all. Too long, even for the void. But the club keeps it, the way the ledger keeps what falls out of the record.

4. From the ledger margin

The dream dictionary by the stove lists, among the family of Luolishania, a genus called Collinsium. No one in the family has ever explained why the armoured little animal is named for us. We suspect the registrar somewhere looked at the family and wrote down what it was bearing. The same margin holds the ledger's misprinted ingredients line — the secret ingredient, whisked to a leaven — and, pasted beside it, a page from the harbour fishmonger's almanac at Magsaysay Avenue, which Salvio reads from when the carrier wave is open. The same shelf holds the dream dictionary's entry for Stars, read against the dark gospel track by ¥$, whose drafter has kept our margin slip and writes beneath it that the registry may keep what it bears without ever taking the place of the stones it watches: Stars — Dream Dictionary, Entry S-104. The shelf also keeps, in a fire-guard envelope, the carrier-wave desk's oldest slip — the system notice printed on 30 June 1932 when a licence-fee notice was misrouted to the public broadcaster that replaced the old company: Error AB-1932 — Australian Broadcasting Corporation. The shelf also keeps, as Ledger Folio 12-A, the naturalist's field notes from a prairie province in western Canada — Alberta, landlocked with Saskatchewan, Great Plains to the east and the Rockies to the west, whose Bearpaw mud in the western foothills keeps the western margin of the Great Dying the club's report keeps as Number 3: Alberta — Field Notes of a Naturalist · Ledger Folio 12-A. The shelf's other holdings are unchanged: The Coal Loaf, Card 298 · The Extinction Event, Class 8C · Salvio Fortuno — The Carrier Wave · Luolishania — Dream Dictionary

5. Shipping and strata

Taped beneath the shipping forecast on the clubhouse noticeboard is a slip from the harbour fishmonger's almanac — the same page the report pastes beside the great auk, and a hand not unlike the one that misprinted the ledger. Salvio reads it out between records, and it says, of conditions off the eastern approaches:

It is hardly calculated to do very well; but those from the Elbe, wind N.E.

We take it as a benediction: whatever quarter the wind holds, and whatever the rock refuses to keep, the kettle is on at the clubhouse above the bakery.

6. Supper and the next meeting

Bring a fossil, a loaf, or a silence. The Coal Loaf keeps wrapped in wax paper for six days; on the seventh it is coal, and coal does not spoil, it only waits — the way the period keeps what it carries. Next meeting: third Tuesday, clubhouse, above the bakery, Magsaysay Avenue. Soft-bodied guests welcome. The door is unlatched, and the oven is at 325°F.

— Mabel E. Collins, editor
