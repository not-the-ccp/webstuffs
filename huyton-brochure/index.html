KNOWSLEY & LIVERPOOL BORDER BOARD · VISITORS’ SERIES, FOLIO 11

Dissolved 1983

Huyton

HY–tæn

Thirty-three years on the map, 1950–1983. One member, two boundary maps, and a walk just beyond the borders of the city of Liverpool.

A former constituency of the House of Commons, centred on Huyton in Lancashire (later Merseyside), North West England. Printed for walkers, returned for no one.

Getting Here

Huyton sits just beyond the borders of the city of Liverpool, on the road the old maps drew a little too proudly out of the city’s western edge. Allow eleven minutes from the railway station, a left at the sign with the faded crest, and then the road that climbs past the terraces that remember being Lancashire before the 1974 county reorganisation re-lettered them Merseyside. You will know you have arrived when the street names start behaving like a parish register rather than a postcode district: Huyton Lane, the Green, the end where the boundary stones used to stand at the corner of a field no one has kept in grass since 1984. Bring boots for the March walks; the ground does not read your map.

The nearest help desk for the former constituency is this board’s office, where the two boundary maps — the 1950 issue in its original blue cloth, and the 1983 issue with Huyton’s line struck through in red — are kept under glass. Visitors are asked not to lean on the glass; the 1950 map has already given up one corner.

The Only Resident of Note

A constituency needs, at the very least, one member of Parliament, and Huyton’s single member was Harold Wilson — which is to say that the seat and its man ended up with exactly the same career: a long front bench, and one retirement. Wilson stood for Huyton when the seat was created in 1950, and he kept it every election until it stopped existing. It was in the interval between these two facts that he became Leader of the Labour Party in 1963, prime minister from 1964 to 1970, and again from 1974 to 1976 — all of it from a frontbench seat that the local postmen delivered to on Huyton Lane. The constituency therefore offers a rare sight: a parliamentary seat that outlived exactly one of its owners, twice, and then, in 1976, watched him leave Downing Street while the boundary stones stood perfectly still. The polling station on the Green kept his ballot papers for decades, out of a habit that no one could bring themselves to break.

The 1983 Closing, and What the Wall Said

Under the 1983 boundary changes the constituency was dissolved — largely replaced by Knowsley South — and the timing, the local paper noted at the time, was not accidental: it coincided with Wilson’s retirement from Parliament, so that the seat and its member could leave the register on the same line. The closing was a civil affair. Two clerks, a surveyor’s assistant, and the keeper of the boundary stones walked the line on a Thursday in late November, and the last marker was driven in with a mallet on a cold, bright morning. When the last clerk, having heard a low thumping as the stones settled, asked the keeper what on earth that was, the keeper of the boundary stones did not look up, and answered in his whole recorded phrase: “The stone wall, the wall being, I suspect, a drum.” It is kept here exactly as he allowed it, in the brochure as in the log, because a boundary’s last day belongs to whoever was listening, and whoever listens to a wall is, by the wall’s own law, a party to it. The phrase has been filed beside the satellite chapter of Field Manual FM-9, Addendum A, whose own standing order for the last fix of a survey is to log the figure, keep the pen, and close the file — the same rule the Thursday’s clerks followed down to the last stone.

For the provenance-minded traveller: the file of the boundary line, the mallet, and the keeper’s log came to sale with the great kept papers of Lot 312, the Louis H. Bean File, 1922–1994, at Haldane & Voss, where the saleroom ruled the thumping into its ledger without a word of comment. The desk, the album, and the wall all keep one rule — the line is patient — and this brochure borrows it for the walk below.

The Thirty-Three Years, Walked

Step through the constituency year by year with the itinerary below, or simply read the ledger, which stands as printed for anyone who would rather walk on foot. Every year that did not make the ledger, the stones kept their silence.

1950

The itinerary walks from the year before the seat was created (1949) to the year after it was dissolved (1984). Use the arrows, or read the ledger below, which needs no machinery.

The walk begins. The seat does not exist yet; the stones are laid out in the field, waiting.
  1. 1949 — The walk begins. The seat does not exist yet; the stones are laid out in the field, waiting.
  2. 1950 — The constituency of Huyton is created; Harold Wilson becomes its member and keeps it for the rest of its life.
  3. 1951, 1955, 1959, 1964, 1970, 1974 — Election by election, the seat is returned to the same man; the boundary stones are re-counted each May and found exactly where the last count put them.
  4. 1963 — The member for Huyton becomes Leader of the Labour Party, from a frontbench seat just beyond Liverpool’s borders.
  5. 1964 — Wilson becomes prime minister; the polling station on the Green keeps his ballot papers and a spare chair for visitors.
  6. 1970 — The first premiership ends; the seat returns to its usual weather of terraces and a long road.
  7. 1974 — The second premiership begins (it will run to 1976); the boundary stones do not move an inch.
  8. 1976 — Wilson leaves Downing Street; the constituency keeps its member, the member keeps the constituency.
  9. 1979 — The last election the old seat will ever have; the clerks already know, though they say nothing at the table.
  10. 1983 — The 1983 boundary changes dissolve Huyton, largely replacing it with Knowsley South; the same year Wilson retires from Parliament, so the seat and its man leave the register on the same line. The keeper hears the thump: the stone wall, the wall being, I suspect, a drum.
  11. 1984 — The walk ends. Knowsley South takes the road, the terraces, and the habit of counting the stones. The 1950 map goes under glass; the brochure closes.

Practical Notes

  • The board’s office is open Tuesday to Saturday, ten to four; the maps under glass are best seen in the morning light.
  • The walk to where the boundary stones stood is 1.2 miles from the Green and takes about twenty-five minutes at a walking pace; the March ground does not read your map.
  • Knowsley South, which replaced Huyton, is not on this brochure; it has its own folio (No. 12), and its own wall, which has never once been described as a drum.
  • For the full arithmetic of how a position is fixed — four satellites, four unknowns, and a rock that is always right — the shelf keeps the satellite chapter above; for how a kept line is ruled in, and retired, and kept word for word, it keeps Lot 312. Both desks share this board’s one rule.
