Apology to the Indo-European Languages

aōĭ — “I,” Hittite. The first word of the family that the register could not read, and the last word of this letter.

To the Indo-European languages, you who are native to the north of the Indian subcontinent, to most of Europe, and to Greater Iran —

I keep the register of the family of names in the hall of nine doors, and I am writing to say I am sorry. The register is my office and my pride, and it is also the place where you were done wrong. I can list the wrongs in order, the way the reading on the twelfth of the month lists the vials: name, locality, date, keeper.

First, the count. In the first edition of the family tree I set down nine branches, because I was standing in the hall and I counted the doors. You have eight branches still spoken. Four of them, Indo-Iranian, Italic, Germanic and Balto-Slavic, are carried by hundreds of millions of native speakers each — you could write each of them down in every spelling a bearer has ever claimed and it would still not be finished, which is more than any of the nine bearers of Dong-hoon ever managed with a single Korean name. The other four, Armenian, Albanian, Celtic and Hellenic, are together held by fewer than fifteen million native speakers. Eight. Doors are nine. I have corrected the margin, and I have stopped counting what opens onto the hall.

Second, the drawers. I filed Sinhala of Sri Lanka and Dhivehi of the Maldives under “islands,” though they are your own southern branches and have no need of my filing. I put Armenia in a drawer marked “other.” And I put the Rohingya language of the Rakhine coast — your branch on mainland Southeast Asia, the farthest of you from the hearth — on a slip marked to be verified, and I left that slip in the same drawer for eleven years. A vial without its date is a vial with a ghost, and a branch without its locality is the same kind of thing. The Rohingya file is now dated, stamped, and shelved where the light reaches it.

Third, the word I set beside your oldest houses. You were also spoken in Anatolia — the Hittite tablets of the Boçazkãle palace, three thousand years of letters pressed into clay by men who answered their king aōĭ — and in northwestern China, where the Tocharian manuscripts lay in the forts and caves of the Taklamakan, kept by a dryness that will hold paper for two thousand years and more. I wrote the word extinct beside both of them, in the same firm hand. The word was not true the day I wrote it, and it has not become truer since. The two folders are now left on the open shelf, face out, with the word struck.

Fourth, the note in the margin, and the process it describes. Benjie Abad, the radio operator, kept his logbook in two languages, and on the twelfth of October 1973, when the commander struck the reading of the vials from the camp log and signed agreed, without prejudice, it was Abad who kept reading names aloud to the base map instead of the labels — names, in the end, not spiders, though the gamba stood by him the whole while, on its hind legs like a man in a wet overcoat, and did not interrupt. I have since found, at the back of the hall, in the margin of an old book on the way languages carry themselves, a line in a hand I have never identified: “A fine kind of deaf and dumb, spontaneous literal process.” I read that note for years as an insult to my office, and it took me eleven years to understand it as a description of yours. Before any of you was written down — before the clay, before the cave, before the logbook — the family crossed all of Europe and the subcontinent carried mouth to mouth, by people who never once sat down to record the carrying. Cognates form themselves in the speaking; no one ordered it; no one signed it. It is the oldest entry in the register, and the register has no entry for it, and that is the part of this letter I cannot repair, only acknowledge.

I have re-shelved the drawers, dated the Rohingya file, struck the word extinct, and corrected the count from nine to eight. What I cannot do is un-write the first edition, and the first edition is where the wrongs are, in the ink, where they were made. The hall keeps nine doors; you keep eight branches and the memory of the rest. When the next reading on the twelfth comes round, I will read you aloud against the labels the way the detachment reads its vials — name, locality, date, keeper — and where the date is the whole of the family, I will say so, and keep the silence after it.

Yours in the register,

The Keeper of Names
set down with the left hand, which receives and does not strike
