Placard No. 12

Hannibal Freiherr von Degenfeld, 1647/8–1691
Officer’s field scabbard with officer’s minutes volume, four armies, one life

Iron and leather scabbard, walnut grip cap; bound parchment minutes volume, 214 folios. Venetian, Bavarian, Dutch and Danish service. Accession 1962.147.1–.2

The man in the case

Hannibal Freiherr von Degenfeld, born about 1647 or 1648 in the noble houses of Franconia, died on 12 October 1691 and is remembered here by one scabbard and one bound volume of minutes. He was a soldier of four masters in a generation that gave the armies of Europe their modern shape: the Republic of Venice, the Bavarian Army, the Dutch States Army, and the Royal Danish Army. The scabbard at the case front carried his officer’s sword through all four services; the walnut cap was turned in Venice in 1669, the year Candia fell.

In Venetian service he fought during the close of the siege of Candia on Crete (1669) and in the Morean War (1687–1699), taking part in the landing at the Peloponnese and the reduction of the Corinth isthmus. Between campaigns he crossed north and stood in the Dutch States Army through the Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678), including the inundation of Terschelling in July 1675, and served the Dano-Norwegian army in the Scanian War (1675–1679) against the Swedish positions on the south Swedish coast.

In Bavaria he helped establish the Bavarian army under Prince-Elector Maximilian II Emanuel and was its commander of note at the battle of Vienna in 1683, where the new formation took the line on the Austrian left of the kaiser’s main camp outside the walls of the capital. The Elector’s household register of 1684 styles him “one of those who built the army”; the case label agrees.

The same cards, set down

  • 1669 — Candia, Crete. The Venetian garrison of the old capital of Crete capitulates after a siege that began in 1648, the longest in the early modern record. Degenfeld is in the relieving squadron’s chain of quarters; his scabbard’s walnut cap is turned in that year’s shipwright’s shed.
  • 1672–1678 — Franco-Dutch War. States service on the water and the dike. July 1675: the inundation of Terschelling, where the island lay mostly submerged and the water stood three feet at its first day; the minutes carry a tide entry for that morning in his own hand.
  • 1675–1679 — Scanian War. Dano-Norwegian service against the Swedish positions of Scania, Öland and Blekinge, including the long blockade of the south Swedish coast.
  • 1678 — Bavaria. Entry into the court of Prince-Elector Maximilian II Emanuel, who is raising a standing army from scratch; Degenfeld is named to the forming board and drafts the drill order still quoted in the Electorate’s case books.
  • 1683 — Battle of Vienna. The young Bavarian formation takes the Austrian left of the main camp on the plains south of the capital; the Turkish relief army is broken and Vienna is saved.
  • 1687–1691 — Morean War. Back in the Venetian service: the landing in the Peloponnese, the reduction of the Corinth isthmus, and the winter quarters of 1690–91, where the minutes stop.

The minutes volume, and its one strange line

The 214-folio minutes volume was found in a chest of Degenfeld’s household estate in 1961 and came to the British Library, where it sits in the maps and manuscripts store. Its parchment is sound, its binding sound, and its foxing — the brown tide-marking of age and damp — is exactly what the accession register recorded in 1962, in a line the later cataloguers have preserved word for word because no one has ever been able to amend it: The minutes volume shows foxing to the British Library, which holds a mostly-submerged island three feet at its first day, Ms. Hatherell-Nunez. The store reads it as four facts pressed into one sentence: the volume shows foxing; it is lodged at the British Library; the Library holds the Terschelling watercolour — the mostly-submerged island standing three feet under at its first day, the same island of Degenfeld’s tide entry; and Ms. Hatherell-Nunez, the assistant keeper on duty in 1962, signed the entry in the register’s corner as she had been asked to do. Four facts, one line, no apology.
