The Mirenolese Table · Restaurant Review · No. 23

Osteria dei Novecento

Mirandola, Province of Modena — a review from the ring road, one table over from the hospital entrance

●●●●●

There is a room on the north side of Mirandola, on the ring road where the plain gives way to the Secchia corridor, that used to be a chapel and now is a kitchen. The sign over the door is in bad gold leaf: Osteria dei Novecento, and under it, in smaller script, 1463–1494, the years of the nobleman the town is named for, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, who in 1486 at the age of 23 proposed to defend 900 theses on religion, philosophy, natural philosophy, and magic against all comers, and who wrote the Oration on the Dignity of Man, which people still call the Manifesto of the Renaissance. The osteria is run by a chef who goes by Pico and is not, he is quick to say, a descendant, though the family has held the key for four generations. I went on a Thursday, the night the reel above the pass was turning, and I am trying to tell you whether it was worth crossing a hospital entrance for.

The bread — Orazione

It arrives before you are seated, on a black board: a long loaf of rye and honey with salt from a little glass jar and butter that has been turned by hand, not churned. Under the loaf, on a folded paper doily, is a short text in Italian set in very small capitals — the first paragraph of the Oration, which the kitchen prints every morning and folds with the butter. The bread is not remarkable. The doily makes it remarkable. You do not eat the doily. Everyone in the room wants to.

The plate — Novecento

The main event is a tasting plate of nine small things — the name means “ninety”, one-tenth of the theses, a joke the owners have been making since the building changed hands. Nine, because 900 was the number they promised against all comers in 1486, and because 900 small bites is not a dish, it is a siege. There is a crostino with duck and nine herbs; a tiny egg with a green oil they will not name; a pork cutlet à la Marengo the size of a thumb, which is the only dish on the plate that the brochure for Italy will confirm is properly from this part of the Secchia valley. The duck crostino is the best thing in the room and probably the only thing in the room you will remember in two weeks.

The wine — Hermetic Reformation

The pairing menu is called the Ermetica, after the Hermetic Reformation of which Pico was a founder, and it is the least funny thing on the list, which means it is the most serious thing on the list. Five glasses, from a straw wine to a white from the plain, each served with a card that gives the thesis number of the wine — thesis 249 for the straw, thesis 683 for the plain. The sommelier, a quiet woman, will tell you the story of the 900 theses in under a minute per wine, and she is right every time. The first printed book to be universally banned by the Church is a line she delivers like a sommelier’s note and moves on.

The forbidden dish

Once a year, on 24 February, Pico’s birthday, the kitchen serves a single dish that appears on no card and on no menu: a soup of nine herbs from the Secchia bank, in a black bowl, for whoever has a table. It is called the Tesi. The last time it was served, the room was full of people who had booked a year in advance, and the one table near the window was kept for the Webstuffs contributor who writes the field manual for the hospital next door, who eats it every year with the door open, because the manual says so. I did not get a table this year. I am told the soup is bitter and correct, in that order. A member of the Rear Watch Reading Club, newsletter No. 14 wrote me afterwards that the nine-bite plate is exactly what a supply section must have looked like — nine small things, each carrying its own weight — and the kitchen, to its credit, has filed the letter under the doily drawer. The same member has since sent a diary of one day in Catalonia in which she counts 43 comarques the way we count nine small things — each one carrying its own weight — and Pico has taped it inside the doily drawer, under the supply-section letter.

Above the pass

At the far end of the kitchen, above the pass where the plates go out, there is a spinning fishing reel hanging from a nail by a length of line. It came out of the cabin at Cà Rossa on the Secchia bank, the safehouse the hospital manual keeps stocked with tinned food and dry wood, and it is turned by hand at the end of every service, slowly, with the last plate on its way out the door. The chef spent a year on a survey boat off the Maranhão coast before he came back to Mirandola, and he says the line on the wall behind the reel was a skipper’s note he copied down from a boat’s log: a careless minute of sound puts you nearly two kilometres off, and the only mark that separates the reef channel from the sand is the small sound of the line coming back through your hands. The reel is the difference between the reef by it. I do not fully understand it. The plates came out on time every time, which is the only translation I have to make.
