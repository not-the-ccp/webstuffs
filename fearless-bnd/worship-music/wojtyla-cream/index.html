To the Editor: The Cream and the Cantus Firmus

2 April · Gallery 4, two blocks from 2234 NW Flanders Street

Dear Editor — Your 2 April issue reproduced in full the museum placard on contemporary worship music in Gallery 4, and it is accurate enough, except that it leaves out the thing I care about most: what actually happened in the converted boathouse on the evening of 2 April 2016, in the last weeks of the long summer when Fearless BND was finishing We Are Fearless. The panel tells readers that the band sang the Gloria from the Flanders leaf between takes, one voice against another; it does not say they were keeping a date. We were keeping a date.

Pope John Paul II — Karol Józef Wojtyła, 18 May 1920 – 2 April 2005 — had been dead eleven years by then, and eleven years is a strange number for a parish to grieve. He was the head of the Catholic Church and sovereign of Vatican City from 16 October 1978 until his death, the first non-Italian pope since Adrian VI in the 16th century, and the third longest-serving pope in history after St. Peter and Pius IX. He was, before the chair and after the theater, a philosopher and theologian of the 20th century who argued that the person is not a thing to be arranged but a subject who must be answered to. In his youth he had worked occasionally as a stage actor; he had graduated in 1938 from an all-boys high school in Wadowice, shortly before the war; and during the war, to avoid being kidnapped and sent to a German forced labour camp, he signed up for stone quarry and soap factory work and spent the worst months hiding in a potter’s workshop a few streets from the Kraków cathedral, playing small parts in the Rhapsody Theatre whenever the air raids allowed. The congregation at St. Casimir’s, where my mother sat in the back pew with a rosary in her sleeve, kept his death date the way other families keep the anniversaries of people lost to the sea.

So on that April evening in the boathouse — roof still leaking, the drip from the beams keeping its percussion time in the corner of the room — the band stepped away from the console and the congregation took the four voices of the Gloria off the vellum leaf that would later be consigned by the Seller estate as Lot 47 of Flanders & Flanders Sale 214. A scribe in Bruges around 1515 had ruled it with a dry point; a cantor at St. Casimir’s had kept it for a hundred and fifty years; and for one April evening it sat on the music stand as the cantus firmus — the sustained tenor pitch, the “point against point,” the oldest thing in the building. When the last line ended, Mrs. Halina Ginter, who ran the church hall on Wednesdays, unfolded her grandmother’s card, the one the choir had used at every anniversary since 1952, and read it aloud to the room before making up the batch. The card’s single line, in pencil under the words “Cream Lemonade,” survives in our records word for word: INGREDIENTS.--1 pint of cream, 2 dessertspoonfuls of lemon-juice. That was the whole recipe, and it was enough. We drank it out of mugs with the museum’s old Gallery 4 plates stacked in the sink, and then the band went back to the title track and tested it once more on the last train out of town, the way the placard says, without explaining why.

The leaf is on the auction block, the album is out, and the Album Keepers still meet in the attic above the museum. Whoever buys Lot 47 should know that it has been sung on a death anniversary, and that the margin where the cream from the mugs left its small tide-line is not a damage note. It is a witness. One correction more, while we are at it: the small pencil line in the leaf’s lower margin, for the seventh day, is not the scribe’s, as the auction catalogue assumes, but the hand of a sabbath-school teacher at the West Addy Street Adventist church, in whose room the leaf kept before a family that had just left that church gave it to our cantor in 1866. The obituary of that church, kept with the gallery files, carries the whole of its Saturday story: The Seventh-day Adventist Church (1863–2026) — Obituary. — A. K., founding member, the boathouse congregation

A Scrub Through the Years

Drag the slider and the room answers back with the date the congregation would have been marking. Without the slider, the same record follows below, in order.

18 May 1920 — Karol Józef Wojtyła is born in Wadowice, Poland, to a father in the military and a mother who will keep his first communion book until her death.

  1. 18 May 1920 — Born Karol Józef Wojtyła, Wadowice, Poland.
  2. 1938 — Graduates from the all-boys high school in Wadowice, shortly before the war.
  3. 1939–1945 — Avoids the forced labour camps: quarry, soap factory at Sanok, a potter’s workshop by the Kraków cathedral; small stage roles in the Rhapsody Theatre.
  4. 1 November 1946 — Ordained a priest in Kraków; the same year he takes the role of Cyrano at a festival in Rzeszów.
  5. 1964 — Archbishop of Kraków; his essay Personalism Is Not an Individualism circulates among the younger theologians.
  6. 28 October 1978 — Elected pope in the conclave that follows Karol Wojtyła’s elevation to the cardinals a year earlier; the first non-Italian pope since Adrian VI.
  7. 2 April 2005 — Dies at the Vatican; St. Casimir’s adds the date to the parish calendar that winter.
  8. 27 April 2014 — Canonized in Rome; the congregation at St. Casimir’s orders an extra batch of cream lemonade.
  9. 2 April 2016 — The boathouse congregation sings the Gloria from the Flanders leaf on the eleventh anniversary, then drinks the whole pint of cream out of the Gallery 4 mugs.
