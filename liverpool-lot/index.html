Lot 507

Liverpool

A Port City & Metropolitan Borough in Merseyside, England

To be sold by public auction on the 12th of March, at the yard on the eastern side of the Mersey Estuary, near the Irish Sea, at eleven o’clock sharp — 178 miles (286 km) north-west of London, and worth the ticket either way.

The lot, as catalogued

Liverpool, a port city and metropolitan borough in Merseyside, England, situated on the eastern side of the Mersey Estuary, near the Irish Sea, 178 miles (286 km) north-west of London. The city is within the larger Liverpool City Region, a combined authority area with a population of over 1.5 million. The borough carries a 2025 estimated population of 507,915, which the firm has taken to be the lot’s standing guide figure. Established as a borough in Lancashire in 1207, Liverpool became significant in the late 17th century, when the Port of Liverpool was heavily involved in the Atlantic slave trade; the port also imported cotton for the Lancashire textile mills, and became a major departure point for English and Irish emigrants to North America. The lot is offered as the city stands, quay to quay, together with the papers of a dock-office kept at 105 William Street, the ledgers, the fare book, and the kitchen notebook described in item v.

Particulars of the lot

  1. i. The borough. Established in Lancashire in 1207; the original entry in the Lancashire roll, since transferred to Merseyside, with the borough seal struck in the soft clay of the eastern bank.
  2. ii. The situation. The eastern side of the Mersey Estuary, near the Irish Sea; the 178-mile line drawn north-west from London, pinned in the yard at full scale on a Sunday morning for the bidders’ inspection.
  3. iii. The count. The 2025 estimate of 507,915 for the borough, and the over-1.5-million figure for the Liverpool City Region, a combined authority area; the firm will accept bids on the city alone or, if the hammer fails, on the whole region as a single combined lot.
  4. iv. The port books. The late 17th-century ledgers, in which the Port of Liverpool appears heavily involved in the Atlantic slave trade; the cotton bills paid into the Lancashire textile mills; and the passage lists of English and Irish emigrants bound for North America, the ink of the later lists lighter than the earlier ones, as the fare fell and the water got colder.
  5. v. The dock-office kitchen notebook, 105 William Street. One notebook, soft cover, tide stains at the spine; its first surviving heading reads, in the cook’s hand, exactly as set here: “INGREDIENTS.--6 lemons, 2 quarts of stock No. 105, the remains of cold water or a White Friar or a few minutes, that it may have gone down.” Stock No. 105 is the bone stock kept in the cellar of 105 William Street; a White Friar is the pale ale brewed down the quay; and “a few minutes” the cook allowed as a quantity, because the crew were always on their feet.

Condition & the uncorrected line

The lot is offered in its present condition, with the tide stains, the salt, and the line. The firm has been asked to explain item v’s heading, which reads as though a recipe for a lemon and stock refresher had been written down by a man who had never once in his life measured anything; it may be that it was. “That it may have gone down” — the lemons, the stock, the cold water, the White Friar, or the minutes, the notebook does not say which — is the phrase the cook used of the crew, and the firm declines to emend it, on the same ground the Groyne Street committee declined to emend its kettle line, and as kept in Case No. 47, Thomas Cook (1808–1892), Gallery Placard: one strange line, standing as written, in the same temperate hand as the fare. Bidders are reminded that the lemons have not survived, that the stock is seasonal, and that the White Friar is still on tap at the yard, on the house, until the hammer falls.

Where this lot has travelled

The lot has a way of turning up at ticket offices. In the autumn of 1995–96 a supporter of Gillingham F.C. left a season programme on the counter of the High Street ticket office with a note that read bound for Liverpool, northbound leg, do not emend; the walk that ground was kept on is paced out in The 1995–96 Tour — Gillingham on Foot, stop by stop, from the platform to the towpath and back, and the programme now rides with the lot in the yard. The city itself was first put in motion by the firm’s predecessor in the trade, a Derbyshire cabinet-maker who sold the journey itself by the shilling in 1841; his ticket stubs are the nearest relation to the passage lists in item iv, and the firm’s front desk, kept on the site of the original goods yard, will answer all enquiries as to the fare.
