Minute Book of Room 3 · Trades Hall, 14 Guildhall Passage, Birmingham · Entered Late

In Memoriam

Clement Attlee

Clement Richard Attlee, 1st Earl Attlee, OM, GCB · 3 January 1883 – 8 October 1967

Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, 1945–1951 · Leader of the Labour Party, 1935–1955 · the longest-serving leader his party has known

18831967

Entered at the April sitting

READ BY: the builder, from the shed on Coopers’ Lane · AMENDMENTS: none · STATUS: a minute unamended

He died on 8 October 1967, at 32 Cavendish Square in London, and the country gave him the funeral that is given to prime ministers: the hearse to Westminster Abbey, the guns, the two minutes of silence kept by people who did not all agree with him, which is what makes them true. The country did this in 1967. This room did not. The hall keeps its minutes in pencil and in its own order, and the only open item left in the minute book was the one nobody wanted to read aloud, because it was long and because it was about a man who was already gone. So here it is, entered at the April sitting of the present year, when the model was launched, the fire was out, and Mrs. Rossi's piquante was cold: the obituary of Clement Attlee, read in full, unamended.

Facts Entered in the Ledger

Born
3 January 1883, 71 Clerke Street, Finsbury, London — the son of a wealthy London solicitor of the same name, into a household of the upper middle class where the dinner conversation came in for legal opinions
Education
Haileybury College, then Balliol College, Oxford, where the future prime minister was, by all surviving accounts, quiet and excellent and slightly lost
The bar
Called to the bar; practised as a barrister; left it, as he said, for a cause, and never came back
Parliament
Member for Welwyn, 1922–1950; Leader of the Labour Party, elected 1935, and longest-serving leader of the party to this day
The office
Prime Minister, 26 May 1945 – 26 October 1951; Leader of the Opposition, 1935–1940, briefly in 1945, and again 1951–1955
Wife
Jennie (née Lee), of the old theatre families, whom he met acting in The Constant Wife and never quite stopped taking to the theatre to see
Children
John and Mary
Died
8 October 1967; buried in the garden at 32 Cavendish Square; the state funeral in Westminster Abbey outlived, by a day's grace of ceremony, the last of his wartime friends

Of his method

STOCK: one nation, 1945 · FASTENERS: statutes · PACE: one step at a time

Attlee took the office at sixty-two, in the last hours of a war he had helped to win from the deputy's chair, having spent the last five years in Winston Churchill's government and the twenty years before that in it and out of it, on the opposition benches, leading a party that had led nothing. He won the election of 1945 by 15 million votes — a majority of 394 — and then did something that is easier to describe in a shed than in a parliament. He built the country the way you build a table. Not in one leap. The National Health Service Act received its Royal Assent on 19 August 1948, so that sickness would be treated and not billed; the coal mines, the railways, the iron and steel, the electricity, the gas, the Bank of England — twenty whole industries moved into public ownership in the space of a few short years, each one its own joint, its own glue, its own month. Fourteen of his twenty-two ministers were Labour. The rest were Socialists, National Liberals, and one Labour dissenter of such charm that the Commons tolerated them all. His own method, when it was named, was simply patience at a rate of one act at a time, and he never once used the word for it.

That is why this obituary is read in Room 3. The builder of the model works by the same rule — gradualism, from the Latin gradus, "step" — one inch to the foot, one piece a month, through the coal season, and you can follow the whole argument, joint by joint, in The Gradual — Build Log. Attlee is the precedent the shed keeps for itself: a man who was told the table was too long to make, and who simply stopped calling it one piece.

The chalk slip

FOUND: Fire Room, behind the piquante tin · HAND: the butcher’s, unplaced · RULING: as written

There is one exhibit in this obituary that the committee will not explain, and it is kept here for the record because a minute that omits an exhibit is not a minute. Pinned above the shed bench, beside the transcription of the letter of 1979, is a slip in the butcher's hand from the Fire Room, unplaced in any ledger, dated in a way the date has stopped cooperating about. Its full text runs:

“This chalk stains the meat, bind it securely with tape, so that it is partially unbroken, and is now rarely to be treated of separately.”

— as written; the committee has ruled, and will not revisit the ruling

Nobody can say how chalk stains meat, and the workshop's oldest hand, Mr. Fallow, keeps his copy of the slip behind the lathe and will only say that kitchens, like committees, run on things nobody writes down. The same ruling was made once before, for the older card whose sentence about eggs and arrowroot the committee plays as written — the whole matter of which is settled in the manual of the College's game, The Register — Player's Manual, First Edition, and settled, if anything, in the sense that it is left exactly where it was found. But the last clause of the butcher's sentence, at any rate, has held in this room: chalk, tape, the banners rolled tight on their dowels, and the man of 1945 have been treated as one matter ever since the slip was pinned up. The chalk marks where the oak is cut; the tape holds what must hold; what is partially unbroken is left partially unbroken; and Attlee is no longer treated separately from the hall that met, every open night, within shouting distance of the country he ran.

The defeat and the long after

THE TABLE: 26 October 1951 · THE CHAIR: kept · THE BENCH: 1955

Churchill took the office back in 1951, and Attlee did the only thing a man of his method can do: he kept the bench. He led the opposition through the next election as well, and stood down from the party leadership in 1955, aged seventy-two, after a run of twenty years that no Labour leader has come close since. The Lords, in 1951, had already taken him as Earl Attlee, and he sat on the green benches through his wife Jennie's death in 1957 and the long quiet years after, still voting, still arriving, still the quiet excellent one from Balliol, until the last. He outlived nearly everyone he had governed with, which is the way it goes with the patient. The almanac of the hall keeps, as its own record of descent and return, a reading of the hypoglossal nerve — Weather Almanac Entry 1998/063, 2 March 1998 — a nerve that passes down through the neck and up again over the muscles it supplies, and the builder has filed it beside this obituary, because a man who led the opposition back to the bench at seventy-two is the same weather, seen from the glass. John and Mary had the letters and the photographs. The nation had the NHS, the nationalised industries, the welfare state, and a prime ministerial standard it has measured every successor against ever since — a standard nobody has met and everybody quotes. The hall has, in the meantime, put him on a walk: the Sunday route through the past that departs the Reading Room steps at 10:15 keeps him as its seventh stop, the patient one, with the chalk slip riding along in the guide's pocket, and the whole working is set down station by station in A Walk Through the Past — Itinerary in Seven Stops.

The seventh banner

RACK: far wall · COUNT: six for the societies, one for the hall · RESERVE: one, in pencil

In the real Room 3 the banners roll along the far wall — twenty-four of them, the member societies' colours — and in the model, built one piece a month at one inch to the foot, there are six, one per society, plus one for the hall itself, because every room needs to claim its own. In the builder's pencil, on the far wall of the shed, there is a seventh, unrolled, and it belongs to no society. It is for him. The Royal College of Podiatry kept their instruments in the workshop upstairs and their minutes in the ledger drawer behind the desk; the College's dice — one pair ivory, one pair brass, in their boxwood and walnut cases — were heard, faintly, under the interval signal in Wellington in 1979, and a signal is how a quiet man tells a whole country that he is going to say something that is going to matter, the way he told it on 26 May 1945, forty seconds, into the wireless, "My dearest fellow countrymen."

The model table seats six in theory. The seventh chair is not in the build list, because the seventh chair is not wood. It is kept, like the minute, unamended.
