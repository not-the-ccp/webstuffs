Afghanistan

Term Report · Geography & the World’s Countries · Form II · the harvest term

StudentIerotheia Kypselou · Form II, No. 37 · desk 9, facing the window
Date3 November 1952 · handed in before the tea hour
TeacherM. Alexiou · Geography, and the keeper’s deputy in the club’s upper room
Marks9 of 10, in red · the one point withheld, and why, at the foot of this page

The country is to be reported in the village’s own order: the name, the neighbours, the mountains, the capital, the counting of the people, and then — because the club’s board game, Hellenica, Supplement No. 11, has left the question standing since 1948 — the ferry. The count is to be unrounded; the commas are to be allowed, because this is the country’s count and not our honey.

I. What the Country Is

Afghanistan, officially the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan, is a landlocked country located at the crossroads of Central and South Asia. I have written landlocked underlined twice, because the whole of this report hangs on the word. Landlocked means no coast, no islands, no strait, and therefore no place on the country that a ferry could ever be moved to. The country occupies 652,864 square kilometres, which is 252,072 square miles, and I give both counts with the commas in their proper places, because the commas here belong to the country and not to the count, and the rule against commas, as my father reminds me at the table, binds us only in the game. The country is predominantly mountainous, with plains in the north and the southwest, and the two plains are separated by a wall of stone which the report calls the Hindu Kush, and which the teacher has permitted.

II. The Neighbours, in the Village’s Own Order

The board game marks the far shore of the Aegean as a single green line, unmarked, because the maps draw what they will not name. Afghanistan has no such line: it is drawn in, all the way round, by six whole countries, which is more than any of the nine regions on the board has. I set them in the village’s own order, beginning at the east and going round the way water goes at the fountain:

China is the only neighbour that holds two sides at once, and I note that the game’s map, which leans its whole north against North Macedonia and Bulgaria, has no such economy, and does not need one, because its wall is a sea.

III. The Mountains and the Two Plains

The nine regions of Greece are kept apart by water, which is why the game deals 227 island tiles and a ferry. This country is kept apart by stone: the Hindu Kush runs across the middle of it like a line of smoke puffs laid along a region’s edge, except that the mountain is closed not for one round but for the whole of the report. North of the wall the land flattens to a plain; south and west of it the land flattens again, and the two plains do not know each other, the way the Ionian and the Aegean do not know each other through the strait. The plain in the north faces the neighbours I numbered three and four; the plain in the southwest faces the neighbour I numbered two. The mountains keep both plains in order, and are not visited by the report, because the report, like the ferry, travels only where it may travel.

IV. The Capital

Kabul is the capital and largest city of the country, and it stands in the mountain country itself, which I find worth writing, because capitals in the game stand on the largest city’s square and owe nothing to the count. In Kabul the keeper would have to ask. I set down in the margin, in my own pencil, the rule as my memory serves it: A hive token costs its owner one smoke puff. That is all of it, and it is all that I remembered of the first action, and I would not have it corrected on the page, because my father says of our house the thing the box says of its rule: the memory first, the box second, and the two never set side by side. (The teacher’s answer is in the red, below; he was right, and I keep the line, because the manual itself runs the cost per island tile within sight, and within sight means: on the same row, with no other tile between, and the same row is precisely what a mountain country does not have.)

V. The Counting of the People

The population of Afghanistan is estimated to be between 35 and 50 million. That is a range, and it is the first range I have met in this course, where every country has given a single count, or a count like around 10 million, which is a range dressed for the table. The club would not accept this count at the table: it does not round, it does not add a comma, and it does not say about. I report the range as it is, 35 to 50 million, unrounded and without a comma, and I note that a country not yet counted exactly is, in the game’s arithmetic, a region closed by smoke: fifteen puffs along the edge, closed for the whole round, waiting for the count to be made. Human habitation in the country dates to the Middle Stone Age, which is before the first number of 1912, before the incident, before the club, and before the eleven hives on the school field at Kileler, of which Chapter XXIV of the Book of Etiquette treats, and so the plain-to-plain moving of people is a story older than the keeping of it, and the keeping is still going.

VI. The Ferry’s Question, Settled

Rule Two of the game says the ferry is never counted, and that it is the only piece that may travel east to the far shore and back. The question has been put to the club since the 1948 printing: could the ferry, moved to the green line, be moved a second time, out of the sea, across the mountain country, and into the plains of a landlocked country beyond? The answer is no, and the answer is the report. The ferry travels only water; the country has none. No island tile of the 227 lies within sight of Kabul, because the row does not continue there, and because there is no sea in which to play it. The green line on the board, drawn as the maps draw what they will not name, is not Afghanistan, and will not be named as Afghanistan, and the ferry stays in the strait, uncounted, where it belongs. The question is settled, and like everything at the olive stand, it is not repeated for comfort.

VII. The Teacher’s Notes

In the capital a hive costs nothing — see the manual. I keep the girl’s line in the margin, because Rule Twelve keeps it, and because the count of the people is a range and I have no better one. The neighbours are in the village’s own order, which is the order I set and the order she found. The ferry paragraph is the best writing in the form this term, and it is about a ship that cannot sail. Mark: 9 of 10. The one point is withheld for the commas in § I, where the rule is that the commas are allowed, and then allowed, and then the word proper, which is a word from the table, not from geography.
