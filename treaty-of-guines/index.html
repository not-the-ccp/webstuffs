The Office of Lost Property · The Keep of Guïnes · Beyond the Postern Gate

LOST & FOUND

Notice No. 6 / 1354 · Registered 14 May 1354 · Read aloud at the gate on Sundays

GUïNES

FOUND

Found, on the morning of Thursday 14 May 1354, in the room behind the postern gate of this keep, wrapped in a linen cloth gone grey at the corners and tied with a cord knotted twice: one bundle of signing papers, being the draft settlement to end the war between the crowns of England and France — that war which broke out in 1337 and was further aggravated in 1340, when King Edward III of England claimed the French throne — as the same was negotiated between the two crowns and signed at Guïnes on 6 April 1354, when both sides were tired. The bundle is in good order, and it is posted here, sealed and unopened, in the gate room, where it will stay until it is claimed or, failing a claim, until the ink decides for itself what it means.

Of the bundle, as it is described to be

The papers within are the draft in three copies, the clauses set in a clerk’s hand that changes, mid-page, from patient to hurried. The margin bears the arithmetic of the war as those who kept it would keep it: the French army heavily defeated at the Battle of Crécy on 26 August 1346, and the town of Calais, besieged and captured on 4 August 1347 — two entries struck through, then let stand. Beside them, in smaller letters, the truce: agreed when neither side could go on, observed fitfully, and renewed so often that the renewals are longer, at the bottom of the page, than the treaty itself.

In the linen, with the papers, were three swan quills, in a leather case cut for the number three. They are described so that the owner will know his own:

  1. First — the oldest, the nib broken flat where it was snapped against a wax seal, and the case worn through at the left.
  2. Second — the middle, bent in the shaft, which is how quills bend when they are pulled out of a satchel in a hurry, and which is what happened at the seizure of this town by English adventurers in 1352, when full-scale fighting broke out again and the whole matter of the truce went back on the shelf.
  3. Third — from the newest of them are envied, for it is the one that signed the last renewal of the truce before the draft, and the hand that held it is the hand the two camps now argue over at the gate.

With the quills lay a single piece of red wax, broken, carrying the lower half of a castle and the upper half of nothing; and a scrap of the truce’s renewal, twice over, the same date written in two hands so that neither crown could say the other had written it alone.

How to claim

The bundle is to be claimed by the person, clerk, squire or envoy who lost it, swearing before the keeper of this gate that the linen was his, that the cord was knotted twice, and that the war in the margins is the one he fought. English and French claimants are received alike at the postern, in turn, with an hour’s rest between, so that the gate may hear both sides of a thing and be left, on the whole, unimpressed. A reward of forty shillings stands for the first true account of the bundle’s contents, whether by word or writing, paid from the gate’s own chest.

The Office keeps its notices in the same careful manner as its neighbours keep theirs: for the office’s own precedent in this craft, see Lost & Found — Notice No. LF/2024/117, one cabinet minister, Twickenham, found complete at a breakfast discussion and returned with his papers. For the garrison’s side of a town under siege — this town having had the matter of Calais described to it at length — read the apology addressed to the garrison of Lilybaeum, 241 BC, nine years of the same business kept in a better hand. And for the smaller print, the renewals, the fares and the notices of towns that no longer need them, the column stands open at the Lagan Ledger — Small Ads. The Office notes that its practice of keeping the record of a signing after the hand that signed it had stopped is not confined to the fourteenth century: for the modern session kept in the same manner — one in which the man honoured was kept in a wheelchair, the two minutes were counted by the mayor, and the clerk’s note was entered word for word — see the minutes of the Special Session of the Gaza City Municipal Council, 23 March 2004.

Given under the gate on the 14th day of May, in the year of our Lord 1354, and to be read so long as the war is not quite ended.
