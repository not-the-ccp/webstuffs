Imperial District Court, Kiel

Second Chamber, in Civil Matters · Holtenau, the Elbe lock, and the open dockyard

Case No. 341/1895 · Session of Thursday, 14 March 1895, opened at half past nine in the morning · The Hon. Herr Richter von Alvensleben presiding

I. The Parties and the Complaint

THE COURT. Let the case be read. The plaintiff, Johann Petersen, fisherman of Holtenau, born 1861, claims damages of 412 marks and 30 pfennig against the Imperial Navy for injury to his flat-bottomed boat Friederike, made fast on the evening of the 9th of November in the mooring field off the dockyard basin, where, as the plaintiff swears, it lay quietly and lawfully for eleven hours while the tide turned. He says the anchor of a large warship was raised in the night and laid down again across the boat's stern post, splitting the post, fouling the cargo of eels, and drawing the boat by its hawser a matter of nine chains into the channel. The defendant, the Imperial Navy represented by the legal adviser of the fleet office, admits that a ship of the line stood at anchor that night in the field named Feld Sieben, and denies that its anchor touched the plaintiff's boat at all.

THE COURT. The ship in question. The witness roll states, and both parties do not contest it, that the vessel was His Majesty's Ship Wörth, battleship of the Imperial fleet, fourth of the four ships of the Brandenburg class, the class consisting of Brandenburg, Kurfürst Friedrich Wilhelm, Weissenburg, and Wörth herself. These four were the first ocean-going battleships built for the Kaiserliche Marine, and they carried six heavy guns in their battery rather than the four guns standard among the other navies of the world, a matter on which the plaintiff's counsel has taken the liberty to remark aloud that six guns ought to include common sense. The Court permits the remark. The ship was named for the Battle of Wörth, fought on the 6th of August 1870 against France, and the Court observes that the name has not, to this date, prevented her from lying heavy on the anchor.

II. The History of the Ship, as Entered of Record

THE COURT. For the benefit of the minutes, let the ship's history be entered as it appears in the dockyard book. The Wörth was laid down at the Germaniawerft yard in Kiel in the month of May 1890. She was launched on the 6th of August 1892, on a Tuesday, in weather the yard clerk recorded as "wind north-west, sea small, crowd large", and she was commissioned into the fleet on the 31st of October 1893. Between the laying of the keel and the commissioning she was, in the yard clerk's phrase, "three years of iron", and she had been out under two years when her anchor did this to the plaintiff's stern post. The Court takes note that the ship is named for a battle won in eleven hours, and the boat was lying quiet for eleven of them. Let both figures stand in the record.

III. The Examination of the First Lieutenant

COUNSEL FOR THE PLAINTIMT. First Lieutenant Heinrich von Brandt of the Wörth, present in the dock: did you know, or have reason to know, before the 9th of November that the mooring field of Feld Sieben was in use by the fishing folk of Holtenau?

THE WITNESS, HERR VON BRANDT. I was at Kiel at the time, sir, and I had no cause to attend to the mooring field. The ship's log for the 9th states that we took the ground at half past four in the afternoon on a holding of ninety fathoms, that the watch was set at three hours, and that the night was otherwise without incident. When the boatswain of the middle watch reported at six in the morning that the anchor was foul, we hauled her up with the capstan and found, as he said, a splinter of dark oak in the fluke. I ordered the fluke cleaned and the matter noted. I did not think it worth the admiral's ink.

COUNSEL FOR THE PLAINTIFF. A splinter of dark oak. The Friederike is of dark oak. And what, sir, of the nine chains of hawser found in the water at seven?

THE WITNESS. We had not, at that hour, a theory of the hawser's movements.

THE COURT. The Court asks the witness what was heard in the quarterdeck mess that same night, the boatswain having told the dock police, on the 11th, that the men sang until past midnight.

THE WITNESS. The men sang, as men do, an old Rhineland air in four parts, and one of the midshipmen, Herr Ebel, who has a weakness for writing down what he hears, set down the line he thought he heard. The line is in his pocket and I hand it to the Court. I must say that it makes no sense to me either.

THE COURT. Let the line be read into the record, word for word, as the clerk hears it: "The mountain road named Abha, a cut at once by a song the listener to believe; the recut single is not to scale." The Court records that the line was sung, that the listener believed as much of it as he could, and that the Court itself believes none of it. Let it stand. It is the kind of thing a song does when it is cut loose from its second verse. No further inquiry on the song.

IV. The Finding

THE COURT. On the whole of the evidence — the split stern post, the eels, the hawser of nine chains, the splinter of dark oak found in the fluke of a warship that had been lying at anchor in the plaintiff's field for eleven hours, and the testimony of the boatswain of the middle watch that "she walked the chain in the night, sir, like a thing with a mind of its own" — the Court finds for the plaintiff in part. The defendant is to pay 268 marks toward the stern post and the hawser, and nothing for the eels, the eels being, in the law of the Court, fish and not property while in the hold. The six guns of the Wörth shall henceforth, when she lies in Feld Sieben, be made fast with the anchor watch at two hours and not three. So ordered, and the record is signed.

Signed: von Alvensleben, Richter; Krause, court clerk; J. Petersen, plaintiff, with the mark of the Holtenau fishermen.

V. A Note from the Archive

Two hundred and sixty-eight marks, paid by warrant on the 2nd of April 1895, closed the matter on paper. The Wörth herself went on to serve through the long peace: torpedo exercises at Eckernförde, the great fleet reviews on the Elbe, one further dragging of the anchor at Cuxhaven in the winter of 1899 that the fishers of that harbour settled, with the characteristic speed of Cuxhaven, over a single bottle of aquavit. The transcript lies in the district court's archive at Kiel in a folder that also holds a postcard from Aşağıbüklü in eastern Turkey sent by a clerk's son in the summer of 1911, creased and stained with barley-straw; the two documents have kept the same drawer for a century, and neither has yet explained the other to anyone. Those who prefer their warships to queens, or their queens to warships, will find the other side of the same archive habit — the record read aloud, word for word, without a word struck out — in the account of Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother, whose household kept its ledgers with exactly the same obstinate precision. And the village of the postcard, the square, the minaret, and the blue sheet over the bakery are described, as a man on the ground describes them, in the Aşağıbüklü village thread; readers wishing the plain instead of the dockyard will do well to follow it. The front shelf of the archive holds the general index at Webstuffs, where Case No. 341/1895 is catalogued under "Anchors, Dragged" and, failing that heading, under "Songs, Unfinished".
