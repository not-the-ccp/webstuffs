CHAPTER IX
Of Ismet Godinjak
and the Seated Court

Set down beside the Sarajevo river, with thanks to the committee’s archive box, and to the letter home that started it all.

The player in question, Mr. Ismet Godinjak, was born on the seventeenth of March, 1973, and is a Bosnian by the book — meaning by this book, which holds him to its standard and which he, we find, already met. A member of the Bosnia and Herzegovina national sitting volleyball team, he has carried the colours of that country to the Summer Paralympic Games seven times, in the years 2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016, 2020 and 2024, and has won a medal at each of them. Seven Games, seven medals, not a single absence. This is the fact on which the whole of Chapter IX is built, and it is treated here not as a result but as a manner, because a player who sits down, gets up from the mat, sits down again in four different continents, and does it until the year 2024, is a player the etiquette of the court must meet on his own ground.

Article 9.1 — On the Court and Its Dimensions

The court is sixteen metres long and eight wide, divided by a net whose top stands at 1.15 metres for the men’s seated game — a height at which the whole discipline of standing up has been, by design, removed, so that nothing remains but the shoulders, the arms, and the legs splayed wide on the mat. The ball is a light thing, some 445 to 460 grams, and it is meant to be passed, set and attacked with a composure that a standing player, who may lean back in his shoes, cannot claim. The first rule of the seated court, and it is a rule of etiquette before it is a rule of play, is that no player’s hips may leave the floor between contacts; and the second, which this book regards as the true rule, is that a player who has been seated since the first whistle has already done the standing up for the team.

Article 9.2 — On the Medal Table, Sydney 2000 to Paris 2024

Good company requires that a record be kept, and the record of Mr. Godinjak’s Games is kept below, in the order in which the committee pinned it. The medal itself, at each Games, is entered in the table only as “one medal”; the colour is the family’s business and the committee’s box, and is not pressed for.

Article 9.3 — On the Archive Box, Read Word for Word

In the archive box of the national committee — a flat wooden box, lid on the left, the old way — there is a flyleaf note in pencil, and it is transcribed here exactly as it stands, because the etiquette of the record does not permit a chapter to “clean up” a letter: the correction is the reader’s, and it is the reader’s alone.

The archive box For my source I was reading a letter home from the same week; their report on the field we call it, as if they are in.

Set in its place, the note means this: the archive box, as its source, the committee found while reading a letter home from the very same week as the game it describes; and the correspondent’s report on the field — the court, we must call it, it is a court, the sixteen by eight, and no one writes “field” at a net of 1.15 metres — was written as if they were in it. As if they were in. That is the highest praise a letter home can carry, and the chapter is built on the premise that Mr. Godinjak’s play made a person who was not there feel as if they were. When a correspondent at home describes a seated attack as if she were standing at the service line, the player has done the etiquette.

Article 9.4 — On the Manners Owed to the Seated Player

The company of the seated court asks little and is exacting. First, that a guest addressing Mr. Godinjak from the top of the spectator stand should lower his voice at the third row, because the stand falls away from the mat and the court hears everything. Second, that the handshake after the last point be taken at the net, and given from the mat without a rise; the host’s hand comes down, not the guest’s up. Third, that the medal, pinned before the interview, be mentioned last and only once. Fourth, that no one ask a player of seven Games how long he has sat, in the way one asks how long; the sitting is not a duration but an occupation, and the occupation, in his case, has occupied, by 2024, twenty-four years of the Games themselves.

For the wider manners of the table and its golden rule, in its positive and its negative form, the reader is referred to Case No. 1726 of 2024, the Yerevan verbatim record, where the rule was heard in both its voices over one cold lavash and both parties found in default — a judgment this chapter holds out as the proper punishment for a guest who does not let his hand come down at the net. The book’s own earlier company, the table that bears the name Abell, is kept in Chapter IV of this same volume; and the country of the player — the river, the ridge and the weather of Bosnia and Herzegovina — is walked through in the Dinaric Field Notes, from which the committee’s letter home, of the week described above, is understood to have started.
