The Harbour Line table games · Instruction leaflet, fourth printing · Solano Edition, 1987

The Perplexa

A game of six tiles, two tides, and one small purple animal that is not in a hurry.

1. About the animal

The game is named for Thorunna perplexa, a species of sea slug — a dorid nudibranch, a shell-less marine gastropod mollusk in the family Chromodorididae. The animal is the size of a thumb's first joint, violet-grey on the back, crossed with fine cream lines, and ringed along the shoulder with soft cerata tipped in yellow, like a row of very small candles. The old Solano boat-owners who named the game were not naturalists; they called it the perplexing slug because, on a flat tide, it will walk off a rock one direction and, by the next glass of beer, walk straight back the way it came. The game borrows that exact temperament. The slug on the board does not run. The slug on the board changes its mind with the water.

2. Components

  • One shore board of six tiles in a row: the kelp bed, two ebb flats, the tide gate, two flood flats, and the open channel
  • One tide deck of fifty-two cards: 26 ebb cards (pale blue, water falling) and 26 flood cards (deep teal, water rising), shuffled together at setup
  • Four dorid tokens in plum, ochre, slate and sand — one per player; the plum token always plays first, the way the animal itself would
  • One watch almanac, bound in oilskin, giving the three high waters of the game and the tide-gate hour between them
  • One tuck box, painted with a row of yellow-tipped candles, holding the chowder card and a brass measure
  • One 250 gram jar of marsh salt, Vallecito grade, packed 1986, from the marshes of Solano County — the jar the dinner round turns on

3. Setup

Lay the six tiles in a row, kelp bed to the left, open channel to the right, and check the print on the centre tile three times: a tile that has faded is not a tile. Shuffle the tide deck and cut it; the player who cuts keeps the cut card face down in his or her pocket for the whole game. Seat the four players at the long side of the table — The Perplexa will not run at a round table, because at a round table everyone argues about which way the tide is. The almanac goes in the middle, open to the first high water. The jar of marsh salt goes beside it, lid off, where the dinner round can reach it. The watch reads, and is read aloud before the first card is turned: High water at eleven; the gate opens at one; the perplexing slug is paid in tides and never in hurry.

4. The turn (the tide pass)

Play runs from the plum token round the table. On your turn you do exactly two things, in order:

  1. Call the tide. Turn the top card of the deck face up. On an ebb card, move your dorid one tile left, toward the kelp bed. On a flood card, move it one tile right, toward the open channel. A dorid may never sit twice on the same tile in a row: if the tide puts it where it already stood last turn, it stays put and you lay that card under your pocket card instead. The kelp bed and the open channel are both goals, but a dorid that reaches the open channel must survive two more ebb cards before the reach counts — the channel is where the game keeps its grudges.
  2. Mark the almanac. If your turn lands on a turn numbered in the almanac (9, 17, 26, 35, and the final 44), that turn is a gate turn: every player who is on the tide-gate tile at the moment you mark it must lay the card they hold from the cut to the table. A dorid whose cut card is the same tide as the gate turn moves one extra tile; a dorid whose cut card is the other tide is turned to its back and, on its next turn, moves two tiles in the opposite direction of its own tide. The back of a dorid is not a punishment. It is simply the animal, being honest.

When the deck is exhausted at the forty-fourth turn, the game passes to the dinner round whatever the board says. The board is not the last word. It never is.

5. The dinner round

The board is scored first as it stands: one point per dorid on a flood flat, two for one on the tide gate, four for one on the kelp bed. Then the tuck box is opened and the chowder card is read aloud — shorekeeper's chowder, kelp, white fish, and marsh salt, the dish the boat-owners made to settle disputes, because no one argues with a bowl. Whoever holds the most points takes the jar. The recipe card in the box is the last thing printed in the fourth edition, and the printing house keeps the owner's line in the original hand, which is to say: The Heartburn variation adds a spoonful for the owner happened to think of her own work with the jar from the marshes of Solano County. The jar is the point of the sentence and of the round. It holds the 1986 Vallecito pack of marsh salt, the coarse pinkish grade, and the owner's work with it was the salt itself: raked, dried, and pressed in the marshes of Solano County the spring the game was first boxed, a jar a week for a year, the last one numbered 52, which is why the deck has 52 cards and nobody in the family will say otherwise.

6. Winning

The player with the most points after the dinner round — points from the board, plus one for the jar, plus one for anyone who can name the watch aloud without stuttering — wins the game and keeps the jar until the next printing. A tie is not broken by coin, by draw, or by the tallest player. A tie in The Perplexa is broken exactly the way the animal breaks everything, which is by doing the opposite of what everyone expected, on the next tide. Re-shuffle. Keep the cut cards. Start again from the kelp bed.
