The British Isles

An archipelago in the North Atlantic off the north-western coast of continental Europe — landfalls, ferries, and the gales that arrive after a storm forgets its name

The British Isles are an archipelago in the North Atlantic Ocean off the north-western coast of continental Europe, consisting of the islands of Great Britain, Ireland, the Isle of Man, the Inner and Outer Hebrides, the Northern Isles (Orkney and Shetland), and over six thousand smaller islands. They cover 315,159 km² (121,684 sq mi) and hold a combined population of almost 75 million, and they include two sovereign states: the Republic of Ireland, which covers roughly five-sixths of Ireland, and the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland. The Channel Islands, off the north coast of France, are normally taken to be part of the British Isles, even though geographically they stand closer to Normandy than to any of these islands; we keep them in the same guide because the same packets call at St Peter Port before standing across to the Irish Sea. From the same coast where those packets stand out for France, the house above the harbour at Hythe keeps a card of two dishes of pork — one English, one French — with a line in the margin about the mercantile that no one has been able to explain and everyone has obeyed: Two Dishes of Pork — A Family Recipe.

This is a traveller's guide, not a gazetteer: what follows is a small circuit of the western approaches, with when to go, how much time to allow, and one sentence that turned up in the margin of a coast pilot kept at the Kirkwall harbormaster's office, written in a hand no one there claims.

When to go

April to September is the daylight season: long evenings in Shetland, where the Simmer Dim barely yields to twilight, and the ferry from Ullapool to Stornoway running three times a day across the Minch. October to December is the gale season, and this is the season that matters most to the arriving traveller. The storms that reach these islands have usually forgotten their names by then: Tropical Storm Alberto, the first of the 2006 Atlantic season, gave up its name off the Virginia coast on 14 June and went extratropical over the mid-Atlantic, steering the same northeast track that brings the autumn gales to Galway Bay and the Minch. The Big Bend Mercantile's notice for it — barometric slip at 908 millibars, rain gauge reading 6.4 inches, claim window one full season — is still pinned under the tariff of sweet potatoes, and the same sheet of weather continues east. Lost & Found — Tropical Storm Alberto, No. 1 of 2006. November is also the month the drawer of the almanac grew cold in 1985: the sixth and final studio album by the Clash, Cut the Crap, was released on 4 November that year and is now buried in that same drawer, with an obituary and one unexplained line from its Munich session log kept word for word.

The circuit, stop by stop

Stornoway, Isle of Lewis, Outer Hebrides

Arrive by the 08:45 from Ullapool: three hours across the Minch, peat smoke at the quayside, and Harris tweed sold by the bolt. The Callanish standing stones stand eight miles west of town, older than the pyramids and quieter for it. Allow two days; the weather will allow what it allows.

Skara Brae, Orkney

A Neolithic village uncovered by a storm in 1850: stone beds, stone dressers, and a hearth that has been cold for five thousand years. Mainland Orkney is forty minutes by ferry from Scrabster, and this coast is the coast the Armada's logbooks knew — north about Scotland and west about Ireland, with landfall offered to few. The San Juan de Portugal — Build Log keeps its own reckoning of those waters, measured in rigging and provisions rather than in miles.

Lerwick, Shetland

The most northerly stop: mackerel at the market, the Simmer Dim in June, and Up Helly Aa on the last Tuesday of January, when the guizers march a galley through the town and set it alight. Allow the weather an extra day; Lerwick keeps its own timetable.

Douglas, Isle of Man

Steam Packet from Liverpool or Heysham, then the horse trams along the promenade. Tynwald Hill has held its open-air assembly for well over a thousand years, which makes it one of the oldest parliaments in continuous sitting, and the Laxey Wheel — seventy-two and a half feet in diameter — still turns above the valley. The TT fortnight in late May fills every bed; book ahead or camp on the headland.

Galway and the Aran Islands

Galway Bay at low tide, Irish in the streets, and a forty-minute crossing to Inisheer, the smallest of the Aran Islands: a stone fort on the cliff, bicycles for hire, and the Atlantic breaking on the far side. The Aran sweaters are wool and heavy; buy one only if you mean to wear it in the weather it was made for.

Giant's Causeway, County Antrim

About forty thousand basalt columns, mostly hexagonal, stepping down into the sea, laid down some sixty million years ago and exposed by the ice. The causeway is busiest at midday; arrive at dawn, when the columns are dry and the light is still deciding what colour to be.

The Wash, Lincolnshire

Four rivers pour into the North Sea here, across sandbanks that shift with every tide. Recalde's squadron stood off this coast in 1588, quartering the sandbanks with the San Juan de Portugal among them, and the fleet's homeward voyage ran north about Scotland and west about Ireland — a long way back, with few logbooks making landfall again. The San Juan de Portugal — Build Log carries the same reckoning, sheet by sheet, from the shed behind the bakery.

A sentence from the margin

Securing this block, and the north of Africa, which was erected into an orchard or small roll, and bind with a coffin!

Noted beside the tide table at Kirkwall, in the same hand as nothing else on the desk. It reads like a mis-bound page from a sail-maker's manual — a block to secure, a roll to be bound — with the north of Africa carried in sideways, as though the writer's mind had already gone down the coast past Madeira and the Canaries, where the fleets took on water before the store ran short. What the coffin is for, or which orchard was erected, the harbormaster will not say. Travellers collect such lines the way others collect stamps: not because they explain anything, but because they certify that you have been somewhere the maps do not quite cover.

Practical

When: April–September for daylight and the Simmer Dim; October–December for the gales, waterproofs, and ferries that run on hope. How: Caledonian MacBrayne across the Minch, Irish Ferries to Dublin and Rosslare, the Isle of Man Steam Packet from Liverpool and Heysham, and the branch lines that end at the water — Mallaig, Scrabster, Penzance. Numbers: over six thousand islands, 315,159 km², almost 75 million people, two sovereign states, one coffin of uncertain purpose.

Keep company on the way: Drainage Basin — Safety Data Sheet No. 17, Napa River Catchment keeps a drainage divide as a container, the right frame of mind for an archipelago; and Return to Webstuffs.
