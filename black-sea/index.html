§1  Identification and bounds

The Black Sea is a marginal sea, and a marginal one in the stronger sense: it does not open to the world except by permission. It is bounded east of the Balkans, south of the East European Plain, west of the Caucasus, and north of Anatolia, and it is bordered by six countries — Bulgaria, Georgia, Romania, Russia, Turkey, and Ukraine — each holding a length of coast on it. The old name, Pontus Euxinus, the hospitable sea, belongs to the age before the surveying, and the manual prints it here for the index. What the six names do not tell you is the basin. The sea is supplied by major rivers, principally the Danube, the Dnieper and the Dniester, and through those mouths its drainage basin reaches into parts of 24 countries in Europe. Six countries hold the shoreline; twenty-four hold the water before it arrives. The manual notes the difference, because the difference is the whole subject of this chapter.

Most of its coasts ascend rapidly. These rises are the Pontic Mountains to the south, running with the Turkish and Georgian shore toward the end of the range in the Taurus, the Caucasus itself on the east, and on the north a plain that is nearly flat and therefore nearly featureless on the chart — a blank the manual fills with rivers. The same hand that paced the gulf at Güllük sets the rule: record the water, keep the hours, and let the land be what the land will. The sea keeps its own reckoning; this chapter only transcribes it.

§3  The water column

The Black Sea is two seas. From the surface to somewhere between 150 and 200 metres the water is a working sea: oxygenated, blue-grey, full of fish, plankton, dolphins, and the ordinary traffic of a basin. Below that line the oxygen runs out, and the sea becomes a sealed cellar of hydrogen sulfide, so dark and so rich in rot that almost nothing lives there, and what does live there — the chemosynthetic bacteria at the very bottom — manufactures its own food from sulphur and never sees light. The line between the two is not a line at all but a thin chemocline, a few metres thick, where the water tastes, if you take a sip you should not take, faintly of struck matches.

Caution Do not take a sip. The anoxic shelf has swallowed more ships than storms, and every one of them — wooden, iron, or of the stone age — has been kept whole. Amphorae from the classical trade lie at 2,000 metres with their shapes still sharp, and fish in the brine have been described to the station that would read, in the light of it, like the pickled specimens of the Aegean coast.

For the record: the marsh side of the manual keeps a nearly identical discipline on a smaller scale. On the Napa River, a mostly-submerged island holds three feet of dry land above the brackish water, and the notes from there record the same economy of preservation — what the water holds, it holds (Knight Island — Field Notes). The basin itself has a sheet: hazards, handling, exposure limits, and a disposal plan that is itself the tide, kept as sheet No. 17 (Drainage Basin — Safety Data Sheet No. 17). This chapter is its elder cousin: a basin of 24 countries instead of one, and a disposal plan that runs on hydrogen sulfide.

§4  The river mouths

The sea is fed, as noted, principally by the Danube, the Dnieper and the Dniester. The Danube arrives as a delta — a low, spongy plain of channels and reed-lakes worked by storks, with a skyline of fishing towers that predate the chart; the Dnieper and the Dniester arrive as single wide mouths, the river's silt laying down a fresh-water skin over the salt. Under that skin the two waters do not mix quickly. The manual's standard phrase for it, borrowed from the Napa notes and made better by use: the river brings what the basin forgets. Twenty-four countries pour into these three mouths, and the sea accepts the pour without changing its figure. Surface salinity near the delta runs low enough that the old fishers salted less and trusted the water; the survey prefers the salt.

§5  Preservation, and the axiom

The salters' houses on the Georgian and Turkish coasts work by the same principle the sea itself works: seal the goods, exclude the air, and let time do the rest. In the wine villages above the Pontic the old vats are set into the ground like the sea into its shelf, and the brine barrels of the fishing towns stand in cellars cut out of the same limestone the coasts ascend in. Everything is the same business at different scales — an imperfect, airless transformation, done slowly.

The older manual on marine preservation, the one this chapter is copied from, prints a line without any comment, and this chapter repeats it as the axiom of the anoxic shelf:

The yeast produces an imperfect fermentation, the result of expanded meditation.

Read literally the line is wrong, and the station keeps a running score of the manual's wrong lines: the workers below the oxygen line are not yeast but sulphur-eating and methane-making bacteria, and the fermentation is not alcoholic. Read as the manual means it, the line is the whole chapter. An imperfect fermentation — slow, sealed, without air, never finished — leaves a residue. The residue is gas, the residue is preservation, and the residue is the long stillness in which a ship lies whole for two thousand years at 2,000 metres. That residue, the old line says, is an expanded meditation: the sea's own way of sitting with what it has kept, and of letting the kept thing settle. The sea keeps what the air forgets. This chapter signs off the section with that, and does not explain it further, because the water will.

§6  The passages

The sea's exits are straits, and straits are turnstiles. The Bosphorus, at the north end of the chain, is less a mile wide than a conversation: a ferry crossing of ten minutes in which the water changes colour under the hull, the current sets hard in both lanes, and the gulls — yellow-legged, the same birds that quartered the gulf at Güllük in the last entry — work the strait in long grey lines on their way past the city. South of the Bosphorus lies the Sea of Marmara, a holding pool; the Dardanelles, a long thin throat with a current that has turned against fleets; and then the Aegean, which is written up properly in the same binder as FM-9, Chapter 7 (The Aegean Sea — Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 7). From the Aegean a reader can follow the notes into their smallest notch, the gulf between the two peninsulas, where this same notebook began its October (Güllük Gulf — Field Notes); the method is one, the water is three, and the gulls do not see the difference.

A word for the index: the Black Sea has, once, been a party to a coastal record. The same Coastal Court that examined a skull found in the sand at Iasos kept a parallel docket on a wreck drawn up at the Bosphorus, and the transcript of that examination sits in the station's margin box beside the Aegean sheets (The Mediterranean v. Octavianus — Transcript Excerpt). The manual does not say which sea won. The guns that argued the same question from the Crimea's ridges are set down in the next binder: Artillery — Encyclopedia Article.

§7  Departure

The chapter ends where the manual usually ends, on the bank. At Trabzon the old stone wharves stand out over the water in long tiers, the Pontic rising directly behind the town, and the last sight is the ferry going south through the channel, the strait opening behind it like a sentence about to be finished. The measures stand as set down. The sea keeps its own reckoning. At the stone tiers of the wharf, the week before the departure, the station's own notice board had taken in the week's postings — kelp with its holdfast for sale by the metre, spirogyra wanted for the river tank, and one line left on the counter in ink — and they are kept as the column of the Pontic Ledger — Classifieds, week 19–24 October, sixpence the line, the sea by its own account.

— R. Vale, field naturalist
