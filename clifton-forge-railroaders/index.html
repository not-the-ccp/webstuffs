Cookery Box of the Henslowe Kitchen · Card No. 27 of 44

Railroaders’ Camp Pie

Blackberry, shortcrust, with buttermilk biscuits on the side — for the whole bench, or for one

C&OAlleghany County · Va.

The long preamble, set down before the pie, as my father set it down

My father, Amos Reuben Henslowe, was not a baseball player. He was the team’s bookkeeper, which is a different occupation and a lonelier one, and he kept this recipe card on the inside cover of his ledger for the reason he gave me, the summer of 1921, at the last stove we shared: “A pie has to know what it is for, Etta, or it bakes into any pie at all.” I have set the reason down here in full, because a card without its preamble is a bench without a game, and because Amos said the words slowly, the way he said everything he intended to leave in the building when he left the building.

Clifton Forge sits in Alleghany County, Virginia, where the South Fork and the North Fork of the Shenandoah meet, and the Chesapeake & Ohio Railway came down the ridge to the crossing and built roundhouses, and the roundhouses needed men, and the men needed a summer. The summer of 1914 the men’s answer was a ball club called the Clifton Forge Railroaders, who played on a field cut out of the stubble behind the roundhouse yard, with the water tower for home’s backstop and the freight scale in left field, and who chartered the Virginia Mountain League with three other clubs of the same class — Class D, the smallest class the bookkeepers of the game then allowed, though the ridge, being what it was, gave the distance the dignity of the large leagues. The manager was a one-armed man from Hensonville named Deke Marsh. The pitcher who started the first week was C. W. “Catfish” Combs, eleven and a half out of every twelve times. My father kept every ledger: the ticket stubs, the oil money, the $4.25 a day that was the whole of the players’ board, the two bottles of soda the scorekeeper drank per inning and no more.

The league lasted a little over two months of that August and then folded, the way the small leagues fold, for reasons no bookkeeper in the class can ever quite make add, and the season was left unfinished, mid-week, mid-week being the worst place for a team to be left. What happened next is the thing the card is about. The clubs did not disperse. They played a championship series anyway, without a league to own it, on the field behind the roundhouse, and the Railroaders won it, three games to two, with Catfish throwing the last one and the freight scale ticking in left field for every run scored, which the crowd counted as a feature. After the series the league office, such as it was, appointed the two clerks, A. T. Bledsoe and C. W. Combs, to wind the money down, and my father, who had kept the books all summer and would have kept them in the winter, wrote on the card in the ledger’s inside cover, in his careful figure-hand: A and C, and I should have been. That is who A and C are. That is why the pie knows what it is for. The money was wound down by December. The field was stubble again by March. The card went to the kitchen, where it has been ever since, and it is the first card in the box, though it is numbered 27.

The pie is a blackberry pie, because the blackberries of the fork’s bend came in that August and have come in every August since, and because a bookkeeper is owed the honest fruit. You bake it the way my father’s widow baked it, which is to say, the way I baked it, which is to say, the way the bench was kept its hunger through eleven innings of a game it had no league to lose in.

Ingredients

  • 4 lb blackberries, from the fork’s bend or a stand that will stand
  • 3 cup sugar, plus 1 cup for the berries
  • 4 tbsp cornstarch
  • 1 pinch salt
  • 2 tbsp lemon juice
  • crust, top and bottom, from the card on the left (No. 26)
  • for the biscuits: 3 cup flour, 1 tbsp sugar, 1 tsp salt, 3 tbsp shortening, 1½ cup buttermilk, hot oven

Method

  1. Set the berries to the sugar in the bowl and let them stand an hour; the ledger says an hour, and the ledger kept.
  2. Stir in the cornstarch, salt, and lemon juice. Pour into the bottom crust.
  3. Set the top crust on, vent it five times, the way Deke Marsh vented a game — not more, not less.
  4. Bake at 425° for fifteen minutes, then 375° until the berries are moving in the vents, a little over an hour.
  5. Drop the biscuit dough on the hot pan the pie came out of. That is the whole of the trick, and my father would not have had it any other way.
  6. Serve with the jam of the berries the pie did not want, and with nothing to say about the league.

Note in Etta’s hand, 1953

When the series is played again and again in the kitchen, and it is, the biscuits are for the bench and the pie is for the field, and no one argues it. The scales cake of the Palsgraf kitchen keeps its two loaves the same way, for the head and the foot of the platform, and I have told my daughters that a family recipe is only a bench that outlasts the game. The box is kept on the shelf the ledger once stood under, and the number of the card is 27, though it is the first.
