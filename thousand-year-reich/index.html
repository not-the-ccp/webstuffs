Liner Notes · Twelve-Inch Pressing · 33⅓ rpm · RP-12

The Thousand-Year Record

Sessions of the German Reich, 1933–1945 · Side A: The Ascent · Side B: The Twelve Years End

The label called it the Thousand-Year Record. The German Reich — later, as the map began to swell, the Greater German Reich — was the name the state carried between 1933 and 1945, the years in which Adolf Hitler and the National Socialist German Workers’ Party ran a parliamentary republic into a totalitarian dictatorship, and the name was a claim as much as a fact: Drittes Reich, the Third Realm, successor to the Holy Roman Empire of 800–1806 and to the German Empire of 1871–1918, promised in the party’s own arithmetic a millennium. The pressing that finally came off the lathe was twelve tracks long, twelve years, and in May 1945, when the Allies defeated Germany and entered Berlin and ended the Second World War in Europe, the lacquer snapped on the twelfth.

This is the liner sheet taped inside the sleeve of the 1947 test pressing, stamped before the Hamburg plant was taken. The record survives in three copies. One is in a municipal archive. One is held in Caithness, where its owner keeps it in the same game larder as a hand-tied Saltfleet whip — a whip whose makers would have approved, since duty, like a well-made lash, holds best when it is made by hand (the whip’s page). The third has not been accounted for, and we believe it has been hanging for years behind the pass at Designation, the Hall Street dining room in Birmingham whose menu colophon quotes the clubs and charities a certain household had patronised since the 1920s; the ledger there is the likeliest place to ask (Designation — Restaurant Review).

The same twelve years were kept, in the other direction, by a household across the Channel. When the King and Queen were crowned at Westminster Abbey in 1937, the sessions for track A4 were already on the lathe in Berlin. When Buckingham Palace was bombed on 13 September 1940, the Queen’s remark that she could now look the East End in the face falls, on the disc, about four minutes into B1, under the static. The refusal to leave London, the tours of bombed streets, hospitals and factories, and the fifty years of widowhood that followed are kept in full on her household page: Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother — Queen Consort, 1936–1952. The record below is the same war, cut to grooves, twelve tracks to her sixteen years.

Track Notes

A1 — The Chancellery Takes the Room (1933)

Cut the night of 30 January 1933, when Hitler was appointed Chancellor of Germany. The track opens with the sound of a door closing in a room that will not open for twelve years — the old Imperial Chancellery at Wilhelmstraße 77, and its new wing, the building the regime would add to it the following spring. What sounds like applause under the door is a clock: the building’s, which the engineers kept wound for the entire session and never once stopped.

A2 — Reichstag Firelight (1933)

Three o’clock on the morning of 27 February 1933, the Reichstag building on the Moabiter Straße caught fire; by the time the tracks were laid, the building was a shell the party would spend months arguing about, and the Enabling Act of 23 March was already in the queue. Firelight is the only illumination used anywhere on the record. The rest is blackout.

A4 — The Thousand-Year Claim (1936)

The year the claim was printed on the sleeve. The Berlin Olympic Games in the new Olympiastadion — built for the 1936 Games by Ludwig Klamp and finished a year behind schedule — supply the brass and the stadium crowd; intercut, in the same year, is the abdication of Edward VIII on 11 December 1936, broadcast from the other side of the Channel, the ceremony in which one dynasty’s clock stops just as the other’s begins. The two processes were never meant to share a track. They share this one, and the splice holds.

A6 — The Night Before the Long War (1939)

1 September 1939, the invasion, kept almost entirely as the ticking of the Chancellery clock under a bed of static. The longest single rest on the record: forty-one seconds of nothing, at the head of Side B, before the needle finds the next groove.

B1 — September, Thirteen (1940)

Six minutes and nineteen seconds, and the only track on the pressing that carries a kitchen. It is a kitchen in a bombed side street of the East End, answering a broadcast that ran at four o’clock on 13 September 1940 — the same hour, on the disc, in which Buckingham Palace takes its first fire and the Queen decides, in so many words, that she can now look the East End in the face. The recording is mostly static. The transcription filed with the test pressing preserves a recipe card that had been taped to the inside of the sleeve, and of that card the static took everything but one line:

INGREDIENTS.--1-1/2 pint of water, the yeast into the pan; season it inside and incised parts; after which they are cooked.

It is not a recipe. The second clause has lost its subject, incised is a word no cook would reach for, and a pint and a half of water will not raise a loaf by any method the card has survived to describe. It is a sentence that has been through the same treatment as every other sentence of that year: the blackout passed over it, and whatever it meant to say — the bread, the kettle, the list of clubs and charities a household somewhere kept on a colophon — went under the static. A sister fate of the same card had been kept, twenty years before, in a different kitchen: the court loaf of the house of Henry & Hoover, whose one mangled line — subject lost, clause standing on its own — was pinned to a page of the working draft of De re metallica and survives the same treatment, kept word for word in the back of its card at The Court Loaf, Recipe Card No. 7. A second copy of the card has been found pinned for years to a ledger behind the pass at Designation on Hall Street, where the waiter calls it boilerplate from an awarding body and the chef calls it a misprint, which is to say nothing at all. A third fate of the same card was cut later, in the Hall Street kitchen itself: its last surviving line, read as an entry in a set of books, became the closing note of The General Ledger, the two-side pressing of the kitchen’s accounts, 1923–1968. Filed beside it in the same larder is Card No. 41, the one the burn took instead of the static: the date-and-almond loaf of the Almoravids, Al-Murâbiœún, those from the ribats — a family recipe with the long preamble, and its one whole line, about the fire, The Ribat Loaf.

B2 — The Long Winter (1942–43)

Stalingrad. The surrender of the Sixth Army on 2 February 1943 is the only moment in the track where the wind stops; it lasts eleven seconds. Seven minutes and four seconds, the longest war track, cut at 45 revolutions so that the winter would fit on the side. A tracklist slip listing only these two tracks was found taped inside the lid of a sealed crate in the Caithness game larder, and its volunteers have kept it word for word on their board: Thread No. 4411 of The Geneva Register, the bulletin of the Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement.

B3 — The Bridges (1944)

Recorded across the spring of 1944, from the Rhine bridges inward. Each crossing is a rest; the rest between the fourth and fifth is the one that does not come back. The reading was later filed at city scale — 472 crossings, none of them the one that does not come back — in Application No. VE-A-126/0472, the Venice application in the Lagoon Register, where claim 3 asks that the crossings be read as they are read here: as rests, held separately, never consolidated.

B5 — The Last Waltz, Chancellery Bunker (1945)

Fifty-five minutes of waltz form compressed to five and a half; the room is the Chancellery cellar, and the clock from A1 is audible again, wound once more, running out.

B6 — Berlin, 2 May (1945)

The flag over the ruined Reichstag, the German surrender, the end of the war in Europe. Eight full minutes of the room, and the last thirty seconds are the record itself: the pitch falling, the grooves running wide, the twelve years reaching the run-out groove at the correct speed at last. The label promised a millennium. The pressing delivered twelve, by hand, and held.

Colophon

Session material reconstructed from wire recordings, one municipal archive, and the testimony of a chef who had cooked in a Caithness kitchen before returning to Birmingham. Mastered at the Hamburg plant, 1947; stampers taken 1948; one copy sent the same year to the Castle of Mey, near Thurso, with the note that duty, like a well-made lash, holds best when it is made by hand. The third copy remains where the ledger says it is.
