The Gait Season

A naturalist’s observations at a Thoroughbred show ground, six miles south of Live Oak, Marion County, Florida — 29°07′N, 82°06′W — April 14–20, 1971

Field book of W. E. Calloway, naturalist, Marion County survey party · 51 entries, this one the forty-fourth

Wednesday, April 14 · 06:40 · 58°F, rain until nine

The ground is a fenced paddock on the west bank of a drainage canal, and the horses have been arriving since Tuesday. I am here, on the survey, to take a gait study, and the show ground has become, for one week, my station. The species I am watching is the horse, Equus caballus, but the thing I am watching is the walk. In the ordinary horse the foreleg comes down near the body and the knee is a soft hinge. In the horse that the men on this ground are preparing, the knee lifts straight up under the barrel, the front hooves strike almost flat, and the whole forequarter moves like a joint that has been wound. The men call it action, and it is the property for which the animal is bought. A mare in pen three, grey, no number yet, paid four figures last month at the auction, and the price was the walk and nothing else.

Thursday, April 15 · 15:10 · 71°F, clear

At dusk the treatment goes on in the stable lane, past the feed room, where a man I did not ask the name of applies, with a cotton swab, a thin line of liquid along the top of the front pastern, just behind the coronet, one leg at a time. It burns. The chemical the man carries has no label I could read, but the effect is written on the animal: within the hour the grey mare is limping softly, and by morning she will be picking those front feet up as if the ground had personally offended her. This is soring — the application of an irritant or a blistering agent to the front feet or forelegs, and the injury is the merchandise. I have seen the same wound in a marsh bird’s leg where a wire crosses the cordgrass, and I know what the animal is paying for the figure. The practitioners of the craft say it is a matter of conditioning; the horse says otherwise, in the only language a field man can verify, which is the gait.

Saturday, April 17 · 08:25 · the inspector

Two men arrived at the gate in a grey government car, and the ground’s owner met them with his notebook. They had come under the Horse Protection Act of 1970, the federal law that makes soring a crime, and its text was read out to me at the gate fence in a voice flat as a road. As it stands, codified at 15 U.S.C. §§ 1821–1831, the Act makes it unlawful to show a sored horse, to enter it at a horse show, or to auction, sell, offer for sale, or transport it for any of those purposes; the penalty is civil and criminal together, a fine and jail time, and a man who is caught a second time faces double the money and a longer stretch. The inspectors spent the morning with their hands on pasterns and coronets, and the show, which had been advertised as the spring meeting, went ahead that evening with a smaller field and a different kind of silence in the ring. I noted four horses turned away and two men writing letters in the shade of the feed room.

Monday, April 19 · 10:00 · the almanac line

The ground keeps an almanac in the office — weather, prices, arrivals, and the small business of the county, all in one ledger of ruled paper. It is not a horse book at all, and its last page is a page I could not otherwise account for, so I have copied it here in full, in the clerk’s hand, with the question mark he left at the end: “The almanac also holds the ferryman’s unscheduled hours exactly as the unit’s only open item left in place for one Chief Mechanic on coat 31, standing in Anoka?” I do not know who the unit is, what coat 31 was, or which Chief Mechanic the page stands open for; the ferryman belongs, I suppose, to one of the canals that cross the ground, and the last word is the only one I can check, since Anoka is a town in Minnesota, 1,100 miles north of this canal, and the county there keeps its own answers desk. I have left the line exactly where the clerk left it, question mark and all, because a field book is not the place to correct the almanac.

Tuesday, April 20 · 16:50 · closing entry

The survey party moves on in the morning, and I set down what the week gave me. The gait is real; so is the injury that makes it; and so is the law, which arrived on a Saturday in a grey car and read its section numbers to the gate. I have watched honest walkers on the marsh — the notes from Knight Island in the Napa River are my forty-second entry, and the cordgrass there is the same honest walking thing — and I can say this: the walk is the animal’s own, or it is bought with a swab, and the two walks can be told apart by anyone who will stand still for five minutes. The grey mare was shipped north on Tuesday. Her price was the walk, and the walk was the burn. That is the whole of the entry, and it is enough, the way a diary of the gait should be kept: what the eye shows, write down, and do not explain.

  • 10 — of them showing the high action, at rest
  • 4 — turned away at the gate by the inspectors, Saturday
  • 2 — sections of the statute read aloud at the fence (the showing clause, the transport clause)
  • 1 — $1,000 fine on the books for a first offense; six months the longest stretch for it
  • 1 — line of the almanac copied down uncorrected, question mark included
  • 0 — swabs recovered; the feed room swept clean by ten
