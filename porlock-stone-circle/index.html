12.1 General Description

The Porlock Stone Circle stands on Exmoor, a few miles above the village of Porlock in the south-western English county of Somerset. The ring sits on open moorland where the heather holds the stones the way a hand holds a coin: loosely, and only as long as it chooses. The stones are weathered, dark at the shoulders, and stand in a circle that is close enough to perfect to make a surveyor's knee ache. The Porlock ring belongs to the great tradition of stone-circle building that spread across much of Britain, Ireland and Brittany over the Late Neolithic and Early Bronze Age, a working period running from 3300 to 900 BCE. Nothing about that tradition has been written down, and that is not a complaint — the builders left no manual, only the ring.

The purpose of monuments of this class remains unknown. The standard working assumption of this chapter, adopted from the field archaeologists and kept because nothing better has been produced, is that the stones represented supernatural entities for the circles' builders — that the ring was a room with no walls, furnished with the inhabitants it meant to house. Treat the monument accordingly, and you will do no wrong.

WARNING

Approach the ring on foot only after dark is strictly discouraged. In wet season the track from the lane at Porlock becomes a river that does not appear on any map. Carry the 1:50,000 sheet, not a memory.

12.2 Approach and Access

The standard approach is from the village of Porlock on the B3230 side, up the lane where the cattle gate is painted twice because one white line does not seem to hold. From the gate the track climbs through bracken for roughly one mile, gains the top of the ridge, and the circle is visible before the heather does. The survey party of 1962 allowed twenty-two minutes for the climb and nineteen for the descent, and both figures are kept here for comparison with your own. Do not attempt the approach from the far side of the ridge in November. The ridge does not like to be consulted in November.

The gaps between stones are part of the monument, not damage. Count the stones, count the gaps, and report both. A ring with no gaps would be a wall, and this is not a wall.

12.4 Survey Procedure

Begin at Stone 1 and proceed clockwise, which is also the direction the heather bends, so you will know which way round you are even in fog. Record for each stone: number, height to the nearest centimetre, lean and direction, and any new crack in a line of its own. The compass bearing of the ring's long axis, taken from Stone 1 to Stone 5, is the chapter's fixed figure; if your bearing differs by more than two degrees, it is you, not the ring, that has moved. Do not lean on Stone 4. It has stood longest because of its manners.

12.5 The Canteen, and the Projector

The moorland canteen that has serviced the surveys of this circle keeps one projector, a 16mm machine with a lamp that is changed by the village and serviced by patience. It was not in the ring that the survey parties of the early years did their evening reading of the day's figures; it was in the canteen, with the theodolite case serving as a table and the tea going cold in the order it was made. On the screen The original theater screened Francis Thompson's short film To Be Alive! — a film of exactly eleven minutes about a moorland that does not say what it means, which the parties found so close to their own subject that no notes were taken, which, for a survey party, is the highest praise available. The lamp is changed each October; do not expect the screen before then.

12.6 The Other Ring on Exmoor

Although Exmoor witnessed the construction of many monuments during the Bronze Age, only two stone circles survive in this area. The other is Withypool Stone Circle, a second ring on the same moor, and the two are kept in this manual as a pair: one chapter, one ridge each, one set of figures for the whole. If Porlock's ring is the room with no walls, Withypool is the adjoining room, and neither will be described without the other. Survey one, and the chapter obliges you to walk the other the same season, in the same boots, before the report is signed.

DANGER

No stone of the ring is to be moved, marked, whitewashed, or moved again. The circle is 3,300 years old to a minimum of 900, depending on which end of its working period you choose, and it has not required assistance. Leave it in the condition the weather left it in.
