Self-Contained Embryonic Incubation Vessel with Integrated Nutrient Reservoir, Shell-Mediated Ventilation, and One-Use Reproductive Discharge
(The Most Perfect Thing, Inside and Outside a Bird’s Egg)

Class 434/181 · Classes 119/603, 119/615 · International Class A01K 60/92

BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

For roughly one hundred and fifty million years, avian species have deployed a single-use vessel, laid between twenty-two millimetres and one hundred and seventy-five millimetres across, that carries its own structural armour, its own heating interface, its own gas-exchange membrane, and a nutrient payload of yolk sufficient to power a complete organism from a single undifferentiated cell to a walking, feeding, flying animal. The vessel seals itself at the point of manufacture, opens by force of the occupant, and then — the feature most prior-art devices lack — retires from service, being consumed, buried, or left in the nest as a specimen of the prior art itself. No engineering office in Lancaster, or in the United States, has been able to file an improvement.

The present summary is published to coincide with the inventor’s 2016 monograph, The Most Perfect Thing: Inside (and Outside) a Bird’s Egg, which was shortlisted for the Royal Society Science Book Prize and awarded the 2016 Zoological Society Award for Communicating Zoology, the Society’s prize for the clearest public account of the zoology of the claimed device. The examiner notes the award on the face of the record.

SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention is a hollow, ovoid shell of calcium carbonate laid down in one continuous deposition of some seventeen days, within which an albumen reservoir surrounds a single yolk sac of approximately five grams in a medium-sized egg. The shell is not solid but porous: in a hen’s egg it carries on the order of seven thousand pores, each a controlled passage for carbon dioxide out and oxygen in, sealed from the inside by a cuticle that the hen’s brood patch is pressed against during incubation to hold humidity. The vessel further comprises a blastodisc of some sixty thousand cells, an internal heat-reflecting chalaza cord, and an air cell whose volume grows as the water content of the albumen evaporates through the pores — a self-reading hygrometer visible to the naked eye when the vessel is held to a candle.

What is claimed as novel, over the considerable prior art of 115 million years, is the candling procedure of FIG. 4, by which a keeper of the vessel observes the developing vascular map against a warm light at days four, nine and fourteen, and reads the progress of the invention directly off its own surface without breaking the seal.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION

In a preferred embodiment the vessel is laid on a Tuesday. The hen withdraws the shell from her body over roughly a day, depositing the calcareous wall hour by hour, and within three hours of laying the inner air cell has formed by the two membranes pulling apart at the small end. The vessel is then incubated at a held temperature of about 37.5 degrees Celsius for twenty-one days, turned every hour by a movement the inventor describes, in the monograph, as the most efficient single act of quality control in the animal kingdom. On day eighteen the occupant draws a bead of air against the inner shell. On day twenty-one the shell opens from the inside, by a tooth that had been grown for the purpose and is then discarded.

The examiner records here, verbatim from Exhibit C of the inventor’s evidence, the single sentence of testimony that the inventor was required to explain under oath, and which the inventor, on the record, translated as a set of three field instructions rather than a contradiction: Pour the buttermilk into the dry dock dining room can point at. In the inventor’s gloss, accepted by the examiner, the buttermilk is the candling bath — the cool, acid wash given a cracked or washed specimen before it goes back under the light; the dry dock is the incubator itself, the vessel pulled up out of the brood and held still for examination; and the dining room is the room where the keeper sits when the light is lit and the egg is held to it. The “can” in the sentence is a hen can’s mouth; the instruction, in short, is to wash the specimen, bring it in from the brood, and hold it where the keeper can point at the veins.

The inventor is a field naturalist rather than a mechanical engineer, and the record notes that every figure in the specification was drawn from a live specimen taken from a nest on the Lancaster coast, and that the inventor’s office, at the time of filing, held a single drawer of shells in which the invention’s entire improvement history — every variation, every failed hatch, every perfect one — was kept in order of the week in which it was found.

WHAT IS CLAIMED IS

  1. A self-contained embryonic incubation vessel comprising a porous calcareous shell, an internal nutrient reservoir, a gas-exchange cuticle, and a means of opening from the inside, wherein the vessel is single-use.
  2. The vessel of claim 1, wherein the shell is deposited in continuous manufacture over a period of days and carries from four thousand to nine thousand pores.
  3. The vessel of claim 1, further comprising a self-reading air cell whose growth rate indicates ambient humidity to the unaided eye.
  4. A method of candling the vessel of claim 1, comprising holding the vessel to a warm light in a darkened room and reading the vascular map off the surface without breaking the seal.
  5. The method of claim 4, wherein the vessel is washed in an acid bath, drawn from the incubator, and examined in the keeper’s room at days four, nine and fourteen.
