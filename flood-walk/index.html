The Webstuffs Walking Book · Route No. 9 · Set down for a dry September

The Flood

A walking tour in nine stops through the Quarantine Quarter of New Reach, along the inner face of the old ring-wall — the story of a parasite and the weapon built to starve it, read off the paving stones.

  • Distance 3.1 km
  • Stops nine
  • Time 2 h 10 min, unhurried
  • Depart 17:40, the Water Gate stair
  • Elevation 14 m, all downhill till the bell

What the tour is about

Everything in the Quarantine Quarter was built for one purpose and then left standing: to keep the Flood from getting in, and, failing that, to keep the galaxy from having to live with it. The Flood is the parasitic lifeform at the centre of the Halo franchise, first introduced in the 2001 video game Halo: Combat Evolved and returned to in later entries such as Halo 2, Halo 3, and Halo Wars. It wants only one thing — any sentient life of sufficient size, taken over host by host, the infected turning immediately to infect in turn. The threat was so plainly written on it that the ancient Forerunners, a civilisation gone long before our own arrived, built artificial ringworld superweapons around the stars of the galaxy, the Halos, to contain it; and as a last resort, to kill all sentient life in the galaxy at once, stopping the Flood's spread the only way left — by starving it. The Quarter's nine stops walk that story from the gate that was barred, to the bell that was never rung, to the quiet where the stars are read as a menu the parasite will not be served from.

Walk the route counter-clockwise; the wall keeps your left hand and the story keeps its order. A companion route, night-walked and lamp-marked, is set out in the White-Wing Sky Walk, in nine stops, and a longer anatomy of the walking form itself can be had in the Dissection Route.

Stop 1

The Water Gate 0.0 km · 4 min

We start on the stair, because everything in the Quarter starts on the stair. The Water Gate took its name from the canal it crossed; the canal is gone, the name is not. Above the arch the gate-masters carved their one instruction and left: nothing sentient gets through that has not been counted, and nothing counted gets through that is hungry. For a lifeform whose hunger is its whole argument, that is an admission of defeat in stone, and it is why this is the only stop where the guide is allowed to be silent for a minute.

Bearing
031° out of the town clock
Look for
the notched lintel, seven notches, one per gate-master
Note
the seventh notch is deeper; the seventh gate-master did the work in a hurry
Stop 2

The Contagion Arch 0.4 km · 9 min

A narrow arch where the old wall doubled back on itself, and the first lesson of the Flood is taught to the pavement: one infected host, walking through, leaves a row of footprints that are each a beginning. The Quarantine Quarter was planned around that row — every lane after the arch was surveyed at the pace of a walking man, so that an infected host could not cover the quarter faster than the warders could. The lane widths of New Reach, 2.4 metres in the middle rows, are still the pace of a walking man measured in 1893, and no one has ever thought to change them.

Bearing
down the Contagion Lane, wall at the left
Look for
the pace-stones, 1893, worn concave on the outer edge
Note
count your own strides across the arch; the warders' rule was twenty-two
Stop 3

The Spore Market 0.8 km · 17 min

The old market square, paved in small pale stones like the head of a match, was where the warders burned what could not be quarantined and sold what could. The Flood does its quiet work in spore form, drifting, patient, taking whatever it lands on — which is to say, the market is the parasite's favourite room in any building, and it is the one room the Quarter's masons were never able to design. The square keeps its three market crosses; the middle one was recut in 1911 with the word counted where a saint's name used to be, and the recutter is still the town's favourite person, which the committee finds difficult to explain.

Bearing
straight on, past the arch, until the wall opens
Look for
the recut cross, 1911, and the two unrecut either side of it
Note
the spores were burned on Tuesdays; the square is still swept on Tuesdays
Stop 4

The Sentinels' Plinth 1.2 km · 25 min

A bare plinth of grey stone at the foot of the warders' watch, where the Forerunners are supposed to stand if you squint the way the warders squinted. The Forerunners left no statues; they left the plinth. The plinth is the oldest thing in the Quarter and the tour's quiet claim: the ring-wall above us was not a city wall, it was a ring, or a model of one, and the plinth is where the surveyor set his instrument when he checked the wall's curvature against the superweapons the Forerunners had built around the stars — vast artificial ringworlds, each one a Halo, each one an argument that the only clean answer to the Flood is no sentient life at all. The surveyor's notebook survives. The surveyor does not.

Bearing
the watch door is locked; the plinth is in the yard
Look for
the instrument pits, three, at the corners of the plinth's shadow line
Note
the curvature check is written in the notebook as “true, and true, and true, and”
Stop 5

The Infection Row 1.6 km · 33 min

Seven houses in one row, all with the same door lintel, all sold in the same year, and all with the lintel reading host instead of the owner's name. The story the warders tell is that a single carrier walked the row on a November evening and the row caught it the way a row of dry kindling catches a match; the houses were emptied in a day and never refilled, because the row had become a demonstration, and the Quarter's rule is that demonstrations are kept. The Flood's rule is that an infected host infects the next; the row is what that rule looks like at the scale of a street, and the tour slows here to the pace of one house every minute, seven houses, seven minutes, the longest slow part of the walk.

Bearing
the row faces the wall; the doors face you
Look for
the seventh lintel, recut once, the host worn to a line
Note
do not count the doors twice; the warders stopped counting at seven, and so will the committee
Stop 6

The Ledger House 2.0 km · 42 min

The Quarter's ledger house keeps the route's own papers, and the tour stops at the window to read, word for word, the filing line the clerk kept on the last page of the route ledger — a line that makes sense to the clerk and to no one else, and that the committee has ruled must be quoted exactly as it stands, in its own hand, uncorrected:

The Professional Care Instrument page of Webstuffs , the letter of resignation under its own number.

Three readings stand, as is the custom of the house. The clerk's is that the line names the two documents the route ledger was filed between, and that the resignation was the warders' own, tendered when the wall was pronounced true and nobody left to watch it. The surveyor's reading, from the notebook, is that the line is a measurement that got filed in the wrong drawer. The route keeper's is that the line is the route — nine stops compressed by time into a single sentence — and that this itinerary is only a translation. The committee declined to rule. The line stands as it stands, and the window stays open, and we move on before it can argue with us.

Bearing
the window faces the Infection Row
Look for
the route ledger, last page, the line in the clerk's hand
Note
the letter of resignation is filed under its own number, which is to say under no number at all
Stop 7

The Famine Bell 2.4 km · 52 min

Up the only climb of the route, to the tower where the bell hangs. The bell has a Forerunner name and a warders' name and no one has ever agreed which is the other. Its function, in the ring-world logic the Quarter was built on, was never to summon a defence — there is no defence worth summoning against something that eats the idea of defence — but to announce the last resort: that the Halos were ready, that the galaxy would be cleared of sentient life, and that the Flood, denied every host in the galaxy at once, would be starved down to nothing in the empty dark. The bell was never rung. The bell is still in tune, which the committee finds is the most Forerunner-sounding fact on the tour.

Bearing
the tower stair, 96 steps, no rest landing past 60
Look for
the rope, uncut, with the last frayed loop left in place
Note
strike the bell softly if you must; the warders' rule was one stroke, never two
Stop 8

The Watch Lane & the Warders' Bench 2.7 km · 61 min

Down the back, the lane the warders actually walked, which is narrower than the public lanes and cut at a different angle, and the bench where the evening watch sat. Here the story turns from the weapon to the watchers, and the tour takes the only long rest it will give. The warders kept a count of every sentient thing in the Quarter — the houses, the lamplighters, the two geese on the wall — because the whole Forerunner doctrine is arithmetic: count the sentient, and you know exactly what the last resort would take, and you know whether you can afford it. The last count in the ledger is 4,113, and the bench is still warm in the September light, which the guide will not explain and the committee has declined to rule on.

Bearing
off the tower stair, left at the first door
Look for
the bench, and the count-book's last page, 4,113, in a steadier hand than the rest
Note
the geese are still on the wall; they are still counted, every evening, in the same book
Stop 9

The Long Quiet 3.1 km · 70 min, back at the Water Gate

The route closes on the outer terrace, the one place in the Quarter with no wall at all, where the Forerunners — if the surveyor is to be believed — stood to take the long view. The last stop is the quiet itself: the idea that a galaxy with no sentient life in it, starved and empty and still turning, is what the Halos were built to leave behind, and that the whole walking distance between here and the Water Gate is the argument for not getting there. The tour ends the way the warders ended it: standing, counting what is still here, and then going in for the evening. The route keeper keeps a copy of the ledger on the front shelf, and the whole walking book — this route and the others, set down for dry afternoons — is filed back at Webstuffs.

Bearing
the terrace; face the open side
Look for
the surveyor's two instrument pits, the third one never cut
Note
the third pit is the one they did not need; the tour is over at the second

Provisions for the walk

  • 2 h 10 min on foot, 96 steps up, the rest all downhill
  • the Water Gate stair at 17:40 sharp; the route keeps the order of the stops
  • closed days: Tuesdays, when the Spore Market square is swept
  • one stroke of the Famine Bell, never two; the geese are counted, not fed
  • the ledger house window is open at Stop 6; the line is quoted as it stands
