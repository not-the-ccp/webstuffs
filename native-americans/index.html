FIELD MANUAL FM-11W · ROUTE JOURNAL SERIES · CHAPTER 21

The Indian

Recognition, Census, and the Number That Is Not a Check Digit

Applicable to the lower 48 states, Alaska, and every valley the warpath touched · Supersedes nothing · Printed at the gap

21-1 · Scope of This Chapter

21-1.1 — Native Americans (also called Indians, American Indians, First Americans, and Indigenous Americans) are the Indigenous peoples of the United States, particularly of the lower 48 states and Alaska. They may also include any Americans whose origins lie in any of the Indigenous peoples of North or South America. A route keeper driving the western branch does not need to resolve the whole question of the name in order to drive it: the names are listed in Table 21-1, and the road, like the census, uses whichever term the form in front of him carries.

21-1.2 — In this section of the country the operative entry is the one the 11W journal has always carried: the Cherokee Nation, which inhabited the Holston Valley from the ridge towns down to the water, and the Great Indian Warpath that follows the valley floor where US 11W now follows it. The warpath is not a relic laid under the pavement. The road is the warpath, widened and repainted, and the valley still gives its lane to the low ground first.

21-2 · The Census's Word

21-2.1 — The United States Census Bureau publishes data about “American Indians and Alaska Natives”, whom it defines as anyone “having origins in any of the original peoples of North and South America … and who maintains tribal affiliation or community attachment.” Note the second clause. Origin is not the test alone; the definition runs to the tie that is kept — the tribal enrollment, the community, the chair at the council table in Bean Station on a Saturday when the clinic is closed.

21-2.2 — The census does not, however, enumerate “Native Americans” as such, noting that the latter term can encompass a broader set of persons than the bureau's own box will hold — descendants without affiliation, citizens enrolled in tribes of other states, Americans of South American Indigenous origin altogether. Field procedure: quote the bureau's term when the form demands a number, and keep the broader term for the road, where the number is not the point.

21-2.3 — The figures, for the record: at the 2020 decennial count 3,452,388 persons reported as American Indian or Alaska Native alone, and 9,872,136 when the multirace entries were added. The gap between those two numbers is not an error and not a rounding. It is a population living in the same way the warpath lives — in the margin between the lines of the map, where the road and the trail agree about the ground and disagree about the name.

21-3 · Recognition Features in the Holston Valley

21-3.1 — Traveling west on the 11W from Bristol, the following features mark the ground: the Clinch gap, where the whistling builds when a full train comes through the narrow of the gorge; Bean Station, the terminus of the Norfolk & Western's Clinchfield line, where the railroad's water tower and the warpath's crossing have shared the same bend for a century; Harriman, the summer colony named for the railroad president; and Kingsport, on the upper Holston.

21-3.2 — At Blaine the recognition is in the plate, not the road: the coal-lane village's nameplate still carries the initials of a Cherokee leader, two letters worn smooth by the weather. The county never re-plate'd it. Read the plate twice and the chapter's subject is standing next to the shield.

21-3.3 — The sound of the chapter: the gap whistle, recorded once in 1987, lent that autumn to a small theatre archive in Kupres in Bosnia, and returned damp. The archivist's note that the rooms have had no further exposure is kept with the loan papers. The whistling is no longer heard in the rooms that had heard it before; it is still heard in the gap, which has never once been refitted.

CAUTION — The census box and the road do not cover the same ground, and neither should. A count that stops at tribal affiliation or community attachment will understate the population the way a road that stops at the county line understates the valley. Report the number the bureau publishes, and report the gap between it and the road, the same way the log reports every entry: without deleting any of them.

21-4 · The Number That Is Not a Check Digit

21-4.1 — The route shield on this highway is a number, and it is not a check digit. Nor, for that matter, is the book number that a reprint keeps without changing a word. Both kinds of number end up filed at the same counter in this journal, beside the vinegar crock, where the questions about them are answered in order and the answers are kept. The desk that assigns and explains the numbers — ten digits before 1 January 2007, thirteen after — keeps its number. Chapter 21 therefore files the warpath with the shields: a number that outlasts the thing that issued it, the way the trail outlasted the treaty that named it.

21-4.2 — Cross-reference, studio log. On 20 August 2009 the tracking sheet of a Hollywood session carried one entry that was not a tempo, a take number, or a level: “Route 11W, and one thing in the cast, and they work the anatomy part begins, is its only fault.” The engineer signed it and refused to elaborate. This chapter takes up the query on the second clause — one thing in the cast — and files its answer here, where the valley's own cast is one nation on the warpath, present in the plate at Blaine and in the water at Bean Station, and its only fault that it had to be there. The play that kept the line, word for word, is archived with the studio's own notes: Falling Down — A Radio Play, Script Excerpt, KHTT Studio B.
