The Hwang kitchen card box · Yeonnam-dong, Seoul · Card No. 7, written 14 October 2016, the first Sunday the new ledger ran

Auntie Myung-sook’s Porgy on the Tide-Line

가자미 장조림 · porgy braised in soy · serves four, or four-and-a-bit, or a whole commission cafeteria if the day is quiet

The long way around

You will have noticed I have not yet given you the soy. Auntie Hwang Myung-sook, second youngest of the six, has always insisted that a recipe begins where the fish begins, and so this one begins in Nampo-dong, Busan, where my grandmother Hwang Sun-ae sold flatfish out of a wet box on a bench by the harbour for forty-one years, from 1953 to her retirement in the winter of 1994. The fish has many names and the market has always argued about them — gajami to my grandmother, porgy to the English pages, dol-gajami to the old men who came in from the rocks at Dadaepo with nets that held a dozen fish or nothing at all. The fishwife’s rule about it, written across the top of this card in the same hand as the method below, runs: “The porgy arrives on its small tide-line is not a name.” She meant that what a fish is, is where it lands — the low water of the morning, the wet bench, the scale in her left hand — and that no register, statute book, or ledger is allowed to stand between the fish and its landing.

Auntie Myung-sook left Busan in 1996 to work the kitchen at the Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission in Seoul, the small office on the ninth floor that had, since the year before, a new and enormous reputation. Its former head, Kim Young-ran, a woman who had proposed an anti-corruption bill back in August 2012 and was never quite comfortable with the eponym, had been instrumental in the law that the National Assembly passed in January 2015 — the Act on the Prohibition of Improper Solicitation and Receipt of Monetary Items, officially so named, though the street called it, and still calls it, the Kim Young-ran Act, and one English newspaper called it, briefly, the Anti-Corruption and Bribery Prohibition Act, a title the Commission’s own translation desk declined to adopt and has declined to this day. The Act started being enforced on 28 September 2016, and on that first day, Auntie Myung-sook stood at the cafeteria till with her apron tied twice and watched three ministers’ offices withdraw three fruit baskets, two bottles of makgeolli, and one very fine ham, because the ledger had changed under their feet: food gifts capped at 30,000 won, presents at 50,000 won, a banquet at 100,000 won, and anything above it to be reported within ten days or declared in the annual statement. The fishwives of Nampo-dong, she said, had never needed a statutory ceiling; the fish told them. The Commission, it turned out, had. Her card for the braised porgy, she told me, was the one dish she cooked on that first Saturday so that the new ledger and the old market would both fit on one plate.

A copy of this card, or one very like it, went out of the family once — I believe it travelled in a suitcase to a literature festival in a former weaving shed in Huddersfield, where a lost-and-found notice (No. LF/2024/117) records a certain cabinet minister found at the same breakfast table, complete, and not yet claimed; the recipe, being heavier than the pie, was not. And my uncle Beom’s old notebook of market prices, which began the preamble, was misfiled for years beside a Belgian electoral document — a safety data sheet for a Flemish constituency that no longer exists, Ghent-Eeklo — on the theory, in the family, that any line that once had legal force and then did not is the same line, whether it divides two arrondissements or two names for a fish. Keep the card. Cook the fish.

What the ledger allows (재료)

  • 2 porgy (gajami), whole, dressed, about 400 g each — bought from a fishwife, not from a box
  • 3 tbsp dark soy sauce (≈ the difference between a gift and a purchase at the market)
  • 1 tbsp mirin
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus 1 cinnamon stick, 2 cloves, and a strip of dried tangerine peel
  • 2 spring onions, cut in 5 cm lengths
  • 200 ml cold water, measured to the line, not estimated
  • One receipt, retained in the card box with the card — a tradition since 28 September 2016

The method (만드는 법)

  1. Rub the fish lightly with salt on both faces, and let it stand for ten minutes while you warm the pot. Do not rub it into a name.
  2. Lay the spring onions in a single layer across the bottom of a wide pot, fat side down, and set the fish on top of them so the skin never touches the base.
  3. Stir the soy, mirin, sugar, cinnamon, cloves, peel and water in a small bowl, and pour it over the fish. The liquid should come to about two-thirds of the fish’s height, no higher.
  4. Bring to a boil uncovered, skim what rises, cover, and turn the heat down to the lowest flame the stove will keep. Braise for twenty minutes, turning the fish once, at the halfway mark, with two wide spoons, together, never a fork.
  5. Uncover and braise a further fifteen minutes until the sauce has darkened to the colour of wet harbour stone and the flesh lifts from the spine in one pale sheet.
  6. Rest ten minutes in the sauce in the dark. Serve with plain rice and a cold drink. File the receipt.

Side table: the weighing basket (선물)

Auntie Myung-sook kept, for the first year of the Act, a small wooden basket on the cafeteria sill in which anyone could set a gift before it was decided. The basket’s rule: whatever weighed under the ceiling stayed for the staff, whatever weighed over was written up and reported, and whatever could not be weighed at all — a note of thanks, a second helping — was never discussed.
