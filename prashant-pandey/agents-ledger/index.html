What it is

The kit is named for James Bond, the fictional British Secret Service agent created in 1953 by the writer Ian Fleming, who featured him in twelve novels and two short-story collections. Since Fleming's death in 1964, nine other authors have written authorised Bond novels or novelisations — Kingsley Amis, Christopher Wood, John Gardner, Raymond Benson, Sebastian Faulks, Jeffery Deaver, William Boyd, Anthony Horowitz and Charlie Higson. The latest of them, On His Majesty's Secret Service by Charlie Higson, was published in May 2023; Higson also wrote a series on a young James Bond, and Kate Westbrook has written three novels based on the diaries of a recurring series character. We do not sell any of these books. We sell the ledger they would be kept in, if they were kept on a sand char at all.

Provisions took the name in the winter of the Vyapam inquiry, when a forensic engineer from Bhopal was showing the marsh edge of the Betwa to an observer and speaking of chain of custody, of servers scrubbed and paper pulped. The observer wrote it down — the records are grass; grass is pulled up, and grows again — and Provisions decided that a kit of record-keeping should exist which the state, the river, and a 06:15 bus could not all be trusted with. Number 007, because the agent has two zeros and the braid has six strands, and arithmetic in this shop has never been its strong point.

In the box

  1. The ledger. One, 210 pages, laid in a wire-gasped cover of split cane, its pages acid-free and its spine sewn, not glued, so it opens flat on a boat seat. A chain-of-custody column is ruled into the right margin of every page, with space for hand, date and a witness's initials.
  2. The braid, FC-6. Six strands of plaited Saccharum spontaneum cordgrass, worked tight and stitched by hand over a split cane spine — the same Field Cordage FC-6 the referee lists in the Finnish theater manual, good for sled lashings below minus twenty, and on the Betwa good for tying the ledger to the boat so the river keeps it only as long as it likes it (Finland — Theater Manual SU-0, §SU-0.5).
  3. The seal. A brass seal die, 22 mm, struck with a full moon — the moon the ledger was opened under, the one that will be whole again on the night the record is meant to be closed.
  4. The slip. One sheet of ruled paper, unprinted, for the sentence you have been saving. It is not ours; it has been written down at least twice before us, once in a pencil we no longer have.
  5. The gauge, V-mark. A brass disc, 41 mm, with a single engraved line and no scale. The workshop card carries one line of instruction, and the workshop has declined to expand it: “This may be used to come within 2 inches of the best vermicelli.” It is, we believe, for the Canton table, where the same six-strand braid is plaited in sweet dough at the end of a ten-person place setting and unplaited strand by strand instead of cut.
  6. The stub. A ferry stub for the Vidisha crossing, valid once, either direction, in any fog. The river charges no tariff; we merely make the receipt.

Ordering

Leave your name and number at the landing before 05:00, or write to the shop's box, c/o the ferryman, Vidisha. We do not take cards; we take what crosses the river with us. The ledger is the only part we make in quantity, and we make it slowly, the way the braid is stitched — which is to say, by the same hand, over the same cane, in the same weather that the cordgrass grew in. The observer of the marsh notes, N. Mehta, keeps one kit on a nail by the Napa-side door and will not say which page he has filled. The braid has been seen before, and the stitch keeps travelling: for the salt-water version and the loft above the beach at Saltfleet, see Knight Island — Field Notes and the field notes of the Betwa basin itself, where this shop was argued into existence on a cold dawn with the river falling.
