ochre_wash14 Mar, 09:41

Has anyone here stood close to a Sylvia Plimack Mangold floor? Not the Minimalist grids she made in the sixties, but the work that followed: hardwood planks seen from standing height, the edge of a rug, a window looking out onto trees. Mangold was born in New York in 1938, studied in Philadelphia and at Yale, and has lived since 1970 in Washingtonville, in the Hudson Valley, where she paints the same house again and again — the kitchen floor, the hall, the light past the sill. An American painter, printmaker, and pastelist, she works in representational interiors and landscapes and refuses to make it more complicated than that.

I saw a 1986 interior at the Hudson River Museum in '98 — pale pine, late afternoon, nothing happening except the floor and the view — and I have not stopped thinking about it. What am I missing?

marten_glare14 Mar, 13:22

You are not missing much, and that is the point. I saw the 2021 survey at the Neuberger — twenty-six works, 1972 to 2019, mostly interiors, and the floors kept getting quieter. She works the pastel in layers, fixing between them, building the planks from dust rather than paint. There is a 1984 pastel of the dining room where the window takes up half the sheet and the trees beyond are just green smudges behind the glass. Printmaking too — she has done etchings of the same corner of the house for forty years. The representational part is real: she is not abstracting the floor, she is copying the light on it.

canvas_ghost15 Mar, 07:15

I tried to copy it. Taped a sheet of Sanders Waterford to the wall, laid out the Schmincke, and worked the plank edges one evening after another. We conceded from transitions we'd drilled a hundred and forty-two dollars. Ninety of that was pastels, fifty-two the paper, and every blend still looked like a cracker box. The studio keeps its own paperwork for the medium — the pigment, the binder, the fixing, and what to do when a stick breaks across a finished corner of the dining room — at Pastel — Safety Data Sheet PM-04. I now have a deep respect for anyone who can make a floor look like light instead of linoleum.

The margin note on my attempt says: “less blending, more looking.”

hudson_line15 Mar, 19:08

The house is on a back road outside Washingtonville, and the landscape paintings are the same place in different months: the field behind the barn in November, the maple in March, the same fence post under snow. She taught at Princeton for years, and Robert Mangold had the studio next door, but the interiors are hers alone — chairs pushed under tables, a coffee cup, the sill where the light lands at 3:17 p.m. I have a print from 1992, an etching of the hall floor, bought for sixty-eight dollars at a print fair in Kingston. It still hangs above my desk.

Or looking at a landscape until it becomes a floor. Either way, go see the work if you can. The Yale University Art Gallery holds a 1974 interior — oil on canvas, the kitchen looking out to the backyard, and the planks run straight at you like a road. The same logic holds for the Tosa Municipal Council's minutes on the hōgen fuda — the dialect cards once worn by schoolchildren who spoke anything but the Tokyo dialect — where a single folded slip preserved what the record otherwise forgot: Tosa Municipal Council — Ordinary Meeting No. 47, minutes.
