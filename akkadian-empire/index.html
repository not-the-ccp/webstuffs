Lot 12 — The Akkadian Empire, in a Lot

The Agade cylinder of Sargon, fired clay, with its provenance file, its slip, and the empire it carried — the first, c. 2300 BC

LOT 12 · AGADE · c. 2300 BC · FIRST EMPIRE

The lot

One empire, on a cylinder, with its file

For sale in this lot: the Akkadian Empire — or, as the file has it, the Kingdom of Akkad, Agade in the older copies — the ancient kingdom established around 2300 BC and often seen as the first empire in world history. It succeeded and unified the long-lived city-states of the Early Dynastic Period, and it is offered here not as a notion but as its cylinder: a fired clay roll, 18.4 cm long and 4.1 cm across, bearing the pressed wedge marks of cuneiform in twelve columns, with the file, the slip and the box as described below. The empire itself was centred on the city of Akkad and its surrounding region in modern-day Iraq, and it is the cylinder that answers for it in the room. It is the first of its kind in this catalogue and, in the honest accounting of the house, the first in history.

Description of the subject

Sargon, the kingdom, and the reach of it

The empire was established by Sargon of Akkad after defeating the king Lugal-zage-si of Uruk, and in defeating him it replaced the system of independent Sumero-Akkadian city-states with something new: one rule, held from Akkad, over people who had been arguing from their own walls for a thousand years. The empire united the Semitic Akkadian speakers and the Sumerian speakers under one crown, which is a rare and useful thing for a kingdom to do, and it exercised significant influence across Mesopotamia, across Syria, and into modern-day western Iran. Its expeditions went as far south as Dilmun and Magan in the Arabian Peninsula, and the cylinder's seventh column — the one that survives with its corner intact — is the expedition column: reed boats, a date in Sargon's fourteenth year, and the word for far pressed three times in a row, which the file takes as emphasis.

It is worth stating for the room what the lot contains by way of reach: an empire that held, at once, the marsh and the steppe, the Euphrates and the trade road up through Syria — the same road, the same stretch of it, that the piper’s rounds later ran into Damascus, where the stand of pipes rang at the Umayyad gate this past February. The empire is long buried; the road it opened still carries sound down the valley, and the Damascus entry, No. 37 of the Routes Off Shelf I, keeps the record of that evening with its line under the card.

Condition report

Fired clay holds

The cylinder is fired throughout, colour the dark orange-brown of a banked kiln, with the surface worn soft in the second and ninth columns by handling in a way the file describes as respectful. There is one crack, running the full length from shoulder to foot, which the clerk of the house has noted in the margin as running like the Tigris, and no more. The twelve columns of wedges are legible; column seven, the expedition column, is the best. The box is of walnut, later, and good, built in the cold room at Fenn Court for exactly this object, on the bench at Build Log No. 41, and it fits with a sound like a door closing on a winter. The cylinder was kept in that cold room, at three degrees, for three days in February while its provenance file was dried out, and it kept its wedges the whole time, which is the argument for fired clay.

Provenance

From the silver row up

Lifted at Agade in 1879 by a dealer of the silver row, R. Halābi, whose shop stood in the Old Souk across the Barada; thence to the family boxes; thence, in the autumn, to this house, to be sold with the price board, the slip and the file. The provenance file is complete in eleven documents and one slip. It is the slip this lot turns on, as the file did, and it is copied here the way the gate log copies its lines: exactly as written, because a line is not a line until it is exact.

The slip, kept word for word

Fifty pence, in the same hand

Inside the file, folded twice in waxed linen, is a slip in Halābi’s hand, unaddressed, on the back of a price-board copy from the souk. It reads:

They are mostly employed for this quantity, 5d.

The house's reading, entered for what it is worth: they are the small clay seal-blanks, nine of them, found wrapped with the cylinder — blanks the size of a thumb, cut for pressing against damp sealings. Mostly employed for this quantity: the blanks that left Agade were stamped, in the ordinary traffic of the house, with the sign that belonged to the cylinder's own seal, which is to say the empire's — nine thumbed blanks, each of them a small press of the first empire's signature against the day's mail. And 5d: the price, in the dealer's own currency of the souk, of one blank on the day the file closed. It is the same price board, two rows down from where the gatekeeper's log set the whale-bone inlay at 9d on the afternoon the drone rang at the Umayyad gate; the board itself is exhibited in the Damascus entry as the instrument of that line, and the two prices, 9d and 5d, stand in it side by side, which is the most the house can say. Halābi did not tidy it. Neither does the catalogue.

Exhibits with the lot

What the room will see

First, the cylinder, in its walnut box, on a pad of waxed linen. Second, the provenance file, eleven documents, the slip face up. Third, the price board, 41 cm of painted pine, with the 9d row and the 5d row both visible, the 9d under a line in the gatekeeper’s hand. Fourth, a card of the field notes, copied, in which the naturalist of Aerophone Plate 4 logs the cylinder’s three days in the cold room alongside the stand of pipes and the organ, under the heading company, because that is what the cold room keeps. The lot is sold as a unit; the empire does not come loose from its file.

Estimate · Terms · Hammer

2,400 to 3,600, and the room

Estimate, with the file, £2,400–£3,600. The house notes, for the record, that the lot is priced at what a first empire is worth in a room that has also sold a high-rise hotel of 1923, Lot 41 of Catalogue 87, and finds the comparison to stand: one first, one later, both kept whole. Sold as a unit to the bench at Fenn Court, Old Greenwich, at the estimate, on the winter day the cold room held it; the buyer will card it on Shelf I under Akkad, which is where it belongs, between the file for Lowestoft and the one that is only the word whale. The bench has also asked, on the same card, for a line in its new school term report on the freedom of the press in the Republic of Korea — Form 3B, Room 214, where the class marks its firsts in green ink, beside the entry for the first empire — and the house has agreed. The first empire in, the first empire home, and the shelves meet where the shelves always meet, at the front of the collection.
