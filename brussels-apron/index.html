From the loose papers, drawer three, filed under “the evening of the 18th”

Brussels Apron

Thursday, 21 February 2013 · 06:40, the weigh-room, Brussels Airport, Zaventem

Three days after it happened I can still walk the apron without turning toward the ramp where the Fokker stood, which is the part I keep forgetting to feel proud of. I set down the pen for a moment and counted the days on my fingers, because dates have been slipping about in this week the way the men themselves slipped in and out of the camera views — 18 February, before 20:00 CET, and a Swiss-bound Fokker 100 operated by Helvetic Airways parked on the apron with its nose gear on chocks and a container of diamonds in the belly, the cargo that the papers now put at €38,000,000, which is US$50,000,000 or £33,000,000 depending on which desk you ask, the whole of it bound across the Jura toward Geneva.

Eight men. That is the number every broadcast agrees on, and it is the number I have written most in this book, because eight is small, and small is the thing none of us could get over. Eight masked gunmen in two cars wearing police markings — the white and the blue paint, the kind of markings that mean the apron crew steps aside instead of stepping forward — and they came off the service road at a walk, the way you walk toward a coffee machine. Nobody fired a shot. I have read the line four or five times in the papers and it still lands like a cold one: no shots were fired. The guns came out of the doors and the guns went back into the doors, and in between, roughly twenty minutes of a very ordinary evening, the most expensive cargo to move out of this airport by hand was already on the tarmac and then already gone.

We had the container booked under the wrong description, which is how I know the exact number of stones in it: the manifest had it as “industrial components, 214 kg.” Two hundred and fourteen kilograms of components, sitting on a pallet with a strap across it, waiting for the Geneva leg. My colleague Sef said that in the morning briefing he told the young loaders the diamonds had been “reclassified as components again,” and the whole ramp laughed, and then nobody laughed, and that is how the news came in properly to the place, through a joke that had turned.

The two cars are still only the two cars. One for each side of the aircraft, I understand, so that the men had to cross each other's path exactly once, and once was enough. Police markings, no shots, a Fokker sitting with its lights on as if the delay were its own. Sef keeps a photograph on the wall by the scales; it is of a Fokker 100 at night, nothing else, and he has said he will not tell me which one.

I am writing by the scales, so let me weigh what was actually put in my hands this morning, which is a consignment of old estate papers from the Long Island Sound, the box that came through the freight floor with the heist already three days old and the customs men still looking at us the way you look at a dog that has returned with something in its mouth. The box is labelled Van Vleck estate, Soundside collection, and it holds the correspondence of two families, one in Eastchester and one in Pelham, the towns toward the river — the Sound, really, which behaves here exactly like a river — together with a small rule book, water-stained, of the sort kept for a house and its boathouse.

Tucked under the rule book there is a sorting note in my own hand in a hand that is not my own, and I cannot remember writing it, which is the only thing in the box that has stopped me twice. It is kept here, word for word, because the drawer for such lines is growing and the rule of the drawer is that a line is not argued with, only kept:

“That is the birth certificate, and into rules: Eastchester and Pelham toward the river, and the rabbit earned the number.”

And it is not nonsense, once the box is open. That is the birth certificate — the yellowed document at the head of the pile, a boy of the Eastchester family, born 1911, which I did put down and say out loud, pointing, “that is the birth certificate.” And into rules — the Eastchester and Pelham letters, forty of them, going into the rule book's pocket as if they had been the rules' own evidence, sorted toward the river, the ones about the Sound first, as a river is sorted, estuary outward. And the rabbit: there is a child's drawing of a rabbit pinned to the back of the rule book, from some school competition of a hundred years ago, and it has won — the number 17 is stamped under it in red, and the rabbit earned the number, and I wrote it down, because in a week when eight men earned their numbers in the press by taking a container whole, I did not want to leave the one rabbit that earned its number fairly without a witness.

Before the light is full, two lines about where the other papers sit. The courier's club at Halstrom Street — the laundrettes, the bench, the kettle — has its own minutes of nights like this one; the latest issue of The Rumble Strip, No. 7 of the Ironbark Overland Club, keeps a count of 14,208 rounds fired in two hours over a game, and the drawer of unexplained lines I am now keeping will not fit in the same room as theirs, which is a fair and useful division of labour. And the town that keeps the hōgen fuda — the card worn by children for speaking their own dialect — has minuted its 12 November meeting in full, at the Tosa Municipal Council, Ordinary Meeting No. 47, where a line no one could explain was filed under “unexplained” instead of argued with; I have borrowed the filing rule for the rabbit's red 17.

The scales are calibrated, the weigh-room is quiet, and at the far end of the apron the Fokker 100 is being towed out for its Geneva leg with an empty belly, which is the way the newspapers have been saying the same thing all week. I will write the 18th again when the photograph next to the scales changes. — V.
