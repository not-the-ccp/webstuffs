Kingsmead Primary School · History & Home Life · Term Report Form 3

The Munich Mouser

A report on the chief mouser of the Cabinet Office, submitted in fulfilment of the second Wednesday of Term 2, and checked against the caretaker’s ledger, three newspapers, and one small medal.

Presented by
Dora E. Finch, Class 5A, seat 11
Supervisor
Mr. C. H. Aldridge, History & Home Life
Subject of the report
Bob, known as the Munich Mouser and also simply as Munich, c. 1936 – c. 5 August 1943
Office held
Chief mouser, the Cabinet Office, 1937–1943

1. Who Bob is

Bob, nicknamed the Munich Mouser and also known simply as Munich, was a cat who served as the chief mouser to the Cabinet Office from 1937 to 1943. He was born, the caretaker’s ledger says, c. 1936, “in a hatbox, on the steps, in the rain,” which is the only time I have seen a birth record written like a weather report. He died c. 5 August 1943, on a Sunday, in the store room under the third-floor window, which the ledger notes he had “chosen long before he needed it.” He served under the prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, from 1937 to 1940, and his successor Winston Churchill until 1943. I have counted the months. It comes to one hundred and sixty-five, give or take a season, which is longer than I have been alive by exactly one summer, and I found this out by accident and now I cannot un-know it.

The ledger is a primary source and I will credit it once. The rest of the hatbox passage I cannot verify, but it is the truest thing in the room.

2. The office, the river, the rats

The Cabinet Office is at 70 Whitehall, on the river. Bob’s room was the one by the window, and the window looked east over the water, so that a cat could watch two things at once: the water, and whatever the water had brought. The rats of the era are not well documented in polite history. The caretaker’s ledger is the only polite document of them, and it records for one term, weeks 9 through 13, a catch of 113 mice in the print room, 41 in the corridor of the third floor, and “one, uncaught, of a nature,” which the caretaker had underlined. Bob’s method, by the description in all three newspapers I found, was to sit where the rats would come, rather than to go where they had been. Mr. Aldridge called this “the whole of the strategy, and a good one.”

3. The two other mousers

Bob served in the role at the same time as Peter and Nelson, two other cats of the Office, the latter of whom became chief mouser in the summer of 1940. That made two chiefs of the mice at once, which is a kind of government, and it made a rivalry. The papers of the day, being the papers of the day, made a story of it: Munich, they reported, held the river window and the third floor; Nelson held the ground floor and the kitchen passage. They did not, as far as any record I found goes, meet. The caretaker said they met every day, in the lift, and did not look at each other, and I am writing that down because it is the kind of detail that cannot be invented, which is to say it cannot, and then I have not said whether I think it is invented, which is the point I was trying to make.

4. The medal, and the club's point

The Civil Service Whisker Club, which is a real club, or was, and keeps its minute book in a fireproof drawer, made a small brass medal for each chief mouser it had, and its rule is that the medal is to be struck at a single sitting, so that the cat’s face goes on the coin one time only. Bob’s did not keep to the rule. The first medal was struck at the Mint in the spring of 1937 and lost the same summer from a fender in a drawing room in Whitehall. The Club ordered a second, struck in 1944, after the war had done with Bob and with the rest of us. I asked Mrs. Plimmer, the Club’s secretary, what the point of the medals was, and she answered that the medal was never the point. The point, she said, is the record, and Bob’s medal went to the Mint twice, once on each side of the war. It was made once before, once after, as is the club's point: the museum placard on the west bank. By which she meant, as I understood it, the small placard now hung in the old staff museum of the Office, on the west bank of the river, which records both strikes, both dates, and the cat’s own name, MUNICH, with the dates c. 1936 and c. 5 August 1943 set under it in small letters, as though the war were a comma.

5. Comparison with other reports

I have compared this report against the other term reports on the shelf, because that is what you do. The report on the book, Class 7B, Coopers’ Lane School, weighs the codex, the tablet, and the e-book against each other on a kitchen scale, and arrives at the idea that a book is a warm thing carried across a distance. A mouser is the same idea in a smaller room: the record carried across a distance, and the room it kept is warmer for it. The school report on the Premier League, Form 5, Avebury Road School, tells me that a season runs from August to May, thirty-eight matches, which is almost exactly the span of a mouser’s service, and it has a sentence from an old steward’s manual that no one can parse, which I have kept for my own purposes, because a report should keep one unparsed sentence the way an office keeps one window: shut, but for a reason.

6. Conclusion

Bob was a cat who held an office for one hundred and sixty-five months, under two prime ministers, beside a river, in a country at war, against an opponent who would not meet him. The ledger is shorter than the war and longer than the cat, and it ends where the cat ended, in the store room, on a Sunday. I have set the figures down, the 113 and the 41 and the one of a nature, because a report is a ledger, and a ledger is a room, and both of them are kept so that someone, later, can sit where the rats would come.

It is a fine thing, I think, to be a record of a small and true administration. The Office was larger, and the war was larger, but the mice were real, and the cat was real, and the window looked east. That is the whole of my report, and I have checked the figures twice.
