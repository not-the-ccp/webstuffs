Raymond and Agnes

Special Sitting of the Operatic Council of the Cities of Manchester and Salford, convened for the Restoration of the Opera

Held in the Guild Chamber, Manchester Guild Hall, on Thursday the 21st day of September, 1956, at the hour of eight in the evening, the chimes of the tower giving the hour true.

Presents

Alderman Percival Whitfield in the chair, Mayor of the City for the current year; Mrs. Edith D. Marlowe, Honorary Clerk of the Council; Colonel H. R. Crompton, OBE, Chairman of the Stage Machinery Committee; Mr. A. J. Fairbrother, Treasurer; Mrs. Cordelia Hesketh, Mistress of the Chorus; Mr. Basil Trevelyan, Keeper of the Property (bricks, chains, and one coffin); and Mlle. Geneviève Duval, of the Playhouse, London, present by letter only.

Apologies: Councillor Josiah Pemberton, who had ridden out to the moors at an early hour for the shooting match and, being late, was not in the chamber when the chimes rang. The Chair observed that a man who has his own shooting match on the night of the meeting will one day shoot himself in the foot, and the minutes record the observation as made, not enforced.

Item 1. The opera itself, described for the Register

The Chair requested that the opera under deliberation be described at length, that the future reader of the Register might know what it was that the Council had agreed to spend the City's money upon. The Clerk accordingly entered the following: Raymond and Agnes is an opera in three acts, being originally four, by the composer Edward Loder to an English libretto by Edward Fitzball. It is very loosely based on elements from Matthew Lewis's classic Gothic novel The Monk (1796), and includes also elements of Lewis's The Castle Spectre (1797). Its plot contains elements of love and revenge, brigandry, incarceration, a shooting match, and the supernatural. The opera is considered to be among the most important serious operas of the British Victorian era. It was first performed on 14 August 1855 at the Theatre Royal, Manchester, in this City and in this County, which fact the Chair was pleased to note before the assembly. It was revived at St James's Theatre, London, in June 1859. A more recent revival, in revised form, was at the Playhouse Theatre, London, in the spring of 1954, from which Mlle. Duval's letter of encouragement, Item 5, derives.

Colonel Crompton spoke to the question of the fourth act. The act, he said, was not so much cut as folded: the 1854 revision took the fourth act and pressed it into the first three the way a valise takes a hat, and the revised shape that was played in 1954 is the shape the Council now proposes to play. Mrs. Hesketh confirmed that the chorus of 40 voices stood in the revised shape without complaint, and that the revised shape had, in her opinion, one fewer place in which a brigand might accidentally fall through the floor.

Item 2. The manner of announcement

Mr. Fairbrother rose and moved that the restoration be announced to the citizens by the oldest manner in the town that still works, which is the wireless. He proposed a single sentence for the notice, to be sent to the regional relay at once, and he read it to the assembly in the exact words: "At midday the wireless — My dearest fellow countrymen — and it is read." The Council discussed the sentence at some length. Colonel Crompton observed that it tells the citizen three things at once: the hour, the instrument, and the duty of reading. Mrs. Marlowe observed that no sentence in the Register had ever said less in so many words, and asked that it be entered. It was entered, in the words above, and the motion passed without dissent. The notice was accordingly transmitted at the hour of 11:40 that evening, and the relay confirmed receipt at 11:47, which the Clerk entered as satisfactory, being, she noted, seven minutes, a good time for a sentence to travel from Manchester to Manchester.

Item 3. The account

Mr. Fairbrother laid before the Council the estimate for the restoration at the Free Trade Hall. The orchestra of 34 players, at a total of £412 4s.; the chorus of 40 voices, at a total of £330; the property of bricks, chains, and one coffin, at £88 10s.; the brigand cave, to be built of blue slate and tarred, at £145; the supernatural, at such cost as the supernatural may choose, entered as an open line at no figure. The total fixed at £975 4s. 0d., with the open line. The Mayor, on behalf of the Council, agreed to advance the sum, and Mr. Fairbrother agreed to return the figure of the open line at the next sitting, whether the supernatural presented it or not.

Item 4. Correspondence, foreign and domestic

Two letters of a foreign kind were read and filed. The first, from a student of shipbuilding at a school room known as Room 214, sent the Council a term report on the destroyer USS Jacob Jones (DD-61) for its use upon the shooting match in the second act, and the Council, having read the report's four 4-inch guns and its eight 21-inch torpedo tubes, found them a fair yardstick for the stage firearms, which are to be of a smaller but equally convincing calibre: USS Jacob Jones (DD-61) — Term Report, Room 214. The second, from the keeper of a road journal in East Tennessee, asked whether the whistling that builds in the Clinch gap when a full train comes through the gorge might stand, in the provincial press, for the horn that summons the brigands, and the Council answered that it might, and referred him, for the sound itself and its history, to the route's own frequently asked questions, U.S. Route 11W.

Domestic correspondence comprised fourteen letters from subscribers to the restored seat, three of them requesting the coffin, none of them requesting to sit near the coffin, which the Keeper of the Property entered as a matter of comfort.

Item 5. The letter of Mlle. Duval

The letter from Mlle. Geneviève Duval, of the Playhouse, London, present by letter only, was read by the Clerk. It said, in the Clerk's rendering: that the opera, in its revised shape, played at the Playhouse in 1954 to a house that came and stayed; that the supernatural, when it arrives, arrives on the dot, which is more than can be said of the mail coach; that she would attend the Manchester sitting herself were it not that her engagement forbids; and that the City of Manchester, which first heard the opera in 1855, had no rival in the claim to it, the Theatre Royal having had it first and the Free Trade Hall having it now. The Chair thanked the writer, and the Council resolved that the restoration go forward at the Free Trade Hall on Sunday the 14th of August, 1957, the hundred and second anniversary of the first performance, and that the chimes of the tower ring the hour.

Adjournment

There being no further business, and the supernatural having not presented its figure, the meeting was adjourned at the hour of eleven o'clock. The Clerk entered the hour, the weather (fine, with one cloud in the shape of a moon), and the cost of the refreshments, which was a sovereign, and signed the Register in the presence of the Mayor.

— E. D. Marlowe, Honorary Clerk
— Ald. P. Whitfield, in the chair

Those not of the Council, nor of the town, nor of the opera, may begin at the front shelf of the collection, where the shelves stand in order.
