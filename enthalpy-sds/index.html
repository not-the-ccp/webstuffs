H Enthalpy

Safety Data Sheet for the quantity H, the sum of a thermodynamic system’s internal energy and the product of its pressure and volume. A state function. Handle by its initial and final states only; do not follow the path.

Issued by the Valence Assay Office, Oxidant Bank, Inner Sphere, Valence City · Emergency desk: ring the assay bell twice before the evening transfer

1 Identification

Product nameEnthalpy (H)
SynonymsTotal heat; the heat-content of the system; what the foundry ledger called “the count that doesn’t care how you got there”
SymbolH — from the Greek enthalpein, to heat; the assay office notes the resemblance to a vegetable label is accidental (see Section 2)
DefinitionH = U + PV, where U is internal energy and PV the pressure–volume term
ClassificationState function. Path-independent. No fixed composition; no fixed weight; carries no number of its own
Standard state298.15 K, 100 kPa (one atmosphere, conveniently provided by Earth’s ambient air over Valence)
SDS no.H-1, Rev. 4, superseding Rev. 3 of the foundry period (1897–)

The substance is encountered daily in chemical, biological, and physical systems measured at constant external pressure, which is precisely the condition Valence supplies: the Oxidant bank, the Reductant bank, and everything between them, under one quiet atmosphere. It is the reason the bridgekeepers’ heat log can be a ledger at all — each evening transfer across the Chelation Bridge changes the enthalpy of the quarter by a number that depends only on which metal centre began the crossing and which ended it, not on the route the electron walked.

Hazard statement H241: in exothermic contact (negative ΔH) the system gives up heat to its surroundings; surfaces nearby will feel it first. H242: in endothermic contact (positive ΔH) the system takes heat from its surroundings and the bench, the teacup, and the operator’s hands will feel the absence of it.

Precaution P403: store only as the difference ΔH between a defined initial state and a defined final state. Absolute H cannot be read from the desk; report changes, never totals, unless a zero has been chosen first (Section 8).

Misidentification memo · Sanitary Office, 14 March 1903 · kept word for word

In the first spring of the assay office, a jar on the lower shelf — labelled only H, in a hand that was afterwards never matched — was surveyed by the visiting hygienists alongside the other unlabelled stores. The jar held, on the balance of the evidence, a preserved turnip. The memo on its classification survives in the office file, uncorrected and unexplained, and is reproduced here as it was read into the minutes:

“It has generally been classed by hygienists among flatulent vegetables, and is in some other countries in search of her hedgehog.”

Sanitary Office minutes, 14 March 1903. The jar was later found to contain nothing at all, which the office recorded as its zero state. The hygienists’ line was not struck out, and the office has decided it will never be.

Misidentification hazards: H is not a substance and will not behave like one; it has been mistaken, at the counter, for heat, for temperature, and for a kind of vegetable (above). It is none of these. It is the bookkeeping that makes constant-pressure heat measurements honest.

3 Composition / information on the pressure–volume term

The PV term of the definition is the work W done against constant external pressure Pext to establish the system’s physical dimensions from Vsystem, initial = 0 to some final volume Vsystem, final, as W = PextΔV. Read plainly: the term is the price of making room for itself by displacing its surroundings. A gas that forms during a reaction must push the atmosphere aside, and the atmosphere charges for it.

For solids and liquids at common conditions the term is very small — a mole of liquid water at one atmosphere occupies so little volume that its PV contribution is on the order of a centimetre of heat, negligible against the internal energy — and is fairly substantial only where gases are born, consumed, or expanded. The assay office’s rule of thumb, painted above the foundry scale since before the foundry closed: count the gas; ignore the soup.

Pressure–volume work bench (one atmosphere)

Displacement ΔV = 22.4 L  ·  work W = PextΔV = 2.24 kJ at 100 kPa

Twenty-two point four litres is, at the standard state, roughly one mole of gas. That is the whole of the gas’s rent: a small, fixed, and perfectly boring sum, charged the moment it exists.

5 Accidental release

If a reaction proceeds at constant external pressure — as every reaction in an open beaker does, under the weight of Valence’s quiet atmosphere — the heat exchanged with the surroundings is ΔH, no correction needed. This is the quantity’s principal virtue and the reason it appears in so many measurements in chemical, biological, and physical systems: at the one pressure the whole city already lives at, the ledger balances on its own.

If a gas is released instead, contain the displacement. The released quantity pushes the air aside as it arrives, and every litre it takes out costs exactly 100 joules at the standard pressure — the bench has watched a kettle of steam at full boil run up a rent of several kilojoules before anyone remembered it had to be paid.

6 Handling and storage

Handle only between states. Do not track the quantity through an intermediate condition and expect the account to be better for it: because it is a state function, the value of ΔH for a journey is the same whether the journey is direct or is broken into as many steps as the operator pleases, including steps that never actually happened. The assay office permits, and the foundry depended on, bookkeeping through imaginary intermediate states — Hess’s method — provided the first and last states are real and the arithmetic is checked twice.

Store this sheet with the bridgekeepers’ ink log: the same shelf, the same rule. The log keeps date, time, centres, outcome; this sheet keeps what the heat did while the electron was walking. The two records reconcile to the joule every season, which is more than can be said of most ledgers in the city.

7 First aid

  • Exothermic burn (ΔH < 0, heat released): remove the system from the warm bench, let the surroundings do the cooling they have already agreed to do, and do not add fuel out of sympathy. The neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base releases 57.3 kJ per mole of water formed — the most reliable small fire in the manual.
  • Endothermic frost (ΔH > 0, heat absorbed): cover, insulate, and wait. The system is borrowing heat, not destroying it; the borrowed amount comes back when the reaction is reversed, which the assay office has verified in both directions at the assay bell.
  • Confusion with temperature: separate the operator from the thermometer, read Section 1, and remind them that a system can be endothermic and still feel cold, or exothermic and not yet warm, and that neither feeling is a measurement.

8 Stability and the zero

Stable, indefinitely, in any number of vessels. The quantity does not degrade, does not react with itself, and cannot be spilled, because it is not in the vessel: it is of the state of the vessel. Its one instability is bookkeeping, and it is cured by the standing convention that the standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero — carbon as graphite, oxygen as O2 at 100 kPa, bromine as the liquid. Every number in Section 4 is measured against that silence. Choose a different zero and every number moves; the differences do not, and the differences are the only part of the subject that is allowed to speak.

9 Records, neighbours, and cross-references

The sheet is filed with the documents that share its shelves. The Inner Sphere visitor’s guide describes the quarter where the evening transfer is measured: the Copper Citadel’s green window, the Iron Keep’s amber, the ligand lamp, and the six-copper admission; its almanac keeps the line about the last grizzly of the city, foraged out of the foundry’s slag heaps in 1897 — the foundry whose scale still carries the painted rule from Section 3. The grizzlies themselves kept their number in a different ledger: the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies season card, where a crowd counted in dozens went 14–2 and the branch gymnasium’s coal ledger was, decades later, reconciled against this sheet without dispute — exothermic all the way down, and every joule of it accounted for.
