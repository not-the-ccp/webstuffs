Department of British and Medieval Antiquities · Keeper's Manual No. 14 · British Museum, London · Revised Impression

Rupert Bruce-Mitford

Field procedure, find recording and the Sutton Hoo ship-burial — a keeper's game of excavation, conservation and publication in twenty-one turns.

Rupert Leo Scott Bruce-Mitford (14 June 1914 – 10 March 1994) was a British archaeologist and scholar who spent the majority of his career at the British Museum, primarily as the keeper of the Department of British and Medieval Antiquities, and was particularly known for his work on the Sutton Hoo ship-burial. His preceding two generations had lived largely abroad: his maternal grandparents as early settlers of Canterbury, New Zealand, and his parents only returning to London for his birth.

This manual governs the keeper's game named for him. The board is the site; the pieces are the finds; the score is the monograph. One player takes the Keeper's chair — rulings, context, publication — and up to four take trowels. The game opens on the sand at Sutton Hoo, Woodbridge, Suffolk, where Mound One covered a twenty-seven-metre ship impression, its rows of rivets still legible after thirteen hundred years, and it closes only when the third volume is bound.

1The Keeper

Bruce-Mitford joined the British Museum in 1938, served through the Second World War, and was appointed keeper of the Department of British and Medieval Antiquities, holding the post until 1977. He directed the second Sutton Hoo excavation, 1965–1971, oversaw the conservation of the ship's finds — the gold buckle, the garnet studs, the purse-lid, the helmet — and oversaw the production of the monumental three-volume work The Sutton Hoo Ship-Burial, termed by the president of the Society of Antiquaries as “one of the great books of the century”.

2Components

  • Field trowel: leaf pattern, 11 cm blade.
  • Sieve: 5 mm mesh, 30 cm frame.
  • Find bags: twenty-four, pre-numbered.
  • Index cards: A6, two hundred, ruled for context.
  • Pencil: 2H, with eraser.
  • Acetate overlays: twelve, for plans.
  • Keeper's log: bound and ruled; Build Log No. 9 is reserved for marsh conditions.
  • Dice: one d6 for soil and weather, one d20 for find condition.

3Setup

Unroll the five-metre grid over the trench and peg the corners. Lay the ship impression at the centre — keel line north–south, rivets at ten-centimetre centres — and cover it with damp hessian between turns. Shuffle the find deck and place it beside the log. Appoint one Keeper; the Keeper reads the rulings, holds the context cards, and decides when a volume is ready for press.

4Turn Order — Twenty-One Turns

Each turn runs through five phases, in this order:

  1. Survey: peg the grid; record the quadrant on an index card.
  2. Probe: trowel and sieve; roll the d6 for soil condition. A roll of 1 is rain — halt play and cover the trench.
  3. Lift: bag each find in its own numbered bag; never lift the ship impression before it is drawn.
  4. Record: enter find number, context, quadrant and depth on the card; file it in the log.
  5. Conserve: the Keeper rules on treatment — iron rivets in situ, textile pseudomorphs lifted whole, nothing cleaned before it is recorded.

Weather roll: Fair ground, dry sieves.

5The Sutton Hoo Ship-Burial (Special Scenario)

Scenario: Mound One, Sutton Hoo, 1939 and 1965–1971. The ship is twenty-seven metres long; its ghost is a stain in the sand. The special rule of this scenario: the impression must be recorded in full before any object is bagged. The find deck contains the gold buckle, the garnet cloak-studs, the purse-lid, the helmet, the silver bowls, the whetstone — each scored by condition on the d20, and each entered on its own card with a quadrant reference. The game ends for this scenario only when the Keeper signs the final context card.

6Find Recording — Standing Order 9

Every find receives a consecutive number. Its context — quadrant, depth, soil, association — is written on the index card while the find is still in the tray, because context outlives the object. The paper that records it is kept in the log, and the log is kept in order.

“The marshes do not intend to be kept in Build Log No. 9 and the sewn-up lamb stew left the berth, which is to keep the paper that records it.”

When this order is drawn, the referee halts play, seals the current find bag, and reads the entry aloud before the next turn begins. It is not to be explained.

7The Three Volumes — Win Condition

Volume I: Excavation and the Ship, 1975. Volume II: the finds, their dating and their conservation, 1978. Volume III: context, catalogue and legacy, 1983. The Keeper wins by completing all three volumes before turn twenty-one ends; if the final turn passes with a volume unwritten, the finds remain in their trays and the score is zero.

8Notes

Round eleven of the game has moved the board to Devonshire, where the grid is pegged on the clifftop path between Torquay and Paignton, the chair passes at turn seven, and the find-deck ruling is read into the log word for word; the members' account of the whole round, post by post, is Thread No. 218 of The Turn Table — the Devonshire Scenario.

The Saltfleet rigging loft keeps its lash patterns in a drawer and its standing orders in a ledger; the Department keeps its finds in trays and its contexts on cards. Both are record games — the hand-tied whip from the rigging loft above Saltfleet beach is catalogued The Saltfleet Whip — Hand-Tied Coastal Lash, turn by turn, tie by tie.

The same arithmetic governs a small village and a small site archive: two ancient parishes, one parish hall, one fire to keep, and a count of 749 souls. Ashley, Cambridgeshire is catalogued Lot 217 — Ashley, Cambridgeshire, Fenn & Marlowe's 110th Autumn Sale.

The same card-by-card discipline is kept, on a smaller shelf, by the Heorot Society in that same village, who read Beowulf at one folio a month and print the room’s words exactly as spoken in their newsletter, The Gif — Newsletter No. 9 of the Heorot Society.
