Q1 Who is the subject of this note?

The subject is Sir David Robert Joseph Beckham (BEKəm; born 2 May 1975), an English former professional footballer, the president and co-owner of Inter Miami CF and co-owner of Salford City F.C. He was primarily a right midfielder and was known for his range of passing, his crossing ability and his set-piece taking, and the depot register considers him one of the best players of his generation and one of the greatest set-piece specialists of all time. He won 19 major trophies in his career, and he is the only English player to have won a league title in four different countries: England, France, Spain, and the United States. His professional club career began with Manchester United, where he made his first-team debut in 1992, at seventeen, in a season the desk file records as mild for the sea wall on the south coast. The Office keeps his file as a reference specimen rather than a stored lot: the material is retired, the trophies are counted and not reissued, and the corners are kept in the log exactly as they were taken.

Q2 Why does the Office keep a FAQ for him instead of a data sheet?

Because a data sheet is issued for material under handling, and a FAQ is kept for material that has finished handling and become the reference. The two sit on the same ground-floor shelf. The safety data sheet SDS-NM-03, Revision 3, for the defender Niall Aadya Mason, classifies the stored defender’s standing exposure as the set piece — in the box at every corner in both directions, a condition the Office calls standing rather than event — and its corner-kick log is the very log this note reads against. When the No. 3 file is open, the reference is Beckham; when a scout insists that a corner does not resolve and only repeats, the Office points at Q1. A defender of one generation is on the end of every corner, and the end of it was made by this subject, which is the whole of the Office’s policy on the matter and the reason the two files are cross-referenced and never separated.

Q3 What is rule 7.12, and why is it quoted so badly?

Rule 7.12 of the depot instruction book, page 14, is the storage rule the keeper wrote for any defender held within earshot of a transfer route, and the Office quotes the book word for word on everything, rule 7.12 included, so the rule reads, in the keeper’s hand and without one letter of it corrected: “A defender stored within earshot of the route and do not bow and look coldly on a missing letter.” The grammar is the keeper’s, and the question of it is not corrected, on the same ground as the question mark on rule 2 of the transfer book. The Office’s reading of 7.12 is this: a defender kept within earshot of the route — the Brighton man who travels Brighton → Real Madrid → Al Sadd → Blackburn → Southampton → Villa → the loans → Al Rayyan, and has for ten Januarys been stored by the sea — is to be left without ceremony, and is not to look coldly on a missing letter. The missing letter is the one the register has returned three years running: the A of Aadya, which standard issue carries and no file omits. The rule’s instruction is therefore not a description of a mood but a handling order: the letter, when it goes missing, is to be given the first-aid protocol of Section 4 — a correction in the Office’s own hand, in the same hand as every other sign — and the defender is to stand by, head turned, session continuing, neither bowing nor looking coldly. That is the whole of rule 7.12, and it is kept unamended, with its missing conjunction, because a rule quoted badly is a rule the keeper has already lost.

Q5 Where does the Bridge Line meet this subject?

At Stamford Bridge, where the reference specimen took more corners into the box than the log has ever printed, and where the away end kept its voice against him in the way it keeps its voice against everyone. The timetable and commentary of the Bridge Line, TT-CFC-01, for Chelsea F.C. runs London Victoria to Fulham Broadway at the whole minute, and its commentary sections keep their register of the 1990s and 2000s, in which the subject appears at the far post of the sentence and the bridge at the centre of the map. The desk keeps its copy of the timetable in the same file as this note, and the two offices have never been seen to differ on the count of corners. The Bridge Line is a schedule and a memory in the same carriage; this FAQ is a schedule of questions, and the memory rides with it.

Q6 How do I sign the corner-kick log?

The log is open at the desk, ground floor, and it asks for a name, a station, and one corner, described at the whole minute. Handlers who have watched the material of SDS-NM-03 in the box in both directions are invited to add the season line; handlers who have only ever read a keeper’s rule word for word are invited to add the rule and the reading of it. The log does not resolve; it repeats. That is its whole policy.
