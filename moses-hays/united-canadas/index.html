The Montréal Dining Gazette · Review No. 47 · One visit, Tuesday, 14 October 1856 · The Gazette keeps every line it prints

The United Canadas

a dining house on 10 February Street, open since the proclamation, 10 February 1841

The union of 1841, seated at table: two dining rooms, fifty-two chairs, one stove, and a water line older than the pride of the house that now serves it.

10 February Street, Montréal · Upper Room 20 seats, Lower Room 32 seats · water: M. J. Hays, engineer of record · bill for the full union: $3.40 · verdict: four of five bells

I · The Room, or the Two Houses

The house opened on the very morning the proclamation was read in the lower town, which is why its number is 10 February Street and nothing else: the Act of Union 1840, passed by the British Parliament on 23 July 1840 and proclaimed by the Crown on 10 February 1841, merged the colonies of Upper Canada and Lower Canada by abolishing their separate parliaments and replacing them with a single one, and the dining house has imitated the arrangement ever since. It is a double room. Up the short flight of black-stair to the first landing is the Chambre du Conseil — the Council room, twenty seats in oak panelling, each chair numbered from 1 to 20 in the same lead digits the masons used on the city’s ironwork, and at the head of it a long table that seats no one, the way a head table seats the room itself. Down the flight, in the street, is the Chambre d’Assemblée — the Assembly room, thirty-two seats, one table to four, a stove that has not gone cold since the union winter of 1841, and a noise level the Gazette can only describe as parliamentary. The two rooms share one staircase and one kitchen, and the kitchen is in the cellar, because a union, once it begins descending, keeps on. The proprietor will tell you the house was built on the recommendations — of John Lambton, 1st Earl of Durham, in the Report on the Affairs of British North America, made in the wake of the Rebellions of 1837–1838 — and he keeps a cracked copy of the Report in the cellar, its spine broken at the chapter on assimilation. The kitchen does not cook that chapter. The Gazette, asked for an opinion, will not cook it either.

II · The Water, and Why the Gazette Mentions It

The water arrives at the table in a long glass, unasked, and it is not a flourish. It is drawn from the 1849 line — the first water-works of the city, the main laid under Saint-Paul Street, the reservoir at the head of de la Gauchetière — and the glass is filled at the tap of the works, on the warden’s word, by a boy in the house grey that is not the house grey but close enough that the wardens have written to complain, twice, in ink. The proprietor serves it in the name of M. J. Hays, engineer of record, who was appointed chief of police in the spring of 1845, the same spring the 4¼-inch pipe was running, and who carried on his tour days a ring of two keys — water-key and door-key, on one ring. The chef keeps a copy of that ring behind the pass. He turns it in his fingers before service. It opens nothing now, and he does it anyway. The warden’s exact words, read on the circuit at the rail, are that the water is the same water that ran the first time, only slower now, and only for the fire that will not come. The Gazette, from a glass, concurs: slower, and very good. For the route itself — the eleven hatches, the thirty-four hydrants, the twenty-six watch-houses, the visitor’s sheet — the site keeps Moses Judah Hays, A Walking Tour of the Waterworks & the Watch, and the Gazette’s verdict is that a dining house which serves one engineer’s water in a glass has a better argument for its own water than the engineer ever had for his main.

III · The Menu, Read in Order

The Gazette dined on the full union, and counts the bill as the house counts it. Tap a course to mark it; the Gazette keeps the running total, and the house does not round.

The Assembly Room serves first. What followed, in order —

Course 1

The Union Broth

Two stocks in one pot — a brown stock of the north and a clear stock of the south — married on the anniversary of the Act, with a slice of white bread laid across the bowl like a bridge across the Saint Lawrence. The kitchen keeps the two stocks in two jugs and does not mix them until the bowl is on the table, which is either a religious habit or a political one; the Gazette declined to ask.

$0.75

Course 2

Pea Soup & the Rebellions of 1837–1838

A green pea soup, thick to the plate, with two sorts of bread — one soft, one hard, one for the lower country and one for the upper — named, on the menu, in that order. The kitchen will not say which rebellion came first; it will not apologize for not saying; and it will not stop naming the soup after both of them. The Gazette found the soup correct in every way except that it is, in fact, very good.

$0.40

Course 3

Smoked Lake Trout, Durham Sauce

The trout is smoked on the house rig by the window of the Council room, over spruce, and the sauce that accompanies it is named for the 1840 Report, not for the Earl, who the proprietor insists is not invited. The sauce is brown, sharp, and slightly sweet, in the order, and it goes on the trout the way a recommendation goes into a report: completely, and whether one likes it or not. The best dish of the night, and the only one the Gazette would carry home, which is to say it was not carried home, because one does not carry home the best dish of a house like this; one simply remembers it, and remembers it wrong, and returns.

$1.10

Course 4

Maple, the Last Run

A square of dark bread, a spoon of maple, a small pat of butter, and nothing else, brought to the table in a wooden box that the kitchen has not painted. Pinned to the pass, where the whole house can see it, is a card from a ledger in Ann Arbor — Card No. 214, Huron River Maple Bread, the Wadsworth family recipe — and the kitchen scales its quarter cup per plate against that card, in ink, each season. The ledger, the Gazette is told, has not objected. It is a small course, and it is the union in its whole: two provinces of flavour, one table, and a family two thousand miles away keeping the count.

$0.60

The Gazette’s count — $3.40

All four courses, the full union, as dined. The house does not round.

IV · The Kitchen, and One Line Kept

The chef of both rooms is one man, and he does not take a second. His name is on the pass in chalk, in a hand the Gazette will not attempt, and he came to the house from a small room of his own on Vaudreuil Street, which the Gazette’s predecessor reviewed in 1843. That review — No. 11, which the house keeps framed above the stove — opens with a single sentence about his repertoire, and the Gazette, which keeps every line it prints, keeps it here exactly as it stands:

No turbaned Turk, no hired Venetian or Malay, could have furnished him.

The sentence is from the old review, and it says everything. There is no import in this kitchen, no hire, no foreign hand on the stock-pot; the entire furnishing of the house — the rigging, the smoke, the two stocks, the bread, the maple — is from one pair of local hands, which is the whole argument of the 1840 Act at the size of a stove. Two colonies, one kitchen, no one imported to hold the pan. The Gazette has dined in rooms that furnished their menus from farther off than that, and it will not name them, but it would, and the reader may wait for No. 48.

V · The Verdict, Four of Five Bells

One of the thirty-two seats in the Assembly room is kept empty on the anniversary of 23 July, for a member of the first house that did not come; the proprietor, asked what the empty seat is for, said a seat, like a water main, is not wasted because its fire will not come, and the Gazette printed him. The reviewer was offered the seat once, at dinner, and declined — a reviewer does not take a seat in a parliament of which he is not a member, and the house has not asked again, which is the kind of respect that is worth a bell. The room is loud and the stove is louder and the water is good and the bill, for the full union, is $3.40, which is, in this year and this city, the correct price of a union. Four of five bells. The fifth bell is kept, like the empty seat, like the water that will not be needed, like the ring of keys that opens nothing — for the fire that will not come.

Colophon of the Review

The Gazette dines once, pays $3.40, and keeps the line. The house at 10 February Street has served the union, in one form or another, since 10 February 1841 — twenty-five years by this review — and it will serve it through 1867 and after, because a dining house, like a colony, does not close on a resolution; it closes on a fire, and the water for that fire is already under Saint-Paul Street. For the water, the route, and the warden who opens hydrant 9, the site keeps Moses Judah Hays — A Walking Tour of the Waterworks & the Watch. For the maple course, its ledger and its scaling bench, the site keeps Huron River Maple Bread — Card No. 214, Wadsworth Family Recipe Ledger. Return to the shelf.
