Chapter VI — The Truceless War

Being a field account of the mutiny of the foreign levy (241 BC) and of the rising of the African settlements against Carthaginian control, carried on to suppression, late 238 or early 237 BC. Entered in the camp book at the baggage ground south of the city, and never re-entered.

1. Summary of the Trouble

The war to which this chapter is devoted began at a pay table and ended at a pyre. When the First Punic War closed in 241 BC — the war of twenty-three years against Rome, fought principally in Sicily and at sea — the army that had been raised for it could not be paid, because the treasury had been spent the way a treasury spends when the war it was budgeted for turns out to be longer than the peace it was budgeted under. There were about 20,000 foreign soldiers in the camp near Carthage — Spaniards, Gauls, Balearics, Ligurians, and men of the Greek cities of Sicily — entitled to the wages their contracts named, and holding three years of the arrears against the city. The 70,000 African natives who had served alongside them, the Libyans who had given their sons to the standards and received no wages at all but the promise that service would end in a lightening of the tax on their land, decided the same morning that the soldiers’ quarrel was theirs.

The mutiny lasted, from first unrest to last fire, roughly thirty months: from 241 BC to late 238 or early 237 BC. The city suppressed both the mutiny and the revolt, and at no discount the city has ever allowed to be printed. This chapter records the conduct, the arithmetic, and the price.

2. How the Pay Table Broke

The soldiers first made their demand in the ordinary way: a petition, a deputation to the board, a request to be paid at the contracted rate or discharged with a bonus and passage home. A compromise seemed to have been reached — discharge, with wages up to a fixed date and a bonus — and the deputation went back to the camp. It did not come back the same way.

Two men broke the camp open. The first was Spendius, a Roman scribe taken in war, a gladiator by trade before his captivity, who had sat at the table as a clerk and watched the compromise being written down in the city’s hand. He came back to the camp and told the men the written deal was a trick of the scribes, that the city intended to pay nothing, and that a man’s wage is worth less than a man’s knife. The second was Matho, of the African levy, the most capable soldier the camp produced, who took the command of the mutiny with the plainness of a man taking up a drum. To them was joined Doxyllus, a Sicanian of the Sicilian garrisons, who brought the Greek cities’ grievances and a taste for a longer war. These three — the clerk, the soldier, and the Sicilian — are the whole staff of the Truceless War, and the chapter is named Truceless because, once begun, no armistice between the camp and the city was honored for a day.

The mutineers’ first act was the massacre at the baggage ground, where the city’s pay officers were put to the sword between the mules and the coin chests. The second was the taking of Utica, the colony town on the bay to the west, whose harbor the army needed for its iron and its ships.

Two debts the city owed to this war, and paid. The first was Masinissa of the Numidians, who came to the siege of Utica with his father’s horse and stayed to ride in every campaign after, paying the city in cavalry the way a man pays a debt he intends to keep. The second was the discipline itself: no auxiliary body was ever again left three months unpaid in a camp, and the pay table moved into the city where the board could see it, which is the whole of the reform, and it was worth thirty months.

Arrears are the only number in this chapter that a quartermaster may work himself, and the table below is set out so that any keeper of the coin chest may test the city’s liability at any month of the war. Enter the body of men unpaid, the months held over, and the contracted day-wage in drachmae (the city’s standard contract ran one drachma per day with rations in kind, which is 30 drachmae a month for the full month). 5. Of the Printing of the Pay Notices

The pay notices — the tallies of who was owed what, printed in red ochre ink on the papyrus the city issued at every camp — were the paper this war actually ran on. A soldier’s contract was the notice; his mutiny was the notice, refused. The town printer at the shop on the street of the scribes set the notices through the whole war, and the shop’s own account of it survives in a single receipt fragment pinned to the back of the camp book, entered in the shop’s own hand, word for word, as it stands:

I paid in advance For more classified printing of this paper for them, it put a shop's day against a body, with the higher mounds.

Fragment, street of the scribes; the “higher mounds” are entered in the margin as the two spoil mounds outside the western gate, where the unclaimed of the baggage ground stood until the rains. The shop’s day was a day; the body was the 20,000. The receipt is all the arithmetician of the war left.

The reform, when the war was over, was a simple one and it is entered here because it cost nothing: the notices are printed at the city’s expense, the shop is paid by the city, and the soldier holds the notice against the chest, not against the quartermaster. A body that has paid in advance for its own notice does not burn it, and a camp that does not burn its paper does not burn its gates.

6. Cross-References of the Shelf

The argument of this chapter — the pay against the body, the garden against the flock, the needle at Under the Wall — is kept at domestic scale in two neighbors on the shelf. The field book of Iroquois, Louisville, at South Bank Station holds the same watch at the price of the porches: a heron on duty against a runway, a row of grackles in line down the street, and a resident who has paid in advance, in sixty years of the same screen door, for the noise he will not sign against. And the player’s manual for MEANJIN, 1978 Edition, sets the same rule as a game on the Brisbane floodplain, where one flock card takes twenty to fifty points from a garden plot and the needle decides what a row is worth — the only wild card on the board, and not a metaphor, which is the word the mutineers used for the compromise.
