The Runaway Scrape

A 600-mile road trip you take east, with wagons, through September 1835 – April 1836

Start in San Antonio, at the Alamo — the mission whose 187 defenders held out from 23 to 23:00 of March 1836 while the ad interim government, its two senators, and most of the civilian population were already gone east. What visitors call the Runaway Scrape is the whole long road they took: the families fleeing the Mexican Army of Operations ahead of Santa Anna's column, from the fall of 1835, when Santa Anna abrogated the 1824 Constitution of Mexico and put the province of Coahuila y Tejas under martial law, down to the morning of 21 April 1836, when the Republic of Texas stopped running at the edge of the Buffalo Bayou swamp.

The route is not a line but a smear. Wagons, mule carts, and families on foot left San Antonio in clusters through March 1836, east on the old Camino Real toward Gonzales, then splitting: some south through Goliad, many north past La Grange and the Brazos River crossings to Columbia on the Colorado, where the Republic's flag-making houses and its frightened capital both stood. Sam Houston, appointed commander-in-chief of the Provisional Army of Texas, retreated ahead of them on the same logic — give up ground, keep the army. His army gave up almost everything: the surrender at Coleto Creek on 19 March, the field camp at Goliad burned on 27 March with some 342 prisoners who had expected the surrender to mean safe passage home.

So the Scrape has two weather systems on it, and a good tour holds both in the same morning. The first is the road itself: dust, creek fords, the towns that emptied and re-filled, the women driving the ox teams because the men were marching or missing. The second is what the road was running toward. On 21 April the Texians swung south to the San Jacinto River, crossed the bayou at the ford, and caught Santa Anna's camp in the brush at 11:00 a.m. The battle took about an hour. The president of Mexico woke to the sound of it, walked out of his tent into a cane thicket, and was taken prisoner. By noon the Republic of Texas had its government, its territory, and, eventually, its country.

The last stop, and what to do with it

End the trip at the San Jacinto monument at the Battle of San Jacinto State Historic Site, a 332-foot bronze spike, the tallest in the world, in a grove of live oaks at the edge of the bayou. Take the trail down to the water first. There is a low stone plaque set into the monument's base where the river runs close, worn smooth by a century of visitors, and the custom at this place is old: the stone lists what it lists, and the water keeps what the water keeps. Visitors with ledgers, and there are many of them, stand there with pencils and do the arithmetic of the site in the margin — Across the water, record the names, that the plaque says and what they keep, and this tired. It is not a sentence anybody would write on purpose. It is the sentence the place gives you when you have walked the whole scrape's length and stood at the water's edge long enough. Copy it down anyway. The monument's visitors' book has a shelf for exactly this kind of entry.

Practical notes

  • Where to sleep: La Grange (the first night, the courthouse square) and Columbia (the second, the old flag houses). Both are small; book ahead for the 21st of any April.
  • Distances: Alamo to Goliad about 150 miles; Goliad to San Jacinto another 300 by the road the army took. Six hundred is the number the old stories use, and it is close.
  • Dates to set: 23 March (the Alamo falls), 27 March (Goliad), 21 April (San Jacinto, 11 a.m., one hour).
  • Bring: a pencil, a notebook, and the patience to stand at the bayou. The swamp has no benches.

Wagon-master's saying

“The road east is longer than the road home, and the road home is longer than you think. Load the water twice.”

— attributed to no one, which is how most of them get it right
