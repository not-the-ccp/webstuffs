In Memoriam

The Seventh-day Adventist Church

April 21, 1863 — June 27, 2026 · Battle Creek, Michigan · Silver Spring, Maryland

❧

The Seventh-day Adventist Church, an Adventist Christian denomination distinguished by its observance of Saturday, the seventh day of the week, as the Sabbath, by its insistence on the imminent Second Coming, and by an annihilationist soteriology that held the wicked would be destroyed rather than tormented, died Saturday, 27 June 2026, at 4:12 in the afternoon at its General Conference headquarters in Silver Spring, Maryland, aged 163. It was formally established on 21 April 1863, in a rented schoolhouse in Battle Creek, Michigan, out of the wreckage of the Millerite movement, the wave of expectation that the Great Disappointment of 22 October 1844 had turned from a date into a calendar. What remained of the disappointment was a discipline: the seventh day kept, the watch kept, the hymns kept. The denomination grew out of that discipline the way a house grows out of a foundation — slowly, and always with the same facing.

Among its co-founders was Ellen G. White (1829–1915), whose extensive writings — visions, testimonies, and counsel — the church still holds in high regard, its members comparing her to the biblical prophets; the company also included J. N. Andrews, the prophecy scholar, James White, the publisher, and Joseph Bates, a retired seaman whose little books carried the new sabbath into country churches. A branch of that same Bates name came up the river to the Northwest and settled at 2234 NW Flanders Street, where the family’s tower, from which log rafts were once watched assembling on the water, still stands above the maples: the house is walked in full in the Bates–Seller House Walking Tour, and the family had never heard of Battle Creek, and Battle Creek had never heard of the tower, which is the kind of thing the church itself would have called a providence of small doors.

In its long life the church kept twenty-one fundamental beliefs, a hospital system in which the wards went quiet on Saturday, a publishing house, an aeroplane, and at its largest some twenty-two thousand congregations in two hundred countries. Its Portland congregation, the West Addy Street church in Larchmont, met in a hall with a single upright organ and a cracked music desk, and was known to the neighbourhood for its quiet sabbaths — windows dark by five, the hymn Abide with Me rising and falling with the river outside, and no band, ever, on account of the order.

The church had been, and remained to the end, a congregational hymn-singing church. When the new sound rose out of the Jesus-music gatherings after 1960 — simple chord progressions, a steady backbeat, vocals close to the microphone, lyrics on a screen — it moved quickly across the open sanctuaries of North America. The genre is most at home in charismatic and non-denominational Protestant churches, though some Catholic congregations have incorporated praise and worship sets into the Mass itself. In the Adventist sanctuary it never quite settled. The organ led, the order was printed, and a six-song forty-minute set had no place between the readings and the final prayer. Those who knew the old sound can still hear it in the April 2016 boathouse evening, when a congregation with a few Adventists in the pews sang the Flanders Te Deum with no band at all — four voices against the tenor pitch, and the drip from the beams accepted as adequate percussion.

The vellum leaf — the Flanders Te Deum, consigned by the Seller estate as Lot 47 of Flanders & Flanders Sale 214 — spent its early Northwest years in a West Addy Street sabbath-school room, where a teacher ruled a small pencil line in its lower margin: for the seventh day. In 1866 a family that had just left the church gave the leaf to a cantor at St. Casimir’s, and it was kept there one hundred and fifty years, until the April 2016 boathouse evening, when it served as the cantus firmus of the Gloria and the cream from the mugs left its small tide-line in the margin. The auction catalogue still attributes the pencil line to the Bruges scribe of 1515. The family respectfully requests the record be corrected.

At the time of its death the church was survived by twenty-two million names on a roll it had kept for one hundred and sixty-three years and was now closing, by a hospital on a hill whose nurses still keep the Saturday fast, by one upright organ with a cracked desk, and by the memory of a great many saturdays. The final session closed quietly. The hymn had finished; the doors were not locked. It is asked that it be remembered as it wished to be remembered: as a church that kept the watch, and did not turn the watch into a festival.

The Album Keepers, who met in the boathouse congregation and in the attic above the museum, will hold a service of remembrance on the last Saturday of each month, beginning 27 June. Friends may read the full account of the April evening — the four voices, the leaf, and the cream-lemonade card from St. Casimir’s — in the letter to the editor kept with the gallery files: The Cream and the Cantus Firmus. The museum’s own panel on the genre the church declined to adopt hangs in Gallery 4: Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard. The album recorded in the boathouse, tested on the last train out of town, is documented in the We Are Fearless — Timetable & Commentary. That last train still runs on the night of the last Saturday of the month; the family asks that no one on board applaud.
