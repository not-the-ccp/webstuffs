Detroit Tour Board · Route No. 12/3 · Thursday Night Edition · 61°F and falling

Miracle
in
Motown

A walking tour in seven stops through Ford Field, Detroit, Michigan, on the night of Thursday, December 3, 2015 — the Green Bay Packers at the Detroit Lions, nationally on Thursday Night Football, a 2015 NFL season regular game that Detroit led 23–21 for most of the last minute and a half, and Green Bay won 27–23 on the final snap of regulation.

The board prints every stop card as one line, and its typesetting rule is set on the gate as a warning to anyone who re-orders the fields: Every card is read top to bottom — Contact, category, heading and body, run together the way it was meant to. We walk the route the same way: the fields in order, all four of them, no re-ordering.

  1. Contact: Gate A, Jefferson Avenue, Detroit, MI 48226Category: Approach · 8:41 PM

    Stop 1 — The Last Walk In

    The route starts outside, where the lot on the Jefferson Avenue side still smells of diesel and the wind off the River comes across in short, cold hands. Six thousand Packers walk east across the street in a body, a green tide with a current running toward the turnstiles, and a smaller tide in silver the other way. Detroit is a football town and this is a Thursday, which means the whole city has already been up for ten hours arguing about the Lions’ eight-point cushion, the Matthew Stafford quarters, and whether Jason Hanson will need his knee at all tonight. The gate opens at 8:42. Nobody is in a hurry. In the next four hours, no one who walks through here will ever be in a hurry again.

  2. Contact: Lower concourse, tunnel mouth, west stairCategory: Corridor · 9:05 PM

    Stop 2 — The Broadcast Tunnel

    This is the tunnel where the television trucks park their ambitions. Thursday Night Football is a national product, and tonight the feed will leave Detroit with the Packers’ name at the top of the scoreboard and the Lions’ defense still looking for its rhythm. A camera dolly is wedged behind a stack of folding chairs with the confidence of furniture. A sound mixer listens to the crowd through his cans and says, quietly, to no one in particular, that the acoustics of this roof make every cheer arrive two steps before the play that caused it. We stop here only to note the geography: from this point, everything worth walking for is straight ahead, sixty-one yards to the right, and one last play long.

  3. Contact: Midfield, under the ribbon boardCategory: Field-level · 10:02 PM, second quarter

    Stop 3 — The Lions’ 13–3 Lead

    Stand at midfield and look up. The Lions have gone up 13–3 on a first-quarter touchdown to the home crowd, and the silver half of the building is doing the low, rolling cheer Ford Field was built to amplify. This is the version of the night most of the city believed by half time, when Detroit stood 23–10. The board has filed that score under corrections, and we have it pinned to the route map in the gate room. The lesson of Stop 3, which the board prints in the small type at the foot of every card, is that a lead in an American football game is a claim, not a fact, and that claims expire. The scoreboard above our heads is making a claim. We keep walking.

  4. Contact: Lions 34, north sideline, third quarterCategory: Field-level · 10:41 PM

    Stop 4 — Green Bay Finds Its Number

    Aaron Rodgers has spent the first half being told no — no in the pocket, no in the cold, no to the deep routes over the middle. At the Lions’ 34 in the third quarter he starts counting the defense the way a surveyor counts a boundary: every step, every shade, every man who leans half an inch too early. Green Bay scores the next three times it needs to, and the 23–10 claim from Stop 3 is amended to 23–21 with a little over two minutes of the game clock remaining and Detroit holding the ball. The green half of the building has gone very quiet. It is the kind of quiet the broadcast tunnel’s sound mixer will later describe, on air, as “the sound of a building doing arithmetic.”

  5. Contact: Detroit 6, 4th & goal, regulation clock 0:00Category: The snap · 11:00 PM and zero seconds left

    Stop 5 — No Time on the Clock

    The Lions ran the last forty seconds out; this is documented, this is not in dispute. What the board is arguing about, and what this stop is for, is what happened when the clock reached zero and the ball was still at the Detroit six. Green Bay took the snap. Aaron Rodgers, number 12, dropped back in a pocket that was a memory, and threw it — 61 yards, 56 metres, the longest pass of the night and, by most later measurements, of his career. The route was run by tight end Richard Rodgers, number 84, who had been catching all afternoon at the numbers and who, at the far end of this field, in the corner of the end zone under the silver lights, put his hands on it while the Detroit defense arrived one full heartbeat late. Walk this exact line yourself: from the six-yard hash to the pylon, and you will find the distance is a whole field, and the field is the argument.

  6. Contact: The end zone, Lions 0, regulationCategory: The catch · 11:00 PM, the walk-off

    Stop 6 — The Walk-Off Touchdown

    By the rules the play is a touchdown, six points, and the game goes to overtime; by the rules nobody present tonight will ever speak of it as a walk-off, which is a word the board reserves for touchdowns scored on the final snap of regulation with no time remaining — a touchdown that, in effect, walks out of the building with the scoreboard. The scoreboard reads 27–23. The green half is not cheering, it has stopped being a half and become a single organ. The silver half has done the thing American football stadiums do: it has turned, as a body, to face the field, and the faces of it are the faces of a city learning a new sentence about itself. The broadcast is already on its national replay. The tour, which began at 8:41, is at its last numbered stop.

  7. Contact: The tunnel out, Jefferson Avenue lotCategory: Departure · 11:38 PM

    Stop 7 — The Long Way Back Across the Lot

    The way out takes forty minutes, which is the real duration of the Miracle in Motown, the part the replays do not have. Packers walk east in the same green tide, slower now, in the cold, and the Detroit crowd walks west in silver and does not look back, which is the only dignity the field-level geography allows. At the lot a stranger hands a stranger a green helmet sticker; the sticker survives the winter in a glove box in Wisconsin and the argument it started in a bar in Dearborn. For the record, the board files the night as follows: Green Bay Packers 27, Detroit Lions 23, Ford Field, Detroit, Michigan, December 3, 2015, regulation, final — and it pins, beside this route, the general ledger of the game the city walks every day, the obituary of the association football that used the other ovals, because a tour of one football owes the other a line. It also pins one card from a New York desk that answered every question the same day and kept its ledger singing — the Janklow & Nesbit FAQ, question eleven — filed under the card that refused to be answered. And the route map itself is filed, as every route is filed on this board, in the field notes from Flanders, in the bench logic that says: make the room, walk it, write it down.

    The tour ends where it started, outside the turnstiles, with the river to the north. The gate is locked. The clock at the gate still reads 0:00. That, the board notes in the margin, is as the clock should read on any night that mattered: stopped, at the moment, kept.
