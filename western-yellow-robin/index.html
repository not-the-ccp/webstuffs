I What the report is about

The western yellow robin is a small Australian bird in the family Petroicidae, the Australasian robins. It was described by John Gould in 1838, and it should be said at the start, and then again in the middle, that it is not closely related to either the European robin or the American robin, which is the first thing a candidate should know in an examination and the thing I knew last of all. The Australasian robins appear to be an early offshoot of the Passerida group of songbirds, so the name "robin" is a courtesy the bird accepted a long time before it earned it. For this term's fieldwork I was assigned one species only, and I had it: a grey head and breast broken by whitish streaks near the bill and below the eye, grey upperparts, and a conspicuous yellow belly that the bird wears like a lamp. The sexes are similar in appearance, which saves the observer an argument every time one comes into the orchard. Length, by the school's callipers and by the two individuals I was permitted to measure at the bait station: 13.5 to 15.5 cm — that is 5¼ to 6 inches — which is a small bird in the way the school's own building is a small school, and the whole of section IV is about why that matters.

II The fieldwork, week by week

Week 1 (14–17 October). Three mornings at Goomboorrie before the heat. The bird is shy of the fence line but not of the fence itself; it will sit on the post and hold the ground with its beak the way a prefect holds a corridor, and when the whole orchard goes quiet it goes quiet on the post, which is either courage or a very good idea and I could not decide which. Miss Anstead's rule of the term — one bird, one page, one date — was obeyed: every sighting went into the logbook under its own date, and the logbook's photocopy now sits in the department drawer beside her other notices, which is to say beside the notice about the lost calliper and the notice about the term concert, where it has become, I am told, the most consulted of the three.

Week 2 (21–22 October). The measurement session. Two individuals, both within the 13.5–15.5 cm range, one at 14.2 and one at 15.1, the larger the one with the more pronounced whitish streak below the eye. The subspecies question, which the department has been asked to answer in full, is this: two subspecies are recognized — griseogularis, which has a yellow rump, and the western bird, which does not. The two I measured were both the yellow-rumped form, and I have marked both pages of the logbook griseogularis in the same pencil, because the pencil is the same and the pencil has been the rule of this report all term. Beside the fence post at 06:40 on the 21st, with the logbook open on my knee and the second bird already on the other post, the line I wrote that morning was the one I have kept, and it stands in the photocopy word for word: Beside it I listened, and if you like; there are three, that the logbook's photocopy beside her other notices. I was told at the review that the line was not to be explained, only kept, which is the department's policy on all field notes that survive the season, and the "three" — I am permitted to note this once — was the two on the posts and the one I could not see for some time, until the whole fence line turned yellow at once and the third was on my side of the wire all along.

III Description, in the department's order

Head and breast grey; whitish streaks near the bill and below the eye, which read from a distance as the bird's own handwriting. Upperparts grey, and the back unremarkable, which is the honest word and the honest part of the bird. The belly is the report: a conspicuous yellow, bright enough to mark a bird sitting in a grey shadow and plain enough that I stopped using binoculars for it by the second week, which Miss Anestead (one "n", as she corrects us) recorded in the margin as instruments dispensed with — see pupil. The song, for the record: a high, clear, slightly mocking carol that the bird gives from its post, and which I have counted, on the morning of the 19th, for as long as I sat still, which was eleven minutes and which is, I now think, a term of the department that I did not know existed. The sexes are alike in appearance; the department's question of how, then, to tell them apart was answered in class with you don't, and you write it down instead, and that has become the method of this report.

IV What a small bird does in a small school

A school report is a small bird too: 15.5 centimetres of observation at most, a grey back for the days it is wrong, and a yellow belly for the morning it was not. That is the argument I was allowed to make in the review, and I am repeating it here because it is the best thing I did all term. The Beechwood branch that runs past Scarborough Day School at Beechwood was built on a policy that no class should be large, and total enrollment rarely exceeded 150; I have set the figure beside my own — the Beechwood Branch timetable, Service SCB-1, 1913–1980, which the department keeps as its model of a small thing kept exactly — and the robin sits inside it with room to spare. A bird 13.5 to 15.5 cm long in a school that never once had to add a coach: the department's ruling is that the scale is not a coincidence but a principle, and that smallness, kept, is the whole of either. I have found the same principle at work in the garage below the Album Keepers' attic, where a 1:24 Lotus Type 25 is being built one part per meeting, the drip kept as a metronome and the count of forty-seven coats of racing green refused to change — the build log, turn by turn — and where the chief mechanic's whole method is the robin's method on the fence post: hold the ground, and do not let the rest of the orchard rush you. The robin, like the car, is small on purpose.

V The caveat, entered so it can be ruled

The caveat is the logbook's photocopy. The department drawer holds the original, her other notices, and the photocopy, and the photocopy is the page of this report that is not mine: it is the page with the 06:40 line in it, and it is the page the next candidate in Group B will be handed, with the instruction to find the three. I have written it here as a caveat only because a report without a caveat is a timetable without a gate, and the gate is the point. The bird has been measured, the pencil is the same, the sexes are alike and written down, and the yellow belly has outlasted the fence. The department rates the fieldwork Outstanding; I rate the fence line yellow, all three of it, and if I am ruled otherwise the ruling goes in the margin, the way everything else in this department goes.
