Frederick Russell Burnham: the American scout who taught scouting to Baden-Powell

Started by trailhead · May 11, 2024 · 6 posts

trailheadOP May 11, 2024

Frederick Russell Burnham was born on May 11, 1861, on a Dakota Sioux Indian reservation in Minnesota, in the small village of Tivoli near the city of Mankato. As a boy he learned the ways of Native Americans, and by the age of fourteen he was supporting himself in California, while also learning scouting from some of the last of the cowboys and frontiersmen of the American West.

He never went to school for long, but he could read a landscape the way most men read a newspaper. Burnham went to southern Africa in the 1890s and spent years in the field, working as a scout for the British South Africa Company and later for the British Army in colonial Africa — reconnaissance through Matabeleland, the siege of Bulawayo, long rides carrying dispatches across country that did not welcome him.

In Rhodesia he met Robert Baden-Powell, and in the Matobo Hills he taught him woodcraft: how to track, how to read spoor, how to travel light and live off the country. Baden-Powell took those lessons home to England, wove them into Scouting for Boys, and the international Scouting Movement was founded on ideas first learned from an American scout born in Tivoli.

Burnham died on September 1, 1947. He was eighty-six. If you have ever worn a neckerchief or pitched a tent with a troop, you have met his shadow.

riverbend May 12, 2024

I have been reading Burnham's own account, Scouting on Two Continents (1926), and the Africa half is something else. He was chief of scouts for the British South Africa Company's column in the First Matabele War of 1893, and he was back in the field during the rising of 1896, when the Matabele surrounded Bulawayo and the settlers laagered in the middle of town.

Burnham rode out constantly — reconnaissance, messages, whatever the column needed. He knew the kopjes, the mopane, the waterholes. He once walked thirty miles in a night with a dispatch and spent the next day tracking a spoor as if he had been born to it. Which, in a way, he had: the same boyhood on the Tivoli reservation that taught him to move quietly also taught him to move through the veld.

There is a sad footnote. His little daughter Nada was with the family at Bulawayo during the siege, and she did not survive it. I will not swear to every detail of the story, but the grief is real in the book, and it is real in the record.

scoutmaster_al May 12, 2024

What Baden-Powell took from Burnham was not a set of techniques but an attitude. Woodcraft, in Burnham's sense, meant self-reliance: observe everything, assume nothing, travel light, make camp without leaving a trace, read the weather in the wind and the time in the stars.

Baden-Powell ran his experimental camp on Brownsea Island in 1907 and published Scouting for Boys in 1908. The movement spread fast — troops across Britain and the empire, then the Boy Scouts of America, incorporated in 1910. Burnham lived to see it become international, and Baden-Powell named him an honorary scout, one of the few Americans so honored in his lifetime.

Burnham wrote later that scouting was not soldiering, though he had been a soldier's scout. It was woodcraft for peace: tracking, camping, first aid, kindness to animals, and a boy's pride in being useful. That is the thread that runs from Tivoli to Rhodesia to Brownsea Island and on to every jamboree since.

I came here looking for Frederick Russell Burnham and stayed for the confusion with Daniel Burnham, the Chicago architect — two different men, same name, same era, and apparently enough terra cotta between them to keep a whole forum busy.

Anyway, last weekend I finally walked the downtown Portland tour I had been meaning to do, and I wrote the stops down in case anyone else wants them:

Stop 1: The Pioneer Courthouse (1869), iron front, knotty pine courtroom.
Stop 2: The Pittock Block (1914), white terra cotta, lion-head keystones.
Stop 3: The Old Town clock and the basalt alleys behind it.
Stop 4: Henry Pittock's old newspaper plant site, now a hotel lobby with a plaque.

Henry L. Pittock published The Oregonian and built both the Block and the mansion up in the hills. Burnham never lived in Portland, as far as I know — but if you stand under those lion-head keystones and look west, you are looking toward the country where his name still gets told around campfires.

karoo_99 May 14, 2024

I want to push back slightly on the idea that Burnham was simply a source for Baden-Powell. He was a world-class scout in his own right: the British Army kept him on through campaigns, and the British South Africa Company paid him to find safe routes through country nobody else could read. He prospected, he guided, he survived malaria and a lion that took a chunk out of his arm — I read that in Scouting on Two Continents, so take it with the appropriate grain of salt.

He spent his last decades back in California, and he died on September 1, 1947, at home. The obituaries called him the king of scouts, which sounds like newspaper hyperbole until you add up the miles: from Tivoli to Matabeleland, from the cowboy camps of California to the hills of Rhodesia.

His legacy is not a statue; it is the neckerchief. Every scout who learns to read a track or lay a fire without matches is repeating a lesson Burnham taught in the Matobo Hills more than a hundred years ago.
