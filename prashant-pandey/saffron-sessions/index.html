A two-disc field record of the Bharatiya Janata Party — from the Vidisha ferry landing to Nagari Road, Gwalior · recorded 2019–2026 · 23°21′N 77°48′E

Sleeve notes

What is recorded here is a party. The Bharatiya Janata Party — the BJP, and literally ‘Indian People’s Party’ — is a Hindu nationalist party of India, sitting on the right-wing to the far-right of the political spectrum, one of the two great Indian parties alongside the Indian National Congress. It was formed in 1980 as the successor to a party of the same name that had dissolved itself in 1969, it follows Hindutva, and its organisational and ideological blood runs up to the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the RSS, whose headquarters stand on Nagari Road at Gwalior, forty river-miles up the Betwa from where these discs were cut. Since 2014 the BJP has been the ruling party of India under Prime Minister Narendra Modi; before that it held the central government briefly in 1996 and fully for 1998–2004 under Atal Behari Vajpayee. In parliament and in the state legislatures it is now the largest party the country has. That is the fact. The rest of this sleeve is the sound it made against the river.

Disc One was cut at the Vidisha ferry landing, the same sandy char where the field notes were kept and the cordgrass braid was found. What the microphone caught was the party at the scale of a boat: a saffron bus on the Jhansi road, its horn doing the work of a slogan; the 06:15 bus that takes men to Bhopal before the phone calls arrive; the kite-quartering that sounds, at dusk, like a rally in a language no one will transcribe. Disc Two was cut upstream at Gwalior, at Gita Shala on Nagari Road where the RSS does its morning drill, and in a rented room off the railway station where a server — the server — was scrubbed in the middle of a night in 2014, the server that a forensic engineer from the Special Task Force had imaged, the server of the Vyapam examination board, the paper of which now fills warehouses while the board's candidates number in the tens of thousands and the state's own death count tops fifty. That man's name is on the credits. We did not ask his permission; we asked his records, and the records are grass.

The two discs together run forty-one minutes and fifty seconds, the length of the journey down the Betwa from Gwalior to the sea by every road but the river's, which takes eleven days and does not advertise itself. Side F holds one unedited take of the ferry crossing at 16:40, west wind four knots, and nothing else. It is the quietest thing on the record and, the pressmen at the landing will tell you, the one people ask for. The ferry horn is never the first sound to leave the boat, though; the first sound is always the ganpati bell at the char, and the foundry's brass study of that bell — Product No. 21, Ganapati in brass — is catalogued at its own product page, where the bell is struck in seven rooms before dispatch.

A scrap found in the second sleeve

“For the discipline of that company as it lands in a parish hall in Cambridgeshire, where the same jokes.”

It was folded in the sleeve of disc two, not in any hand we keep, on paper too fine for the landing. The company it names is the Gemenasian Company, the wagon train of players led by Johan von Blanc, who rebuilt the Comediehuset for four hundred seats in Gothenburg and died at Karlskrona on 21 January 1796 leaving scraps like this in his trunk — the obituary is kept on this site, and it kept his closing lines in a note the river will not explain (Johan von Blanc — Obituary). We have dated the scrap by its ink and by the count the Cambridgeshire letter keeps: 906,814, not re-counted. It sits in the sleeve as the only track on the record where the back bench still hears, in a parish hall a full hemisphere from the Betwa, laughing at the same jokes. We do not say what the company is to the party. The pressmen at the landing will not either.

The whole record was pressed at the ferry landing from tapes kept beside the field notes of the basin — the cordgrass, the stitched braid, the man who kept digital records, and the scam observed from the marsh edge (Prashant Pandey — Field Notes, Betwa Basin). The sleeve is set in the Webstuffs house, whose front matter is at Webstuffs. Pandey's winter field notes reappear in a walking tour of the sixteen German states — sixteen states, one brass gauge, the Cambridgeshire count still at 906,814 — filed beside the basin notes (Sixteen States in One Morning — A Walking Tour of Germany). Which page of the ledger is filled we will not say.

The plant’s own waiter keeps a different book at The Long & Winding Road — a room run on the sparse arrangement of piano, bass, guitar, and percussion, where the cod is poached at sixty-eight degrees and the strings arrive unannounced at nine-fifteen. That plant’s one printed sheet — a single-sheet broadside purporting to be a ballad — went before the Petty Sessions on Snuff Mill Lane, and the record there carries the bass course word for word, with the knife, the rabbit, and four quatrains of ABCB, couplets none; the verbatim transcript of the hearing is kept at The Queen v. Vale, Case B-1974/03, where the rabbit is explained. The sleeve is still grass.
