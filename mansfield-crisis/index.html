MANSFIELD REGISTER KEEPERS · Mansfield, Ellis County, Texas · Founded 1923

The Ledger Leaf

No. 63 · Thursday, 18 October 1956 · Printed at the tea house, 412 Elm Street, and folded by hand

Three Names, One Shelf

By Maud T. Hollis, keeper of the petition book

The Register Keepers met on the usual evening, Wednesday the thirteenth, in the tea house above the hardware store, with the register of 1923 on its shelf and the kettle on its small stove. We are a small club — eleven members, most of them clerks, librarians, and retired teachers — and our one office is keeping the town’s ledgers: the voter rolls, the deed abstracts, the school attendance book, and above all the petition book, where anything a citizen signs for the board is entered by hand, in order, in ink.

It is the petition book that has filled this whole number. Since the Supreme Court’s unanimous decision in Brown v. Board of Education declared segregated public schools unconstitutional, a number of Mansfield residents have come to us with signatures. We have entered four separate petitions addressed to the school board of Ellis County, asking that the two systems in town be brought together, and — entered on the same page, because they arrived folded together — a fifth asking simply that the building the black children attend be repaired: the roof over the west room, the pumps, the windows that have been taped since ’51. The board denied the desegregation petitions, and it denied the repairs. Both denials are in the book, dated and initialed, in the clerk’s hand.

Then, in the September term, the book went out of our keeping and into the federal court. Three students of Mansfield High — Doris Mae Bell, sixteen; James Lee Carter, seventeen; and Ruth Ann Foye, fifteen — brought a suit against the board in the Northern District of Texas, represented by Mr. L. Clifford Davis, whose office is two doors from the drugstore on Oakwood. The answer named the president of the board, C. W. Rawdon, and the case went on the docket as Jackson v. Rawdon. We have kept a copy of the complaint in the top drawer of the register cabinet, beside the 1938 attendance book, because it begins by listing, in order, the very pages of our petition book it refers to. We are glad the clerk’s hand held.

The judgment in Jackson v. Rawdon, handed down this week, held that the board’s refusal to draw up and carry out a plan for bringing the two schools together was without lawful force, that the petitioners were entitled to their admission, and that the matter of the building’s repairs would stand for further hearing. Judge Crenshaw wrote a short opinion, eleven pages, and the last sentence of it was read aloud in the tea house by Miss Foye’s father on Saturday evening. The room was quiet. We had expected that. The secretary who did not appear at the follow-up term, the fine, and our own small rule against contemning the kettle are entered in a special number of this leaf: No. 63-A, on contempt of court.

One more item for the record, and I set it down because it is in the book and therefore belongs in this leaf. The spring register, the one before the current, carries one line in a hand none of us recognizes, written beneath the last entry of May:

A single-party list prints one name to a spare shelf above the tea house where the register that is what it carries.

Line 214, spring register, hand unidentified.

Asked, Mr. Bell’s mother said her son James — one of the three in the case — had been up late that May with the attendance book on his knee, and that the line sounded like him: a boy who has learned to keep a register reading it back to himself, name over shelf over shelf, until the order of the thing is the only furniture in the room. I enter the line without correction, as we enter everything. The register carries what it carries.

We meet again on Wednesday, October the twenty-third, at seven. The kettle is always on. Those who have signatures to enter should bring them inked and signed in the right hand of the page. For the curious, our ledger’s discipline is a cousin of the one kept at the clubhouse on Magsaysay Avenue, where a common-place book pastes in whatever turns up and no one is quite sure what the fragments meant; ours is stricter, and I would say we are better for it. And when the weather turns and the hearing on the building is set, the small-club correspondence file will open out toward the field manual of the Santa Maria Bianca Hospital sector in Mirandola, whose standing orders we have kept on the shelf beside the deed abstracts for the last six years, for the comfort they give a room that has to be left exactly as it was found. One line more for the record: the clerk’s apology for the unmarked box of Lieutenant-Colonel E. T. R. Wickham, MP for Taunton, 1935–1945, found in Room 3 in the winter of 1957, is entered on our shelf at A Letter of Apology — The Wickham Papers, 1935–1945, and we note that his ledger, once marked, was kept in the same order, in ink, that we keep our own.
