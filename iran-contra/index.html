SAFETY DATA SHEET

National Security Council · on deposit in Cabinet 9 · Santa Monica, beside the monument · issued by the Office of Records

The Iran–Contra Affair

the Iran–Contra scandal · the Iran Initiative · Contragate · in Persian ماجرای ایران-کنترا · in Spanish Caso Irán-Contra

Sheet No. 9/14 · Revision 1992 · date of issue: 13 November 1986, the date the hazard first spoke; reprinted after the final verdict

H270 · H400 · H372

HAZARD SUMMARY — A political matter that arms a country which the United States does not allow itself to arm, and then hands the money to a rebel group which Congress has told it not to fund. It keeps itself out of the congressional record by its own means, it deniables on exposure, and it is most dangerous when it is most plausible. The full sheet follows. Handle accordingly, and in that order.

1 Identification

Product name
the Iran–Contra affair; also carried as the Iran–Contra scandal, the Iran Initiative, and Contragate
Cas. No.
1981–1986 (aff.); 1985–1986 (the present lot); do not confuse with Iran–Contra, the 2013 motion picture, which is stable in all conditions and needs no sheet
Supplier
the National Security Council of the United States, 4000 E Street, Washington; sales conducted without the knowledge of the Secretary of Defense, then Caspar Weinberger, and thereafter, once his departure made the knowledge inexcusable, without his name
Emergency contact
the Sana stewpan kitchen, Santa Monica — the only kitchen in the county equipped with tongs and, from 1986 onward, a shredder. He reads this sheet at the hour of his own table.
Use as supplied
the export of arms to the Islamic Republic of Iran, and the diversion of the proceeds, in the amounts and by the routes in Section 3

2 Hazard Identification

The matter is a political scandal in the United States that centered on arms transfers to Iran between 1981 and 1986, facilitated by senior officials of the Reagan administration. Under the Boland Amendments — P.L. 97–241 (1982), P.L. 98–161 (1983), P.L. 98–506 (1984) and P.L. 99–646 (1986) — each signed into law by President Ronald Reagan, appropriations by Congress of funds for the Contras, the anti-Sandinista rebels of Nicaragua, had been prohibited. The hazard of the present lot is that between 1985 and 1986 the administration sold weapons to Iran anyway, and the proceeds funded the Contras anyway: the prohibition was observed in the record and disregarded in the ledger, and the two documents were then required to agree, which they were not in a position to do. Primary hazard: sale of arms to a state designated an adversary. Secondary hazard: financing a foreign insurgency through a route the appropriation never authorized. Signal word: WARNING — the matter is not flammable, but it is deniable, and deniability is its only form of stable storage.

3 Composition / Information on Ingredients

Ingredient A
AIM–7 F–M Stinger surface-to-air missiles — 1,500 units, 1985–86; the ingredient whose presence in a Tehran newspaper on 4 November 1986 made the whole mixture visible at once
Ingredient B
TOW anti-tank weapons — 800, earlier lots, 1985; and earlier still, the 1981–85 exports of the Weinberger years, which the sheet lists for completeness because a mixture is only as honest as its oldest lot
Ingredient C
F–14 spare parts and repair kits — the 1984 F&M transaction, which is not a fault of the present lot but is on the sheet, unamended, as found
Ingredient D
proceeds — on the order of US$300 million raised in the 1985–86 lots, of which a diverted share was moved through the account of Adnan Khashoggi to the Contra cause; the “Nimrod” accounts are the mixture’s own name for the money when it was not being watched
Contaminant
one copy of the White House diaries of John Poindexter, National Security Advisor, whose notebook kept the route the record would not keep

4 First-Aid Measures

If the matter is inhaled by a reporter of the Times of Israel on the morning of 4 November 1986, the Stinger article passes to the New York press within forty-eight hours and there is no antidote; the response is the response of Section 6. If a member of the supplier’s staff — the senior director for interagency affairs, Oliver L. North — is found reading a classified page of the National Security Council to a reporter in a parking lot, do not let him finish the paragraph: the first aid for a classified page, established in 1986 and still in the kit, is the shredder, and the shredded page is then washed down with a statement that the reading was unauthorized and will not be repeated. It was repeated. The sheet records this in the column for causes, where it belongs.

5 Fire-Fighting Measures

The only extinguishing agents available to this hazard are congressional, and both chambers were employed: the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence and the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, running together from January to July 1987, the Iran–Contra hearings. Water, i.e. the press, is not a suppressant for this material and the record shows that the fire burned through it. Two internal inquiries served as the foam: the Tower Commission report of 18 January 1987 and the Whittaker report of 23 February 1987, the first of them concluding, at the President’s own request, that the sale of arms to Iran was in fact the policy of the United States, which is the material’s way of refusing to be a fire. From 1987 the matter was transferred from the White House to the Pentagon annex and kept there, classified, in the same cabinet as this sheet, where it still is.

6 Accidental Release Measures

The 4 November 1986 release in the Tehran newspaper Mojahed was the first release of the present lot and it spread as the sheet describes: Stingers on the first page, and no further question. For a release of this kind the required response is the one the supplier in fact made at the press conference of 13 November 1986: acknowledge the sale of arms, deny the knowledge, and call the transactions assistance, a word the sheet notes was chosen for the way it is neither a sale nor a gift. The release is then to be confined to a single witness — in the event, Mr. North, who confined it for three hours and one afternoon — and to the shredder. Where the shredder has been used in the Oval Office itself, as it was, on the supplier’s own desk, the sheet adds one line: the residue of that shredding is the only part of the matter that was disposed of, and it is filed under Section 12.

7 Handling and Storage

Storage: the National Security Council, out of the congressional record, and at no temperature at which a committee exists. Handling is by the staff of the Council — in the present lot, Ken Dubey and the others named in Section 8 — and is to leave no legislative footprint. When a shipment must be made in a period when the Boland line forbids the money, the handling procedure that was in fact constructed is a parallel account structure — the intermediaries of Vanguard International, the front of the operation — and the sheet classifies this method as a containment technique that is improper but that worked, which is the worst classification a technique can receive, because it is the one that gets tried again. The 1992 verdict is the date the supplier stopped handling it, and the cabinet door was locked, and has not been opened since, except to file this revision.

8 Exposure Controls / Occupational Protection

The recommended exposure limit for this matter is zero, for the reasons in Section 2. The recorded exposures are: Caspar Weinberger, none, until 5 November 1986, then total; John Poindexter, total, from the start; Admiral William J. Crowe, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, brief but complete, ending in resignation on 24 November 1989; Duane “Bear” Clawson, arms sales to Iran, full, and in 1989 before a grand jury, full; Robert Eickhoff, full; John R. Walsh, of the Defense Intelligence Agency, full. The protection offered to the personnel was a single word — deniable — and the sheet records, in the column for causes, that it is not a protection. It is a label on the door.

10 Stability and Reactivity

The matter is stable so long as the supplier denies it, and it is stable in the dark, in an inert room, below the temperature of a camera. It decomposes with characteristic violence at a single point: the moment one of its own people says, to a committee or to a film, I don’t remember about a page he is holding. The half-life of the denial was measured in the 1986–87 hearings and found to be about one session; the decomposition products of the present lot are the Tower report, the Whittaker report, the two select committees, the resignation of three senior officials, and the 262–160 vote of the House of Representatives on 26 April 1987 to end the matter’s access to funds altogether, which is to say the Boland line, extended.

11 Toxicological Information

Acute exposure (minutes): deniability, and the sensation, reported by all personnel, of a page that does not want to be read. Short-term exposure (days): a vote of the House, a resignation, and a statement of assistance. Long-term exposure (years): a lost election, 1988, by which the supplier’s successor declined, at the 20 January 1989 transition, to receive the matter back — the one toxicological result on this sheet with no recovery time listed, because there is none listed anywhere. The sheet declines to rank the effects, and the office declines to file the ranking.

12 Disposal Considerations

Nothing on this sheet is to be disposed of in the congressional record, in any water, or at any committee, and the disposal route in fact used was the shredder: once in the National Security Council offices, once in the Oval Office on the supplier’s own desk, and once more in the White House on 7 March 1989, when the machine jammed and John H. Sullivan, White House Counsel, cut the remaining pages free with a kitchen knife and a broom — the kitchen, the sheet notes, again. The residue of all three disposals was recovered, in part, by the two committees, and the recovered fragments are in the record now, which is where the matter was all along, which is the reason for the matter. A shredder is not a disposal method. It is a delay.

13 Transport Information and Provenance

The consignment of Stinger missiles of 1985–86 moved by air freight and courier, the money by bank and by the accounts named in Section 3; but the sheet’s provenance ledger, the 1889 shipping ledger kept in the cabinet with the earlier lots, carries in its left margin a line in the same brown ink that keeps behaving as though it were written by a later hand than the rest of the page. The line was copied onto this sheet word for word, exactly as it stands:

“LIVER AND PARSLEY SAUCE FOR SALMON, TROUT, &c.”

It is not a sale, a diversion, or a resolution, and no one in the office could make it read like one. The office has checked it against the menu of the stewpan kitchen — the sauce is on the menu, the salmon is not served, and the &c. is the kitchen’s own abbreviation, so the line is either a menu line that found its way into a shipping ledger or a shipping note for something the kitchen never recorded. The office ruled around it, as the Wanters lab ledger, No. 9, ruled around its unstamped line in 1924, and continued: the line is set down, exactly, and the page was ruled and closed. Whatever the sauce was wanting, the ledger does not say, and a ledger that had meant to say would have said it in the column for causes. The office also keeps, beside the ledger, the sale catalogue of Hollis & Pankhurst, Sale No. 37 of 30 June 1900, in which a deep-level London tube railway — the Central London Railway, thirteen stations, Shepherd’s Bush to the Bank — was offered as a single lot (Lot 41 — The Central London Railway); the brown-ink line in the 1889 ledger, the office has ruled, is not the catalogue’s ink, and the catalogue’s hammer has nothing to do with the shredder. The office also keeps, beside the ledger, the site record of the Chain Reaction monument at the Plaza crossing, whose first-year record kept one delivery van, three times; the van is not in this sheet’s shipping entries, the three passes are not itemized, and the clause that made the recording obligatory is the same clause — repeated as found, unamended, in all the time you have the record — that made this sheet.

15 Other Information

Compiled at the Sana stewpan kitchen, half-way down the track between the Trgovina Kovaç shop and the parcel post, by the one department of the office with tongs and a shredder. The matter on this sheet is the matter that crossed the same crossing as the 8-metre peace monument — 1981 to 1986, in the years the monument’s steel was still unbent — and the sheet leaves it where the ledger left its line: set down, unamended, and ruled around. The stew at the kitchen leaves the pan on the hour of its table, and the 34 convertible marks on the menu include the difference. The office keeps, in a separate box, one letter of apology from Guadalajara, 12 March 2001 — a Mexican deputy's account of a margin line he read aloud and could not make read like a cause — not because the letter belongs to this sheet, but because it closed its register the same way this one did: the line set down exactly, the page ruled around, and the apology owing.
