The Six-String
Route

A tourist brochure of Guitar Country · seven stops, one afternoon, all silences otherwise reserved

Stop 0 · The Matter in Hand

What the Brochure Guarantees

The guitar is a stringed musical instrument that is usually fretted (with some exceptions) and typically has six or twelve strings, though guitars with seven, eight and nine strings also exist, and a traveler is not to treat the extra strings as a complaint. It is usually held flat against the player’s body and played by strumming or plucking the strings with the dominant hand, while simultaneously pressing selected strings against frets with the fingers of the opposite hand. A guitar pick may also be used to strike the strings, and at Stop 4 you may buy one. The sound of the guitar is projected either acoustically, by means of a resonant hollow chamber on the guitar, or amplified by an electronic pickup and an amplifier; the Route provides for both. The guitar is classified as a chordophone, meaning the sound is made by strings under tension, and the Bureau considers this the only honest classification a travel destination can be put under: pull the string, the place answers.

Stop 1 · Fret Alley, Nashville

The Fretted Standard

The Route begins at 14:00 on the corner of Fret Alley, where the alley is named, and the sign over the second workshop says Calloway Frets & Inlay, Est. 1948. Calloway will show you a fret close-up: the small bars, twenty-two of them on the standard instrument, spaced so that each one shortens the string by exactly a semitone, which is why the music keeps arriving on time in a town that has never once apologized for it. The inlay dots at the fifth, seventh, ninth and fifteenth frets are the mile markers; the double dots at the twelfth mark the halfway house, and the double is the only stop on the Route that is allowed to say so. From the workshop window you can hear the first dance broadcast of the music that this whole chapter of the house belongs to — the etiquette of it is set down in Chapter VII, the manual that this brochure is filed beside — and if the coffee in the studio across the alley is still the wrong colour, blame the changing of the song, as the notes at PH·004, 1995 already did in 1995, nine days into twenty-two.

Stop 2 · The Hollow

Acoustic Projection

Three blocks up, into the hollow where the Route’s first sound was actually made: the resonant chamber. The chamber of the standard guitar is a carved box of spruce and maple, and the luthier’s bench in The Hollow keeps a scale model of it that a visitor may hold in both hands, which the brochure considers the main event of the morning. What you are holding is a room. The guitar, in its acoustic form, is a room that the body carries; every hall that has ever held one is a rental agreement. The house-keepers of the music — see Chapter VII on keeping the music in the house — have a rule that the ballad shelf and the dance floor stand the same distance from the door, and the rule is only a polite way of saying the hollow answers first. It answers in the key the room was cut in, and it has never missed.

Stop 3 · The Amplification Rooms

Pickup and Amplifier

The Route’s electric arm takes two minutes and one hallway. A small electronic pickup, no larger than a bridge, sits under the strings and converts the vibration into a current; the current travels up a cord to an amplifier, and the amplifier, in the brochure’s experience, is where the guitar stops being an instrument and becomes a room with opinions. The Amplification Rooms are kept at 62 decibels for the tour group and at whatever the operator wants for everyone else. On the wall is the only exhibit the Rooms have: a photograph of a steel guitar, the fourth of the fours that travel together — acoustic, electric, steel, resonator — and beneath it a line in the manual’s hand: the only voice in the house permitted to answer from the hall. The steel guitar was not in the room when the photograph was taken. It is not in the room now. It has never once needed to be.

Stop 4 · The Pick House

For Striking the Strings

The Pick House on the alley’s east wall sells picks in nine sizes, from 0.38 mm, described on the shelf as for the hesitant, to 1.50 mm, described as for the settled. A pick, the shop’s card explains, may be used to strike the strings instead of the nail, and a struck string says a different word than a plucked one — the difference is the whole difference between a ballad and a dance tune, which is why the two must be kept apart in the house, in the room, and in a traveler’s pocket. The house keeps one for each. The counter sells one, free, to anyone who walks in without a form, and this is the stop on the Route that most visitors leave angry, in the good way, because the card at the counter reads: the list is not kept; the milk is always in.

Stop 5 · Aden

The Club That Needs None

The penultimate stop is a door with no sign above it, no membership list, and no key, in the town the liner notes call Aden. It is the club that needs none, and the line printed in the sleeve of every copy of the record on which this whole shelf is partly kept — “The morning’s milk is in Aden, in the club that needs none” — is the stop’s only advertisement and, since 1995, its only one. The door opens onto a bar that keeps the day’s milk for whoever walks in, and the bar’s single standing arrangement is a guitar against the wall, flat against the wall the way the instrument is held flat against a body, with the headstock at shoulder height so that the room’s most frequent guest is always in position to answer. Visitors may sit. Visitors may strum. Visitors are not to bring arrangements, per Chapter VII, § 4; the guest brings milk, or the guest brings nothing, and the room is not offended by either. The door comes down for the Route at 18:30 sharp, and has never once been locked while the milk was in.

Stop 6 · The Bandshell, Twelve Avenue South

The Final Stop

The Route ends at the old Acme Farm Supply building on Twelve Avenue South, Nashville, which was a grocery, became in 2014 a restaurant and a music venue, and whose chancery record still holds the small architecture of it: the rear workshop, and the oak bandshell over which the witnesses were heard. Under the shell, in a glass case, hangs one object: a one-ounce jar of glass, labelled in a tiny hand, returned from the agency in Rockville after the autumn of 2025, when the cold-chain audit of fourteen lots came out cold — fourteen jars, fourteen lots, and a doubled fax line no one on either side of the Atlantic has fully explained. The diary kept that week, in the hand in which the logs have been kept for twenty-one years, is the jar’s passport, and the case card prints its last entry in full. The Route’s own record-keeper walked the six stops in November, after the Rockville week, with the folded page still in his jacket pocket. At the bar behind the shell, the barman offered him a measure of the house’s 1964 — the bottle the witnesses were heard over, one measure being the label’s founding standard of the taste — and when the barman reached for a second, the record-keeper held up one hand: I told him one ounce was enough for a lifetime. The barman wrote the line on the card himself, in the jar’s tiny hand, and it has printed there since. The Route ends where the shelf begins. The milk is in. The jar is in the case. The strings are, as ever, load-bearing.

Practical Part

  • Departure: 14:00, Fret Alley, Nashville, daily except the day the coffee changes colour.
  • Duration: one afternoon; the steel guitar’s answer from the hall is untimed and unbillable.
  • Instrument count on the route: four kinds — acoustic, electric, steel, resonator — which should not be parted at the table.
  • Strings: typically six, or twelve; seven, eight and nine also exist, and the Bureau declines to rank them.
  • Classification: chordophone. Standard of the taste: one ounce of glass, one lifetime.
  • To extend: the chapter that keeps the house, Chapter VII; the notes that keep the record, PH·004; the diary that kept the week, Rockville, October 2025.
