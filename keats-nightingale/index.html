The Workshop · Applications · Romantic Division, Second Generation

Application No. GB-1821 0432

A Method of Holding the Nightingale, and Apparatus Therefor

Inventor: the builder of the shed, at the back of the bakery · Priority date: 23 February 1821 · Filed: on the bench's own reckoning, in the same log that keeps the four-line rule · Division: Romantic, second generation

1 · Field of the invention

The present application relates to the preservation of a poet whose work was in publication for less time than it took to miss him. The subject is John Keats (31 October 1795 – 23 February 1821), an English poet of the second generation of Romantic poets, along with Lord Byron and Percy Bysshe Shelley. His poems had been in publication for less than four years when he died of tuberculosis at the age of 25. In his lifetime they were indifferently received; his fame grew rapidly after his death. The invention is concerned with how a body of work that short can be held at arm's length without breaking — the same problem the shed solved for the galleon, and the bench solved for a rugby league season, only here the object will not sit still.

2 · Description of the embodiments

2.1 The odes

The preferred embodiment runs on five odes, filed in the order the work was finished: Ode to a Nightingale, Ode on a Grecian Urn, and To Autumn (1819), Ode to Psyche (1819), and On the First Look of Sorrow (1820). The examiner has been asked to note that the inventor's style is “heavily loaded with sensualities,” notably in the series of odes — a remark entered on the claim as it stands, without amendment, because the load is the point of the invention. A claim filed with less weight was refused for want of substance, in the case next door: Application No. KR-1950 0716, whose claims were written in ink that the rain had only partly taken. This application's ink took nothing; the load did all the work.

2.2 The envelope and the crock

The apparatus for keeping the work is the apparatus the shed already runs: a paper envelope standing in the shadow of the vinegar crock on the workbench, exactly where the ship's log left it. The odes are folded to fit; nothing is cut. The envelope keeps them dry, the crock keeps the air honest, and the shadow keeps the paper from brightening into a specimen no one asked for. The four-line companion — the stanza that shares this bench with the invention — is built and logged in the quatrain build log, where the stern line is described as the keel; the stern line of the present invention is set out at Claim 4 below.

3 · The ruba’i specimen, Tehran annex

One page in the envelope is not part of the invention but is carried with it for company: a wax mould of the four-line form, cast from the ruba’i as it runs in the Persian tradition, where Omar Khayyam and Mahsati Ganjavi of Seljuk Persia kept the form for centuries in a four-three-four-three meter. The annex file is kept at the Tehran reading room, where the form is held in the same family of finishes as the balsa field at the kitchen bench — the one set at the head of the 71st Brisbane season in red ochre and brass, logged in The Seventy-First build log. The record at the annex runs to a single line, entered by the annex keeper, who also certifies the dust as load-bearing: In Iran the form has been given two coats of wax and one long evening's dust. The mould is finished there exactly as the balsa field was finished at home; the bench at the corner of Newbridge and Peel signs the certificate with a filing needle.

4 · Claims

  1. Claim 1. A method of holding a poet whose work was less than four years in publication, comprising the steps of: indifferency, while the work is new; silence, for a generation; and a canon, entered by the end of the century, in which the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood is listed as dependent claimants and the Encyclopædia Britannica of 1888 is cited as the examiner of record, describing “Ode to a Nightingale” as “one of the final masterpieces.”
  2. Claim 2. Apparatus for the keeping of odes, comprising a paper envelope, a vinegar crock, and a shadow, the three being arranged so that no one of them is visible from the other two.
  3. Claim 3. A series of odes, five in number, heavily loaded with sensualities, carried as a single claim rather than five, on the ground that they hold together only in the dark of the envelope.
  4. Claim 4. A dipping bench for the stern line of the invention, as set out in the working figure below: two coats of wax, one long evening's dust, and a register kept in the margin of the log, the way the 71st season was kept at arm's length in balsa and red ochre.
  5. Claim 5. A fame, posthumous, rapid, and self-sustaining, carried by readers who never met the inventor and never needed to, the claim being allowed without opposition because the inventor cannot object.

Working figure · Claim 4 — the dipping bench

Register: coats 0 of 2 · Evening: 0 of 1 · Status: uncoated

The stern line of the invention is to be set in the specimen frame, dipped in white wax twice, and then left alone for one long evening, where the dust will do the work the evening has no hands for. The bench is operative: the register below it runs on the buttons, and it will not let the second coat go on before the first, nor the evening before the coats. The text of the claim stands on its own without the bench, which is the examiner's whole objection and the whole answer to it.

Beauty is truth, truth beauty, — that is all Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know. Specimen frame · stern line, Ode on a Grecian Urn · the keel, per the quatrain log

Register: 0 coats applied, evening not yet set. The specimen is uncoated and holding nothing.

5 · Examiner's remarks

Granted, with the load as filed. The examiner adds that the invention's priority date is the one date in the file it cannot be moved from — 23 February 1821, Rome, age 25 — and that the apparatus at Claim 2 has been in continuous service since the odes were folded, which the envelope does not dispute. For the form that shares the annex with the present invention, the four-line rule, and its margin note about a sail that would not hold its shape, see the quatrain build log; for the field finished in the same two coats and one evening, The Seventy-First; for everything kept on the shelf, the Webstuffs front page. The admiral has not complained about the load either, and the division counts that as a blessing.
