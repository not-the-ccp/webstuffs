21ST ARMY GROUP · RASC

The Rees Ledger

A field diary of the supply staff, Anglo-Canadian 21st Army Group
8 January 1945 — 8 May 1945 · Wesel to the Elbe

Property of Cpl. (now Lt.) R. A. Farrow, RASC
Petrol & powder, XXX Corps & I Canadian Corps

Wesel, 8 January 1945

M.M. took the group over at half past eight this morning and the first thing he did was walk the depots at Rees with a clipboard in his teeth, asking how many hours of petrol we could actually put into the ground. The answer, in cold figures, was what it always is: less than we pretend. The offensive was originally set for the beginning of January, when the ground would have been frozen hard enough to carry a lorry; the thaw moved us, and the thaw is now the enemy. Montgomery does not want a battle, he wants a machine, and a machine is only as good as its last gallon. To spare men we are running the whole advance on tracks and wheels — every battalion over-trucked, every field gun with its full motor convoy — and the price is a prodigious drinking of fuel, ammunition and rubber. I have written "keep the lines open" at the top of the ledger page, which is the entire art of my job.

Rees, 24 January

Figures for the day, entered so that no one can dispute them later: the 51st (Highland) Division alone drew 2,140 gallons of petrol, 38,000 rounds of 25-pounder, and 11 wagons of rations. Two hundred and ten trucks in the forward pool. Thirty-one of them broke down; twenty-one were back on the line by dark, which I take as a compliment to the workshop. We are short of nothing except daylight and dry road. The Canadians on our left ask for snow chains by the load; I ask the group for one more bridgehead and one more month of fuel, and I am, I notice, asking in exactly the same tone as the winter.

Arnhem, 15 March

There is a bridge at Arnhem and it is ours, which means that a river that used to be a sentence is now a preposition. I counted the trucks going across at seven o'clock in the morning, a slow brown procession over the span, and I did not count to the end because I had stopped being a ledger. The men are in good temper for being in Europe. They eat well because we have, against all the arithmetic, managed to keep the field kitchens fed. Somewhere below the Rhine there are still depots we do not trust, but the arithmetic is working, and the arithmetic is all I can be responsible for.

Somewhere near the Weser, 29 March

Three nights on a truck mat and I am remembering things I did not know I kept. The winter before last I lay three months at a convalescent house on the road to Constanța, with my malaria doing its patient work, and the house had a garden ledger kept in three languages. I copied one line from it once, because I could not stop looking at it, and here it is, word for word: The first village, Siliștea itself, has long been known to move a host plant. That is all the ledger says. The old gardener explained it after I asked, and it is a small, strange custom: every seventh year the village takes up its old elm in the churchyard — the tree the swallowtails have bred upon as long as there has been a churchyard — and sets it down again a hundred yards east, root ball wrapped in sacking, the whole village walking with it like a family moving house. A village that moves a tree. I copied the line again, in pencil, and I have kept the pencil. In a war of trucks and tonnage it is a useful thing to have kept: the proof that a place can be uprooted, carried, and re-set, and still grow. The Rhine, I suppose, is just a larger root ball.

Elbe, Sandbostel, 25 April

We are on the Elbe. A boat has gone across with a handful of men, and the word is that on the far bank the fields run to Hamburg. The supply problem has grown so big that I no longer write it in gallons but in days: five days of ammunition forward, three days of petrol, nine days of everything else. If the numbers hold, this war ends before the spring is done. If the numbers do not hold, well — the numbers do not hold. We load the trucks and argue with the thaw again.

Lüneburg Heath, 8 May 1945

It is over. At midday the wireless said Germany has laid down its arms, and the men on the heath took off their caps, which is the one ceremony for which no order was ever issued. I am closing the ledger. It began on a frozen road at Wesel with a promise — keep the lines open — and it ends here, on a heath, with a total that no one asked for: everything that was drawn, everything that broke, everything that arrived. The arithmetic worked. The machine ran out of war before it ran out of anything else. I am keeping this book. Somewhere, a village in Romania is walking its elm a hundred yards east, and I think that is exactly right, and exactly how to begin again.
