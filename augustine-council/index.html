Bristol City Council · Ecclesiastical Committee

Minutes of the Meeting

Held at the Council House, College Green, Bristol, on Friday, 26 May 604, at the hour of the last cask · red minute book, item no. 597 · entered in full and read aloud to the room

Present & Apologies

  • Alderman C. F. Gillingham, chair of the committee, who kept the kettle on and the minute book open at the same time.
  • The Warden of the Floating Harbour, holding the tide book, which is also, on this date, the committee's calendar.
  • J. Echarri, clerk, deputed by the Pamplona Football Society, second desk by the turnstiles, Gate C, El Sadar — deputed in person, with the folio.
  • Brother æthelstān, prior of the Benedictine house at Christ Church, whose own monastery was founded from the Roman one, and who sat with his sandals off out of respect for the flagstones.

Absent without leave: Alderman Bulkington, last seen passing the turnstile at three o'clock with a tin of sardines under his arm. The sister of the record room, on leave. The quorum was counted at seven of nine, the kettle not being counted, and the meeting was declared to have begun.

Item 1 The quorum, the hour, and the anniversary

The chair called the meeting to order at four o'clock on the 26th of May, being the hour the minute book keeps as the hour of the last cask and, according to the best of the books in the record room, the very hour of the death of Augustine of Canterbury — a Christian monk of the early 6th century who became the first Archbishop of Canterbury in the year 597 and is taken in these minutes to have died on 26 May 604, though one book on the committee's own shelf, the thicker of two, says 605. It was resolved that the committee would take his whole record into this minute book tonight, in order, as the red book keeps its other entries, from the Rome in which he began to the Canterbury in which he ended, and that no item would be carried while the chair's spoon was still raised. It was so carried.

Item 2 The prior of the Roman house

The prior's office was reported. Augustine held the priorship of a Benedictine monastery in Rome in the early 6th century, and in 590, as prior, went in an embassy to Gaul to treat with the Frankish court — the same Frankish house whose princess would, by this committee's account, prove decisive some thirty years later. The report further records the market story, which the committee heard without laughing, and only once: that a certain pope, Gregory, saw a crate of English boys on sale in a Roman market, asked who they were, was told Angli, and is reported to have answered that they ought to be angels. On that report the pope began to plan a mission. In 595 he chose the prior for it — the choice of a man who had, by the committee's count, never seen England, and had not yet, by any book, set his feet in Gaul a second time. The motion to record the choice was carried, and the Warden noted in the tide book that 595 was a low-water year.

Item 3 The fleet, the provisions, and the nearly turned-back matter

The Warden was called to report the shipping. The mission sailed with ten ships and a company of forty monks and two deacons, and in the winter before the crossing the company lay at Boulogne on the sea shore, where, the report says, they feared to land and considered turning back — a motion the committee found familiar, having its own. The townspeople of Boulogne, it is reported, told them their provisions were all sold out and they would otherwise die of hunger; the monks thereupon bought provisions and learned that Kent lay nearest and most open to them, and by lot the prior was named their leader. It was resolved that the memory of the ten ships be kept in the floating harbour, locked off from the tide the way the harbour itself is locked off, where a company of small craft stands drawn up in line beside Brunel's SS Great Britain in the Great Western Dockyard, one per ship, with names on the bows and no sardines aboard. The Bristol entry in the house gazetteer — Bristol, city and ceremonial county on the Avon — carries the harbour's own record, and the committee ordered the two to be cross-referenced in the rate books.

Item 4 Kent, the king, and the queen

It was recorded why Kent was the country chosen. Èthelberht, king of Kent, commanded a major influence over the neighbouring Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, and he was married to Bertha, a Frankish princess and a Christian, who was expected to exert some influence over her husband — and did exert it, in the committee's opinion, to the full extent of her standing. Bertha kept with her a Frankish chaplain, Laurentius, and a small church that had formerly stood in the city of Rouen, which had been brought across and set up near the king's house, and it was in the shadow of that old church that the first baptisms in England by a Roman bishop were said to be ready to happen. The missionaries had come by sea and, before the king's court, had landed at Ebbsfleet by the River Ebble, where the king's people had held the shore and the company had stayed a day or two in their ships before being admitted — a delay the Warden, who keeps the tide book, was pleased to note fell at low water again. The committee entered the whole of it, and entered it again in the margin.

Item 5 The open-air court and the baptism

The report of the audience was read. King Èthelberht, warned that the monks carried the contagion of spiritual vapours, chose to meet them on the heath rather than in his hall, in an open court, and there he sat and listened while the prior spoke to him of the everlasting fire and the everlasting life, as one hears a foreign tongue, in the committee's words. The king's answer, when it came, was that the gifts were fair in human estimation but uncertain in trust — and yet he gave the company a habitation in the city of Canterbury and leave to preach. In the same year, 597, Augustine was consecrated the first Archbishop of Canterbury, the baptism of the king followed with some ten thousand of his people at once, and on the ground the king had granted them a church was built, dedicated to St Peter, with a monastery beside it, and a bishop's seat that has not since gone empty. The committee took no vote on the arithmetic of ten thousand, which is the kind of number a minute book is allowed to keep without checking it, and moved to the next item.

Item 6 The synod and the question of one day

It was reported that the archbishop kept the peace of the sees by a synod, held in Kent in 603, to which came the Irish bishops of the Columban mission from the north, and that at that synod the old questions were aired: the reckoning of Easter, the cutting of the tonsure, the washing of the feet at the office of the gospel. The Irish party were asked why they kept their own course when the whole world kept another, and their answer, carried up to the committee and read here without comment, was a count — that the usage they kept they had held for a matter of sixty years, and would hold it while they lived. The archbishop's own reply, which the red book sets down in the short form, was: we all keep one day. The committee recorded both answers in the same hand, which is the record room's way of saying it will not referee the matter, and closed the item.

Item 7 The dinner interlude and the absence of Alderman Bulkington

At this point the service of the meeting was interrupted by the dinner. A tray passed along the committee table from the side of the record room to the side of the harbour, bearing, in the order the house keeps, soup, a pie, and a large bowl of broccoli dressed in the manner of the floating harbour — in small clusters, and with a grudge. Alderman Bulkington's chair was empty, his sardines still on the plate, and the chair, who had been expecting him with the tide book under his arm, set down his spoon, cried, "Bulkington! where's Bulkington?" and darted over the brocoli, where he was met by the Warden, who had the presence of mind to hold the bowl. A search was at once instituted of the corridors of the Council House, the stair by the strongrooms, and the gate by the Avon, and nothing was found of the alderman but a wet footmark and the sardines. It was resolved, upon the motion of Brother æthelstān, that the clerk deputed from Pamplona — the Pamplona Football Society, Notice No. 402, whose office files its own missing men with equal patience — be asked to keep watch for Alderman Bulkington at the turnstile, as his Society keeps watch at its own, and that the reward for his return be one season ticket, Norte stand, seat 47, which the committee regards as the best seat in the house for a man who will not sit. The notice is to be renewed each first Thursday of the month, and the minutes of this search are to be entered beside the minutes of this meeting, which is where they are. The auctioneers at the Society's own desk, Pamplona & Echarri, Sale No. 402, are asked to extend the same watch at the turnstile of their Lot 38, one professional football league, entire, where the hammer is to fall only when the Society is patient no longer, and where one line of the caterer's ledger has been filed unsold, having no reserve price and, on the auctioneers' count, no appetite either.

Item 8 The death, the pallium, and the order of the book

It was reported, and the room was kept quiet while it was read, that the archbishop died on this day, 26 May, in the year 604 by the best count — or 605 by the thick book — in the monastery he had built at Canterbury, in the sixty-second year of his age. Pope Gregory, who had sent him, who had sent the pallium and the letters, and who had named the mission, had himself died on 12 March of this same year, so that the two of them are entered in the minute book in one year, which the committee found to be a fact and sat with it. The pallium was reported to have come from Rome in the archbishop's last year, a band of wool for the shoulders of the primate, and it was ordered that the band be described in the book as the committee describes its own silver: in words, and in the place where the light is better. It was finally resolved that this minute be read and signed, that a true copy be laid in the sister office at Gate C, and that the red minute book, item no. 597, be closed at the hour it was opened, being the hour of the last cask.
