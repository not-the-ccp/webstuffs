The Dreamer’s Concordance · kept at the desk by the ticket desk · Entry No. 215

A Rugrats Chanukah

Also entered under Chanukah (the onscreen title), The Rugrats Chanukah Special, and Dreaming the Sixty-Sixth.

1. The dream itself

To dream A Rugrats Chanukah is to dream, on a December night, that the children have grown into the holiday and the holiday has not grown into the children. It is the first dream of the fourth season and the sixty-sixth of the whole year of dreams, and it is dated in the Concordance to the night of December 4, 1996, which is when the thing first ran in the United States on the small blue channel, and it runs for twenty-five minutes, or for the length of a candle, which is to say a long time. In the dream the babies — Tommy, Chuckie, and the twins Phil and Lil, with Dil and Susie and Angelica at their edges — find themselves cast as the main characters of their own celebration. They imagine the latkes being made with them in the recipe. They imagine themselves lighting the candles. The parents have gone out, which is the dream's only true and terrible fact, and every interpreter of the Concordance agrees that this fact cannot be disputed, only footnoted.

While the children are the holiday, the adults are the argument. Grandpa Boris and his long-time rival Shlomo, the two men the Concordance has known since Entry No. 91 and who have since appeared in no fewer than eleven other dreams, feud over who will play the lead in the local synagogue’s Chanukah play. The role they fight over is a small one — one minute of speaking, a part with a robe and a line about oil — but the grudge behind it is forty years deep, which is twice the number of candles the dreamer is allowed and still considered careful.

2. Who appears

  • The babies, as principals. If they appear in the dream wearing their own faces at full height, the dreamer is being told that a celebration has been written with them in the cast without their audition. If they appear at their own small size, the dreamer is being told the same thing in the kinder way.
  • Grandpa Boris. The older of the two claimants. He has already, in this very dream, lost the lead once and does not say so, which the Concordance counts as the play's quietest act.
  • Shlomo. The rival. He arrives with his own robe in a bag. The robe is the dream's truest property and may not be removed.
  • The parents, absent. They have gone out and left the children with the candles. Every entry in the Concordance that features an absence of this kind is filed beside South America — Entry No. 214, where the whole continent lies down in one sheet of water and nobody on the ferry is a parent either.
  • The synagogue audience of one. The play has a single seat in the front row. It is held. It is always held.

3. The symbols, kept in the desk

The chanukkiah
Nine flames, eight of them for the nights and one — the shamash — for the lighting of the nights. To dream the shamash unlit is to dream that the helper is on holiday. Use the table below to light the dream night by night, as the Concordance requires of its readers.
The dreidel
Four faces, four letters. Spin it in the dream, or spin the one at the desk below, and read the landing. A dreidel that lands the same letter three times is not an omen; it is a rehearsal, and the play is the point.
The latke
Served in the dream with applesauce and with a warning about the oil. The warning is always the same and is always right.
The synagogue play
One lead, two claimants, one minute of speaking, forty years of history. The cast list is kept in the granary, word for word, exactly as written by the hand that wrote it. The granary also keeps, pinned above his locker of the second time. A copy of the program from the year before, when the lead went to a cousin nobody in the dream remembers.
The candle that will not go out
If one flame holds past the time of its night, the dreamer is the lead. The dreamer should be pleased and slightly afraid in that order.

Night 0 of 7 — the desk is dark; click any candle to light it in order, shamash first.

Spin when the dream stalls. The desk dreidel is brass, four letters, no fifth side.

4. Interpretation

It is a sign of being cast. The dreamer has been written into a celebration without an audition, and the dreamer knows it, and the dreamer is small relative to the part, which is the whole of the matter. It is a sign of the argument that is not an argument: two old men, one robe, a minute of speaking, and the sense in the congregation that the real production has been running since before either of them was born. It is a sign, if the dream runs on a Friday in December and the oil is on, of the parents who have gone out, which the Concordance treats as the one true symbol and files with the others under absences, ferry crossings, and the night of December 4, 1996, when the special first aired and the ticket desk found its door open.

Where many American children’s television programs keep a Christmas special, this one keeps the other holiday in the same drawer, and the drawer is what the dream is really about: a household that holds its lesser-known celebration with exactly the same seriousness as the loud one, and better. The dreamer who dreams this is a household, and the household is being told to light it.

5. Prognosis and the one warning

Good, with conditions. The dream clears by the eighth night provided the dreamer attends the play, watches all of it, and does not leave during the minute that belongs to the man who lost it. The lead, by the record, went to Shlomo in the version the Concordance keeps, and to Boris in the version the granary keeps, and both records are filed. The warning is this: do not dream this dream in February. The Concordance has three entries that say only February, and they are kept locked, and the oil, in February, is on.

6. Filed beside

Entry No. 214, the southern continent lying down in its water, where the ferry crosses on Saturday and the table seats twelve: South America — The Dreamer’s Concordance, Entry No. 214. The ledger of the granary itself, with its lockers and its pinned programs, is entered in the main book at the desk: The Dreamer’s Concordance — the desk and its drawers. A candle lit here is a candle counted there. The desk also keeps, in the same drawer, a page of classifieds for two men who share one name, Timothy Well and Steven Dunn under Well Dunn, and the Concordance files it with the dreams of households that hold a lesser celebration exactly as seriously as the loud one: Well Dunn — The Arena & Rundown, Classifieds, Page 7.
