The Rub

Songs for the rhymes themselves · Side A and Side B · recorded in three nights in the shed behind the bakery, the winter the admiral’s galleon was in the cattle trough

About the record

Here is what the label sells, in the label’s own words: a rhyme is a repetition of similar sounds — the same phonemes, or sounds close enough to pass in the dark — in the final stressed syllables and any following syllables of two or more words. Most often that kind of rhyming, the perfect kind, is a conscious thing, done for its musical or its aesthetic effect, in the final position of lines within poems or songs. Broader out, a rhyme may also refer to other types of similar sounds near the ends of two or more words: the near rhyme, the eye rhyme, the slant rhyme, the rhyme that only works if you are a little tired. And the word has drifted further still; it is now sometimes a shorthand for any brief poem at all, a nursery rhyme, a Balliol rhyme, a thing too small to frame, so it gets sung instead. That last meaning is the one this record keeps. Eight tracks, one per kind of rhyme the shed had on hand, recorded the way the four-line stanza was kept: small, honest, and only as long as it holds.

The reel machine was borrowed from the baker next door, on the strength of his not wanting it in the bread house. The ceiling fan stayed on through tracks A2 and B3, because it is the only clock in the shed that the songs have ever kept time with. The vinegar crock sat on the workbench exactly where the ship’s log left it, and the quatrains in the paper envelope by its shadow sat out of the way, which they should have, because they are the same business as this record in a different shape. If the stanza is a frame built for a problem of shape, the rhyme is the sound the frame makes when two of its planks are brought back to the same sound at the end of a sentence.

Side A

  1. A1The StemPerfect rhyme, announced. Line one does the whole job: it sets the meter and names the rhyme the rest of the song has to keep. Three takes; the third is the one where the voice stops being careful.
  2. A2aab aThe quatrain scheme as a song: first, second and fourth come round to meet, and the third keeps its distance on purpose. The fan is on in this one. The wheel of the table game walked the same 4–3–4–3 while this was being written down.
  3. A3The Third LineA song for the line that refuses to resolve. It holds out, and the stern line has somewhere to land because of it. Recorded standing, because the chair had been moved and I did not feel like moving it back.
  4. A4Bone on BoneThe title track. The only one that was performed rather than sung: the left hand, in a cast, tapping the workbench in the four–three pattern, with a voice over it from the right hand’s notes. See the sleeve note.

Side B

  1. B1Two Sous a SheetFor the printer in Salon-de-Provence who claimed the seventh centurie as his own and sold the quatrains loose at two sous a sheet — the trade the word “rhyme” has always flattered. The quarrel stands in the court record at the Consular Court transcript, 17 June 1563, closed without translation, and the song does not translate either, which I take as the court’s opinion.
  2. B2The Rhyme in ‘Me’The rhyme the shed has banned. It is sung once, deliberately flat, so the ban has a record of itself. No one in the bakery is asked to enjoy it.
  3. B3Nursery, BalliolThe brief poems: the ones the word “rhyme” has come to mean by itself. Short enough to fit in an envelope, which is where the quatrains live, in the crock’s shadow, where the vinegar cannot get to them. The fan hums under it, as it did under the six stanzas that became the quatrain build log.
  4. B4The SternLine four. It lands the poem with a keel and then stops. The record ends the same way, with the needle lifted in time and the last sound of the room left where it fell.

The sleeve note, kept word for word

The back cover carries one line from the loose page, in the builder’s own hand, and it is kept exactly as written. It arrived with the acetates, folded once, stuck to the underside of the matchbox tin that held them, and the hand on it is the tired hand of the left-side cast period, the pen working from the other side:

“I had a loose page of writing, six stanzas long, about the broken bone are rubbed together.”

The grammar was never amended, and neither was the count: the page is six stanzas long, and the bone is the one. The third metacarpal of the left hand, broken the February evening a spar of reed came loose off the galleon’s mainmast fitting and took the hand with it, the same winter the ship went into the cattle trough. Six weeks in a cast. The stanzas were written standing up, right hand, about the cast and the tapping and the way bone, in the old books the baker keeps, was rubbed together and set in a time that the songs only approximate. That is why track A4 is performed rather than sung, and why the record’s title is a sound and not a name: the rub is the sound the cast made against the workbench, and the sound is the rhyme, and the rhyme is all the label has. The loose page itself went back into the log where the other loose page lives — the one about the model and the admiral, in the quatrain build log, which mentions a page of six stanzas and never says what they were about — and the shed now keeps two loose pages in one log, the way the galleon keeps its sails: one to hold, one to remember.

Technical notes

Cut to acetate in a run of 40, numbered, pressed in the next town over on a Tuesday, shipped back in the bread crate the baker uses for everything. One voice, one reel, the fan at A2 and B3, the bench at A4. The filing stock for the title plate came off the brass ruler that started at the 1978 kitchen bench, which is why the label carries two trades in one mark — the field at Bench No. 3, The Seventy-First, and the stanza at the shed, which outgrew its log into this record. For the ship the left hand broke the bone on — the galleon, the trough, the sails, and the long passage the stanza will never make — the whole build stands in the San Juan de Portugal build log, and the ship’s own margin lives on in stanza No. 1 of the quatrain log. Everything else kept on the shelf is at the Webstuffs front page.

If you hold the disc up to the lamp, the groove pattern is the aab-a scheme, four lines of groove around a hole. The admiral, if he is anywhere in this shed, has not complained. I count that as a blessing, the same count the build log does.
