The Harbour House Cards · Card No. 17 · entered at Hythe, Kent, Michaelmas 1989

Two Dishes of Pork

also: the Channel pork · one dish for the English side, one for the French

Two braised dishes, six to eight servings  ·  three hours, mostly waiting  ·  best made the day before

What the water is for

The English Channel is the arm of the Atlantic Ocean that separates Southern England from northern France, and at its northeastern end it links to the southern part of the North Sea through the Strait of Dover. It is the busiest shipping area in the world: tankers and ferries, container ships and fishing smacks work the same water, kept apart by lanes and by nothing else. It is about 560 kilometres (300 nautical miles; 350 statute miles) long, and it varies in width from 240 km (130 nautical miles; 150 statute miles) at its widest to 34 km (18 nautical miles; 21 statute miles) at its narrowest, in the Strait of Dover. It is the smallest of the shallow seas around the continental shelf of Europe, covering some 75,000 square kilometres (22,000 square nautical miles; 29,000 square miles), and everything about it is measured in crossings rather than in miles — the packet from Dover, the steamer from Boulogne, the smack out of Hythe at four in the morning.

The Channel aided the United Kingdom in staying an island when other kingdoms were not so lucky: the Armada stood off in 1588, the invasion barges waited at Boulogne in 1805, and in June 1944 the same water carried the fleet across toward Normandy while the houses along the English shore kept their lights low and their ovens high. My great-aunt Elsie kept this card at the house above the harbour at Hythe, where the harbour wall dates from 1792 and the ferry lights cross the water every twenty minutes through the night. She wrote that she took the recipe from a cook at Boulogne who had taken it from a cook at Dover, and that each of them claimed the other side had invented it, which is probably true, and which is probably the point: the dish is the water itself, split in two and served at the same table.

The two dishes

One dish is cooked the English way — pork shoulder braised with cider, quartered cooking apples, onions, bay and thyme from the Downs, finished with butter and served in a deep dish with potatoes boiled in their skins. The other is cooked the French way — the same shoulder, the same cut, braised with cider and calvados, finished off the heat with thick cream, Dijon mustard and tarragon, and served in an earthenware dish wide enough to hold the juices. Elsie wrote the two dishes side by side on the same card, with a ruler line down the middle, as though the Channel itself could be kept between them on the table. It cannot, of course: the smells meet in the kitchen, and the plate is shared either way.

The only line in the card that no one has ever explained and everyone has always obeyed is written across the middle in brown ink, in the same hand as the shipping forecast pinned beside the stove: “The mercantile is not necessary to make 2 dishes of pork do not quite cover.” The kitchen's reading of it, given on every occasion it has been asked, is twofold. First, that nothing needs ordering from the mercantile's list — not the tinned goods, not the sweet potatoes kept under the tariff, not anything that arrives by packet — because the pork and the cider and the apples are already in the house, and the water does the rest. Second, that two dishes do not quite cover a table for six, so there is always a third thing, or a loaf, or a neighbour invited at the last minute, and nobody goes short. We have never once succeeded in making the sentence mean anything else, and the pork has never once objected.

The clipping beside the card is about Tropical Storm Alberto, No. 1 of the 2006 season, which moved ashore near the Big Bend mercantile in June and then took the northeast track that this water knows — the same track the autumn gales keep to Galway Bay and the Minch, losing their names over the mid-Atlantic and arriving all the same. Lost & Found — Tropical Storm Alberto, No. 1 of 2006.

The packets that call at St Peter Port stand across to the Irish Sea, and they stand across this water too; the traveller's guide keeps its own reckoning of these approaches, sheet by sheet. The British Isles — Traveller's Guide, North Atlantic · Sheet Nº 4.

The card proper

For the dish on the English side

  • 1.4 kg boneless pork shoulder, cut into pieces of six centimetres
  • 300 ml dry cider, the kind kept in a screw-top jar
  • 2 cooking apples, peeled and quartered
  • 2 onions, thinly sliced
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 6 sprigs of thyme
  • 40 g butter, at room temperature
  • Fine salt, and pepper — the king's share, which the card does not explain

For the dish on the French side

  • 1.4 kg boneless pork shoulder, cut into pieces of six centimetres
  • 300 ml dry cider
  • 60 ml calvados or apple brandy
  • 150 ml thick cream
  • 2 tsp Dijon mustard
  • 1 tbsp tarragon leaves, picked
  • 2 onions, thinly sliced
  • 40 g butter
  • Fine salt

The working

  1. Set the oven at 160°C. Put a heavy pan over a medium heat, brown the pork in the butter in batches, and lift it out. Do not crowd the pan; the browning is the dish.
  2. For the English dish: return the pork to the pan with the cider, apples, onions, bay and thyme. Cover and braise for three hours, or until the meat gives under the fork and the apples have gone into the sauce. Finish with the butter, off the heat.
  3. For the French dish: soften the onions in butter, return the pork, add the calvados and the cider, cover, and braise for two and a half hours. Take the pan off the heat, stir in the cream and the mustard, scatter the tarragon over the top at the last moment.
  4. Serve the two dishes side by side — deep dish for the English, earthenware for the French — with potatoes boiled in their skins and salt at the table. The Channel between them is only as wide as the serving dish.

Notes from the margin

Elsie's mother wrote that the cider was to be bought from the farm and no other, and that the thyme was always counted out loud at Hythe — six sprigs for six people, eight for eight. A later hand, my mother's, added that the card was last used on 6 June 1994, the fiftieth anniversary of the crossing, at a table where two dishes of pork did not quite cover, and a neighbour was invited at the last minute, and that it was, in her words, “fair.”
