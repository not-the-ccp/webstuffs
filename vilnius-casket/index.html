The Royal Treasury · the western wing of Rosenborg Castle, Copenhagen

Apology to the
Bishop of Vilnius

The third of December · in the year of our Lord one thousand six hundred and forty-eight

ST seal of the scribalty

Your Grace, and reverend father in the church of Vilnius,

I write as clerk of this treasury, and I set down one thing only, and set it down plainly, which is that I am sorry. On the fourteenth of October of this year two envoys of Your Grace came up from the harbour with a small casket of black wood bound in silver, and it was understood — by word, and by a note of grey stock folded twice — that the casket should rest here no longer than the passage of the ship, and that it should be returned to them at the next ebb. The casket rested here for nine days. The casket went out at the third ebb. For the difference between those two numbers, and for what the difference cost, I beg your patience, and I will tell you exactly how it came to pass, in the manner of this house, which is to say, once, and without counting twice.

The casket was brought in by a man who knew the water, and who asked, very courteously, that it be kept where the dark was thickest. I told him, and I told him in the spirit of the request, that it would be kept in the second case from the east. I did not add — and this is the whole of my apology — that the second case from the east is the case of the Crown of Christian IV, the old crown, made for the coronation at Aalborg in 1596 and worn once more in this same year, at the coronation of our lord the king Frederick, which is the reason the hall was shut to visitors for the nine days, and the reason the casket stood in the dark beside a king’s head and was not touched. The new guard at the gate, the man who likes his papers fresh, did admit the envoys’ courier, but only after an hour, and only with a shrug, and I had not the authority to open a king’s case in front of a shrug. The crown is where it has always been. The casket was where I had said it would be. Both statements are true, and I do not know how to say sorry in a language that holds both of them at once.

I counted the casket once, before it went into the case, in the presence of Your Grace’s agent, and I will not count it again: a phial of oil, two small silver reliquaries, and a letter of the chapter of Vilnius, sealed in wax of Your Grace’s own hand. The wax has cracked in the cold of the case; I do not say that to excuse myself, I say it so that when Your Grace’s men open the casket at Memel they will know what to look for, and that the crack is the frost of a Danish December and not a hand that was not mine. The letter is whole. I read nothing of it. A bishop’s letter is a door, and the clerk of a treasury stands where he stands when the door is shut.

The casket went south with the ferry at the third ebb, because the ferry at the first ebb was full of timber and the ferry at the second had taken the tide wrong out of the harbour mouth, and I am told — the ferry-keeper told my own hand, over the rail, at the moment of departure — that a line had come down the margin of the captain’s log from a Lithuanian port, written by no one the captain could name. The ferry-keeper read it to me, I copied it into this letter word for word, and I have declined to rule on it, as the council of Ripon Abbey declined to rule on its own chaplain’s cards in the minutes of the winter of 788, which I have read and which I keep in the same cabinet as this one. The line stands thus:

The fits themselves are not obtainable, mint vinegar may be put to the bishop.

— the margin of the captain’s log, copied at the ferry, third ebb, and entered here unruled

I do not know what the fits are. I do not know whose mint, or whose vinegar, or why the vinegar should be put to a bishop at all, and I suspect the man who wrote the line in a port I shall not name did not know either, or else knew and meant us to find out in Vilnius, where the word bishop is not a figure of speech. It is entered here in the same breath as the tides, and it is left, with the wax, to Your Grace.

If the envoys passed the winter road from Memel inland by way of Kaunas, and took the ferry again before it froze, they will have had by now the bread of the little harbour town, which is the only apology this house is certain of; they may have asked at the Carpathian table across the water for the tins that are kept like jewels and counted once, never twice — the Carpathian Cellar keeps the same habit, and I have written them of it, though I am told the writing will arrive after the bread. The casket is on the water, or on the ice, or in the hands of a man who knows the water, and I am the last man who will hold it. For the nine days, Your Grace: I am sorry, in the manner of a treasury, which is to say, with the door closed, and the case dark, and the seal set down true.

Set down by the clerk of the treasury at Rosenborg, with the small seal, which is a disc of wax the colour of old iron, and with nothing else.

Note from the keeper’s cabinet

The casket, by the postscript in the ferry-keeper’s hand, reached the chapter at Vilnius on the eleventh of this month, the wax cracked and the letter whole. The land it crossed from the harbour at Memel is the same land that is now called Lithuania: the Republic of Lithuania, sixty-five thousand three hundred square kilometres on the eastern shore of the Baltic, bordered by Latvia to the north, Belarus to the east and south, Poland to the south, and the Russian semi-exclave of Kaliningrad Oblast to the southwest, with a maritime border with Sweden to the west. Its people number now near two and a half and a half million — two and a half million souls and a quarter, counted in the modern manner, and the clerks of this house will not count the number again. The capital is Vilnius, on the hill above the river Vilnia, as the letter said; the other cities of the road, the ones the envoys would have passed or heard, are Kaunas on the river Neman, Klaipėda at the harbour of old Memel, Šiauliai in the north, and Panevėžys in the flat country east of the river. The Lithuanians are a Baltic people, and their tongue, Lithuanian, is the closest living speech to the old language of the country the old crown was made for, which is a fact the treasury keeps in the same case as the crown, and does not count twice.
