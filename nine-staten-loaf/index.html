Nine-Staten Loaf

a yeasted loaf, cut into nine, from the room above the Flanders Street garage · keeps well, argues poorly

Preamble, on the matter of the yield

The recipe begins the way the yard's fog begins — all at once, and without asking. A nuclear weapon is an explosive device that derives its destructive force from nuclear reactions, either nuclear fission (a fission or atomic bomb) or a combination of fission and nuclear fusion reactions (a thermonuclear weapon), producing a nuclear explosion. Both kinds release large quantities of energy from relatively small amounts of matter, which is the whole trick, and which is why the family has never been able to keep the numbers out of the kitchen. Grandma Whitfield would stand at the counter with a teaspoon of saffron in one hand and say that the majority of nuclear weapons have energy yields between 100 and 1,000 kilotons of TNT, and that a loaf of bread, by comparison, is a yield of about nothing, and this is a good thing, and we should cut the loaf and be done with it. Nuclear warfare is widely considered a global catastrophic risk, and the family's standing rule has always been that you do not serve this loaf to a room where that word is being argued about.

As of 2026 there are nine sovereign states known or believed to possess nuclear weapons: the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea and Israel. Nine. That is the number the loaf keeps. Uncle Dale, who ran the darts league from the same kitchen for eleven years and who kept Card 4021, the 21-Gun Biscuits, in the book's back pocket, says he first heard the count from a shipyard handover ledger in 1962 and never let it go; the biscuits got their twenty-one from the salute, and the loaf got its nine from the states, and the two cards are bound together behind the sugar page.

The yard, the fleet, and the ship that powered itself

The preamble has to go to San Francisco, because that is where the family learned to count in kilotons. At Hunters Point Naval Shipyard — six hundred and thirty-eight acres of waterfront behind two graving docks, the yard that in 1907 took Theodore Roosevelt's Great White Fleet into its care — the fleet's white hulls rode at anchor in the dream dictionary's permanent fog, and the yard's lost-and-found desk kept everything the water returned. The family's real connection was smaller and longer: the Enterprise, CVAN-65, the first nuclear-powered aircraft carrier in the world's navies, was homeported at Hunters Point from 1961 to 1992, and her seamen kept their minutes the way seamen do — in a log, in a hand that slants toward the bow. One of those logs, a water-stamped volume of the Enterprise watch bill, turned up in 1974 on a crate of rigging paint at the yard's disposal gate, and it is still on the family's mantel, spined toward the bay, where the fog does not reach. The dream dictionary entry for the yard (Hunters Point — Dream Dictionary, Entry 714) asks, of the quay, whether what you are keeping watertight still floats; the loaf asks the same question of a pantry shelf, at a temperature you can stand in.

The room, with a parlor on the left

Portland is in the recipe for one reason only: the address. The Whitfield family kept the book, for thirty-one years, in the room above the garage of the house at 2234 NW Flanders Street, which the walking tour calls the Bates–Seller House and which this family calls the place where the parlor is on the left. The entry hall runs the depth of the house; a parlor (left) and Dr. James Seller's old consulting room (right), the built-in cabinets still labeled in his faded script — Tinctures, Bandages, Instruments, Ledgers. The loaf's preamble was set down on that hall's stair, one page a month, between 1986 and 1992, the exact years the Enterprise was still turning over at the yard. The self-guided tour of the house and its six streets (Bates–Seller House Walking Tour — Northwest Portland) does not print the loaf, but it prints the hall the loaf was written in, and it prints the camellia Mrs. Seller planted in 1912, which is where Grandma Whitfield took the first proofed dough on each February, the one bloom, the one crimson. A naturalist's notebook keeps the Minutes of the seamen, arrested by a parlor (left) and Dr. Seller's old consulting room: Contemporary Worship Music — Museum Placard . The placard's amplified Sunday sound is a few streets from the consulting room, and the family has always said the loaf rises loudest in a room where the music next door is playing and nobody is arguing about the word risk.

What you need

  • 900 g strong white flour, plus more for the bench
  • 9 eggs, separated, at room temperature — the nine, of course
  • 375 g unsalted butter, softened — the median of the lot, see the dial
  • 250 g caster sugar
  • 7 g fresh yeast, dissolved in 350 ml warm milk
  • 1 teaspoon fine sea salt, no more; the fog supplies the rest
  • 1 teaspoon saffron threads, steeped in a little of the milk
  • Zest of one lemon, one orange, no more
  • 9 paper squares for the cutting lines, or a long knife and a steady arm

The method

  1. Whisk the yolks with the sugar until the mixture is the colour of the fog off Potrero Point at ten in the morning. Beat the whites to soft peaks, not stiff; the loaf is not a bomb, and the family is precise about that distinction.
  2. Beat in the butter a little at a time. Fold in the saffron milk. The smell at this stage is the smell the book is trying to be.
  3. Sift in the flour and salt and mix to a shaggy, workable dough. Knead for ten minutes, until the dough goes slack, then tight, then slack again and stays there. Do not rush the third slackness.
  4. Fold the beaten whites through the dough in three turns, gently. First rise: one hour, until doubled, in a warm spot with the window cracked the width of a graving dock's door.
  5. Punch down. Shape one long loaf, nine parts, and lay the paper squares along its length as cutting lines. It will look like a row of small boats. This is the correct image; do not correct it.
  6. Second rise, forty-five minutes. Bake at 180°C, fan, for 35 to 40 minutes, until the crust is brown at the ends and the bottom sounds hollow when knocked, which is the sound the family has agreed is “watertight.”
  7. Cool on a rack. Cut at the nine lines. Serve one piece a person, to no more than nine people, in no particular order. The piece that is left over is what you keep for the argument next month, if there is one.

Notes, kept in the back of the card

Do not scale the loaf. The nine is not a quantity; it is the number of states, and the number the yard's watch bill runs to on any given shift, and the number of pieces Grandma Whitfield would set out on the camellia table before she went upstairs to check the mantel. If a friend from the darts league wants a share, he gets a biscuit off Card 4021 instead, and that is final. If the fog comes in off the west and the whole street smells of wet cedar and rain, delay the second rise by an hour; the dough will know the difference, and so, on that one evening in 1989, did the kitchen radio, tuned past the news to a frequency the book does not name. The yard kept its ships watertight; the hall keeps its cabinets labeled; the loaf keeps its nine. That is the whole standing order.
