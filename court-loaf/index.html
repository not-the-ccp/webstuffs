Recipe card No. 7 · Cookery book of the house of Henry & Hoover · third transcription, Palo Alto

The Court Loaf

For eight, or for a household that keeps a courtyard. Baked in the open, hot, on the fourth Sunday of any month in which a translation is finished.

Before the Recipe: Who Made It, and Why It Was Kept

It belongs to the house of Henry & Hoover, and the house belonged, in the years that mattered to this card, to Lou Henry Hoover — née Henry, born in San Francisco on 29 March 1874, in the fourth-floor room over the chandler on Pine Street, where the kettle hung over a coal range and the bread went up at dusk. She was raised in California in its young republic of mines and railroads, the state still counting the gold it had swallowed, and she kept of it two habits for life: the miner’s respect for anything that has to be broken open before it can be used, and the West’s habit of doing the second thing before the first is finished. From the kitchen of that room she carried the court loaf — the white, long-fermented loaf the family called so because it was always finished in the open yard, against the south wall, in a clay oven her father had built with the men from the Presidio. She was a geologist, and she said the loaf was one of the few rocks she had ever helped form.

The preamble owes her the account of the courtyards. In 1897 she met Herbert Hoover, a mining student at Stanford, in the laboratory where he was taught to read a rock by the way it broke; they were married on 26 October 1902, and the honeymoon went, by her choice, to China. The house the years gave them was the office of the Shantung Railway in Peking, 1909 to 1912, where Herbert took the engineer’s post and Lou took the house, the children — Herbert Jr., born 1903, and Lou, born 1905 — and the courtyard itself, a rectangle of paving ringed by the west wing, the north hall, and a wall of jujube trees. In that courtyard she learned to speak Mandarin the way one learns any working trade: by doing the job, for six years, without a contract. She was a polyglot of the household, at ease in Mandarin and well-versed in Latin, and she read her children the court loaf’s recipe in English, the railway’s letters in Mandarin, and the news in Latin, because the news, in those years, was the only thing worth translating twice.

The rest of the card’s account is the country’s account. She led the Girl Scouts of the USA from 1922 to 1925 and again from 1935 to 1937, and in both terms the troop kitchens of the country were fed, in part, from this card — the recipe went out with the handkerchief and the uniform, which is to say it went out with the argument she spent her whole life making, for women’s rights and women’s independence, the independent woman being the one who can feed herself and still translate a mountain. From 1929 to 1933 she was First Lady of the United States at the White House, the wife of President Herbert Hoover, and the house at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue kept the loaf in its cellars as other houses keep it in courtyards — the rule travels unchanged, only the paving is replaced by marble. She died on 7 January 1944, and the card came down with the house papers in a tin that also held the railway letters and a bag of jujube seeds from Peking that never once came up.

What the card was kept for, though, is the last line. In the final years she took on the translation of De re metallica, the metallurgy of Georgius Agricola of 1556, a Latin book about breaking the mountain open, and she was its primary translator into English. The card is pinned to a page of that translation, between the book and the tin, and in the back, in a later and smaller hand than the recipe’s, there is one mangled line carried over from the Latin working draft — the subject of it lost the way subjects were lost in that season of the work, the sentence arriving with the head of one page married to the body of another. The house keeps the line as it arrived, uncorrected, because a misfile is a record of its own, and this card’s rule is the rule the ledger of firsts keeps on the other side of the house: confirm the record before the record confirms you.

The Court Loaf, as Written on the Card

  • Flour, white, 4 pounds, weighed on the railway’s own scale if the scale still reads
  • Water, lukewarm, 1½ pints, and no more — the card says no more, and it means the courtyard
  • Yeast, a piece of the mother, no larger than a walnut, from the tin, not from the shop
  • Salt, 2 fingers’ breadth, the miner’s portion
  • Sugar, 1 tablespoon, or a single date from Peking, split
  • Oil, a little, for the hands and for the clay
  • Flour for the paving, as much as the courtyard asks for
  1. Begin at dusk. Put the yeast into the lukewarm water and set it where the kitchen’s warmth reaches it, while you weigh the flour. The recipe began in a fourth-floor room in San Francisco, and it has always begun at the hour the chandler lit the street.
  2. Mix the flour in to the yeast water, a third at a time, and work it until the dough lies smooth on the board. Add the salt and the sugar or the date. It should take no longer than the time it takes to say the whole preamble to somebody who is not going to bake it.
  3. Let the dough rise, covered, until it has doubled — the long hour. In Peking the family kept its hour by the railway bell from the Shantung line, and the bell still counts for those who have it; at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue it counted by the clock in the colonnade, which nobody asked to be stopped.
  4. Turn the dough out onto the floured paving of the courtyard, against the south wall, and shape it long, the length of a good arm and a little. Set it in the clay oven, covered with the clay lid, and let it ride the whole fire down.
  5. When the loaf is done it should sound hollow where you tap it with a knuckle — a sound, the card notes, like a mine that is about to give. Slice it while it is still warm, and do not ask the table about 1556, which is the year the book was written and the year it has never once claimed to be finished.

Note in the Back of the Card

In a later and smaller hand, below the fold, carried over from the Latin working draft of De re metallica and kept exactly as it arrived:

All his hardy sobriety and fortitude is the courtyard.

Read it properly, and the line makes its own sense. In the working draft his is the mountain — mons, which is masculine in Latin, and the whole book is about him — and the courtyard is where the ore lies out to dry, where the mountain’s temper can be read before it goes into the fire. The line is a mangle, as all the lines of that season were: the clause has lost its verbs, sobriety and fortitude is a character no ore has ever displayed, and nothing in the sentence, as written, is cooked. But read it as an entry, the way the house reads it, and it is a whole instruction: all his hardy sobriety and fortitude is the courtyard — everything the mountain is, temper and all, shows in the open place where the work is done, and so the loaf is baked in the open, where the paving can be read, and the eldest at table has no voice in it. The line is kept, not corrected, on the standing order that a misfile is a record of its own.
