Erinna’s Pot

Lamb stewed with quince and wild thyme, in honey, for seven — the pot for the night of the Distaff

Reason before method. In this house a recipe is not written down without its reason, and the reason for this pot is a woman who lived on this island before any of us had a name for the island. Her name was Erinna, an ancient Greek poet, and the family keeps her the way other families keep a lamp: not on a shelf, in the middle of the work. She is generally thought to have lived on Telos in the first half of the fourth century BC — though some ancient traditions, which Nana Eleni preferred on the strength of the sea, have her a contemporary of Sappho, which puts her on the island a hundred years earlier, in the days when, Nana allowed, the girls could still run the harbour marble at the water line.

What we actually know Nana knew, and wrote in the margin in 1952, is this: Erinna’s childhood friend, a girl of the same street named Baucis, died shortly after her marriage, and Erinna took it the way a distaff takes wool — drawing it out and out until it would not stop. Her long poem The Distaff, a lament of 300 hexameter lines for Baucis, was nearly lost whole; a large fragment of it was recovered in 1928 at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt, in a papyrus bundle, and the first reprint Nana could buy — a 1931 paper edition, priced in drachmas — lived behind the stove for thirty-seven years. The same shelf holds the three epigrams ascribed to Erinna, preserved in the Greek Anthology, and the note from the Suda that her verses were regarded as equal to those of Homer, which Nana read once aloud, at the table, and then went and made this pot. Biographical details about Erinna are uncertain; the ledger keeps them uncertain too.

The meal is fixed by the family: on the evening that falls nearest the recovery of the fragment — the late September night — the pot goes on at three, the quince goes in whole, and no one speaks of who is absent until the honey goes in, which is the line in the recipe where, in Nana’s reading, the 300 lines begin. Seven places are set, because a distaff has seven spokes and because Baucis was eight and a half when she went to the other island and has stopped at that. The one line the ledger could never parse is kept below in full, word for word, exactly as Nana wrote it in the margin beside the pot, with a small circle round it and nothing else:

At the bar kept the ground staff will tell you this is said to have kept the garden's readings from 1963 to 1965 — he does not clean the drawer.

The standing ruling of the kitchen is that the line governs the garden’s ledger drawer on the low shelf by the door: the drawer keeps whatever readings the garden has made, it is not to be cleaned, and no hand of the family is to be asked what the line means, on pain of doing the washing for a year. Rhea, who corrected the page, has never been asked, and the drawer has never been cleaned, and the family is, on this, in order.

For the pot

  • 1½ kg shoulder of lamb, cut into fist-sized pieces, the fat trimmed but not taken
  • 3 quinces, unpeeled, each cut into 6 wedges — they go in whole first and are broken, not chopped
  • 40 g honey, the dark thyme honey, in one piece, not stirred in
  • 40 g dried wild thyme, picked by the hand, every stem weighed against the light
  • 600 ml water, or red wine if the year was good
  • 4 cloves garlic, bruised flat on the board, not sliced
  • 1 stick cinnamon, broken in two, each half into a different half of the pot
  • 1 bay leaf, the old one kept for this, not a new one
  • Sea salt; olive oil, enough to hear the lamb when it goes in

Method

  1. Put the pot on at three. The pot is the black one, the one with the handle cracked in 1962; it has never been replaced, and the crack is not a defect, it is the pot’s way of keeping time.
  2. Brown the lamb in the olive oil, in two batches, without stirring until the first side will not lift, and set the batches apart in order.
  3. Lay the quince wedges whole over the lamb, seam up, the way the family says they keep their faces. Add the water, the garlic, both halves of the cinnamon, the bay.
  4. Cover, bring to the boil, and set the lid on its side so the steam has the gap Nana insisted on: “a pot that cannot breathe cannot grieve.”
  5. Simmer for two and a half hours. Turn the quince once, at the first hour, and not again. Count the first hour in lines if you like; there are 300 of them, and the hour is long enough for all of them.
  6. Pick the dried thyme over the pot as the last hour runs; every stem weighed against the light, every stem dropped where it falls.
  7. At the end, set the honey in one piece in the middle of the pot, where it sits like a seal, and do not stir it. This is the line where no one speaks of who is absent until it has begun, of its own accord, to go in.

Notes kept in the margin

The pot serves seven at the table and no more, and the dish is served in the black bowls with the chipped rims, not the good ones; the good ones are for the years that were good. If a guest arrives who has never been told the name Baucis, tell the name, and go on serving; the question is not asked and the answer is not withheld. The drawer by the door keeps its readings and is not cleaned. The crack in the handle is checked every year in late September and reported, in the ledger, still sound.

Filed with

This page is kept in the same ledger drawer as the Fowlers’ card for stewed quail for seven, page 76, which the kitchen has long held to be the same meal wearing a different hat, and beside the council’s Minutes No. 215, the matter of the far bank, which the ledger received as weather of the same class — a lament that keeps, mid-crossing, with the far bank already set. The whole shelf, the ledger, the drawer, and the one line no hand has ever parsed, are kept from the front page of the miscellany, where the reader is referred and discharged.
