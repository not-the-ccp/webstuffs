Before you set out

The Book of Amos is the third of the Twelve Minor Prophets in the Christian Old Testament and the Jewish Tanakh, and the second in the Greek Septuagint — which is why the desk has always told its parties to start in Greek and let the Hebrew come second, the way the major read everything, whole, in order, without flipping. The book has nine chapters, and this brochure keeps them as nine days. According to the Bible, Amos was an older contemporary of Hosea and Isaiah, active c. 750 BC during the reign of Jeroboam II (788–747 BC) of Samaria, the northern capital, while Uzziah was King of Judah. He is said to have lived in the kingdom of Judah — a shepherd out at Tekoa, a gatherer of sycamores — but preached in the northern Kingdom of Israel, where themes of social justice, God’s omnipotence, and divine judgment became staples of prophecy. The Sunday Greek class reads him aloud the way Paul’s communities were read, as the letter of Helen Vane, filed under No. 48 explains: somebody standing, somebody sitting, the town — or the bench — listening. The man the bench is listening for is set down, in the long entry that ends still in Greek, at Entry No. 48, the biographical one.

Travel advisory. In recent years, scholars have grown more skeptical of the Book of Amos’s presentation of Amos’s biography, and the desk sets this down in its ordinary way: the gate is as the gate is. The text, the cities, the count of nine, and the reign of Jeroboam II are as firm as the bench under the drip, and a tourist who sticks to them will not be lost. As for the shepherd himself, the desk recommends the cave at Tekoa, the sycamore stand, and a small silence in the second chair, which the club has kept for exactly such weather.

The nine days, in order

Press the page-turn at the foot of the column and the desk will walk the days with you, in order, one at a time. It will not turn backwards. That is not a defect; that is the whole of the rule.

  1. Day 1

    Tekoa, gate of Judah

    Ten miles south of Jerusalem, a sheep town with a gate and a stand of sycamores. Amos is “a shepherd of Tekoa, and a gatherer of sycamores” (7:14), and the desk's first morning is spent at the gate where the flock still comes in and the word — “Hear these words which I prophesy against you, O house of Israel” (1:2) — is first read out loud, the way it was meant to be.

  2. Day 2

    Two visions in the valley

    A swarm of locusts raised against the green; a fire kindled by a searcher of the waters that licks the great deep (ch. 1). The prophet pleads, Alas, Lord God; for how will this people live? and the Lord relents, the way a table relents when the best chair is brought round again. Luggage: one gourd, one sycamore fig.

  3. Day 3

    Bethel, the high place

    The great sanctuary of the north, its altar and its grove, the court of the firstborn, the chamber of the king (3:14–15). Here the desk sets the tour's hardest sentence on the wall in the major's hand, in red pencil: “For I hate, I despise your feasts, and I will take no delight in your solemn assemblies” (5:21). The party dines at the gate instead. It is, they report, better.

  4. Day 4

    Samaria under Jeroboam II

    The capital of the northern kingdom, at the height of its long peace, 788–747 BC. “Hear this word, O ye cows of Bashan, which are in Mount Samaria” (1:5). The king's house, the merchants with their ephahs of cheating, the rich man who lies on a bed of ivory and stretches himself on his couch (6:4–7). The desk notes that the prosperity of a city is the very thing the itinerary is coming to look at.

  5. Day 5

    South into Judah, past Uzziah

    Jerusalem, where Uzziah sits on the southern throne while the north holds its breath. The priest Amaziah of Bethel has the message carried: “Go, flee away into the land of Judah” (7:12). Amos answers from the threshold, unhurried, in the tone the club's officers called Greek: “I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdsman, and a gatherer of sycamores” (7:14–15).

  6. Day 6

    The word at the house

    The confrontation at the sanctuary, kept as a full day because the bench takes its time with it. “Hear this word, O ye that make the plowshares for ornament” (ch. 6); the mocking song at the temple, “Let the mountains melt away, and the hills be removed” (6:8); and the verdict on the house: Behold, the Lord will raise up against you an adversary (6:11).

  7. Day 7

    The plumb line, and the late-summer fruit

    On the wall of the sanctuary the prophet sees the Lord standing with a plumb line in His hand, and the measure explained: “I have looked upon the upright line; they are not upright” (7:8). The day's walk ends in a market where the green fruit is in the basket in the late summer (8:1), and the clerk takes the price in the ledger and does not parse it.

  8. Day 8

    The false word, and the silence before

    The prophets of the cities of the sanctuary who prophesy for money (ch. 5 and 6); the people who say, Neither adversity nor prosperity shall overtake us (6:14). The desk sets this day's table with the least food of the tour, because the next chapter wants the room empty. The second chair is brought out under the drip at noon, and nobody fills it. That is not a defect.

  9. Day 9

    The day of the Lord, and the going home

    “Behold, the day of the Lord cometh, that shall be darkiness, and no brightness” (5:18); “The sun shall be darkened at noon, and the moon shall not give her light” (8:9); and then the mercy that ends the tour and the book: “Seek me, and ye shall live” (5:4). The party walks home the way the division walked off the ridge in December: in order, without arguing about which side of it they had been on. The last line is read to the car, or to the chair, and the hush between it and the road is Track 8, do not flip.

Of the ledger's nine entries, the desk has kept one kitchen line word for word, and does not attempt to parse it, the way the division keeps the engineer's instructions: “Roll and skewer the skin is valuable as an accompaniment to various incomes, commencing with-- About £1,000 a year--A cook, upper housemaid, nursemaid, under housemaid, and nursemaid.” The desk understands it to concern the feast of Day 3; the feast was despised; the line, for some reason, was kept.

Good to know

  • The two Bibles. In the Hebrew order Amos is third of the Twelve; in the Greek, second. The shelf by the bench keeps both, and the party will be given the Greek first, in the major's red pencil, because that is where the shelf says a reader should stand.
  • The company. Amos moves in the same years as Hosea and Isaiah, and a well-turned party will hear all three names read off the bench in one breath, the way the Sunday class does, without being asked which is which.
  • The count. Nine chapters, nine days. The desk will not sell a half-fare, and it will not turn a page forward for anyone who has not stood in the day before it. The count has held since before the club had a name.
  • The weather. C. 750 BC is a dry season out at Tekoa and a fair one at the gate. The sun of Chapter 8 is the only darkness on the tour, and it is scheduled, announced, and read out loud in advance, as all darkness on the tour is.
  • The chair. The second chair by the boathouse bench is reserved for the whole return journey. It is set with a place, it is kept in Greek, and the letter under No. 48 has already told you what it does when the reading is finished. The entry that counts the chairs will not tell you again; that is the entry's custom.

The desk is at the gate of Tekoa. The flock comes in. Nobody is told to turn backwards; the page will not let them.
