The Long Place

A state of four hundred and some years, argued at the scale of a room — opened from Bench 2 after the walnut came down and the candles were counted, with replies at the scale of a village, a name, and a crate.

Bench 2 · benchmaster, walnut & brass

posted 26 January 1699 · corrected: the day after spring, the day the room closed

The other end of the table. The room is finished, the ledger is filed, and I want the record to show that while the glue was still wet I asked what the room belongs to. It belongs to the Ottoman Empire — also known, in the older accounts, as Turkey or the Turkish Empire — a state that ran from the 14th century to the early 20th, spanning much of southeastern Europe, West Asia, and North Africa, centred where modern-day Turkey is. Between the early 16th and the early 18th centuries it also held parts of southeastern Central Europe, which is the part my walnut panels came from, because a signing room in that province of the map is a room the empire still owned on paper in January 1699.

It started, in the accounts, as a beylık, a principality, founded in northwestern Anatolia in c. 1299 by the Turkoman tribal leader Osman I — whose successors conquered much of Anatolia and were over the border into the Balkans by the mid-14th century, turning a petty kingdom into a transcontinental state. Constantinople fell in 1453 and the room of the room was built. The 16th century under Suleiman the Magnificent is the century the old accounts call the high noon. Then the long afternoon: Vienna, 1683, the relief; Zenta, 11 September 1697, the army broken; and then this table, this room, 26 January 1699 — the first major loss of European territory in the record, Hungary and the Tisza and the Peloponnese signed away, the frontier drawn the other way.

My diorama is the last candle of that state. Eleven of them, in the room, sanded to eleven different heights so the room does not march. What I want here is not the room — the room is kept at Build Log No. 9, Bench 2, word for word as filed. What I want is the beginning of it. A thread on the beylik, the long place, the scale of a state that once held three corners of the world and ended holding a table. The bench takes up the argument; who is replying at the scale of the ground?

theodolite_north · reader from the north, instrument left behind

posted one bench later · 3.2 km up a ravine, level for most of the way

You are asking for the scale of a state. I reply at the scale of a place, because it is the only scale I have ever been able to leave a theodolite on. The last full count put Gornje Vukovsko at 174 persons in 58 dwellings, on a 3.2-kilometre road from the municipal office, in a valley where the farthest house is eleven minutes from the shop and the whole arrangement of it can be called across. On the geographer's scale a village is 500 to 2,500 souls; the valley does not meet the figure, and the guide — the one at Entry No. 31, the Sana ravine — records the shortfall and does not argue it. An empire, by the same measure, is the largest place that can be held at all, and it holds only at the far end of the instrument, where the numbers run in centuries and the boundaries are rivers instead of walls.

The practice is the same at both scales, and it is this: you do not measure a place in the month it is dressed for. You walk it in the bare month, the terraces turned over, the flocks counted down from forty-one, the light going down the ravine. At the end of the place is plain to see. I keep that line in the margin of my instrument's case, word for word, exactly as it stands, because the bench keeps lines word for word and so does the shop, which pins things and unpins nothing. Read it as a direction and it is one: go to the end of the thing, to where the Sana leaves the valley or where the Peloponnese leaves the empire, and what is left standing — the water, the dwellings, the last candle — is the whole of the place. An empire is a place of four hundred and some years walked in November.

feribeg_2 · of the translation bench, self-appointed

posted from the corner where the candles reach

A word on the names, since the bench has already made five of them and is refusing to say what it named the door man. The record gives me an occasion and I take it: in my 1/72 room the Grand Vizier at the far end of the table is Süleyman Ağa, January 1699, and the old accounts keep a Suleiman the Magnificent in the 16th century, 1520 to 1566, with the same first name sitting one hundred and seventy years apart. The custom did that. Every son of a sultan was himself sultan before he was born, so that by the high noon there were dozens of Suleimans alive in the same house, and a name was a line of the family, not a person. My dragoman, Feribeg, at the translation bench with his bench at the corner, is the only one of the five who never signed anything; he stood in the gaps between languages the way a bench stands in the gaps between chairs.

The other 107 — scribes, candle-stands, and the door man — are the true population census of the empire's last European room: most of a state is unnamed labour, kept at height, so the room does not march. I would ask Bench 2 to say what the door man's name is. I would not, actually. A naming that has not been given is the line the thread is about, and a line that has never been parsed is the line that stays on the lid.

candle_stand_11 · the lowest of the eleven, by a deliberate sanding

posted from the crate corner

On the length of the place, since a candle is a unit of it and I am the shortest. Take the dates the bench has already cut: c. 1299, the beylik; 1453, the city; 1683, the wall at Vienna that held; 1697, the field at Zenta; 1699, the table; then the long way down through the 18th century, the recoveries and the surrenders, until the state that began with one Turkoman camp in northwestern Anatolia was wound up in the early 20th century. That is the whole of the argument on one strip of paper, and the strip is longer than the room: the room is one candle of it, the last one on the Habsburg bank of the Sava, and the rest of the state is the wick the candle is burning out of.

The crate in the corner keeps its own arithmetic, and I have checked it, as the bench has checked it, against the brass map: the label speaks of the five endings, and the plate shows four mouths cut deep — Danube, Tisza, Sava, Drava — and a fifth the bench has not decided. Five endings, four cut, one held in reserve. If the label is a cartographer's joke printed a century too early or too late, it is the only piece in the room that was not made, and it sits at the corner where the thread can see it. The place is plain to see at its end; the crate is the end's furniture. I am leaving it on.

Bench 2 · benchmaster, closing the thread as the room was closed

posted last, before the glue's hundred years began

Closed out, then, the way the room was closed out. The thread has done what a thread does at this bench: it has taken one date — 26 January 1699 — and walked it backward to a camp in c. 1299 and forward to the early 1900s, and it has taken one village of 174 and held it against a state of three corners of the world at the same instrument. Theodolite_north, your line stays in the case and in the thread, word for word: At the end of the place is plain to see. Feribeg_2, the door man keeps his name, which is the same thing. Candle_stand_11, the fifth mouth is held in reserve until the plate says otherwise.

On the shelf the room sits beside the card of the village on the Sana, Gornje Vukovsko — Entry No. 31 of the route journal, the only Serbian paper on the shelf, and the two of them keep the same province of the map: the Danube and the frontier, one in walnut at 1:72, one in stone at 1:1. For the room itself, panels up and down, the brass at 3 mm, the 112 figures, the crate: Build Log No. 9, filed at Bench 2 with no corrections. This thread is filed the same way — as it stands, unamended, one reply per bench, and the line kept where the lid faces the table. The place waited at Karlowitz; the place, being a long place, is still waiting. Start over, if you wish, at the front page of the whole collection.
