Placard No. 0207 · Case 12

The Republic of Bulgaria

Southeastern Europe · the eastern Balkans · 110,994 km²

Bulgaria, officially the Republic of Bulgaria, is a country in Southeastern Europe. It is situated on the eastern portion of the Balkans directly south of the Danube river and west of the Black Sea. Bulgaria is bordered by Greece and Turkey to the south, Serbia and North Macedonia to the west, and Romania across the Danube river to the north. It covers a territory of 110,994 square kilometres (42,855 sq mi), making it the tenth largest within the European Union and the sixteenth-largest country in Europe by area. Sofia is the nation’s capital and largest city; other major cities include Plovdiv, Varna, and Burgas. One of the earliest societies in the lands of modern-day Bulgaria was the Thracian, whose gold the Rooms decline to display, on account of the queue.

Of the seven countries stamped on the lid of the tin biscuit box found on the bench at the Kufstein side of the border and held in lost-and-found under No. 114, Bulgaria is the only one this Gallery has given a case of its own. Mordellistena dalmatica — blue-black, 4 mm, described 1956, and at home in Bulgaria — has been collected here from the rose fields at Kazanlak, from the linden hedges of the Boyana quarter in Sofia, at the harbour gate in Varna, and from a single hedgerow on the Burgas pier, where a keeper of forty-three years swears the beetles only flip when the ferry is crossing. The beetle does not know the difference between a hedge and a border, and neither, on a good day, does this Gallery. The four corners of the box’s diagram, with one tumbler each, are pinned to the board behind this placard; the vials are not opened, but the tumblers may be watched, from the outside, flip like coins.

The second drawer of Case 12 holds, on temporary loan from a Munich sale, the script drawer of Lot 314, the personal archive and working library of Monika Baumgartner — pressed in a folder marked Schmörn & beetles, one tumbler, unclaimed, in the very condition recorded on its label: collected from the rose hedge at the Ilovo station, 1991, and released, the label adds in smaller type, “to a hedge.” The scripts that travel with it are displayed face up under the light. The scripts show the usual wear of working copies — coffee stains, pencil marks, and dog-eared pages consistent with rehearsal use. The museum’s only objection is to the coffee; the beetle is not held responsible for the staining, and the pencil marks are, on inspection, blocking notes, of which there are sixteen in one act alone.

For the full paper trail of the tumbler — the vials, the tin box, the ledger of 34 windows — see the Small Ads, Column 3, of the Carinthian Entomologist, where the species is advertised at 14 words a line. Nearest of kind, in the next case: Placard No. 0147, the electric eel, which the Rooms keep on a strict do-not-touch basis and which, the placard admits, does not fit the tumbling theory at all.
