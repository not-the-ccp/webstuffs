Correspondence to The Gif, Newsletter of the Heorot Society

On the Folklore of This Parish

a letter to the editor · received at the fifth meeting of the winter · read aloud standing, per the rule

ASHLEY CAMBS. FIFTH MEETING · WINTER
749 souls, taken as they stand

To the Editor of The Gif, Newsletter No. 9, back room of the hall, where the door is still the wrong colour —

I write to you, as your readers know me in the book, as the man in the second row: the one who bought the village at the hammer for £861,000 in a voice the clerk described as Newmarket, and who received, that same afternoon, the keys to the hall, the field book, the census figure, and the fog. You will have read of it in the condition report of Lot 217, Fenn & Marlowe’s 110th Autumn Sale, Cat. 44 — 2,250 acres, the two ancient parishes of Ashley and Silverley, 749 persons to be taken as they stand. I have since had three weeks to look about the place I own, and I report to the minute book that what I bought was never really the ground. The ground was only what the folklore happens to be standing on. Folklore, for the record the Society keeps so carefully: the body of expressive culture shared by a particular group of people — its oral traditions, the tales and legends and proverbs and poems and jokes it hands from voice to voice; its material culture, the ways its buildings are put up and left; and its customary lore, the beliefs and the rites, the weddings, the festivals, the dances, the initiations — each one of these, singly or in combination, a folklore artifact, a traditional cultural expression, and just as essential as the form of it, the transmission of it. Ashley, it turns out, has all three on hand, and I have begun a ledger.

Of the material culture I can write fastest, because it is all still standing and mostly still leaning. The parish hall is a building in the traditional style of this part of the open ground, that is, of timber and weather, with a door in the front of it whose colour the two ancient parishes have never once been able to settle, a disagreement the cataloguer entered, for fairness, before the hammer fell. I have been told, on the lane, that the door is not a defect to be painted out; the door is a working artifact, the only thing in the village still doing its work, and it does its work best when nobody agrees on it. The churchyard fence leans in over the boundary and must be allowed to. The lane from Silverley to the hall is straight enough to measure a man against, and has been used, I am told, for that purpose, at least once per century, with the man standing in for the village and the lane standing in for the truth. The kettle in the back room is, per your own newsletter, the seventh member, and I have stopped objecting.

Of the oral tradition I am the most humbled. I own, it appears, a reading of Beowulf at one folio a month, in the old English, delivered standing, with objections in the room, now in its ninth newsletter and its 1,412th line and beyond — the whole of the Nowell Codex’s 3,182 alliterative lines to come through the back room, with the glosses, without apology, the anonymous poet held to the seventh chair as the other man in the room. It is the oldest poem in the parish, and the Society is, I now understand, the parish’s oldest habit: an oral tradition that has, this winter, the good sense to be a shared one, in the full folklore meaning of the word, a tale and legend and poem carried by six voices and one kettle from the fire to the fire.

The customary lore is what keeps me awake. The vendor’s only condition of sale — that the village keep its fire — is a folk belief of the first rank, a superstition with a deed behind it, and I keep the fire because the alternative is to be a stranger in my own acres. The farce played in the hall the night of the sale, by the company which styles itself the Gemenasian, was a festival and an initiation in one, and I was in the room for it, which is to say I was initiated whether I agreed or not, in the manner of the village. The harvest comes on its own time, the ward polls on its own time, and the fog — the fog answers to no auctioneer and has not, in three weeks, once asked my permission to enter the village. I have begun, on the night of a full moon, to walk the road the Academy of Country Routes surveyed in 1911, from the postbox to the hall and east to the Cheveley boundary, because a road that names every field it passes is a rite of passage that runs itself.

And now the defect. In the condition report the clerk’s hand sets down the line which I must read to you, word for word, exactly as it stands in the book, for I am a buyer and the book is the book: “One defect, entered for fairness: the two ancient parishes have never agreed on the manners of such small lots, the house has done in similar lots.” I confess I read it at the table as a defect and only later as a blessing. Read it the way the house reads it — Fenn & Marlowe, who have done similar lots, in similar voices, for one hundred and ten autumns — and it means this: the parishes’ manners, the customs by which such small parcels are kept and sold and argued over, have never once agreed, and the house has conducted its business on that very disagreement, in every similar lot, without a single fence moved. The defect is the agreement. The door is the contract. A folklore artifact that still disagrees, after several centuries, is not broken; it is in use, and it is, I would submit to the minute book, the safest kind of roof in the north.

One last line for the ledger. The Cambridgeshire Correspondents’ Club, in the classifieds of their supplement, The Iron Age, carry a recipe line no member has yet been able to cook, and I say to you: let them not be in a hurry. A proverb no one can parse is a proverb the village has decided to keep for later. That is transmission in its truest form — the artifact held, in trust, at the postbox, with the tea account, until the next reader comes up the lane and stands in the fire.

I remain, as the village requires of me, unsold. The fire is lit, the door is the wrong colour, the kettle is on, and I have written to Fenn & Marlowe to ask, for their next catalogue, that Lot 218 be held back for so long as it takes the seventh chair to be introduced.

Midwinter is on us now, and I have begun a second ledger beside this one — the naturalist's Christmas notes kept at the postbox: the First Sunday of Advent, the twelve days down to Twelfth Night, and the parcel that came by the brightness of the Nowell Codex, entered day by day at Ashley Midwinter — Field Notes, which the hall's kettle has already claimed as its eighth matter. In the same drawer I keep one document from outside the parish that belongs to the ledger’s idea of permanence: the notice that the Neda Agha-Soltan Graduate Scholarship at Queen’s College, Oxford, was made permanent on 3 October 2010, to the last pound, and its account of that Tuesday is kept at 3 October 2010 — Diary, the front garden. Two parcels, the village and the scholarship, both kept by being agreed into existence, and both, I think the kettle would say, in use.

The Buyer, in the Second Row of Lot 217, Ashley and Silverley · four miles east of Newmarket · by the postbox, where the fog keeps the door

The letter is unstamped. It may be received, in the manner of the minute book, by pressing the seal.
