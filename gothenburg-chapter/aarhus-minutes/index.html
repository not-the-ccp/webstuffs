The Town of Aarhus · the Eastern Shore of Jutland · the Kattegat

Minutes of a Meeting of the
Aarhus Harbour and Ropewalk Committee

Held in the upper room of St. Clement’s, above the bishop’s door, on Tuesday the fourteenth of February, 1782, being a little past two o’clock in the afternoon, the room warmed by the choir’s fire and the paper laid out on the long table between the two windows.

Present

The Right Reverend the Bishop of Aarhus, in his own person; the Hon. Jens Møller, harbourmaster; one Rasmus Strand, ropewalker of the outer walk; one Agda Krog, clerk of the cathedral school, keeping the minutes; and Peder Lind, the town secretary, in the chair. The clerk reports the town at twelve thousand six hundred souls, the port at full tide, and the quorum complete; whereupon the chair put the business before the Committee in the order below.

I. Of the town and the river mouth

It was first read into the record, for the benefit of any future clerk, that Aarhus lies on the eastern shore of Jutland, on the Kattegat water, at the mouth of the Aarhus River where the Gudenå comes down out of the moors, some one hundred and eighty-seven kilometres — reckoned in the old manner, some one hundred and sixteen miles — northwest of Copenhagen, and that the town, though young in its brick and its church, is not young in its founding: the settlement goes back to the late eighth century, when it was laid down as a harbour town at the river’s mouth and presently grew into a trading place of note; the first Christian church rose here about the year nine hundred, and in the Viking age afterwards the town took on defences of rampart and ditch, which the Bishop confirmed had been seen with his own eyes from the cathedral’s south stair, in the direction of the old line. The bishopric, said the chair, grew steadily stronger and more prosperous in its day, and what it then built the town still dwells in. No alteration was proposed, and the record was so entered.

II. Of the card from the south, and of the playhouse

The Committee was informed that a gentleman in the harbour, come up the Kattegat from the Swedish side, had been asking after the Comediehuset in Gothenburg and its late director, von Blanc, with a plainness the harbourmaster took to be affection. He had, it appeared, left his calling card at the hall table of a house on the great canal, with the hour of his departure written in the corner, and had been observed, in the pit of the playhouse, to eat nothing noisier than a biscuit and to speak only between acts. The clerk, who had read the chapter the Gothenburg desk has printed — Chapter IV · Of Calling in Gothenburg, of the canals, the cards, the proper ounce of butter — judged his manners sound and recommended that the town answer the card in kind whenever the gentleman next calls. The Committee assented, and it was ordered that one card be prepared, bearing a name, a street, and nothing else.

III. Of the returned lash and the letter from the loft

The Committee received, by the post boat at Lohamne, a letter from the rigging loft at Saltfleet, Lincolnshire, on the far side of the wash-water. It came with the return of the Saltfleet lash, numbered forty-one, twelve feet of bridle leather, which the same gentleman had left with the attendant in the lobby of the playhouse on the third of November and had never gone back for. The loft writes that the leather grip has been refitted, the braid retied, the brass ferrule oiled, and the lash kept in salt air till it should be wanted again; the loft, it adds, is built to repair rather than to replace, and that is the loft’s standing word. The Saltfleet Whip is catalogued in the collection in a page of its own, and the Committee took note of it.

Enclosed with the lash was a small tide-book of the great shallow bay east of the loft’s village, into which the Witham, the Welland, the Nene and the Great Ouse all reach the sea. The letter remarks that the loft keeps the book by the loft window, because the loft’s own beach borrows its tides of that water, and gives the loft’s own words for the matter, which the clerk read aloud and set down: The loft treats it the way the loft reads the beach: The Wash. The Committee looked over the tide-book — the placard for The Wash is kept in the collection beside the loft’s own page — found it true, and ordered it shelved with the harbour ledger, against the season when a gentleman with a lash of his own may stand on the beach and want to know what water the flat lies under.

One further note was entered at the close, of a kind that has no number: that the same loft which kept the lash and read the tide-book also keeps, on the beach shelf, a weather almanac, and that in the almanac’s entry for the year one thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight the water of this bay and the great wind that crossed it from the westward are set down together in a single record of the weather. The Committee looked over the entry — Almanac No. 113 · American English — found it fair work, and left the book on the shelf where a gentleman may turn to it when the card next comes from the west.

IV. Of the clipping and the yolk of one ounce

The Bishop, who has a copy of the Gothenburg clipping kept in the cathedral library, drew the Committee’s attention to the line the bookkeeper there reads as a maxim: Fearing in his hand, a clipping or a little butter, the yolk of 1 oz. The chair observed that the same restraint which governs the table governs the minutes, and that a clerk who grasps too hard at the record is like a guest who drowns good bread in fat. It was thereupon agreed that the minutes of this meeting be written plainly, that the yolk be measured against nothing at all, and that no gloss be supplied where no gloss survives. The record was signed in the margin by all four present, the Bishop last, as is his manner.

V. Of adjournment

The fire was looked to, the windows closed against the February, and the Committee adjourned until the tide-table of April, to meet again in the same room. The gentleman of the card was not present, having gone down the harbour after dark, and his card was left on the table where the chair could see it, as a card is left — at the table, with nothing more in the corner than the hour. It was so done, and the minutes closed.

Entered by Agda Krog, clerk of the cathedral school
Witnessed: the Bishop of Aarhus · Jens Møller, harbourmaster · Rasmus Strand, ropewalker · Peder Lind, in the chair
Sealed at Aarhus, the fourteenth of February, 1782

Read beside these minutes: Chapter IV · Of Calling in Gothenburg, the etiquette of the playhouse across the Kattegat, where the lash was first left and the card first answered; The Saltfleet Whip, from the rigging loft above the beach in Lincolnshire, the twelve feet of bridle leather returned whole; and The Wash, the bay the loft reads the way it reads its own shore.
