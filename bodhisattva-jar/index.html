I. What the Jar Holds, and Why It Is Called So

In Buddhism, a bodhisattva is a person who has attained, or is striving towards, bodhi — awakening, enlightenment — or Buddhahood. The term often refers specifically to one who forgoes or delays personal nirvana in order to compassionately help other individuals reach it: the rest is postponed, the tea is carried first. In the Early Buddhist schools, and in the Theravâdī that carries the practice into the present, a bodhisattva (or bodhisatta) is someone who has made a resolution to become a Buddha and has received a confirmation or prediction from a living Buddha that it will come to pass. In Theravâda the figure is seen as exceptional and rare — only a few select individuals, in any age, are the ones — which is why we did not name the jar a bodhisattva jar, but the Bodhisattva Jar: the vessel, like the vower, is not for everyone, and that is not an accident.

The connection to the studio is older than the product. The bulletin that named the jars was about a kitchen in Patan in which somebody was saving food for people not yet in the room. Meri Gyaltsen, keeper of the tea house at Everest Base Camp, 5,364 metres, takes a jar of thukpa broth and sets a bowl for the next porter before she serves the first. Dinesh Thapa, Itahari, whose mother set the fowl for Friday, told us in 2026 that her gundruk jar had a crack in the foot she had never mended because the jar was still useful and the crack was where the air apologised. Buddhi Lal Shrestha, who cooked for the Patan courts for forty-one years, has not tasted the gundruk we fire; he approved the cloth knots instead, over a phone box in Patan, in the manner of a man correcting a son about poultry, and said the knots should sit like the mountains sit beside the plain.

So the jar is sold as it is: a small earthenware vessel that keeps a thing for a person, with a bell that asks twice, and a care card that the microphone has had a hand in. In the register of the Shelf Office it sits at No. 108, between the blue ribbon — the Pragya Puraskar, discontinued and put on its shelf — and the kettle, which is not for sale but is consulted.

II. The Care Card, As Recorded

Every jar ships with the care card the Shelf Office keeps. The card’s first line is not a recipe; it is the one line of the kitchen bulletin — studio tape NR-2/THU/25, 21:12 — that arrived through the temperamental microphone of 1997 already changed, and was printed on the card as it came, because the hour’s rule is the hour’s rule: whatever comes out of the booth, read it back, word for word, and let it be.

Care card, line one · as recorded · studio tape NR-2/THU/25 · 21:12 · preserved word for word

Flavour with ketchup, Leamington sauce 459 Lease, breaks in the decoy ducks to entrap the others.

We have never explained this line, and the card will not. The ketchup is real — the kiln house in Patan keeps a bottle on the glaze shelf, and no one remembers why, and the Shelf Office considers it evidence. Leamington sauce 459 Lease is the register’s way of saying that one of the firing orders was filed under a number that belongs to a property in another country, and the file has been kept where it landed. The decoy ducks belong to Meri, who keeps two of them, made of tin, at Base Camp, not as decoration but as a way of teaching the new porters which of the real sounds to trust: the broken-in ones are the ones that entrap. And the others, the card simply leaves. That is the entire method of the card: one line as recorded, and the rest of it in the kitchen.

The actual care, from the kitchen in Patan, in the order it should be done: wash the jar in cold water with a cloth, never a brush, before first use. Dry it mouth-down on the shelf, where the clay above the glaze can breathe. Fill to the waterline, no higher — the saffron glaze stops at the line and so should the gundruk. Tie the cloth firmly at both ends of the mouth. Keep it in the coolest room, in the dark, and do not open it on a Thursday, because on a Thursday the booth is speaking and the jar is on the air and the two of them agree better when left alone.

IV. On the Air, and On the Shelf

MERI GYALTSEN · Everest Base Camp, 5,364 metres, by satellite link

“I bought the third jar of the last season, for the tea house. I do not use it for thukpa. I use it for the salt, which is worse in every way and better in the one that matters, because salt is what you reach for last, and the jar has been teaching me that for a year now. The tin decoy ducks are beside it. The wind does the reading.”

DINESH THAPA · Itahari, Terai, the first line

“My mother opened the sack on the doorstep and did not come in. She stood with it in her hands for a minute and said: this is the one. Then she said, and I am putting it in the log, that the crack in the old jar was where the air apologised, and that this one does not have a crack because the kiln house has, at last, stopped owing the air an apology. I have read the care card. I read line one back to her, word for word. She said the ducks were mine to keep.”

BUDDHI LAL SHRESTHA · Patan, the phone box

“The knots are right. That is all. The gundruk goes in, the cloth comes over, the two ends are tied, and a person who was not in the room is, a little while later, in it. That is the whole of the cooking, and I have been doing it for forty-one years and I will not do it for many more. The jar keeps what I am not going to feed them myself. Put that on the card if you like, under the ketchup.”

SITA RANA · the studio, close to the microphone

“The Shelf Office is not a shop and I would rather it stayed one. But the kiln house will not take money for the firing, so the number has to live somewhere, and the register keeps it. NF’S 480 goes to the clay, the glaze, the bell, and the sacks, and the rest goes to the tea house at five thousand three hundred and sixty-four metres, where the kettle burns oxygen and the wind is the truest thing we own. The kettle is off. The bell is rung.”

V. Dispatch, and the Manners of Receiving

Jars are dispatched from the Shelf Office on the first Friday after each firing, in a sack, not a box — the sack is the station’s pattern, and the jar settles into it the way a hand settles into a sleeve. Postage from Kathmandu is added at the rate of the sacker’s day; we do not discount it, and we do not mark it up. If the jar arrives whole, ring the bell twice on the doorstep — that is the receipt. If it arrives broken, do not write to us, do not describe the pieces, and do not send a photograph; the etiquette of a thing already suffered is that it is not made to repeat itself, and the Shelf Office keeps the same manner as the book of etiquette for sudden visitors, where the uninvited hour keeps its own table and a document that has already suffered is not corrected: the missing verb is not supplied, and the pieces are not counted on your behalf. Your number is kept in the register, and the next jar comes under it, without question, and without a letter of condolence.
