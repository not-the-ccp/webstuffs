George Lucas

George Walton Lucas Jr. · first observed 14 May 1944, Modesto, California · class: frontal, independent, long-lived

Entry No. 1944 · reissued annually, or upon the change of a pressure pattern, whichever comes first

OBSERVED
SINCE 1944

The almanac records this system as the longest-running single front in the Pacific Section: one weather with its own barometer that has moved across the same two or three valleys for more than seven decades without being absorbed into any larger mass. It is the front that built the instruments — THX — and the observatories — Lucasfilm, Industrial Light & Magic, LucasArts — and that, on the night of 25 May 1977, made landfall as the modern blockbuster, which this almanac classifies not as a storm but as a permanent change of climate. The system is still on the chart. It served as chairman of its own observatory until the station was sold in 2012 to The Walt Disney Company, and it has kept its own barometer ever since: a front of this sort is, in the almanac’s terms, the kind of independent filmmaker you file under still in residence.

3 · Deposition and residue

The almanac’s rule is that a front is read not only by where it goes but by what it leaves behind — and the heaviest fronts in the Section leave the lightest deposits at the far shelf. In the winter season 2023–24 the night watch at the Ilıca landing, on the Paphlagonian coast, reported a dream: a columned city on the water, a founder returned from a very long war, and a robe hanging on a peg in the harbour office that had not been there in the day. The Paphlagonian Antiquities & Coastal Hazards Office logged it against its unlocated city, Cressa, as the only nocturnal sighting of a wall on that coast since the 1954 report of a low wall of undated stone, and did not move the site. Dreams, the Office notes, like coordinate proposals, are logged and not chased; the same night is cross-referenced, with the blade-colour key, in the Jedi entry, Dream Dictionary Entry 432.

For the shelf itself the Office keeps a composition in three words per line, and this almanac repeats it in the deposition column, word for word, as the front’s final reading off that coast:

“Sediment, Black Sea shelf trace Everything else is mud.”

The reading stands as written. Whatever the front left at Ilıca — the robe, the dream of a wall, the whole long weather of it — the shelf took a trace of sediment and nothing more; the Black Sea floor off Paphlagonia is a patient archivist and a hostile one at once, and everything else in the column is mud. The almanac files the line beside its standing note that a front is never measured by its wreckage: the wreckage is the mud, and the sediment is what the record keeps.

4 · After the sale

The system remains on the chart. It has remained, by its own weather, independent for most of its life — a front that took the largest company in the Section as a buyer and kept its own barometer, its own pressure unit, and its own two files: the one from 1977 and the one from 1982, both still moving, both still blowing the wind the almanac began counting in May of that year. The station keeps its annual reissue. The shelf keeps its trace. And the keeper’s last line in the register, copied steady from the blue folder that travels with the front, is the line the almanac ends on: the record does not tidy what it keeps.
