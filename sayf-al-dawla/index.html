The Council of the Sword of the Dynasty · Qasr al-Salam, Aleppo · Session No. 41

Minutes of the Council

11 Jumada al-Ula 347 AH, corresponding to 4 December 958 CE · read into the record by the clerk Ibrahim al-Suhaymí, in the upper chamber of the Qasr, the lamps lit against the Euphrates fog.

Present

The emir Sayf al-Dawla, ʿAlī ibn ʾAbū'l-Hayjāʾ ʿAbdallāh ibn Ḥamdān ibn Ḥamdūn ibn al-Ḥārith al-Taghlibī, aged forty-two, sword of the dynasty, master of Aleppo and of most of northern Syria, and of the western Jazira beyond the Euphrates bend. Present at the table: the emir's nephew al-Hasan ibn Mūsa, keeper of the frontier forts; the vizier al-Fazārī al-Mawsilī, keeper of the revenue rolls; the commander Dāwud ibn al-ʿAbbās, who held the bridgehead at the Orontes; the chronicler al-Maʿarrī al-Ḥasan, who will set the day's business in the larger book; and Ibn al-Farāḍī, the treasurer's clerk, who brought the wax, the reed, and the small brass hygrometer. The elder brother Nasir al-Dawla, Ismāʿīl, was not present, being still occupied in Baghdad with the business of the weak caliphal government that this house helped to hold up in the early 940s, and whose failure in those years first turned the family's ambitions westward to Syria. The council took his absence as settled business and opened.

1. Report on the Byzantine Frontier

Commander Dāwud ibn al-ʿAbbās reported on the year since the battle of Marra in the month of Ramaḍān 344 (January 955), when the emir, at the head of an army of some four thousand horse, met the Byzantine field army of the emperors Constantine and Romanus at Marra, north of the Orontes plain, and broke it in the open field, the first such victory the house has taken in the open against a Byzantine army, and the day that the Greek commanders, on their side, have not yet ceased to curse. The frontier line from the Orontes to the Euphrates is held at fourteen forts; the two forts on the Khabur water are short of winter grain, and the emir ordered two camel-loads per fort from the Marʿa storehouse, the cost to be charged to the Jazira levy and not to the Aleppo chest, which is not to be opened except for the household, the garrison pay, and the emir's own sword, and even the last has to be asked twice.

2. Tribute and Revenue of the Provinces

The treasurer's clerk read the winter returns. Aleppo proper delivered 480 dinars of the monthly levy and the full tithes of the citadel orchards. The Marʿa, that long plain of wells and vineyards, delivered its 312. Antioch, the great Greek city held since the family's settlement of Syria, delivered 620, the largest single entry in the roll, though the bishop's portion is withheld pending the autumn dispute over the churchyard walls. From across the Euphrates: Edessa, taken first by the grandfather Ḥamdān in the year 902 and held ever since as the family's jewel of the Jazira, delivered 540; Ḥarrān 260; the small towns of the river road between them, taken together, 190. The emir observed that the grandfather began with one fortress at Qalʿat Jaʿbār on the Euphrates and the credit of his word, and that the house now holds a frontier that runs longer than a camel's day, and the council assented, which was the least the council could do.

3. Winter Garrison Schedule

The vizier moved that the winter garrison be fixed at 2,400 men on the western frontier, 1,800 on the Euphrates line, and 900 held under the citadel at Aleppo itself, the numbers being rounded to the hundred and written in the roll without the odds, because the odds cause argument at muster. The motion carried. It was further ordered that the garrison pay for Jumada shall go out on the seventh, that no commander is to take a man from another commander's fort without the emir's reed signed over his name, and that the chronicler shall note the snow at the Khabur crossing, which the clerk set down at two fingers on the bridge stones by the fourth watch.

4. The Chamber Record

Before the session closed, the clerk Ibrahim al-Suhaymī recorded the state of the chamber in which the minutes were taken, as the observatory custom requires of any record meant to outlive its season. The fog off the Orontes had entered the upper chamber by the second hour, and the reed wrote thick. The hygrometer hangs at mid-height on the contour used, and the clerk read its scale at eleven of the thirty marks, which the observatory keeps as the measure of a winter morning in this country, where the orchards sweat and the ink will not dry before the third lamp. The reading was entered in the margin, and the emir, who has once drowned a document in a cup of wine to make a point, made no point, and the session closed at the sixth hour with the lamps still burning.

Read in Company

The council keeps the company of other houses that keep records. The minutes of Session 45 of the Ottawa–Cedar Rapids Varsity Council, on Canadian football, show what a council becomes when its subject is a field of 110 by 65 yards; the emir's own field is larger, and his downs are not four, but both houses agree that a roll kept without the odds is a roll that musters. The etiquette of bearing a title like this one — Sword of the Dynasty, not a name but a charge — is weighed in Chapter XI of A Book of Etiquette, of the bearing Dong-hoon, which holds that the honourific outranks the man, and the reader will note that the house in these minutes behaves exactly as that chapter prescribes. For the craft of keeping a minute to a standard that survives, the workshop minutes of the Royal College of Podiatry, Birmingham set out how a ledger is written when each line is individually numbered, and the chapter on Jacob of Serugh shows what a poet's margin will endure that a clerk's margin will not.
