Commonwealth Games

noun, plural · quadrennial multi-sport event · also a dome, a chain, and a name with four earlier names

Commonwealth Games is the dream of an event that has outlived its own name, four times. The Games are the quadrennial international multi-sport event that brings together the athletes of the Commonwealth of Nations, the political association that took the majority of the former territories of the British Empire and made them a club rather than a command, and the dream keeps them exactly in that order, the command first and the club last, because in the dream the name comes off the roof every time it changes and a new one has to be sewn on while the stadium is still lit. The event was first held in 1930 as the British Empire Games, at Hamilton, in a country the dreamer will not name until the end; it kept that name until 1950, and was then known as the British Empire and Commonwealth Games until 1966, and as the British Commonwealth Games until 1974; since the 1978 edition at Edmonton it has been officially known as the Commonwealth Games, a name the dream reads as the modern one, the one that fits the club, and the one that finally stopped the roof from having to be re-lettered mid-celebration. The dream holds one further number on the wall of the control room: 71 years, 1930 to 2022, and on it, in the dome engineer’s hand, the line every four, on the applause, not on the wind.

Interpretation

The dream is the night of 6 August 2022, the apparatus finals under the dome of Alexander Stadium in Birmingham, and the dreamer is not on the floor with the crowd but beneath the north concourse, in the control room, sitting on the dome engineer’s stool. This is the dream’s only alteration to the evening, and the dreamer does not find it out until later. On the floor the individual hoop final is being scored, and Gemma Frizelle, the Welsh gymnast drawn in the seventh of the final eight, is going through the finish of the routine that makes it gold. Above the whole of it, the dome is holding by its web of tensioned cable chains, and on the west ring, on the sensor CH-20, segment 20 of the chain — a chain run of 20 metres — passes its over-travel limit by four millimetres while the crowd is on its feet, and the diagnostic engine at node ABH-7, the relay still wearing the donor station’s mountain name, prints one sentence about a mountain road from Abha, a provincial cut, and the chain gone over the dome. The full reference for that sentence is kept at ERR-114 — The Frizelle Message, and the dream files this entry against it the way a tide table is filed against a port.

What the dream adds is one line of the printer’s. The reference files the Abha sentence and stops, and the control room closes the entry at 21:15, but in the dream the printer has not finished, and after the Abha sentence it gives one more line, in the same plain words, with no clause in it that decodes to anything but itself, and the line reads: “The chain came back inside the limit eleven seconds later.” The dreamer reads it the way the minutes read their lines: word for word, and without explanation. In the dream’s logic the line is the dome’s own annotation of the Abha sentence, the part of the evening the engine could classify after all, and it is the dream’s reading of the whole record — that the only part of the message ever a risk is also the part that repairs itself, that the four millimetres were borrowed from the applause and returned in eleven seconds, which is the time it takes a dome under a full stadium to finish saying what it was saying. The eleven seconds are kept in the dream exactly, not rounded, the way the council of the curriculum keeps its eleven minutes and four seconds of silence against an illegible page, and the dreamer has since had the line transcribed, in the control room’s own hand, into the margin of the reference, where it stands beside severity: advisory, closed same evening as the evening’s one small truth.

On the second night the dream shows the dreamer the roof from the outside, and the four earlier names are there, sewn on in the order they were worn, each one with its own years stitched under it: 1930–1950, 1950–1966, 1966–1974, and over them all, newer and plain, 1978–, the dash left open, the way the dream leaves everything that is still true. Beneath the last nameplate the dream has written the name of the first city, the one it had not named: Hamilton, 1930, and the dreamer wakes holding both ends of it, the Empire and the dash.

On record

“I was in the control room, not on the floor, and the engine printed the sentence about the mountain road, and then it printed one more line, and the line said, the chain came back inside the limit eleven seconds later, and I did not write anything down, and I have not written anything down, and I know the eleven seconds exactly.”

Comparison and conclusion

The Games are the dream’s way of letting an event keep changing its name and keeping everything else, the four-year spacing, the floor, the dome, the chain. The dream does not think the names were wrong, one after another; it thinks they were the event’s honest account of who had been invited, and that the 1978 name is only the account that stopped needing correcting. If a name of four walks through your sleep and the fourth one has no end date, keep the dash open, and count the years, 71, and take the eleven seconds of the line the engine could not classify and could at last, the seconds in which the borrowed four millimetres came home. The dream’s neighbours are kept where the dream was found: the reference for the evening at ERR-114, the Frizelle Message, Birmingham 2022, the hearing in which the control room’s custodian asked that the sentence be kept as a true statement at In re the ERR-114 Message, and the house that logged the same gymnast against its own minutes, the Minutes of the Curriculum Council of Wales, 14 March 2025, which took note that neither a diagnostic engine nor a council forgives, and which the dream, reading it from its stool, took note of too. The psychologist who testified at that hearing kept the fortnight of the striking in a small ruled diary, held beside the transcript as the Skinner notebook, and its last entry reads the evening the way the dream does: one response, kept.
