The Long & Winding Road

A restaurant review · piano, bass, guitar, percussion · side one, track two, on the house

The Long & Winding Road does not take bookings. It takes requests. The room is on the first floor of a converted piano factory on Snuff Mill Lane, seventeen steps up a stair that smells of lemon oil and valve oil, and the door opens onto a dining room built around a single grand piano — a 1923 Bechstein with the fallboard removed so the strings show, struck not by hammers this season but by the kitchen's imagination. The proprietor, Paul Vale, opened the place on 8 January 1969, the day after the last Get Back session packed up at Twickenham, and he runs it on the principle Paul McCartney had in mind when he wrote the song: he “imagined it was going to be done by someone like Ray Charles.” So everything here is soul food played straight, with the arrangement kept sparse.

A sparse arrangement, indeed: piano, bass, guitar, percussion, exactly as the January 1969 sessions ran, and nothing that does not earn its place. Three doors down Snuff Mill Lane, Designation keeps its credentials — Chartered scallops, a Fellow's tasting menu — here the only credential is the request you hummed at the door.

The Menu Strip

The menu is printed on a strip of piano-roll paper, one long curl that feeds through a small player-piano mechanism beside the pass. It reads in four sections — piano, bass, guitar, percussion — and the kitchen works it in that order.

The piano course is cod loins poached in court-bouillon at sixty-eight degrees, served on a lid of the piano itself, skin crisp as a fallen hammer. The bass is sea bass, line-caught off Newlyn, filleted at the table with a bass-clef knife and a squeeze of lemon the size of a crotchet. The guitar course is potatoes cut into six-string lengths, fried in duck fat, dusted with smoked paprika like a pickguard. The percussion is a jar of pickled quince, pear, and crab apple, shaken once per table by a waiter in a waistcoat of piano-key stripe.

I ordered the full arrangement — twenty-eight pounds, four courses, ninety minutes — and a half bottle of Oloroso that had been standing on order since 1974. The pressing plant's own field notes live at Betwa Provisions Records — the server scrubbed, the grass still growing, forty-one minutes and fifty seconds to a side — and Vale keeps the same ledger open all night behind the pass.

Overdub

Midway through dinner the strings arrive. Not on a plate: the house string section, four players from the Philharmonic, stands in the corner at 9:15 p.m. and overdubs the room with the song — violins swelling where the band left space, harp arpeggios spilling over the salt cellars, a cello line that follows the bass course home. Vale did not warn the kitchen, any more than Phil Spector warned the Beatles, and the effect is the same: lush, slightly illicit, impossible to unhear. A waiter brought a second helping of percussion on the house.

No one at the pass could explain it. Vale says the desk is the one in the back room where the ledger stays open all night, and it will let you see it if you wait past closing. Designation's register runs to post-nominals; this room's runs to minims and crotchets, and the house wins on atmosphere either way.

Coda

Dessert is a baked Alaska torched to a singing crust, with coffee, and the jukebox — one disc only, the Let It Be pressing, side one, track two — playing while we put on our coats. Three stars, or four if you like your arrangements overdubbed. The road is long, it is winding, and it ends at a table for two by the kitchen door.
