The Pitfour Post

Newsletter No. 41 · The Longside Strollers & Estate Antiquaries · 14 October 1971

Secretary: Miss D. R. Crichton, 3 Steens Road, New Pitsligo · Subscription: two shillings, payable in change at the kiosk

From the Secretary

Members will note that Michaelmas has fallen on a Tuesday this year, which is inconvenient for exactly one man in the club, Mr. Ewan Soutar, who keeps his Tuesday evenings for the harp. We met anyway, at the usual table near the window, twenty-three of us, and the minute book records the weather as “Buchan, which is to say, sideways.”

Our standing business this season is the estate itself. The Pitfour Estate, for the benefit of the two members who have recently admitted they had not quite managed it, is the old barony that covers most of the Longside Parish, the long stretch from St Fergus down to New Pitsligo, fifty square miles of it — 130 square kilometres for the French member, Madame Yvonne Pellat, who asked, and who has asked every year since 1963, and whom we have never once corrected. It was purchased in 1700 by James Ferguson of Badifurrow, who thereby became the first Laird of Pitfour, and he, his son James, and his grandson Sir James spent the next two centuries renovating it so substantially that the parish came to be described in polite Edinburgh letters as “the Ferguson experiment.”

At its height, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the property carried several extravagant features of which we are the small guardians: a two-mile racecourse, ploughed out for the steeplechase; an artificial lake, thirty-four acres, impounded in 1763 and never since properly drained; and an observatory, whose dome the council is still arguing about. The original mansion house was extended, then extended again, and then rebuilt outright in 1794, which is how the Ferguson line lost a house and then won it back larger.

The Observatory Dictation, Decoded

Last Candlemas the club was lent, on strict conditions, the dictation tape of the last observatory warden, Mr. Tom Blackwood, recorded the week before the dome was sealed. Our typist, Miss Margaret Tait, transcribed it at forty words a minute, which is too fast for Mr. Blackwood and too slow for the tape. One line resisted her entirely and is given here exactly as she set it down, for the record, and for the members who have already begun a betting pool:

The 140-inch Hale-class telescope in the province — have twelve vents, one for the only long table and said, word for word.

— Dictation tape, tape 3 of 7, side A, the warden's voice, transcribed by M. Tait, 17 February 1971

Mr. Soutar, against his own harp, has proposed the following decoding, which the meeting accepted by fourteen votes to nine: the “140-inch Hale-class telescope” is the warden's proud name for what the inventory plainly lists as a 4.5-inch brass refractor of modest and un-Halean origin, which he had in his head as the great American instrument and never once corrected; “twelve vents” are the twelve ventilation shafts around the dome's ribs, a fact he had counted and was just now going to tell us; and “one for the only long table” refers to the single vent that served the long table of the adjoining instrument room, where the fittings were laid out for two observers. Mr. Blackwood, in short, was saying the telescope and the table shared one draught, and he said it, word for word, the way a man says things at the end of a forty-year posting. The betting pool remains open until the dome is opened, which is to say, indefinitely.

The Autumn Stroll & Its Arithmetic

The annual stroll is on Saturday, 30 October, weather be damned, and this year members are asked to pick their own route from the estate features below, tick as many as they can manage, and note the figures the plan prints out. The club's house rate is two and three-quarter miles an hour with the harp along, and the plan allows one tea at the station kiosk for every mile beyond two. If the plan prints nothing, the rule stands as always: walk the ring, stop where you are tired, and do not let Mr. Soutar set the pace.

Correspondence From the Edges of the Ledger

Two items for the minute book. First, a letter arrived at the station hotel from Mr. Arthur Parry of Bristol, who writes from the stand at Ashton Gate and has sent the club a brass washer — his, from Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14 — to be entered on our board of things found, lost, and deliberately let go, and our ledger, which has been doing that for forty years, will hold it gladly; his own letter, Dear Ashton Gate, is pinned by the window where everyone may read how a crossbar is exonerated, eight to one. Second, our French member has enclosed a copy of a letter to the editor from a data steward in Portland, On Machine Unlearning, and the House on the Hill, on the removal of memories from machines without rebuilding them. Madame Pellat has underlined the sentence about the model made to forget cleanly being “a little house with the lights off,” and has added, in pencil, that we have been doing this by hand for a hundred years on a much larger scale: an estate, a racecourse, a lake, a dome, each one removed by sale, by weather, or by neglect, and every deletion entered in this very newsletter, which is why the minute book is now thick enough to rest a harp on. The rest of this season's items, the minute book itself, and our two shillings are as usual at the kiosk, and the front shelf of Webstuffs will do if the kiosk is shut.

Next meeting: Candlemas, 2 February 1972, table by the window, harp optional and harp encouraged. The secretary will be at the kiosk from ten for subscriptions, and for Mr. Soutar, if it should come, his Tuesday apology.
