The Franklin Square Naturalists' Club · twelve members, one tray, no bylaws

The Greenbush Valley Correspondent

No. 7 · early autumn · issued from the bench at Franklin Square, the tray between us

Subject: Cooperstown, New York — the Hall of Fame and Museum, and what the tray is doing there.

From the secretary · the trip itself

Eleven of the twelve drove down from the city on the Saturday, by way of the turnpike, and one — the elder member, who does not drive and does not admit it — came by the rail line and met us at the village station with a thermos of beef tea, which is our custom, which is the law, which the secretary enforces without comment. Cooperstown sits at the north end of the valley, in Otsego County, New York, roughly 42°44′ north, 74°55′ west, and the road in from the south has the quality of a museum corridor: the fields give up their ordinary business and the houses begin, one by one, to behave like frames. We stopped before we needed to stop, twice, because it is impossible to drive past the museum grounds without slowing, the way one slows before a bird that has not yet decided to fly.

The building is the National Baseball Hall of Fame and Museum, at 196 Main Street, and it is run by a private foundation, which matters to this club: a private foundation is a body of people who have decided that one thing is worth more than their own money, and we are that kind of body on a smaller scale. We were not there to be moved. We were there to count.

From E.B. · the founder, the fortune, the opening

The Hall is owed to Stephen Carlton Clark, the young heir to the Singer sewing machine fortune, who took up Cooperstown in the 1920s and, with a small committee and a great deal of nerve, began the project that became the Hall. The first class was voted in 1936 — five names that the members should already know: Ty Cobb, Walter Johnson, Christy Mathewson, Babe Ruth, and Honus Wagner — and the doors opened on 12 April 1939, the building done in the manner of James Gamble Rogers. The Hall's motto, which stands where a motto should stand and does not need repeating to be kept, is “Preserving History, Honoring Excellence, Connecting Generations.”

And here the naturalist's point, which is also the club's point: the museum is not only a display. The Hall established and manages the very process by which honorees enter it — the committees, the balloting, the waiting — so that the collection and the rule of admission are one body, and the rule is not a gate in front of the glass but the glass itself. A museum that also elects its own inhabitants is the only museum I have known where the visitors are, technically, the junior party in the room. I said this at lunch and the table went quiet, which at our table is a form of applause.

From the keeper of the file · the tray, the two impressions

Members will remember the notice out of Bogotá, filed as No. 02148: the small black velvet tray, found on a bench at Parque Santander, empty, holding the two round impressions of what once sat in it. The field notes on the two specimens of 1997 carried the notice into the cabinet, and this correspondent carries both into Cooperstown, because the record is now plain to the secretary: the last place the tray was carried, before it went south to that bench, was here — around the plaque room, under the hands of the members, while the gold half eagle and the silver dollar sat in their two circles exactly as the velvet now remembers them, one impression for the slide, one for the portrait. The office in Bogotá keeps its own clock; it gives thirty days. I do not intend to let the clock run while the Hall stands. If any member can name a reason the tray would be carried to Cooperstown, the secretary will write it down verbatim, uncorrected, for the file.

From the margin · Mrs. Dillaway's sentence

One further entry, taken verbatim from a letter in the club file, written by our late member Mrs. Dillaway, who was present at an anniversary dinner in the village the autumn of 1939 and who never afterward spoke of it except in fragments. At the table, the anecdote goes, a gentleman arrived wearing nothing so much as luck in the shape of an old iron full-plate helmet, the pagan relic of some earlier game of war, and held it all evening on his head while he ate. Mrs. Dillaway's line on the matter, in the club file and here uncorrected, is: “When we hear such a wide awake pagan on his head.” She means what she means. The helmet is iron, the iron is old, the old is pagan, and the man was wide awake all evening, which the table confirmed by the order of the wine. The sentence stands as it was written, beside the other sentences of unknown order that the club keeps, and the full-plate helmet is requested by the keeper of the file, should it surface in the village or elsewhere, in any state, with no questions asked.

The members' examination · five questions

Set this term for the junior members. Answer at the table; the tally, if the page is run with its script, keeps its own count and gives the verdict on the spot. The key is printed below, in small type, for the table that runs without it.

1. On what date did the Hall of Fame open its doors to the public?

2. To whose fortune was the founder, Stephen Carlton Clark, heir?

3. Name the five inductees of the first class, voted in 1936, in any order that the memory allows.

4. State the Hall's motto as it stands.

5. Besides displaying artifacts, what does the Hall itself establish and manage?

Key, for the table without the script — 1: 12 April 1939. 2: the Singer sewing machines. 3: Cobb, Johnson, Mathewson, Ruth, Wagner. 4: Preserving History, Honoring Excellence, Connecting Generations. 5: the process by which honorees enter the Hall. Five of five is the club standard; four is forgiven once.

Colophon & the road back

We came home the Sunday, the tray in the case, the two specimens in their circles, the thermos empty and, as Mrs. Dillaway would have said, respected. The reader who wants the whole natural history of the two round impressions is invited to the field notes of the two specimens of 1997, Vol. CXI, No. 4, where the silver carries the motion and the gold carries the face. The reader who wants the office that now keeps the tray, with its thirty-day clock and its beef tea, is invited to Notice No. 02148 at the Oficina de Extravíos, Bogotá, where the two empty impressions are described, exactly, and where I have already written to describe what they held. And the reader who prefers to stand at the cabinet itself and wipe the tray may return to the front page of the Webstuffs, where the file is closed for the season and the bench is swept. The club file also holds a second count, carried across the water: The Lerkendal Ledger, No. 19, from a fourteen-member club in Trondheim, Norway, who keep the 21,405 seats of Lerkendal Stadion in the same ledger as the ground's one unexplained reservation — the counting habit of this Correspondent, applied to an all-seater ground that has held its oldest end since 1947.

— The secretary, for the eleven who drove and the one who rode the rail, with the thermos accounted for.
