Dream Dictionary · Entry 717

Okinawa

noun · Japanese Okinawa · also an island, a prefecture, a battle, and a dream

Okinawa is the dream of an island that was scheduled to be the last and did not quite become it. On 1 April 1945 the largest amphibious assault in the Pacific Theater of World War II set foot here, codenamed Operation Iceberg, and in the dream the codename holds: every landing craft comes up wearing frost, and the ice in the water does not melt, because it was never meant to melt; it was meant to be broken. The Kerama Islands around Okinawa were preemptively captured on 26 March 1945 by the U.S. Army 77th Infantry Division, and in the dream that date is the first thing erased, which is how you know the dream has begun. The eighty-two days of the battle, 1 April to 22 June, last the length of one seating: the bell goes at seven, the island is on the other side by the second course, and the tide table does not run a second service.

Interpretation

The Allies, after a long campaign of island hopping, were planning to use Kadena Air Base on the island for the planned assault on the home islands; in the dream the base is built and never used, the runways are planted in sugar cane, and a handful of aircraft stands on them painted white over the reef, remembering sixteen white hulls riding at permanent anchor under a fog that never burns off (Entry 714, Hunters Point, Hunters Point — Dream Dictionary, Entry 714). The island's own word-hoard travels by the prefecture — the forty-seventh, entered at the foot of the filterable table of all 47 in a tourist's brochure of the island country (Japan — Visitor’s Brochure · Pacific Coast & Kofun Circuit) — but the dream keeps no table. It keeps the reef. The reef is the dream's real subject: the row of coral that locks the island in, the row-locks the water keeps, and every sentence that comes back across the sea arrives locked the same way.

On the third night the dream brings up a plaque on a white two-and-a-half storey house standing on the beach with four square columns at the front, and on the plaque is written, in the hand of a class four thousand miles west, the sentence: “It was originally built around 1852 , so it predates the town itself in all but certain from the row-locks.” The dreamer is not disturbed, because the dream knows the sentence belongs to the Shaw–Hammons House — History Report, Form 7C and has crossed the water with name chewed out and replaced with the reef. The house was indeed built around 1852, before the town; the island was before the battle; and the row-locks, in the dream's logic, are the coral of the reef, which holds the house on the beach and lets the date stand in all but certain from them. The dream keeps one other row of the same kind — the row of elephants that went through the gap at Ipsus in 301 BC, counted in the plain of Phrygia in threes and kept by a single hand-drawn plan, at Lot 11, The Ipsus Field Papers, where the date, too, is held by paperwork — but Mr. Ferber will not admit this as dream evidence, but he has read the sentence twice and will not correct the comma. The number itself has been refiled as a distance in the entry that follows: the nautical mile, exactly 1,852 metres, at Nautical Mile — Dream Dictionary, Entry 718, where the row-locks hold the same figure both as a date and as the measure the house stands from the reef.

The war reaches the dream once more by way of a dining room: Oberrender, above Dry Dock 3 in New London, is named for the John C. Butler–class destroyer escort USS Oberrender (DE-344), which spent the last two years of the war on convoy escort duty in the Pacific (Oberrender — Restaurant Review). In the dream the DE-344 shortcake arrives with the card printed with the ship's keel date still in it, and the card has come back to the room in a different hand: 1 April, 22 June, eighty-two days, one seating. The room's single-service rule is the dream's schedule; the two school bells are the reef, ringing once and not ringing again.

Associated imagery

  • Eighty-two days compressed into one seating, called by a bell at seven
  • Landing craft wearing frost, the ice meant to be broken, not melted
  • The Kerama date, 26 March, the first thing erased off the tide table
  • Kadena's airfield in sugar cane, white aircraft standing on the stripes
  • The coral reef as a school row, the row-locks, holding the water in place
  • A white two-and-a-half storey house with four square columns, an 1852 plaque on the beach
  • Two school bells, one service, no second time, in a low brick block

On record

“I was in the seventh row of the reef and the plaque turned to face me. It said the house predates the town, and the row-locks were holding, and the ice had not melted, because the ice was the schedule.”

Comparison and conclusion

In the dream's logic the island and the house lean on each other the way the yard leans on the quay: both are kept standing by paperwork, by bells, and by a date that outlives the use. The island outlives the schedule; the house outlives the town; the dream keeps both, and the row-locks keep the water where the date stands. If an island walks through your sleep wearing frost, ask which one you were about to call the last. Open the reef. Take the 22 June. The front desk of the class miscellany files the entry at Webstuffs, the class miscellany, beside the others, where the row-locks keep it.

The row-locks and the elephants' row are cousins: both hold a date in place, and both are counted by a single hand. Where the dream's count ends at eighty-two days, the field's count ends at 113 circles in the vellum plan — three crossed out, and the plain is quiet.
