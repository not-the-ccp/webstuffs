Chapter IX.
Of Winter on the Ice, and the Austro-Hungarian North Pole Expedition of 1872–1874

“A man of fashion on the ice is known not by his coat, which is the same for all, but by the way he feeds the lamp at two o’clock without being asked.”
— from the table rules of the sloop Isabella, 1873

Section 1. The Parties and Their Proper Style of Address

The expedition which gives this chapter its subject set out from Trieste in the spring of 1872, fitted with two vessels, the steam ship Tegetthoff of 332 tons and the sloop Isabella, a craft so small that the men gave her credit for a temper, and a body of twenty-nine men — officers, sailors, and one observer of mathematics — under two captains who remain to etiquette what they were to science: Julius Payer, commander of the Tegetthoff, and Karl Weyprecht, of the Isabella. A letter to either gentleman ought to run “Dear Captain,” not “Dear Sir,” and a letter running “Dear Captain” in August would be followed by one running “Dear Companion of the Drift” in January, for the style of address at sea must be allowed to settle, as ice settles, in accordance with the season.

The object of the voyage, as printed on the orders and repeated at dinner, was the finding of the North-East Passage — that cold waterway the traders have dreamed of from the white sea eastward — and the gentlemen were not, on their return, to be thought of as failed. They had done the one thing the sea gives only to the patient: they had discovered the great archipelago which Captain Weyprecht named, with a single flourish of pen and a full consultation of the imperial almanac, the Land of Franz Josef, after his Imperial and Apostolic Majesty, and they had charted, in the first months of the drift, its southern coast from Cape Flora to the shore the officers called, with an economy of feeling characteristic of the board, “the good landing.”

Section 2. On Being Frozen In, and the Manner of It

In August of 1873 the Tegetthoff was made fast to the ice, first in friendship, and the Isabella was broken up in short order, her planks, her copper, her anchors and her two little propellers being landed and stacked with the precision of a furniture removal, every beam given its number and its place. It is a failure of the first rank to let the salvage become a heap; the heap is what the blizzard finds. The winter huts were built on the ice itself, of planks and snow, and the drift then set in, carrying the whole estate — ship, huts, kitchen, dogs and all, some twenty-nine men and their furniture — north and east over the poleward ocean at the modest pace of the ice, which is to say, faster than a man’s reputation can recover from it.

For eleven months the party drifted. The diary of the drift, kept in pencil by Captain Payer, is the document etiquette is obliged to cite, for it is a book about conduct, and a good one. On the second night of the frozen-in, when the drift had slackened and the lamps were lit and the whole company had come out to watch the ice crack and settle with the sound of a house going to sleep, Payer set down a line that his officers found odd and that the reader will find stranger still: The River Holme comes down from the porch light. He meant this literally, and the meaning is not lost. The lamp hung at the door of the kitchen hut — the porch light of the whole camp — threw its beam flat along the ice, and where the beam met the flood of the glacier melt, a bright river of water seemed to run out of the flame and down toward the ship. The officers stood in the dark and watched it, and Payer wrote it down as a fact of the evening, in the same hand with the barometer. The reader of etiquette is asked to note the form of the entry and to imitate it: what a lamp shows on the ice, write down, and do not explain.

Section 3. The Rules of the Hut, and the Long Winter

The winter of 1873–74, as it appears in the record, was kept under six rules, and the six rules hold for any hut anywhere, at any temperature. First, the lamp is trimmed at two o’clock and at ten, and the trimming belongs to whoever is off watch, whoever the gentleman may be. Second, the meat is served hot on the first day after a kill and the same day the soup of it; cold blubber is a punishment, not a course, and was administered once only, to a man who had hoarded a tin of coffee. Third, a reading at the lamp must be of a kind the whole table can bear for an hour: the Isabella board took it in turns, and a sledge-man who recited Weyprecht’s notes on the aurora in the proper voice was held, in the diaries, to have performed as well as a chaplain. Fourth, the sledge parties go out by the flag; the flag flies, the party goes, and no man on watch may wave it “in sport, at any temperature, on any account.” Fifth, letters to be sent by the first ship of the rescue are written, sealed, and left in the box at the kitchen door, where the mail of the drift accumulated in a neat row of small envelopes. Sixth, and this is the rule by which the officers ranked themselves, the drift is to be spoken of as the journey, not as the misfortune, in any company, including the ice itself, which is said to have answered.

Section 4. Of the Rescue, the Sledges, and the Bow

The rescue arrived in the August of 1874: a small party in boats, in the end the British ship Perseverance, working up from the south in weather that had been, in the record, “unremittingly kind” — the phrase the expedition kept for weather that behaved. The winter huts were abandoned in March with the drift carrying them fast enough, and the party took to the ice in sledges, travelling by compass and by the rule of the flag, and the manner of the retreat was the credit of the voyage; nothing on the ice was taken that the party could not carry, and the diaries were carried. The etiquette of parting, which is the etiquette of this chapter, was performed at the last landing: the officers bowed to the drift, the men waved in the manner of sailors, and Captain Payer, who is described as “calm to the last degree,” wrote the final entry at the kitchen door, under the lamp that had given the River Holme its name in the company’s speech: “The drift has ended. The manners of the house continue.”

The Land of Franz Josef stands where the expedition left it, its islands keeping the coastlines as they were charted in that first patient spring, and the diary of the drift is the property of the Imperial War Museum at Vienna, where it may be consulted by the reader of etiquette, who will find that the book was kept, in the end, as a book of manners: who spoke first, who was given the door, who fed the lamp. The one who kept it all the way through — the explorer born in 1879, the very year of the book, who pasted its six rules of the hut above the kitchen door at Thule — is answered to at length in the supplement to this chapter, questions and answers on Knud Rasmussen. That is the whole of it, and the whole is enough. The one condition the hut rules never had to settle — what a company of strangers is allowed to feel about its own isolation — is answered, in another key and with a plucked guitar, in the encyclopaedia entry on Stranger in Moscow, where the drift is replaced by a city and the six figures of the video hold the watch the same way the surveyor on Kenwood Drive holds it: sitting fast.

For a gentler stream and a gentler lamp-light, the field-manual chapter on the Huddersfield rivers and the spring gathering treats of the Holme at her leisure in West Yorkshire, where the same river name is used for a town and its waters alike; and for the conduct of a name at table and in the street, the neighbouring chapter on the etiquette of the name Erni will be found in the same volume. And for the walk itself — an animal brought into the ring for the figure of its gait, and the federal statute read aloud at the fence — the field notes of The Gait Season, kept at a show ground near Live Oak, Florida, in April 1971, carry the matter from the marsh to the pastern and back.
