Field Notes · The Continental Line series · Entry no. 22

Interstate 90

Seattle, Washington · Snoqualmie Pass · Yellowstone, Montana · the Northern Plains · Chicago · Cleveland · Buffalo · Rochester · Boston, Massachusetts

Observer: field naturalist  ·  Route: 47°37′N 122°20′W to 42°21′N 71°03′W  ·  Length: 3,099.7 mi (4,988.5 km)  ·  Weather: variable, westerly

Odometer, kept by hand at each stop: 0.0 of 3,099.7 miles · states crossed: 1 of 13 · auxiliary routes passed: 0 of 15

Stop 1 — the western terminus, Seattle

Place: Seattle, King County, Washington  ·  Station: milepost 0, Washington State Route 519  ·  Time: 05:40

I-90 begins, as the records insist, at State Route 519 in Seattle — and the junction does what all good beginnings do, which is to stand in a busy place and pretend to be quiet. I took the line from the water side: Elliott Bay grey-green under the Alaskan Way viaduct, the piers of the fish market still throwing their usual noise, and the first stretch of the interstate climbing northeast over the street grid like a road with somewhere to be. This is the longest Interstate Highway in the United States — 3,099.7 miles, or 4,988.5 kilometres, laid end to end — and from its first milepost the design reads as a single long decision: due east, whatever the country does in between. Thirteen states will be served before the pavement turns itself into Beacon Hill.

Stop 2 — the Cascades, Snoqualmie Pass

Place: Snoqualmie Pass, King & Snohomish counties  ·  Elevation: about 3,006 ft at the pass  ·  Note: first great range

The first mountain test is the Cascade Range, and the freeway meets it honestly — through the tunnel and over the saddle at Snoqualmie, with the Stevens Pass shoulder a few miles south carrying the older road's traffic. Sitka spruce, Douglas fir, and in the cutbanks the pink of bigleaf maple going to autumn colour. Marmot on the scree at the east portal; a single raven working the service road like a toll collector. The mountain does not care about the line; the line, for about a mile, cares only about the mountain.

Stop 3 — the Rockies, Beartooth Pass, Montana

Place: Beartooth Pass, Yellowstone National Park, Montana  ·  Elevation: 8,337 ft  ·  Note: second great range, highest point of the route

The second range, the Rocky Mountains, is crossed in Montana at Beartooth Pass, the highest point on the entire 3,099.7 miles: 8,337 feet of the interstate laid through the back country of Yellowstone. The pavement here is the only smooth thing in a landscape of granite, glacier polish, and elk on the benchland below the grade. I counted eleven elk and one bear working the willow at the river; the trucks do their slow pass-by with horns down, and the whole interchange of traffic and animal life proceeds in its own low protocol. Montana is where the route stops being a road through mountains and starts being a road across a continent.

Stop 4 — the northern Great Plains

Place: Wyoming, South Dakota, Nebraska  ·  Elevation: 3,400 ft and falling  ·  Weather: 40°F, wind NW

Out of the mountains the line drops onto the northern Great Plains and runs for hundreds of miles with nothing to negotiate. Big Bend and the Niobrara bottoms in Nebraska are where I found the sandhill cranes on migration, two hundred strong in the slough water at dawn, the calls carrying far down the highway before the birds themselves were visible. Wheat in every direction, stubble in the fields the combine had just taken. The interstate here is a true linear habitat: fence-line, shoulder, mowed median, and the dark ribbons of the travel lanes themselves, each with its own little fauna of windbreak trees, drainage slough, and the occasional deer that stands in the median like a punctuation mark the surveyor never meant.

Stop 5 — southeast through Wisconsin, to Chicago

Place: the Driftless Area, Wisconsin, and the Chicago area, Illinois  ·  Note: the line turns, and the auxiliaries begin

The route then traverses the plains' last country and turns southeast through Wisconsin and the Chicago area — the first place where the highway stops being a single line and becomes a family. The 15 auxiliary routes of the whole system live mostly in the big cities, and Chicago gets the largest allotment: the I-90/I-94 twins, the Eisenhower expressway, the Kennedy, and the outer belt, all wearing the number or carrying the legacy. In the Wisconsin stretches, oaks over the cutbanks, deer in the understory at the county lines, and the lake's wind in the pines of the north shore wherever the grade dips toward the water. A parallel log from the station naturalists keeps the same kind of ledger for a much smaller line, 3,099.7 miles worth of country condensed to a handful of stops — Ethan Allen, Field Notes — where one frontier man and one marble statue hold the corner of a map this road would cross a dozen times over.

Stop 6 — Cleveland, Buffalo, Rochester, and the eastern end

Place: Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, Massachusetts  ·  Station: milepost 3,099.7, Beacon Hill, Boston

East of Chicago the auxiliaries keep arriving: Cleveland wears its 90 like a collar; Buffalo and Rochester split the number among ramps and bypasses; Pennsylvania contributes its quiet, unshowy stretch of rolling farmland where the route keeps its promise of due east with a patience the plains do not match. The terminus is Boston, at the base of Beacon Hill, where the longest interstate in the country ends, in the manner of all freeways, abruptly and with a sign. I stayed in the harbour town until the ferries ran and wrote the closing entry below the map, because the closing entry is what the log itself supplied, and a naturalist carries what the record gives.

“Various white and brown the pork in the autumn, against the giant planets and calls it the returns of 1995 showed the retreat — 0 5 Opening day.”

It is on the last page, in a hand that is not mine, ink gone the colour of the plains at dusk. I have kept it word for word, the way the old logs keep their unparseable lines: a weather almanac once filed the observation of a single passing Unbreakable, entry 2015/214, on exactly this principle — one record, one line, not explained. Whatever the sentence is about — pork, planets, a season's returns, an opening day — it sits at the end of the longest road in the country like a specimen pinned in the drawer, labelled, and left alone. The road itself, for its part, has gone on running east and west, thirteen states long, fifteen auxiliaries deep, from SR 519 at Seattle to the quiet at Beacon Hill. That is the specimen. The sentence is its tag.
