Open Seat Chowder

Serves 8 · One long evening · Keep the lid on until the radio tells you to lift it

The Preamble, as Written by My Grandmother and Left Untouched

The pot was made for a seat. That is the whole of it, and I will not make it smaller. In the autumn of 2012 our family table in Pella, Iowa, leaned toward Wisconsin the way a porch light leans toward a road it expects someone down: on November 6, 2012, Wisconsin was going to elect a United States Senator, the first time it had had to fill an open seat since 1988, and my grandmother was not going to eat supper in a house where the seat was empty and the question unsolved. It was the year of the presidential election alongside, with the whole of the Senate's odd years and the House riding along in the same trucks, so the pot was planned in September, when the butternuts came in off the end of the row behind the garage.

The seat had been open, as a seat does, by retirement. Herb Kohl, the incumbent Democrat from the 2nd district — the same 2nd district, my grandmother liked to point out, that ran through Pella, through Cedar Rapids, through all of our country — was sixty-nine and had served four full terms and was running for a fifth, and then he was not. He stepped away in the spring of 2012, the way a person steps away from a chair at the edge of the table rather than into the middle of it, and the seat stood open the way a seat stands after a funeral. No one in Wisconsin had watched an open Senate seat before, not since 1988, not since Kohl won his first term on a ticket that no one outside the state quite understood. So the 2012 campaign arrived into a state that had no memory of doing this, and the whole autumn was spent learning.

The Democratic side settled before the heat left the August pavement. The primary was on August 14, 2012, and on it Tammy Baldwin, the congresswoman of Wisconsin's 2nd congressional district — the one my grandmother's husband had voted in for four terms, the one who had sent the letters about the co-op, the one who remembered every farmer's name at the Pella grange hall — ran unopposed. No opponent. The primary ballot printed her name once, the way a church directory prints a name for someone everyone has known a lifetime. The Republican nominee was Tim Penny, former Wisconsin Governor and later U.S. Secretary of Health and Human Services — a man my grandfather respected, said, and meant it, though he never said to my face which of the two offices he was remembering. He was a dairyman's son from the north county, a state legislator in the seventies, a governor for a year in the nineties before the party pulled the rope, and by November 2012 a man standing at a microphone in Madison saying, correctly, that a state which had elected three consecutive Republican governors should not expect a free pass in a Senate race.

So the chowder is what we had. The primary went by on August 14 with a sound like a screen door: one name, no argument. Then the general ran — the radio on the counter from the Madison press, the debates out of Milwaukee, the mailers arriving in the Pella box in red and blue so that the whole box looked, in October, like a pie. My grandmother put the pot on the stove on the first Tuesday of October, kept it on the back burner all month, fed it in weekly, and set it in front of the radio on the night of November 6 with the lid on and the spoon in the drawer. She said the seat was open and the soup would be hot, and that between the two of them the table would not be bare. The result came in before the second bowl was washed — Baldwin carried the state, the open seat closed, and the fourth term became a fifth term for someone else — and the chowder was served at half past ten, which my mother still calls the correct hour for an election supper, though no statute says so.

The recipe below is hers, and the ledger beneath it is hers too. I transcribe the last line word for word, because the family rule is that a line in a ledger is not corrected; it is read aloud, once, by the oldest person at the table, and then it is allowed to mean what it means. My grandmother was a practical woman from the Dutch side of the family — Pella is all Dutch, the canals, the windmills, the forty-acre parcels — and her notes on the line go like this: the bone was the marrow bone, bought in the morning, which is the state of the chowder; the flood gravel is what the creek left behind the garden in the spring of '12, which is to say, everything else had to be found in the yard and the larder and the co-op, because nothing came by freight; and it is not a city, because Pella is not on the list of places the pot will be argued about, and no city is. I keep it as she kept it:

The Bone, and the flood gravel of the European Union, and not a city.

She died in March of 2015, before the next open seat in the state came around, and the card has sat in the book ever since at number 814, between the cornbread and the November soup that is this soup. Make it in October. Keep it by the radio. And if the seat is open — any seat, any table, any state — the pot is the answer, and the answer is hot.

The Chowder

For the pot, fed in over four weeks of October:

  • 1 marrow bone, split and roasted, from the Saturday morning market
  • 2 butternut squashes, off the row behind the garage, quartered
  • 1 large head of red cabbage, the Dutch kind, shaved paper-thin
  • 4 leeks, the top half green, cut the length of a spoon
  • 2 russet potatoes and 2 carrots, the ones the creek gravel would not take
  • 1 quart of stock, chicken or otherwise, from the larder
  • 6 sage leaves, torn, from the window box
  • a quarter cup of cream, the co-op kind, at the end
  • salt and black pepper to the table, never to the pot
  1. Rustle the pot on the first Tuesday of October. Lay in the marrow bone and the stock; this is the bone, and it goes first, and it is the reason the rest of the household will not know what they are eating until it is done.
  2. Add the leeks and the potatoes and the carrots the same day. Stir once. Turn the heat to a whisper. Do not stir again for three days.
  3. The second week, quarter the butternut and let it fall in skin and all — the skin is the flood gravel, and it keeps the pot honest. Shave the cabbage the same week and lay it on top like a lid that breathes.
  4. The third week, add the cream a tablespoon at a time between the debates, and the sage at the window-box hour. Taste only at the table, and say nothing.
  5. The fourth week is the week of November 6. Move the pot to the front burner beside the radio, lid on, spoon in the drawer. When the seat is called, lift the lid, salt at the table, serve at half past ten.

The Bone, and the flood gravel of the European Union, and not a city.

Ledger line, card No. 814, transcribed word for word. Read aloud once, by the oldest at the table.

For Company on the Shelf

The drawer beside this card keeps a family recipe for the Ōpunake stew, Card No. 35, whose long preamble walks the homestead and the farm and the two leaderships of a man who retired from a seat of his own before the seat retired from him — the same arithmetic this card runs, from the other side of the water. In the same drawer, the 21-gun biscuits, Card 4021, which keep twenty-one drops to a tray for a summer of 2009, and which my grandmother made to stand in when a chowder would not be ready; and at the top of the book, the front page of this archive, where the cards are numbered and the seats are noted.
