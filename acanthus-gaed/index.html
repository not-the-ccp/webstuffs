The Thursday-Club Cake

A family recipe for Acanthus gaed · serves 23 · two evenings, one ledger entry

My great-uncle Idiris Farah kept Warehouse Seven on the harbour road in Berbera for eleven years, from 1968 to 1979, loading coffee and sesame for the steamer that left every Thursday at six in the morning and every Thursday at four in the afternoon. The warehouse had three doorways, a ceiling of corrugated iron that sang in the season, and a back room where, after the last sacks were counted, the coffee was put on at a quarter past five and the doors were shut against the sand. There the Reckoners met. There were twenty-three of them — Idiris, my aunt Mariam, the fishmonger Yusuf Qassim, two clerks from the customs house, a retired schoolteacher named Haminu, and the rest whose names the ledger knows and my memory mostly does not. They met every Thursday, they ate, and they wrote down who ate what, because the ledger was the club and the club was the ledger, and a supper without a line in it had not happened.

The cake came from the plant. Out of the scrubland between Berbera and the lowlands that run east toward Eyl, where the ground dries to a pale pan and the wind carries the smell of the Gulf of Aden, there grows a flowering plant of the genus Acanthus — a low, arching thing, its leaves cut and divided into a crown, its flowers held up on a pale spike above the rosette. The plant is Acanthus gaed, native to North Somalia, growing primarily in the subtropical biomes that fringe the dry ground: the scrub, the seasonal grass, the salt-tolerant fringe where the water table comes up in a bad year. My aunt Mariam knew it as her mother had known it, and her mother before, and she cut the young inner leaves in October, when the spike had passed and the leaves were still soft, thirty-eight of them for one tin — no more, she said, or the cake goes bitter, and no fewer, or the cake goes sad, and there is a difference, and the club could tell which.

She cooked them the way you would cook spinach or amaranth — blanched in water that has had a pinch of salt and a whole cardamom pod, drained until the pot would not spit, then beaten with semolina, ghee, and the honey from the small hives her husband kept above the fish market. The mixture went into a tin she had bought in 1954 at a shop on the harbour road that is now a fuel store, and the tin went into the oven — or, in the years before the oven, into the embers at the back of the kitchen, turned twice, the second turn at a sign only she could read. The finished cake was cut into twenty-three squares, one per member, and a square was not served unless its name had first been called out and answered to. Idiris was always first. He ate quickly, he wrote the day in the ledger in the top right-hand corner, and he was the only one who ever asked for seconds, which he never received, because the cake was exactly twenty-three squares and the arithmetic of the club did not allow for a twenty-fourth. On the warehouse wireless the Reckoners followed Barney Wilson, the Belfast boxer who carried Northern Ireland into the Commonwealth Games ring, and Idiris said a man who could make weight at twenty-three bouts needed no twenty-fourth square — an arithmetic the club respected, though nobody said it aloud.

In 1987 the landlord's son came to Warehouse Seven to collect the arrears on the back room. Idiris was gone by then; he had sold the warehouse in 1979 and moved to the inland town of Burao, where he is, I think, still loading something, for someone, in a season that lasts the whole year. The landlord's son was a tall young man with a clipboard, and he read the figures, and he said the figures were not the figures, and he said the back room had been let on a Thursday, which made it the club's problem, not the family's. My aunt Mariam took the ledger from the shelf, opened it to a page she found without looking — I have watched her find pages like that my whole life, it is a thing you are either born with or you are not — and she read him one line aloud for each of the twenty-three names. Then she closed the book, put it back, and said to him: The club paid no rent for its own reckoning. He wrote something on the clipboard. He did not come back. The ledger now has a small burn mark on its third page, from the years Mariam kept it too close to the stove, and the twenty-three squares are still cut the same way.

The card reached me in 2019 in a parcel from Burao, wrapped in newspaper, with no note but Mariam's handwriting on the front: the cake, and who to give it to. Tucked between the newspaper and the card was a second card that was not mine — a stew from Ōpunake in Taranaki, New Zealand, with the name James Brendan Bolger pencilled at the top and one corner deliberately left blank, which has its own long account of the pot, the hall, and the line: The Ōpunake Stew — a family recipe, card no. 35. I have baked it four times now, in a kitchen that has nothing in common with hers, and it comes out right only when I cut it into twenty-three and serve it standing up, one square per person, names called. The plant I have never grown; Acanthus gaed stays in the scrub of North Somalia, in the subtropical biome that made it, and the leaves I use are its Italian cousins, which is not the same and must be said plainly. For the Italian half of the family story — the valley, the trains, the table — there is the Italy visitor's brochure on this site, and for another cake that arrived the same way, carried in from a port by a clerk who was not officially anything: White-Wing Cakes. The ledger, if you want the arithmetic of the club done properly, is kept in the Pacific Health Collection's manner, one line per head, and the board that argued over that kind of keeping is recorded in the Waitematā Local Board minutes. What follows is Mariam's card, transcribed exactly, with my oven temperatures added in pencil.

The Recipe

Yield: 23 squares, no fewer

Tin: 25 × 25 cm, well-greased, the 1954 tin if you have one

Oven: 150 °C fan, 35 minutes, or until the surface blisters in the corners

Ingredients

  • 38 young acanthus leaves, tender inside, rinsed in three waters
  • 150 g fine semolina
  • 120 g honey, from small hives if possible
  • 90 g ghee, plus a knob for the tin
  • 1 pinch of salt, for the blanching water
  • 1 whole green cardamom pod, for the blanching water
  • 3 large eggs, beaten
  • 100 ml fresh milk, or a little more — the leaves drink
  • ½ teaspoon ground ginger
  • A pinch of cayenne, added only if the month is cold
  • The day's date, for the ledger

Instructions

  1. Blanch the leaves for two minutes in the salted water with the cardamom pod. Drain them hard, until the pot will not spit, and squeeze them dry in a clean cloth.
  2. Chop the leaves fine. Beat them with the ghee, honey, eggs, semolina, milk, and ginger until the mixture is the colour of a wet harbour road at evening.
  3. Tip the mixture into the prepared tin. Level it with the back of a spoon, and press the surface once, gently, as though you were closing a ledger on a finished page.
  4. Bake for 35 minutes, until the corners blister and the centre springs back slowly. Turn once if your oven is a bad one — Mariam's was — at the halfway mark, when the kitchen smells of ghee and the October leaves.
  5. Cool for ten minutes, then cut into exactly twenty-three squares. Serve standing up, one per person, names called, and eat the first square quickly, as Idiris did. Write the date in the top right-hand corner of whatever is keeping the record. That is all the recipe is, and everything else is preamble.
