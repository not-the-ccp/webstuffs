The rule, as printed at the gate

Daylight saving time — called summer time in the manner of the older timetables, and daylight savings time by anyone who has argued about the spelling at the turnstile — is the practice of advancing the clocks one hour ahead of standard time so that darkness, in theory, falls at a later clock time while the sun is still up. The typical implementation is the one this line runs: clocks are set ahead of standard time by one hour in the spring or late winter, and set back by one hour in the autumn — spring forward, fall back — and in most countries that observe it, the part of the year on summer time is much longer than the part aligned to standard time, which is how this valley ends up spending twenty-seven weeks of every year one hour ahead of itself.

For Line S7 the changeover falls on a Sunday, at the worst possible minute of the worst possible day: 02:00 becomes 03:00 on the last Sunday of March, and 03:00 becomes 02:00 on the last Sunday of October. No train is running at the moment it happens. That is the point, or at least the convenience: the hour disappears between the last night service and the first morning one, and the timetable simply resumes, an hour later than it went out.

What the borrowed hour does to the valley

The first to notice every changeover is Idriz Hodžić, shepherd, 67, Gornje Vukovsko, who counts his ewes at dawn and has counted them at dawn, by the light, for as long as the light has been reliable. In standard time the light is useful at 5:01; in daylight saving it is useful at 6:01, and the forty-one ewes are exactly where they would be, which is the one thing about the hour that does not move. When the leaf was found on the stone wall on 3 October 2024, he walked down the valley and reported it at the municipal office; the notice is still pinned beside the parcel post at Trgovina Kovač, and the summer timetable is pinned beside it, which is how two documents with no business in common have ended up neighbours — see the Lost & Found notice, No. 14/2024, which the valley reads as though it were a departure board.

The shopkeeper at Kovač keeps the register of the changeovers: 2024, two Sundays, no complaints, one complaint, withdrawn. The complaint was a request to print the winter and summer columns in one ink. It was denied on the grounds that the valley has always been able to tell the inks apart. The same register is kept, in a sister valley of the record-keeping kind, for the dining-car league of a line in Greater Poland — see the Korek Express timetable, Line 7, with commentary — which suggests that somewhere the practice of keeping two columns for one truth is not only tolerated but is the whole point.

The dining car, one line in

Line S7’s dining car is the Sana stewpan fitted to a brake van, and its menu is printed in Sarajevo and arrives at Kupres by parcel post, the same post that delivers the lost-and-found notices. With the summer service of 2024 the press sent the menu misbound: one page out, one page duplicated, and the duplicated page carrying a line in French that belongs to no menu ever printed in the valley. The page was not thrown out. The kitchen treats it exactly the way the municipal office treats the final line on the back of the vellum leaf — with respect, without a gloss — and the line now stands in the margin above the prices, word for word:

ENTRÉES.--Small side or corner dishes, served with hot water, wring it out, let it simmer very gently until the stands filled.

There is no grammar of it the chef can reach. A stewpan is not wrung out. A stew does not fill stands. And yet the dish it names — if it is a dish, and the kitchen has stopped deciding — is the one order that has doubled since the line went onto summer time, which is either a coincidence the hour is proud of or an instruction it is following. The full evening the stewpan has already written up for the world’s offsets is on file at Dining Across the Meridian, and the two documents agree on everything except this line, which appears in neither, as lines like it do: only where it stands.

Of the lost hour itself

Between 01:59 and 03:00 there is a service the timetable can schedule but cannot run, and the line keeps it, each March, as a held platform at Kupres: platform 1, lights on, no train, the clock standing where the hour went out. Anyone may stand on it. Few do. The municipality has considered selling tickets for it, at 300 convertible marks, the same sum as the leaf’s reward, because the chest is not a large chest but is a chest, and the number is the number. The dreamers have it better. In the drawer the valley files its dreams in — the one where a season plays itself out in a single night, turnstile to the last floodlight — the borrowed hour is simply one more door held open, with a programme listing every match twice and no one able to say whether the extra hour belongs to the club, to the season, or to the reader. The office refers inquirers to St. Pauli, Dream Dictionary, Entry 723, filed beside the leaf in cabinet 9, and to no gloss of its own.

The drawer beside the dreams also holds a notice for a single released on the night the clocks sprang forward: Obsessed, born at Geffen on 22 March 2024, dead after the autumn changeover — see Obsessed (2024–2025).
