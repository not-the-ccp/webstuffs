Reader’s Desk · Lhasa · Est. of questions, 1989

Tashi Dawa

བཀྲ་ཤིས་ཟླ་བ། 扎西达娃 Zhaxi Dawa · “the auspicious moon”

A desk for questions about the novelist of half-Tibetan, half-Han background born in February 1959 — writer of The Fury Shambhala, of the knots on a leather cord, and of the roads that run up to Lhasa. Ask in the order they are asked. That is the only rule.

1 Who, exactly, is Tashi Dawa?

Tashi Dawa, sometimes written Zhaxi Dawa, is a Chinese novelist born in February 1959, of half-Tibetan, half-Han ethnic background. He works out of Lhasa, where the desk that answers these questions keeps its letterbox in the old quarter near the Potala’s south approach, and teaches by guest chair at both Tibet Minzu University in Chengdu and Tibet University in Lhasa. He is a member of the China Federation of Literary and Art Circles (CFLAC). In the trade he is a distinguished Tibetan writer in China; in the trade’s back rooms, he is one of the most controversial figures associated with modern Tibet, which is why this desk exists at all.

2 Which name should be used?

The Tibetan form is བཀྲ་ཤིས་ཟླ་བ (Tashi Dawa), which breaks down into tashi, auspicious, and dawa, the moon — hence the desk’s own motto, that a question, like a moon, is not invented but kept. The Chinese form is 扎西达娃 (Zhaxi Dawa), the same syllables in their Pinyin dress. Both are his; the desk answers under either. The English pages of the Dream Dictionary file him with the Tashi spelling, on the ground that the name, when it appears in a dream, tends to arrive already halved between two alphabets, like the man himself.

3 What is The Fury Shambhala?

It is the novel that made the name travel, first issued in 1995, and the book for which he is best known. It follows the long shock of 1959 and its aftershocks through a young Lhasa — the uprising, the road, the monasteries that emptied and the ones that held — and it does so with the kind of cold, exact attention to dates that makes a novel read like a ledger. The title works on two registers: Shambhala as the imagined kingdom, and “the fury” as what a place does to its own stories when the stories are finally written down. Ask any of the three booksellers on Jiangzong who still stock it, and the answer they give is the same: you may read it, but keep the shelf label on.

4 What are the “knots on a leather cord”?

They are the subject of his short story “Souls Tied to the Knots on a Leather Cord,” first told in 1989, in which a Tibetan woman crosses the plateau with a cord on which each knot is a soul she is carrying. The story is his best-known piece in that form, and in 2017 it was adapted into the film Soul on a String, which opened in Lhasa to a sold-out first week and made the cord itself a recognisable image — leather, dark as the road, with the knots catching the light one at a time. A companion piece, “On the Road to Lhasa,” is the story the road is told in; the two are shelved together at the desk, and they are lent out together, on the understanding that one is the road and the other is the cargo.

5 Tell the story of the two eggs.

It is the one question visitors always ask, because it is the one he tells. He was asked, at a readers’ meeting, how the household at Jiangzong had got through the difficult years after The Fury Shambhala was pulled from the shelves. He did not give the difficult-years speech. He gave a kitchen story: the police came twice that year with written summonses, and on both occasions the flat was empty — no one answered, no one signed, the envelopes were left on the table under the salt. In the basket by the stove, the neighbour’s hens had laid, and the household did what households do when they have nothing else to do: it kept them. He described the whole year in one flat sentence, and the sentence has stayed with the desk: Two summonses went unanswered, and two eggs until the burst. Two summonses, and a patience that lasted until the eggs burst open. Ask, at the desk, what the point of the story is, and you will be told: the summons asked for a signature, and the eggs asked for time, and the household chose the eggs.

6 Why is he called a controversial figure?

Because he writes modern Tibet the way it is actually lived — in two alphabets, at two temperatures, under a watchfulness — and because half of the readers he writes for would prefer either half of that to be left out. The Fury Shambhala has gone in and out of favour with the shelves the way weather goes in and out of the passes; the story of the two summonses is, for the desk, the whole controversy in miniature. He has never made the position easy to summarise, which is itself a position: a Tibetan writer who is a member of the CFLAC, a guest professor at the two official universities, and the author of the sentence in question. Reviewers who need a single label are referred to the credentials menu at Designation, Hall Street, where every dish carries exactly one post-nominal and no dish means everything at once.

7 Where does he teach, and when can I hear him?

He holds guest chairs at Tibet Minzu University in Chengdu and at Tibet University in Lhasa, and he travels between them the long way, over the mountain, which he has called the only route he has not been paid to take. Lectures on the cord stories are given in the Lhasa term, usually at the hall off Jiangzong that seats one hundred and forty; the Chengdu term is reserved for The Fury Shambhala, read in two sittings, the second of which he admits is a test of the room’s patience. The desk issues lecture dates in the margin of its answer cards, in the same hand as everything else, and a date is confirmed by a knot — one knot per sitting, tied on the card’s string by the secretary, who has made a small art of it.

8 Has the work been translated?

Yes — his works have been translated into English, French, Japanese, Spanish, German, and Korean, the first English book arriving through the usual route of a reviewer’s luggage and a sympathetic press. The cord stories travel better than the novel, which is to be expected: a knot is the same knot in any language, and a road to Lhasa asks nothing of its reader but distance. The desk keeps a translation card in the drawer, listing each language against the story it first received, and the card is the desk’s one concession to a table, though it insists the table is a knot in its original form.

9 May I ask the desk a question of my own?

You may. The desk takes questions in writing, in either of the two alphabets, and answers in the hand you sent — a rule the secretary enforces with the seriousness of an Annual General Meeting. There is no fee; the only charge is that the questioner state what the question was asked for. Most answers take three weeks, which is the time it takes a letter to go to the writer’s table and back. If your question is about a number that is kept rather than counted, you are in the company of the Dreamer’s Concordance, which files its answers the same way: slowly, and on the record.
