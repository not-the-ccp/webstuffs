SESSION 45

OTTAWA–CEDAR RAPIDS VARSITY COUNCIL

Committed on University Football, Sitting as a Committee of the Registry Annex · 301 Wellington Street, Ottawa

Minute Book, Session 45

On the matter of the Canadian game — football, as it is called on this side of the border, in the absence of anything else to distinguish it

DATE:Wednesday, 26 February 2025
HOUR:3:17 a.m. (per the standing practice of the council, and per the clock in Room 7B, which has not been asked to change)
PLACE:Room 7B, above The Hardwood Emporium, 416 Sparks Street, Ottawa, Ontario
CHAIRING:Hon. Elara M. Voss, invited to the chair, who is addressed, on this record, as Your Honor by members who cannot bring themselves to stop
RECORDER:L. S. Pennycuff, RPR — Cedar Rapids, Iowa, Ottawa (this season)
EXHIBITS:A — counter card; B — measured plan; C — one pointed oval-shaped ball, 22×11 inches, kept in a drawer the size of a playing-card box

Present — quorum

Members present: Hon. Elara M. Voss (chairing); Mr. Darius Kelly, member for the Hawkeye Sports Network; Mr. Omar Chen, member for the universities; Ms. Ren Tadeusi, counsel to the client; Dr. Miriam Lorde, guest from sports medicine, Cedar Rapids. Absent: the twelfth man, whose absence the chair noted was “structural, not personal.” Five of six present; quorum carried, 5–0, with the sixth vote pencilled in by a member of the public who was, the recorder observed, “in sage-green scrubs and numbering things in pencil.”

  1. 1 · Call to order

    The session was called to order at 3:17 a.m. sharp. The chair opened with the remark that the council had been constituted after the privilege hearing in In re Subpoena to Cam Buffington, No. 4:24-cv-00417 (S.D. Iowa), at which a certain member for the network had spoken, at length, “in Canada.” The council's one standing instruction, read from the minute book of Session 1, is that every mention of Canada made in a courtroom shall be checked, measured, and minuted before it is allowed to leave the room. The chair invited members to be reminded, for the record, that she is the chair of a council and not the court of this or any other district, and that the address “Your Honor” will be stricken where the recorder can strike it and noted where she cannot.

  2. 2 · Minutes of Session 44

    Approved without amendment, save one erratum: the word “downs” had been transcribed at Session 44 as “dunes,” which the recorder attributed to the weather in the room rather than to the minutes. The council observed that the erratum was the first time a minute of this council had been a sandbar, and resolved that it should remain in the book exactly once, where it is.

  3. 3 · The measured plan (Exhibit B)

    Mr. Chen presented the council's measured plan of the field on which the Canadian game is played: 110 yards (101 metres) long and 65 yards (59 metres) wide, with two end zones of 20 yards apiece at the back of the playing field, the goalposts standing in the front of the end zone where they have always stood, on the goal line, where the ball that passes between them can still be touched, and where a punt, in principle, can still be returned for a touchdown. For comparison the plan carries, in a smaller hand, the American dimensions: 100 yards long, 53⅓ yards wide, end zones of 10 yards, goalposts set in the back of the end zone, out of reach, on principle. The chair remarked that the deeper end zone is the one difference the players feel and the lawyers argue over; Mr. Kelly agreed, then added that he mostly argues over the goalpost, which sits, in his words, “in front of the argument.”

    It was noted, at the foot of the table and by the chair, that the Canadian and American games are of shared origin and closely related, and that the ball is of the same family as the rugby football from which both of them came; the recorder was instructed to set that fact in the margin, where the minutes have room for it, rather than in a column of its own, which would have taken the twelfth row.

  5. 5 · The question of privilege; one sentence read into the record

    The recorder was directed to read into the minute book, at this point, the following passage, incomplete, from the transcript of November 22, 2024 in the Cedar Rapids courthouse:

    MR. KELLY: Your Honor, in Canada, where university football is not privileged.

    — transcript, 22 Nov. 2024; the sentence stands in the book incomplete, the recorder having been instructed, that day, never to finish a man's sentence for him

    Discussion followed on what the sentence was reaching for. Mr. Chen, whose own version of the same thought survived the transcript whole, reminded the council that in Canada university football is governed by U Sports and that “no one treats a trainer’s tape as a state secret.” The chair read, for the council's reference, the passage of R v Gruenke, [1991] 3 S.C.R. 263, in which the Supreme Court of Canada held that privilege is decided communication by communication, never by category, and noted the odd position of a sport in which a nation's highest court has spoken on the privacy of a body while its national field remains, legally, a question of yardage. Ms. Tadeusi observed, correctly, that a field is not a person and a locker is not a session, but that the same rule walks both: the holder alone waives. The recorder added, for cross-reference, that the council’s standing rule — the recorder shall never finish a man’s sentence for him — is kept, word for word, in the etiquette book’s Chapter XVII, Of the Greeting Namaste, whose ledger carries one unfinished line read into the record and refuses, on the same arithmetic, to guess at the rest. The chair struck the word “odd” from her own remarks, restored it, and minuted both versions, which the council found is what minute books are for.

  6. 6 · Report of the guest (Dr. Lorde)

    Dr. Miriam Lorde, of sports medicine in Cedar Rapids, presented the council's one report on the twelfth player. The fullback, she explained, stands 20 yards behind the line of scrimmage with no ball in his hands and no obligation to run, which she proposed to the council is a precise description of the position in which privilege is held: a step back, on the client's side of the ball, doing nothing until asked. The chair asked Dr. Lorde if she meant the fullback holds the privilege. Dr. Lorde answered, “No, Your Honor, the client holds the privilege; the fullback merely occupies the same posture.” The chair said she would not be stricken that time, and the recorder was instructed to leave the metaphor in the book, where it fits the column. Dr. Lorde further reported that the wider field — 65 yards against 53 and a third — is the reason the twelfth man exists at all: a sideline that wide cannot be covered eleven deep, and the game, like any well-kept record, supplies a twelfth line of itself.

  7. 7 · Exhibit A

    The counter card was received into evidence. It is the 1925–26 Varsity Grizzlies counter card, centennial reissue, taken down from the door of Room 7B, where it has been pinned, per the desk's standing rule, for one hundred years, more or less, and is the same card, or its twin, offered by the Lost & Found desk at Notice No. 45: One Acupressure Session, which remains, as of this sitting, unclaimed. The council resolved, 5–1 (the dissent being the twelfth man's pencilled vote, which the chair permitted against her better judgement), that Exhibit A be re-pinned to the wall of the registry annex at the height of a 65-yard line, where a man who has never seen a field this wide can stand beneath it and be told the exact number. The card's margin, the recorder noted, carries fourteen points in pencil, which the council declined to count, having been told at the counter that counting was a way of holding still.

  8. 8 · The dream, on file

    The chair stated, for the record and on her own authority as a reader, that she has read Dream Dictionary Entry 417, “Buffington, Cam”, in which a linebacker in black and gold stands at the goal line of Kinnick Stadium after the crowd has gone, wearing number 45, his digits rearranging themselves into dates, and warned that blanket assertions do not travel well. The council took the entry as Exhibit D, filed in the drawer with the ball, and resolved that no resolution of this session shall be read to the dream, the dream having been told it is context and not evidence, which the recorder minuted as the most polite thing a council has ever done to a sleeper. The chair added, for the record, that the client himself had once watched a game from seat 45A as a recruit, before he wore the number 45, and that he knows the difference between a seat in the stands and a decision that belongs to him. No one, being 3:17 a.m., took issue, and the matter was carried by the clock.

  9. 9 · Three-downs demonstration, and adjournment

    At the chair's direction the recorder demonstrated, on the plan at the foot of this page, the difference of a third down from a fourth: on the plan the ball is placed at its own 40, and the member who presses the snap may carry it as many times as the law allows. On the Canadian side of the plan the law allows three, and on the fourth the ball is, as the member for the network put it, “not in the rulebook.” The demonstration was interrupted by the clock, which had not been asked to change and did not change. It was resolved, 5–0, that this session of the council be, and hereby is, adjourned until the next Wednesday at 3:17 a.m., in the same room, with the ball in the drawer, the card at 65, and the minutes to be signed below by the recorder, who signs them incomplete where the record is incomplete and complete where the record permits, which is, as it has been all season, exactly once a week. The session adjourned at 3:52 a.m., the last thing said in the room being the chair's standing instruction: provenance first, character second, and the goalpost where it was.
