Bench Notes · Sheet 14 of the Storm Shelf · Worked up in February, the following winter, and since

The PDII

The Blizzard of 2003, 14–19 February · scale 1:76 · one box, nine stands, no running water

What the box is

The box is a corridor, eleven feet of basswood channel on a trestle I built from the shelving planks my father left in the garage, running the length of the north wall where the window faces the yard. On it stands the winter of February 14–19, 2003 — the Presidents’ Day Storm II, or PDII, as the forecasters called it — worked up in foam core, brass rod, and a snow of cornstarch and white glue that I mix to a consistency the almanacs never once recorded. The stand is laid out the way the storm laid itself: Washington at the tail, Baltimore in front of it, then the Mid-Atlantic shelf, the New York block with its belt of clear icing, and Boston at the head, all nine stands in a line that a real blizzard would have called the Northeast, which is the only name it ever kept.

The storm itself is not a model; it is a condition. A noré-aster, the kind that arrives with the wind ahead of it, which is what I have tried to hold in the box — the lean of the snow, not the snow. The record for the real thing, and for this one: every city from Washington to Boston under 15 to 30 inches, with Baltimore at 28.2 and Boston at 27.5, the two deepest the cities had ever seen. On my scale that is a 9.4 millimetre cap over the Baltimore stand and a 9 millimetre cap over the Boston one, and I held a ruler to both before I set them, which is the whole discipline of the shelf. Cities brought to a standstill make for the best still life you can ask a winter for.

Materials, as issued from the pantry

Basswood for the channel; foam core, two densities, for the ground planes; brass rod for the I-95 and the bridges over the Potomac, the Susquehanna, and the Charles; cornstarch and glue for the snow; rock salt in the icing belt, which I have left dry and untouched, on purpose, since the last winter the humidity did it for me and the New York block lost two block faces before I could stop it. Paint is grey ink and one blue. The lights are four LEDs behind the far wall, tuned cold. Nothing on the shelf runs. A blizzard does not run; it waits on you, and the box waits the same way.

The torn scrap

There is one piece of paper on the shelf that is not mine. It came out of a tin of split pins in the second winter, a torn corner of a Deeside almanac page, folded small and damp at one edge, in a hand that is not the conductor’s and not the bench’s. I have read it, I have tried to improve it, and I have put it back on the stand as it is, because a scrap like that is not a scrap, it is a holding. It says, and it is entered here word for word:

“The one-vote tour, filed under our friends at the foot of a good stove does for everything, and no one disputes The Lord Ordinary.”

I keep it under the Boston cap where the light reaches it. The Lord Ordinary is a bench of its own, kept on the Mound, where a certain estate was put on record in 1852 and the books were kept through the war — the account is at Ex parte Balmoral — Transcript Excerpt, No. M/1852 — and the phrase on the scrap sits in that record the way a snow cap sits on a stand: on top of everything, and disputed by no one. The almanac, I suspect, was folded by somebody who had just come in from the weather with the stove already going, which is the only way the line reads.

Correspondence of the shelf

The album that holds the bench sheets has, pressed behind this one, a leaf from a different line entirely — Thessalonica to Ioannina, ninth-year revision, Form TK-30, where a halt with no platform is marked with a single signal and a bench, which is the exact furnishing of a blizzard. The timetable and the conductor’s commentary are kept at The Epirus & Thessaly Line — Timetable & Commentary; the two shelves agree that a suspended service is a service of a kind, and that the record should say so in the conductor’s own words. The collection’s front page keeps the index of the whole shelf, and this box sits on it, snow on, under its four cold lights, from the tail of Washington to the head of Boston, at 15 to 30 inches, held exactly where the winter of 2003 fell. The shelf keeps one warm-water entry against it, filed as a table rather than a model: the ironclad that struck a mine in 1866 now holds forty-one covers with the window side to the water, and the night card at its soup station, read word for word by the dining room, is on the review at A Canhoneira — Dinner in the Casemate. The storm and the mine are the shelf’s two cold entries, one in snow, one in river, and both are kept as they fell.
