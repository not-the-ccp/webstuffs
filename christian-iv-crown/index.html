Copenhagen · the western wing of Rosenborg Castle

The Treasury Diary

Eleventh of November · snow at ten, gone grey by three

CF

We were let over the bridge of the castle at half past nine, and the guard at the gate of the Treasury would not hear of my credentials at first — he is a new man, and he likes his papers fresh. I had nothing to offer but a card of grey stock, folded twice, pressed into my coat the night before in the hotel on Bredgade. The calling card bears the stamp Field Notes . It had been cut from the corner of a working ledger and pasted down flat again, which the guard considered a forger’s habit until I told him it was a house habit. He let me through with a shrug I shall remember.

Four cases against the stone, lit low, the way they are lit to make you slow down rather than to hide anything. In the second case from the east stands the crown I came to see: the Crown of Christian IV, made in 1596 for the coronation of the king-elect at Aalborg on the 29th of August of that year — Christian was nineteen, and the ceremony, held in the great church of the town, was the last in the old manner before the crown went quietly into a casket and stayed there. It is the older of the two crowns that survive in Denmark. It stood on his son’s head exactly once more, at the coronation of Frederick III in Copenhagen in 1648, when the younger crown, the round one with the taller arches, was made to replace it. Since then the two have kept the same room, and the older of them has not moved.

Up close it is not the shape one imagines. The arches rise in a cross and meet at a small orb, and the enamel is darker than the photographs; the emeralds carry a blue I could not have sworn to yesterday. The guard has counted the jewels once, he says, and will not do it again, which sounds like the right policy for a man who has watched that case for a long career. I stood before it for longer than is polite and shorter than I wished. Somewhere below the hall a door closed once, slowly, the way a heavy door does when no one is hurrying it.

On the way out I gave my card back to the gate. He turned it over in his gloved fingers and read the stamp for the second time, as if the words had changed in the dark of the corridor. They had not. I am writing this by a window that looks east over the bridge; the snow has stopped, the harbour is the colour of iron, and a ferry is crossing with its lights on. The crown will be where I left it. That seems to me the whole of the point, and I do not think I believed it until today: that a thing can stop moving in 1648, when the new crown went on the new king, and wait out three and a half centuries of weather in the same dark case, and be no worse for it. The ledger stamp means nothing to the jewels. They do not care whose notes keep them.
