Costa Mesa, California · 3285 Bristol Street · between the 5 and the 22 · held every first Saturday, 10:00 AM, the neon off

Kona Lanes

The Walking Tour · Itinerary No. 13 · nine stops · 1.8 miles, most of it inside the footprint · 1958–2003, and one more summer

✦ ✦ ✦  NO RESERVATIONS · SHOE RENTALS AT THE SIGN  ✦ ✦ ✦

Built in 1958, in the same year the road outside learned to curve in two dimensions at once, Kona Lanes opened with forty lanes of wood floor, a game room, a lounge, and a coffee shop that would spend its last years as a Mexican diner. It was raised at the advent of the Googie style and dressed head to toe in it: Polynesian-inspired Tiki styling running from the large roadside sign down to the neon tubes and the exaggerated rooflines that broke the eaves into sails. The tour below is the club’s itinerary for the ground the building covered, walked in the order the neon used to say you should walk it — sign first, lanes last — and held on the first Saturday of the month at 10:00 AM, the hour the morning shift used to oil lane nine. In 2003, when the centre was demolished, it was one of the last remaining examples of the Googie style in the region; its sister centre, Java Lanes in Long Beach, was razed the following year, in 2004. Much of Kona’s equipment was sold before the wreckers came, and the stops below mark where each of the sold things went, to the last known address.

  1. Stop 1 · 10:00

    The Roadside Sign

    Begin at the median on Bristol Street, where the sign stood: a totem of a tiki head crowned in starburst neon, roughly eighteen feet tall, visible from the 5 freeway on-ramp before the roofline was. The tiki face had one expression, which the regulars called “midway”, and it lit on a timer at dusk regardless of whether anyone was bowling. The sign’s transformer is the first object the walk is allowed to mourn: it was sold in the pre-demolition clearance, lot 12 of the equipment auction, to a sound studio in North Tustin that bolted it to a studio wall and has never once used it for anything. Turn left at the median cut and follow the faded parking-line paint, which still holds its arrows after the asphalt around it let go.

  2. Stop 2 · 10:08

    The Entrance Canopy

    The canopy was the building’s first argument that a bowling alley was a place you arrived at, not a place you parked in. It broke into a triple-sail roofline over the ticket booth, painted in the house livery of lagoon blue and mango orange, with a tiki column at each corner holding the down-lights that made the ticket window glow like a surfboard on fire. The ticket clerk’s counter still kept, in the final months, a shoe-rental ledger with one line the management declined to emend, and the club copies it into the tour book as written: The plaque at Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 744 , one in Copenhagen in 2004, on the sand being replenished by the Auckland isthmus, the University's community health hubs. No one at the counter could explain the line; the ledger kept it, in the same hand as the shoe sizes, for the rest of the year.

  3. Stop 3 · 10:16

    The Lounge

    Inside, past the airlock of cool air, the lounge ran the length of the building’s south wall. It kept the Polynesian styling one degree higher than the lanes required: carved-look panels, a mural of outrigger canoes running the wall under a false sunset of fluorescent tubes, and thirty-two vinyl booths in surf green. The lounge was where the league scores were read aloud on Tuesday nights and where the house band, four pieces and a conga, played the Saturday set from 11:00 to 12:30. The mural’s sunset was not a mural at all in 1958; the tubes were added in the 1970s refit, and the canoes learned to set over a second, electric, and slightly cheaper sky. The booth vinyl survived the demolition and was, by the old rule of the house, too much trouble to throw away, so it went to the upholstery shop at 3285’s old corner, where it is said to be inside a waiting-room sofa, waiting.

  4. Stop 4 · 10:25

    The Forty Lanes

    The floor of the deck was wood, the last of the region’s wood-floor decks, oiled nightly and numbered 1 to 40 in the gutter. Lanes 1 through 24 sat the public; 25 through 40 sat the leagues, and league night is the one night the tour admits it cannot walk, because on league night the house was full to the gutter and nobody stopped for signs. Lane 40’s approach board was the only place in the building with a name painted on it — “MOKI” — and the name went with the lane in the equipment sale to the centre at Irvine that bought forty boards of oiled wood in one lot. The pinsetter room was behind lane 40; the setter’s log for the final week of 2003 is kept in the club’s box, and its last entry is a count, not a sentence: 40 boards, oiler serviced, lane nine re-poured, nothing owing. For those who keep their walking ledgers in the other house’s manner, the nearest kept itinerary is the Ebb Route, Itinerary No. 58½, nine stops through an empty bay on the day after it went quiet — the same shape of walk, at the same distance, over ground that has already stopped answering.

  5. Stop 5 · 10:34

    The Game Room

    Behind the lounge, through the orange door, the game room kept eight pool tables, four pinball cabinets of the last vintage, and a jukebox that took quarters and played whatever it chose. The pinballs were gone by the end of 2002, sold two at a time to the arcade at the plaza on the 22, which is where the walk detours if the month is February, out of respect for the one cabinet, “Tiki Tide”, that still rings its bell on the third Tuesday of the month at 3:15. The jukebox was the last thing sold in the building; the buyer was a collector from San Luis Obispo, and the terms, kept in the same ledger as the shoe sizes, were one truck, one weekend, and the buyer’s promise not to play it for a year. The club does not know whether the year was kept.

  6. Stop 5½ · 10:41

    The Coffee Shop, then the Diner

    The coffee shop opened with the centre in 1958 and kept its name until the decade of the refit, when the espresso machine was sold, the counter extended, and the menu moved across the Pacific to the border: burritos, beans, salsa in four chiles, and a lunch special that cost less than the two-lane minimum. It is the only stop on the walk where the food was still, in the last months, food — the kitchen was cleared before the walls, on the old rule that a building should leave nothing on its plate. The menu board from the final week is in the club’s box, and its last line, in the cook’s hand, is a price: two for the price of one, weekdays before eleven. The walk takes no orders at this stop, and the club reminds everyone that the diner never once served coffee after 1979, which is the whole history of the room in one number.

  7. Stop 6 · 10:50

    The Rooflines, from the Parking Lot

    Step back to the lot’s west edge, where the tour stops to look up, which is the only correct direction for the rooflines. The main deck broke its eaves into two sail-planes angled to catch the low sun, the office wing took a third, smaller sail, and the ticket canopy’s triple-sail already met the eye from the median. In neon, the rooflines ran a continuous tube from the sign to the office wing, lagoon blue on the down-faces, white on the up-faces, so that from the 5 the whole roof read as a single lit wave at night. The tube run was 2,100 feet in the end, after the 1970s refit added the office wing’s section; the neon shop that installed it, from the 9th Street yard in Newport Beach, did not keep the invoices and could not remember the 1958 count, so the house keeps 2,100 as the standing figure. The tubes were sold in the clearance, forty feet at a time, and the walk’s final stop is the last known one of them.

  8. Stop 7 · 10:58

    Java Lanes, Long Beach (by reference)

    The walk does not travel to Long Beach; it keeps the sister centre by reference, the way a ledger keeps a balance it will not book. Java Lanes opened the same year as its sister — 1958 — on the same Googie plan, same tiki styling, same wood floor, and it went up in 2004, one year after Kona, which is the year that decides everything in the region’s Googie count. After 2004 the club asked the question the region was asking and could not answer it twice: what is left? The answer on the books is a sign, a mural, and the equipment, scattered by lot, and this walk is the itinerary the club keeps for the question. The travel desk at the island keeps a parallel entry in its own way — the night of the Looking Glass, Guidebook Entry 72 — for the crossing that goes out and comes back over the same glass, and the walk notes the resemblance without claiming it: both departures, both return routes, both kept in a ledger nobody emends.

  9. Stop 8 · 11:06

    The Demolition Line

    On the lot’s east edge, where the wreckers staged, the tour reads the house’s account of the demolition, which is short, the way the house liked everything: equipment sold prior to the demolition, board by board, table by table, tube by tube; the walls came down in the spring of 2003 in the order the foundations allowed; the wood floor went to Irvine in lot 11 and the pinsetters went to the same centre in lot 13; the neon went to the yard on 9th Street, where it was sold in forty-foot sections; and the sign’s transformer, lot 12, went to North Tustin and has never been used. The ground was graded in June of 2003. What stands on it now is not on this itinerary and the club declines to name it, which is the last and most Googie thing the house does: it breaks off mid-roofline and leaves the eave out for you.

  10. Stop 9 · 11:14

    Back to the Median

    The walk ends where the neon started, at the median cut, facing the 5 on-ramp. The guide reads the standing figure one last time — forty lanes, 2,100 feet of tube, 1958 to 2003, one sign, one sister centre, one year between the two — and the party walks the parking-line arrows back to the cut, the way the regulars used to walk the arrows back to their cars, which is to say fast, and without looking at the rooflines again until they lit up. The fare for the tour is the fare the house always charged: nothing, except the shoes, which you leave at the sign and take back at the sign. The ledger line from Stop 2 stands as written, and the walk closes on it as it stands, unemended, in the same hand as the shoe sizes: The plaque at Okinawa — Dream Dictionary, Entry 744 , one in Copenhagen in 2004, on the sand being replenished by the Auckland isthmus, the University's community health hubs. And for those who want the walking kept in the older house’s manner — a party that bought the day out by the shilling, a band, a river bank — the case that holds the original excursion is at Case No. 47, Gallery 6, where the fare on record is still one shilling per head, round, band and picnic included; the Kona Lanes walk is that fare’s 2003 cousin, two lanes a night, shoes on the house.
