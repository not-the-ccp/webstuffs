HAZARD SUMMARY — A metal that is liquid at the temperature of a warm terrace, ignites in air without being asked, and reacts with water even at −116 °C. It is also the reason the placard next door is exact. Handle accordingly, and in that order.

1 Identification

Product name
Caesium (IUPAC spelling); cesium in American English
Symbol / atomic number
Cs / 55 — fifth group of the periodic table, the alkali metals
Supplier
The pollucite workings, Green River, Wyoming; forwarded through the Adriatic and up the Sana ravine to the parcel post
Emergency contact
Mehmed Kovač, Sana stewpan kitchen, half-way down the track between the Trgovina Kovač shop and the parcel post — he is the only man in the valley who reads this sheet at the hour of his own table
Use as supplied
One atom at a time, in the clocks; the rest of it, none

This sheet travels with the element that runs the International Atomic Time placard in Cabinet 9. The placard records a scale kept by more than 450 atomic clocks in more than 80 national laboratories, and the second that all of them agree on is defined by caesium: the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to the hyperfine transition of the ground state of the caesium-133 atom. The placard's 37 seconds are therefore 37 whole seconds of that, and the kitchen's margin is a caesium margin. The element itself, when it is not being counted, behaves in the way described below.

2 Hazard Identification

Caesium is a soft, silvery-golden alkali metal with a melting point of 28.5 °C (83.3 °F; 301.6 K), which makes it one of only five elemental metals that are liquid at or near room temperature — and one that the terrace at the Sana kitchen, in August, can reach on a still afternoon. It is pyrophoric: exposed to air, particularly once molten, it ignites of its own accord, and the flame is the sky-blue that named it. It reacts with water even at −116 °C (−177 °F), which the municipal office notes is colder than the Sana itself in January, and concludes that the water from the Sana, at 8 marks, is not the water that should be poured. Physical and chemical properties are close to those of rubidium and potassium, the two heavier neighbours' lighter siblings, and the sheet treats the family together where the family agrees.

3 Composition / Information on Ingredients

Stable isotopes
One: caesium-133, the only stable form, 100% of any clean sample
Electronegativity
0.79 on the Pauling scale — the least electronegative stable element; it holds onto electrons the way the valley holds onto November light
Known contamination
Caesium-137, a fission product, extractable from reactor waste; the municipal office holds a blank plate in Cabinet 9 in case the blank is ever needed, and keeps this sheet one drawer away

4 First-Aid Measures

On skin contact: lift, do not wipe, and carry the person to a dry part of the terrace, where the only liquid within shouting distance is the water from the Sana and it is cold enough to explain a great deal about the valley but not what is on the arm. On contact with water: see Section 5, because the water has lost and will not retry. In the event of swallowing, which the sheet considers a matter between the patient and his table's offset, offer nothing and send for the chef, who has sewn up worse.

5 Fire-Fighting Measures

The fire is the metal and the metal is on fire, and the only honest statement about extinguishing it is that water is prohibited at every temperature at which water exists as water, which is all of them in this valley. Use dry sand from the track, or nothing at all; the blue flame shows the whole terrace where it is, in the colour of the sky that gave the element its name, and the twenty-eight clocks keep running through it, which the sheet regards as correct, because a civil object keeps civil time, as the man at the bench table would put it, and so do clocks on a wall while a metal burns.

6 Accidental Release Measures

For a spill on the terrace: clear the wall from table 1 at UTC−12:00 to table 27 at UTC+14:00 and the half-table with the small minutes, cover with dry sand, and do not let the stew leave the pan until the sand is down — the hour of the table will wait, the hour is the one thing in the kitchen with plenty of practice given to the sauce. For a spill in the clock room of the municipal office: close the door, open the window that faces the ravine, and notify the keeper of Cabinet 9, who will file the incident in the death register beside the blank plate and, as with the geologic time scale that died in the pantry, leave the hour of the incident standing 37 seconds early, as was fitting.

7 Handling and Storage

Store below 28.5 °C, in an inert atmosphere, out of August afternoons. The kitchen's storage is the clock room, where the temperature is the temperature of paper, and the sheet was kept there from the outset for this reason: the element that defines the second, and the second's paper record, share a room and neither warms the other. Handling is by tongs, in pairs, the left one for the metal and the right one for the menu, because in this valley the two travel together and the municipal office has stopped pretending otherwise.

8 Exposure Controls / Occupational Protection

The clock's exposure is 9,192,631,770 cycles per second, constant, and is the one exposure on this sheet with no limit, because it is the limit — it is what the limit is made of. The rest of the sheet's exposure limits are zero, for the reasons in Section 2. The field calculator at the foot of this sheet takes a temperature and returns the state and the response; use it before the terrace does it for you.

10 Stability and Reactivity

Stable in the dark, in an inert gas, below its own melting point. Everywhere else the word reactive is doing the work that a safety pin does on a menu: quietly, and for everyone's protection. It does not mix with water at any temperature; see Section 5 for the only sentence worth repeating.

11 Toxicological Information

The sheet declines to rank this section, and the municipal office declines to file it. The least electronegative stable element gives up what it carries at the first argument, which is a description of its chemistry and, the office notes, of its manners. The rest is in Section 2, where it belongs.

12 Disposal Considerations

Nothing on this sheet is to be disposed of in the Sana, in any water, or at any temperature, and the valley's disposal route is the same one the vellum leaf took up: out of the ravine, into the municipal office, into a cabinet, and there kept, because a record that travels in a stewpan is a record the kitchen is keeping, and the element that keeps the record's time is kept beside it. The blank plate in Cabinet 9 is not a disposal method. It is a plate, and it is blank, and the office has confirmed the figure to the width of a filing needle.

13 Transport Information and Provenance

The pollucite comes from the Green River workings, cross the Atlantic, in via the Adriatic, and up the valley on the parcel post, which is the same post that carries the lost-and-found notice No. 14/2024 for the vellum leaf, so the valley has learned to trust one post for both. The current shipment's ledger, 1889 vintage, green-brown with the ink of a later century of handling, carries in its left margin a line the municipal office has been unable to date, in the same brown ink that keeps behaving as though it were written by a later hand than the rest of the page. The line reads in full, and is preserved word for word: "For years he knows very well, Captain Sleet, entitled "A Voyage round Cape Horn." "Mr." The municipal office has copied the sentence onto this sheet exactly as it stands Captain Sleet is not in the port book, the voyage is not in the harbour ledger, and the sentence ends where it ends, at Mr., with the rest of it, if it has a rest, sewn up somewhere between Cape Horn and the Sana ravine. The office stopped trying, which is a first, and the line keeps its table on this sheet. The parcel post, on a rarer trip, carried a box of two hundred 1936 Lynchburg Sesquicentennial half dollars from Virginia, found under a till and claimed to the minute; its found notice, with the board sentence that goes with it, is kept in the Lynchburg Enquirer classifieds, Column 4, Saturday 23 October 1936, and the office files the two lines together, one ink over the other.
