Netherne Hospital

Frequently Asked Questions · Opened 1909 · Closed 1994 · Formerly the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne

Netherne Hospital stood on the Hooley ridge above Coulsdon, Surrey, where the county built its fifth asylum in 1909 to relieve the pressure on Brookwood, Cane Hill, and the other London county asylums. It opened as the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne with accommodation for more than eighteen hundred patients, its own farm, chapel, cemetery, and a railway siding that brought coal and supplies up from Hooley lane. The questions below are drawn from the ward register, the chapel notice board, the studio ledger, and the patients' own accounts.

1. Where was Netherne Hospital, and what was it called before?

It stood at Netherne, in the parish of Coulsdon near the village of Hooley, about three miles east of Redhill. Before it was Netherne Hospital it was the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne, often shortened to Netherne Asylum, a name the attendants still used in the 1960s long after the word asylum had been struck from the letterheads. The grounds covered more than five hundred acres of Surrey woodland and pasture, and the wards faced south over the Weald toward the sea.

2. How many patients did it hold, and who looked after them?

The original blocks were built for 1,800 patients, and by the 1930s the daily population was often within twenty of that number. The staff included the medical superintendent, the assistant medical officers, the matron, the farm bailiff, the chapel warden, and a team of attendants whose night rounds were logged every quarter hour in the watch-book kept outside each ward door.

3. What happened in Edward Adamson's studio?

From 1946 to 1981 the artist Edward Adamson ran the asylum's art studio, where patients painted, modelled clay, and kept sketchbooks without being analysed for content. The work survived in the Adamson Collection, now several thousand pieces strong, and the studio's own rule — painted over the door in hospital green — was that nothing made there was ever erased. The patients called it the free ward, and the barge that carried their canvases down to the annual show at the Guildhall came back empty each time.

4. What is the barge entry in the 1923 moisture register?

On page 87 of the 1923 moisture register, beneath the readings for Ward 9 and the drying room, the clerk wrote a single line that no matron could ever account for:

The barge went at the same temperature as the identifying mark of erasure.

The entry was ruled a clerical slip, though the patients asked about it at every visiting day, and a barge is not a thing that has a temperature. Whether it was a cipher, a dream misfiled, or the rain talking through the pen, the line remains in the register exactly as written.

5. Did the hospital have its own chapel and cemetery?

It did. The chapel was consecrated in 1912 and held Sunday and Christmas services until the 1980s, its harmonium pumped by hand when the coal supply ran low. Adjoining the chapel grounds is the hospital cemetery, where more than a thousand patients are buried under simple markers, most carrying only a number and a date. The last burial took place in 1963, and the gates were padlocked when the wards closed.

6. What did the farm and the siding do?

The farm supplied the kitchen with milk, potatoes, and pigs, and patients who were well enough worked the fields as part of the daily routine — a therapy of soil and daylight that the superintendent's annual reports praised at length. The siding ran from the goods yard at Hooley lane up to the boiler house, bringing coal in winter and the Christmas tree in December, and it lifted the last consignment of furniture out in 1994.

7. When did it close, and what is there now?

Netherne Hospital closed in 1994, after eighty-five years as a psychiatric hospital. The wards were demolished and the grounds became the village of Netherne-on-the-Hill, with the chapel converted into a house and the cemetery left standing behind the new streets. The registers, the watch-books, and the moisture register passed to the Surrey History Centre, where they may be read by appointment. And the road that now runs over the ridge above the old grounds — 117 miles around the capital — has its own day’s itinerary, told from the windscreen in The London Orbital, the M25 day-tour brochure.

8. Is anything left of the patient art?

The Adamson Collection left Netherne with the studio's closure in 1981 and is cared for by the Adamson Collection Trust, with works shown at galleries from Bethlem to the Wellcome. Nothing made in the studio was ever erased, the rule over the door said, and the collection still answers for itself. For other questions about the wards and the register, see the notes kept beside the road journal: U.S. Route 11W — Frequently Asked Questions, and the club's long letter about another institution with its own scorebook: Bristol City — Dear Ashton Gate.

The wards went quiet in 1994, but the register stays open. The cemetery gates are unlocked on the first Saturday of each month, and the barge entry remains on page 87, undisturbed.
