Morphology and colour

Chrysiridia rhipheus is a large, slender, day-flying moth of the family Uraniidae, considered by many to be one of the most impressive and appealing-looking of all lepidopterans. Adults reach a wingspan of 7–9 cm (2.8–3.5 in), held in flight with the long forewing and shorter hindwing tilted so that the whole animal reads as a single oblique panel of glass. The colours are not pigments at all: the blue, green, purple, and copper of the iridescent parts arise from optical interference in the overlapping scales, each scale a corrugated lamella that splits white light the way a thin film of oil on water does. Rotate the wing and the field of blue slides into green and then into a violet so deep it reads as black; the black parts of the wing, by contrast, are genuinely pigmented, and stay black at every angle. The effect is what the coffee-table books have always promised — a sunset, cut along a seam and folded.

History of the name

The species was first described in 1773 by Dru Drury, the entomologist and publisher of The Insects of Florida and of the great folio Illustrations of Natural History. Drury, working from specimens that had come up the coast of Madagascar in a merchantman’s hold, placed it in the genus Papilio, considering it a butterfly; it flew in daylight, and in the eighteenth century the sun was the deciding evidence. It was Jacob Hübner, the Berlin engraver and systematizer, who placed it in the moth genus Chrysiridia, a word built from the Greek chrysos (gold) and iris (rainbow) — a name that describes the wing and nothing else. Hübner’s placement stood, and stands. One persistent embarrassment of the literature is the spelling: many older sources, including a long run of popular catalogues, misspell the specific epithet as “ripheus”, and the error survives in auction rooms and gift shops to this day. The correct form is rhipheus, from the Greek rhiphos, a band or stripe — which, given the wing, was always the better word.

Distribution and habitat

The moth is endemic to Madagascar, where it ranges across the eastern lowlands and the western dry forest country, from the rain-forest edge near Toamasina in the east to the deciduous scrub of the southwest, with scattered records from the central highlands as far up as around Antananarivo. It is a creature of sunlit clearings, the edges of cut forest, and the flowering tamarind and clove trees that mark the old plantation roads, where it takes nectar with a coiled proboscis on the wing. It is solitary and quiet when at rest, often parked on a horizontal branch with the wings spread flat, which is the posture collectors prize, and which is exactly the posture the interference colours were built for.

Flight and life

Unlike most of its order, C. rhipheus flies in full daylight, most often between ten in the morning and three in the afternoon on clear days, and it is the male that does the showing. The courtship flight is a slow, hovering spiral above a clearing in which the male angles his wings through the sun so that the blue passes into gold and the gold into green; the female, if she is interested, drops out of her perch and rises to meet the spiral, and the two wheel together in a way that the early collectors could not resist describing as dance. The larvae feed on a handful of hosts, among them Azadirachta and various figs, and the pupa is a hard, golden-brown case suspended under a leaf, often mistaken for a seed. The life, like the wing, is best watched at a slant.

Among collectors

Famous worldwide, Chrysiridia rhipheus is featured in most coffee table books on Lepidoptera, and it is much sought after by collectors, who pay serious sums for the best specimens — the birds of paradise of the cabinet, the plate that makes the shelf. The Madagascar Uraniid Study Group, a voluntary association of keepers and students meeting twice a year in the reading room above the Toamasina harbour warehouse, keeps the species’ public face: a catalogue of the spelling error, a wall of angle-photographs of a single wing, and a standing rule that no specimen be pinned by the hindwing, because that is the wing that tells the story. It offers support groups and services, and we shelve with care.

For a specimen to earn a place in the catalogue it must survive three inspections: the angle test, in which the wing is turned through sixty degrees and the blue is required to reach green before it reaches violet; the pin test, in which the forewing takes the weight and the hindwing is left unmarked; and the label test, in which the name is read aloud, spelled r-h-i-p-h-e-u-s, and pronounced with the emphasis the word has carried since Hübner set it down in Berlin.
