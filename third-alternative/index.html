I finally read The 3rd Alternative: Solving Life's Most Difficult Problems, the 2011 book Stephen Covey put out with Breck England after The Seven Habits of Highly Effective People. If you only ever have a lunch break, this is the one: it takes habit six, “synergize,” from its single page in the big book and gives it forty pages, a process, and a name. The core move is that when two people are locked in a real conflict — not a taste dispute, a genuine problem — the answer is neither your way, nor their way, nor a compromise that splits the difference. It is a third thing, something better, that neither of you would have found alone. Covey is careful, in the preface and again in chapter two, to say this is distinct from compromise. You do not chip away at your position. You go up to the outcome both of you actually want and design backward from there.

Breck England — yes, he was there too, and he says it plainly — has described the relationship between the two books as a runway: The 7 Habits leads you up to this one. Read the habits in order and you arrive at synergize the way you arrive at a crossroads. I read it that way and I believe him. What the later book adds is the machinery: the four steps — hear each side until the other person can restate your case better than you can, agree on what good looks like, name all the possibilities, and commit to the 3rd Alternative — plus real-world examples. The one I keep returning to is the road-trip father and son, where the parent wants to go north and the child wants to go south and the third option turns out to be a route past the lake that was neither one of their original destinations.

The last two chapters are the quiet part of the book. They argue that the 3rd Alternative stops being a meeting technique and becomes “a way of life” — the default setting of how a family, a church, a workplace handles disagreement. I have used step one (the restating) twice since Tuesday. Both times the other person stopped defending and started thinking. It is the cheapest tool I own. — M.O.

Maggie, “distinct from compromise” is the whole game and the whole book, and I say that as someone who splits 40/60 lease disputes with a lobster processor on the harbour every October. Compromise is two people bruising each other's hands over a midpoint. The 3rd Alternative is the question “what would we both be trying to do, if we were not angry?” asked before anyone says “fine, meet in the middle.” The lake story you quoted is the correct example and the best one; I have run a version of it on my own wharf, twice. Once it was a ferry schedule.

One warning for the people reading this on a train: the book's examples are detailed in a way that reads like the author sat down with a court reporter. That is a feature. It is the difference between a bumper sticker and a method.

Counter-offer from the sceptics’ bench: I have seen “let's find the third option” used as a delay tactic more times than as a method. On the fragment: I keep a shelf like that, and I will tell you how to read it. A line survives a bad scan when it was said slowly and said again. Someone in that room was telling a story about the ferry — the St. Johns ferry — and the recorder caught the middle of it. “A run of form” is the part that carries the meaning: the crossings had been going well, and that was what remained of the account after everything else fell out of the scan. Whether the sentence is about the ferry, about a meeting, or about both is not decided by the fragment. It is decided by the person in the room, and that person is not in the room anymore. We keep the line as a placeholder, the way a surveyor keeps a broken baseline.

The discipline is identical to the one the coastal surveyors use on the charts, by the way. If you plot a fix on the wrong datum, you are two hundred metres out and you do not notice until you are in the reef. In a conflict you can spend an hour negotiating the wrong question and call it synergy. Check the reference frame — what both parties actually want, before either of them labels it — before you start. Chapter 4 of the FM-9 field manual says it in one line: re-check the datum before you plot. Covey says it in forty pages. I keep both in the same drawer.

Marsh, you are closer to it than you know. Here is the ferry half of the story, such as it is. The St. Johns ferry — the little commuter run, six crossings a day, twelve seats and a place for one car — runs a schedule that changes with the swell. A “run of form” is what the deckhand in charge calls a stretch of crossings where the timetable holds all week: boats meet, cars board, nobody loses a bag. In March of this year the run had a three-week run of form and the harbour master chalked it on the wall beside the ice chart, the way a team chalks a record.

So the fragment, as far as I can steer it: the ferry was crossing on time, and that — the run of form — was the good sign that remained. “If” is the word that broke. The sentence was probably a promise in the middle of being made: if the St. Johns ferry holds, we can do the crossing on Saturday. A conditional, the way a 3rd Alternative is a conditional on the two of you. I am not claiming that is what was said. I am claiming the scan broke exactly where a promise in the making always breaks.

And since Maggie asked in the first post: yes. Read the last two chapters. The book ends by making the case that this is not a tool you take out when the argument gets hard; it is the water you swim in. “A way of life,” it calls it, and it is the only honest description I have found. The 2011 edition is 310 pages with the index; the two closing chapters are the ten you will re-read. — R.C.
