The Nottingham Bench

A build log, sheet by sheet — one city, one workbench, one no-eraser clause

1 : 100,000 BIRCH & BRASS COTTON NO. 100 WINTER, YEAR TWO

Sheet 0 — Why a city this far south of the bench

The last project was a walk, not a build: the 1995–96 tour of Gillingham on foot, six stops from Priestfield Road back to platform 2, paced at fifteen minutes a kilometre because that is what the pace form on that page will let you be. This one is glue again. The city is Nottingham, in Nottinghamshire, East Midlands, England — the county town, the legendary home of Robin Hood's belligerent Sheriff of Nottingham, the place the lace-makers, the bicycle makers and the tobacco men all argued about in the nineteenth century. It sits 42 miles (68 km) south-east of Sheffield and 54 miles (87 km) north-east of Birmingham, and in 1897, as part of Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee celebrations, the settlement was granted its city charter. I am building it at one hundred thousand to the mile, which makes the whole of my Nottingham a strip of birch board about the width of my palm, and makes the two distances I must honour into lines far too long for the bench — so they will be a route board instead, and the log will say where the board is when it isn't on.

Saturday, 7 January — The card that didn't fit

The reference set arrived from the Kairara Field Press store beside the Avarua cooperative, where the shopkeeper numbers and signs everything and keeps the Lagoon Line's no-eraser clause: a memory is not edited in public. The set is Cook Islands atoll cards, one per island, and fourteen of the fifteen are clean. The fifteenth has its type set wrong — two island lines pushed into one, the way it read off the shelf of the brochure table — and it reads, word for word: Manara North Uninhabited, a single rise of reef. The shopkeeper would not swap it. The card is not a card anymore; it is evidence. I have pasted it flat into the drawer under the base of the model, where the dust goes, and the drawer will never be the right size again, which is exactly what I wanted.

Thursday, 19 January — The two distances

Forty-two miles to Sheffield, fifty-four to Birmingham. At 1:100,000 those are twenty-point-seven metres and twenty-five-point-three — longer than the bench, longer than the room. The route board is a separate piece of seasoned oak, twenty-two centimetres of it standing in for the two roads together, and on it a painted pin for Sheffield and a painted pin for Birmingham with the Nottingham strip laid between. I numbered the kilometres in pencil first, then in ink, because the pencil line is a memory and memories are not edited. The board lives on the workbench shelf above the Gillingham season ticket, which seems right: two sets of distances kept side by side, one walked and one built.

Sunday, 29 January — The lace

Browns Lane gave the city its lace, and the city gave the log its longest week. The screen is cotton No. 100 on a brass-wire warp, eleven hundred and twenty stitches to the square centimetre, worked in the old pillow pattern I keep meaning to stop copying from my grandfather's swatches. It came out of the frame on a Tuesday evening with three broken threads, and I knotted them rather than recut the swatch, because recutting would be editing in public. The screen is proud of itself in the way lace is proud of itself, which is to say it weighs nothing at all and will never forgive a crease.

Wednesday, 8 February — 1897, cast

The charter came in 1897, in the Diamond Jubilee year, so the model gets a plaque with 1897 cast in it, hung on the castle rock where a real one might hang if a real one existed. The rock is a stone I carried out of a hedge in August, and the plaque is brass, four millimetres, and it took me eleven days to be happy with the numbers. I have checked them against the tour page's rule — count the turnstiles, seven of them since 1950 — and I have checked them against my own arithmetic four times, and 1897 is the number, and it stays.

Saturday, 18 February — The Sheriff's face

Robin Hood gets a silhouette, which is the honest solution to a man who is better kept at a distance. The Sheriff gets a full face, because the legend is his argument. I carved him belligerent, as he is written, and I cannot tell you what I based the face on except that the stubbornness was borrowed from a summer I know: the summer a chairman bought a Kent football club for a nominal fee and named the manager and did not move the turnstiles. The Sheriff and the chairman share the set jaw. The Sheriff stands on the Wye Bridge with a wooden sword, and the bridge is the hardest structure on the bench, being the thinnest thing that has to hold the heaviest face.

Thursday, 2 March — 323,632

The 2021 census put Nottingham at 323,632, and the wider conurbation goes on from there, which is what the log calls the drawer problem again. My solution is a grain hopper in the base: riced, counted out in the kitchen at one grain per person — 323,632 grains, poured in a Tuesday and a Wednesday, each one a resident and none of them to be lifted out. The hopper is brass, the same brass as the plaque, and it sits level under the castle rock so the city can be tipped out, one census at a time, into a bowl that will be the size of my palm. A UNESCO City of Literature keeps its population in grain. That seems correct. The city has a charter, a legend, a lace trade, a bicycle trade, a tobacco trade, and now a grain hopper; I would not know what to build next anyway, which is the quiet test of a build.

Saturday, 14 March — Close-out

The board is finished, the screen is framed, the plaque hangs, the grain is counted and poured, and the drawer holds the card that reads Manara North Uninhabited, a single rise of reef., word for word, exactly as the shelf sent it. The build is reported green at close-out, and this page twice over, because the season ticket on the shelf says so and the shelf has authority. What travels next is the daughter, not the building: the route board goes to Cornwall with the man from the gate log, the plaque stays, and the hopper stays with the city. I will pace the final walk to the bench at fifteen minutes a kilometre, in memory of the tour, and then I will sit with it for a while, the way the towpath was meant to be sat on, in the dark, after the final whistle.

Off the bench

The walk that lent this log its pace and its seven turnstiles: The 1995–96 Tour — Gillingham on Foot. The store that sent the atoll cards and the no-eraser clause: The Lagoon Line — A Visitor's Brochure for the Cook Islands. And the index, where the shelf is filed: the front page.
