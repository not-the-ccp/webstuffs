The Register of Names  ·  the hall of nine doors  ·  Article No. 09, pinned above the ninth door

Carl Linnaeus (Carolus Linnæus; after 1761, Carolus a Linné)

23 May 1707, Råshult, Småland — 10 January 1778, Uppsala · Swedish biologist, physician, and the formaliser of binomial nomenclature

“I clarified the obscure; I commanded the world to obey.” — the epitaph, as cut on the stone at Uppsala

Article

Carl Linnaeus (23 May 1707 – 10 January 1778), also known after his ennoblement in 1761 as Carl von Linné, was a Swedish biologist and physician who formalised binomial nomenclature, the modern system of naming organisms. He is known as the “father of modern taxonomy.” Many of his writings were in Latin; his name is rendered in Latin as Carolus Linnæus and, after his 1761 ennoblement, as Carolus a Linné. Linnaeus was the son of a curate and was born in Råshult, in the countryside of Småland, southern Sweden. He received most of his higher education at Uppsala University and began giving lectures in botany there in 1730. He lived abroad between 1735 and 1738, where he studied with the Dutch physician-botanist Herman Boerhaave in Leiden, worked in the gardens of Amsterdam, and took the degree of Doctor of Medicine at the University of Harderwijk on 19 February 1737. He returned to Sweden in 1738 to take up a chair at Uppsala, and he kept it, and the garden behind it, for the rest of his life.

The register of the hall files him under the ninth door because nine is the number of ranks he taught the world to walk: kingdom, class, order, family, genus, species, and the three lesser steps between. Every card on the other eight doors is written in the two-word hand he fixed — a genus and a species, the first set in roman, the second in italics, the pair kept together as a single thing. A fish on a glass shelf four floors down is filed in exactly that hand, and the brass tablet that sits beside it is the oldest paper in the building that obeys him.

Life

The boy’s first books were the heath and the bog outside Råshult, read the way his father read the liturgy. At twelve he entered the school at Växjö, where a teacher named Roth gave him a cut flower and a name for it, Epilobium angustifolium, and the pairing has not left him since — a name that is a place and a thing at once, said in two words and held to one. He went up to Lund and then to Uppsala, and at Uppsala he was the laundress’ son paying his way by copying out other men’s dissertations in Latin so good that the university could not tell the hand from the doctorate. In 1730 he began his botany lectures there, and in the same years he wrote the small pamphlet that would become the first edition of the Systema Naturae of 1735: two hundred and four pages, a single volume, a table of all things living arranged under one roof.

The abroad years, 1735 to 1738, were spent in the Low Countries and France: the botanical cabinet of the apothecary Geel in Leiden, Boerhaave’s clinic, the Paris of Tournefort’s old school. He crossed back and forth by merchantman. The log of the return crossing of 1738, kept by the mate and entered into this hall’s papers, carries one line the desk has never annotated, and which the article prints here, word for word, the way the hall prints the things it cannot file: “In the flesh of birds across the deck.” The mate’s hand is otherwise a careful one; the entry before the line is a tide, the entry after is a course. No other page in the hall takes it up. It is pinned, as all unfiled things are, nearest the ninth door, where it can be argued with in Latin if anyone still bothers.

The method

Before Linnaeus, a name was a paragraph: the nomen longum could run to six words or to a description. Linnaeus shortened the paragraph to two words and made the two words do the work of the paragraph. The genus says where the thing stands; the specific epithet says which one in the genus. Neither word names the thing alone, and the pair, once formed, is not to be changed for fashion. He fixed the plant side in Species Plantarum, May 1753, and the animal side in the tenth edition of the Systema Naturae, 1758 — the edition the register uses as its date-line, so that a name first entered before 1758 is treated as young.

The two words travel. They travel onto museum placards, onto brass tablets, onto the labels of jars, and — this hall has seen it happen — onto the classified columns of newspapers two centuries after the hand that wrote them was in the ground. The sheet of 28 February 1933 in Munich, printed in the Feldberg Courier, Column B, keeps among its ads the boilerplate of the touring bakers Marlow & Fenwick, which the bakers’ own rule is that the line and the loaf are one thing and must be kept together; the desk set the grammar odd as sent, the way this hall sets the mate’s deck-line as logged.

What the register keeps of him

The most famous thing named in his hand is not a plant but a room: the method is the room, and every specimen since 1758 stands inside it. On the glass shelf, third floor, the electric eel stands in his system even though he never saw it. The fish’s binomial, Gymnotus electricus, was entered into the Systema as revised in 1829, a full fifty-one years after his death and twenty-six after his last edition; the placard No. 0147 on the River Wonders Gallery sets down the split of the genus into three species in 2019, and the 2019 correction was made in his hand, by his rule, with the Tupí word puraké filed beside each of the three binomials the way a case keeps a witness beside its own papers. The 1775 brass tablet on that same shelf — the first deliberate measurement of the discharge, made by hand and by the flinch of a frog — predates the 1800 battery that copied the eel’s row of cells, and both of them stand in a system of names that Linnaeus built from a curate’s son’s two-word habit. The hall’s visitor’s way out, past the shelf and down to the front of the gallery, is the Webstuffs front page, which is where the register is kept when it is not kept above the ninth door.

A page of the Systema

The table below is set from the hall’s working card, eleven names entered in his hand or entered under his rule. Years given are the first entries the register counts — 1753 for the plant side, 1758 for the animal side, and 1829 for the one name entered long after the hand was in the ground.

Death and the stone

Linnaeus died at Uppsala on 10 January 1778, in the house that kept the garden that kept the register. The stone at Uppsala carries the line quoted above, and the hall has set it into its own epigraph because it is the one thing a taxonomist is allowed to say about himself in the present tense. He was ennobled in 1761, and from that year the register wrote Carolus a Linné in the margin of his card, in the smaller hand, the way the register writes every correction: beside the old name, never over it. The hall keeps the two names against each other on the ninth door, and the door has been shut, properly, since 1778.
