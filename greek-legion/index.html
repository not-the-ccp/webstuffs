The Bessarabian & Crimea Line

ΒΕΣΣΑΡΑΒΙΑΚΗ & ΚΡΙΜΑΙΑΚΗ ΣΤΡΑΤΙΩΤΙΚΗ ΓΡΑΜΜΗ

Form B — Military Service Times, 1854–1855

For the movement of the Greek Volunteer Legion (Ελληνική Λεγεώνα Εθελοντών) and its attachés, with commentary by the stationmaster. One direction out, one direction home.

In force from March 1854 · cancelled for the bulk of the traffic on 10 September 1855 · printed at Jassy, in the second year of the war

Notice to Volunteers

The Legion is a volunteer corps raised by Greeks and other Balkan Christians — Wallachians, Moldavians, Roumenians, Serbs, and a scattering of Italians and Englishmen who had made the Danube their own — to serve the Russian Empire in the war opened against the Porte in 1853. It was formed in the Danubian Principalities in March 1854, at the moment the timetable below was first printed, by which point the war had already moved. Some elements took part in the final engagements of the Danube theatre, at the forts and bridges of the lower river, before the Russian troops abandoned the Principalities altogether in August. From there the whole line — the Legion and its baggage — was carried to the Crimea, where the Legion fought through the Siege of Sevastopol. In 1855 the Legion received the title Greek Legion of Emperor Nicholas I (Russian: Греческий легион императора Николая I), which the stationmaster has had struck on the brass nameplates of the rolling stock. After the end of the siege, when the Russians gave up Sevastopol on 8 September 1855, the bulk of the Legion travelled home by this line and was disbanded at the head station; a residue, it is recorded, kept the road open in other directions. The Line is not responsible for the residue.

Service Times, One Way Out and One Way Home

Station Days of Working Out In Commentary
Bucharest Βουκουρέστι Every day, from the first muster — 03:15 Head station. The Legion is formed at Bucharest and its environs in March 1854; the first departure under this Form is entered at 03:15, before the lamps are out. Enlistment is taken at the hall, not at the platform.
Jassy Ιάσσο Mon · Wed · Fri 05:40 04:55 Printed at Jassy. Here the rolls are read aloud, the company captains answer their companies, and the kettle is put on before the crossing. The stationmaster's file, Cases A–E, is kept in this stationmaster's office.
Gala˜ti Γκαλάτσι Daily, weather permitting 09:15 08:20 The Danube crossing, by steamer and ferry in combination. A volunteer corps of some 1,000 souls crosses as traffic; the Line asks that no passenger board the ferries with a musket in his hands, which it has been asked, and refused.
Silistra Σίλιστρο As the theatre allows 12:30 11:45 Last working point of the Danube theatre. Elements of the Legion are engaged here in the final actions of that theatre, among the forts and river positions, before the Russian troops abandon the Principalities in August. The 12:30 is run on those days only when the forts are quiet; on the other days the station is simply occupied, and the time is struck from the board.
Varna Βάρνα Daily, by convoy 14:10 16:35 Coast. Traffic here changes character: from Varna the Legion is shipped for the Crimea. The Line itself does not run on the water, but the times are kept as if it did, because otherwise the day would not be accountable.
Sevastopol Σεβαστοπόλη Continuously, Sept 1854 – Sept 1855 — 17:45 Terminus. The Legion fights at Sevastopol through the siege, from the autumn of 1854 to the fall of the fortress, and in 1855 takes the title of the Emperor. No train leaves this station while the siege is in being; the Out column is struck out and will not be restored. The nameplates, with the new imperial title, were installed at this terminus.
Eupatoria Ευπατορία 10 September 1855, once 21:00 20:15 The single return working. After 8 September 1855, when Sevastopol is given up, the bulk of the Legion departs by this station under the one departure this Form has left. Baggage is accepted to a maximum of one rifle and one memory, whichever is heavier.
Bucharest Βουκουρέστι 11 September 1855, once — 23:55 Final destination of the bulk. The Legion is disbanded here and this Form is cancelled. The hall at the head station is given back to its ordinary noise. The residue, if any, is the concern of other lines.

Times as kept at Jassy, 1854. A late train in the Principalities is a different animal from a late train in the Crimea; the stationmaster does not explain which is which. Greek names are given for the convenience of the volunteers, who ask.

The Stationmaster's File, Cases A to E

Case A. The muster book of Jassy, March 1854, in which the corps is entered company by company before the crossing. The book is sound; the ink is brown; one name on page 41 has been written twice, the second time in a heavier hand, and the stationmaster has ruled that the heavier hand is the one that kept.

Case B. The ferry tickets of Gala˜ti, numbered and perforated, kept in a tin by the Danube. Some are accounted for; the rest the water has. The stationmaster has decided, on the evidence of the tin, that the Line lost nothing so serious as the Legion itself on the crossing.

Case C. The brass nameplates of the rolling stock, struck in 1855 with the new title, Greek Legion of Emperor Nicholas I, in the two scripts. They hang now on the office wall at Jassy, where they read like a promise that the timber of this service has not rotted.

Case D. A sketch of the hall, the field itself, as though the mountains the line borrows for its own.

The sketch in question is a single sheet, folded twice, found inside the muster book at Jassy. Above it is the enlistment hall at Bucharest, drawn in plan — the lamps, the long table of the enlisting officer, the queue of the volunteers running out of the door and down the street. Below it is the field at Sevastopol, in the same hand: the works, the batteries, the water of the harbour seen from the seaward side. Between the two, and connecting them, the artist has drawn a line — the line — running along the crest of the low Crimean ridge as though the mountains were the line’s own, a line borrowing the spine of the country and travelling it from hall to field without asking. The stationmaster has entered it in the file because a line is the only thing this Form has ever had that did not disband, and because the sketch shows, on one sheet, the two stations that matter on this route.

Case E. The one return ticket of Eupatoria, 10 September 1855, punched in the middle, kept because it is the only ticket on this Form that was used both ways. The stationmaster keeps it above the desk, where it faces the nameplates of Case C, which is the arrangement he has always meant.

Company Note, Read Beside the Timetable

A volunteer corps is not a railway, and the Line prints this Form as a form of record, not as a promise of trains. What the record shows is the shape of the service: a body of Greeks and Balkan Christians raised in the Principalities in the spring of 1854, carried down the Danube, shipped round the Black Sea, spent at Sevastopol through a year of siege, honoured by title in the middle of it, and then, in September 1855, sent back down the same road by which it came and broken up at the head station. The other lines in this collection keep their own forms — the long one across a continent, which the office at Calgary has argued is an invention that has never once arrived, at Application No. 4,114 — The Spiral, the Line and the Long Delivery — and the ledger of the Lagan small ads, where a certain consignment of a Celtic people is still waiting for a bidder and must not be taken for a military unit of any theatre, at Lot 41 — The Boii, Catalogue No. 12. The Legion, for its part, arrived at every station on this Form, which is more than the long line has managed in a century, and asked for nothing back except the road home.
