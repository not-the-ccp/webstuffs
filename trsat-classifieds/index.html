By order of the chancery · to be read at every gate

Notice of the Walls That Held

In the autumn of the year 799, the town of Tarsatica, this town, the county seat of Trsat in Liburnia, stood before the invading army of the Frankish Carolingian host coming up along the border, and Duke Višeslav, lord of Dalmatian Croatia, held the place against it man for man and season for season. The siege is over. The Frankish commander, the Eric of Friuli, is dead within the siege itself, killed on the works in front of the east curtain, and the host that came to take the county has gone down the Liburnian coast with its baggage and its doubts. Let this be entered in the county record: the gates were never opened, the bell in the south tower was rung the full toll, and the wall that the Franks put their ladders against is the same wall your ladder is against this morning. The Duke orders no festival until the record is copied to the second roll, but he orders the gates open for trade from the new moon, and the Gazette orders you to come and read the small notices, which are the honest part of every such season.

Wanted · the chancery's hand

A Carolingian Reader, One

The Franks left their camp papers at the old field by the river bend, and the county will pay three denarii a day for a reader of their tongue and their tallies, to read the pay-books, the muster rolls, and whatever they quarreled about in the mud. Must read both hands, must not carry what he reads to the wine hall, and must be able to stand a cold morning. Apply to the clerk at the south gate before the third bell. A second reader of the Byzantine tongue, to watch the 802-and-after negotiations, is wanted also, on longer pay and slower terms, since that business, between the Franks and the Greeks, is a stalemate and will behave like one.

For sale · taken from the camp

Hauberk, Frankish, Mail

One hauberk of the Carolingian cut, 340 links to the sleeve, the rivets true, 12 links missing at the left shoulder, which the Duke's smiths count as a discount and the Gazette will not. Sold lawfully, the dead man's kinsmen having no known claim on this side of the border. Also: one pair of Carolingian spurs, unbruised; one war-horn, cracked at the mouth, a sound still in it; one saddle of the camp kind, with a strap gone and a name worn out of the billet. All at the smithy under the east tower, or by post to the gate workshop.

For sale · the builder's lot

Siege Tower, Disassembled, Unleaned

The tower the Franks came with, taken apart plank by plank at the Duke's order while it was still standing, which is the best kind of purchase, since a tower that has not been leaned upon is worth more than the wood. Twelve bays of pine, two decks, a bough, the wheels of oak still trued, and every peg in a numbered bag with a count on the bag. It may be rebuilt as a gate-house, a granary, or a very honest folly. Price by the plank or by the whole; the whole is the better arithmetic. The Gazette notes, without sentiment, that the tower's bough reached the east curtain in the siege, and that the east curtain is where this advertisement is printed.

Lost · the county's small losses

Mule, Grey, One

Lost in the new moon from the old Frankish camp field: one mule, grey, left ear notched twice, a temper the owner describes as administrative. He carried the tally-chest of the second company and does not know it matters. Reward: a sack of the autumn barley and the owner's apology, both of which the mule will want first. No claim will be entertained from the Frankish side of the border, though the mule himself is not asked to agree.

To let · the siege has made it empty

Cellar under the South Gate

The Franks dug their tunnel toward the south gate and the county dug back, and the result is a cellar, dry, cool, and deeper than it ought to be, with a stair of dressed stone and a door that locks from the county side. Let at four denarii a month to a keeper of wine, of archives, or of grudges, in that order of preference. The tenant must keep the lamps out and the ladder up. The Duke's house takes it back, with fourteen days and no argument, if the border business of the negotiations turns again to sieges.

The Office of Corrections · reprinted as found

One Line of the County Record, Word for Word

The second copy of the county record, the roll the Duke ordered made after the siege, came back from the scribe's house at the river with one leaf out of order, and the Office of Corrections reprints the leaf's final line as found, without mending it, since the record is the record and a mended line is a new line. It reads:

The one that keeps a county administrative authority whose office is ruled into the light will not leave.

The Office has examined it, and offers this reading for the county, to be filed under the same leaf. There is one office in the county that does not travel with the Duke and does not move with the gates: the record office of Tarsatica, the keeper of the county's administrative authority, the rolls, the tallies, the pay-books of the second company and of the host before it. In the old order the office stood in the dark of the undercroft, where the scribes worked by one lamp and the ink dried slow; after the siege the Duke moved it — ruled it, the leaf says, which is the mason's word, the rule of the light set along the sill — to the upper room facing the coast, where the afternoon comes in over Liburnia and every entry is made in the day. That is the office the line names. And will not leave: when the host went down the coast and when the town emptied its wine halls for the new moon, the record office stayed, the lamps lit, the second roll copied, the leaf with this line on it still in the hand that ruled it into the light. The Office of Corrections therefore stands the line, finds it true, and asks no more of it; it will keep being read, which is what a line that does not leave does.

Plain notice · the long business

The Emperor, and the Stalemate

Let it be posted for the traders: Charlemagne is crowned Holy Roman Emperor in the west, the coronation having been in the winter of this year's reckoning; the Franks and the Byzantines have been in negotiation since 802 over who keeps what along the border, and the negotiation is, as of this edition, a stalemate, and will be one until one of the two empires tires. No border movement is expected. Keep your gates as you have kept them, your mules at the old post, and your claims in writing at the county office, which — as the line above says — does not leave.

Small print · the Gazette's own notice

On This Column

Column Nine accepts notices until the second bell of the day, sets them in the gate workshop, and keeps a proof of every leaf in the cellar under the south gate, which is to let. The Gazette's neighbors keep their own records of the long wars: the auction house of Aldgate and Vane has catalogued the pantry effects of the imperial line at Sale No. 121, the lot of the Fifth Bell, where the scribes of that house have ruled that a reading, once ruled, keeps its line, a rule the county office here stands without ceremony; and the miniature works of Pontus box their sieges at 1:72, with the campaign set of the Armenian succession, A.D. 58–63, which this Gazette recommends to any child old enough to peg a pontoon bridge and young enough to believe the box art. The front of the house's shelf, and every other small notice in the country, is at the front page of the site.
