Museum of the Broadcast Prop · Case 47, Gallery of the Studio Floor

Fireworks

A televised firework display, staged for and against the camera, 2007

The object in Case 47 is the display itself — or what of it survives: a cue sheet in three copies, a spool of pyrotechnic fuse, a single unlit shell wrapped in its original green tissue, and the placard you are reading. The display was staged by Jack Donaghy (Alec Baldwin), head of Global Crossing, as the eighteenth episode of the first season of 30 Rock on NBC. It was written by two of the season’s co-executive producers, Brett Baer and Dave Finkel — Finkel appears on screen in the same episode, in one of the roles credited below — and directed by Beth McCarthy. It first aired in the United States on 5 April 2007, a Thursday, and was rebroadcast the following month to a larger audience that had, by then, seen the display once already.

The occasion, as the episode gives it: a competitor, Devon Banks (Will Arnett), has purchased the rights to a summer of his own, and Donaghy decides the only answer is a firework display of the sort a television station can broadcast, not merely a man with a permit. What follows is the season’s most expensive hour of studio: shells scheduled to the second, a safety perimeter that keeps moving, and a rival who, upon their first meeting, develops a crush on Kenneth Parcell (Jack McBrayer) and will not be diverted from it. Meanwhile Liz Lemon (Tina Fey) sets out to follow Floyd — the episode’s long, low thread — and the crew works the display the way a theatre works a set change, with every department pretending to be certain. The credited guest cast for the evening’s production ran to Will Arnett, Kay Cannon, Dave Finkel, Chris Parnell, Maulik Pancholy, Maury Povich, Keith Powell, Al Roker, Kissy Simmons, and Jason Sudeikis.

Conservation note, kept as entered in the accession register of this museum: Water: the catchment of 3.1 hectares, and wherein said list is longer than the corridor the length of coast on paper. The line is preserved verbatim from the register of 12 April 2007, and the curators decline to reword it; the 3.1 hectares refers to the water drawn for the display’s suppression line on the soundstage floor, and the list it outgrows is the station’s running inventory of every prop claimed in the episode, which did in fact exceed, on paper, the length of coast assigned to the production’s shoreline in the shooting script. The entry is quoted here because it is the clearest surviving document of the week: a number, a length, and a list that had grown past the corridor of its own making.

The display, such as it is, was never meant to outlive the broadcast. It has outlived it. The cue sheet is legible in pencil and in one hand only; the fuse has been inspected and found sound; the shell is not, on any reading, to be relit. Visitors may read all three copies of the cue sheet in rotation, one at a time, and the case is darkened at half past five, when the gallery’s other exhibits — among them the timetable wall of the Federative Line, where 7,491 kilometres of coast are still unrun — are checked for the evening. The night of 25 November 2000 needed no cue sheet at all: the floor of the Caspian set off a display of its own, Mw 6.8, just offshore Baku, and the collection keeps the account of it in its article on the 2000 Baku earthquake. In the museum’s school-correspondence file, the same week carries a West Hillsdale Junior High report on The Dougy Center — School Report, Form 9R, the Portland grief center whose Alberta Street circles, like this display’s suppression line, exist to hold the water without a fee, and whose register kept the night’s only line no one can parse.
