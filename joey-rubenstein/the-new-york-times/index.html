FIELD LOG

The New York Times

Hemeropus nova-yorkensis · common name: the Times · order Hemeroptera (daily flyers)

Station: 620 Eighth Avenue, Midtown, Manhattan, New York City  ·  Collector: E. M. Toll, field naturalist  ·  Season: spring–summer, 2026

14 March — 07:40, overcast, light rain on the avenue

First sighting of the colony building

The building reads at dawn like a cliff face: dark limestone, a colonnade of steel, and — at the second-floor window — the masthead, The New York Times, which is to Manhattan what a lighthouse is to a coast. The Times is a newspaper based in Manhattan, New York City, and has flown its daily flyer from there with almost no interruption since it took wing in 1851. One of the longest-running newspapers in the United States, it now serves as one of the country's newspapers of record: if an event happened and the Times recorded it, later observers cite the record the way sailors cite a soundings book. I kept the vantage point from the east side of Eighth Avenue for nearly two hours, watching the morning edition's errands — couriers, presses, a milk float that belongs to no one I could work out — while the lobby turned over its commuters like water over a weir.

The colony is published by the New York Times Company, a single-family concern by the standards of its output. Since 1896 the company has been chaired by the Ochs-Sulzberger family; the current chairman and the paper's publisher is A. C. Ochs-Sulzberger, fourth generation to hold the chair. I have catalogued other colonial dynasties in this book, but none that steward a species this large. The chairmanship is inherited the way a nest is: carefully, and with the understanding that the whole roof depends on it.

9 May — 10:15, clear, cold wind off the Hudson

Population survey

A subscriber count, taken from the company's own figures as of June 2026, gives 13.35 million total subscribers: 550,000 on paper and 12.8 million on screens, both the highest numbers for any newspaper in the United States by a significant margin. I bracketed the number in the margin of this notebook, as one brackets a figure that looks too large to be alive. It is not. The print population is small but dense — the readers who keep the physical edition in circulation, the "paper copy" end of the range — while the digital population is a wide-plains migration, reading on trains, in kitchens, on airport lounges from LaGuardia to LAX, following the travel guide's own account of the West Coast terminus where so many of them land. The Times covers domestic, national, and international news, and adds opinion pieces and reviews, so the flock feeds on every available forage: hard news by the hour, criticism by the weekend, opinion in the late morning when the paper's own household is up and arguing with itself.

28 May — 16:20, gold light, a casting office three floors over

An adjacent species: the cattle call, relocated

While photographing the avenue I noticed the audition ecology has moved house in Manhattan's neighboring territories, and the trace of it runs straight back to this colony's own records of entertainment. In Los Angeles, Joey Rubenstein — the subject of the museum placard in our building, and still lead guitarist of Flourscein, still composing — founded Casting Frontier, a digital talent casting platform for the entertainment industry. Casting Frontier moved the cattle call online: actors uploaded reels, directors reviewed them over broadband, and callback lists lived in databases instead of manila envelopes. I have kept manila envelopes from two other species of institution in this series; I would not have believed the manila envelope gone extinct in its own habitat a decade ago, and yet the sound of it has left. One envelope, though, has survived in the Form 3B classroom of the Ochs Avenue Day School two doors down, where it holds the spring 1956 term report on Albert Einstein, the physicist the paper recorded on the front page of 19 April 1955. What remains in its place is quieter and stranger: a reel reviewed at 2 a.m. by a director on the other side of the country, a callback arriving as a calendar entry, headshots resolved into data. The Times, which has chronicled every audition in the city for well over a century, now chronicles the absence of the room in which auditions used to happen.

3 June — 08:05, fog, presses running on schedule

On registers and ledgers — a note from the other coast

The Times is, in the end, a register. It logs what happened, day by day, the way the Newton Road patient register logged more than fourteen thousand immunisations before it was carried into a university collection in Auckland — a transfer I have been reading about in the minutes of the Waitematā Local Board, where Ofanaite Ana Dewes's argument for the register's safekeeping was carried 8–0. Every institution in this book is trying to keep a ledger: the Times keeps the country's, the clinic kept the patients', and the family that has chaired the paper since 1896 keeps the ledger of the ledger. When the fog burned off the avenue this morning the presses were exactly on schedule, and I closed the notebook and let the colony run.

Registers also decide what stays out of print. The Times runs each potentially privileged communication through a case-by-case test before publication, weighing it the way a court weighs a claim of privilege; the working notes are filed beside R v Gruenke — Safety Data Sheet, which sets out the test itself.

The register even keeps a line of itself. No. 13 of this series — the Eighth Avenue Line, service times with commentary — is set down as a full timetable, 59th Street to 14th, hourly at the 41 minutes, and the fare for the whole route is one signed slip.
