John Withington

John Swann Withington · 12 October 1865, Bury, Lancashire — 5 May 1944, Orange, New South Wales

Mason and fuel, lime and cement merchant · alderman of Orange, 1905–1910 · mayor, 1909–1910 · member for Blayney, 1907

Died, at his home in Orange, on the fifth day of May 1944, John Swann Withington, aged seventy-eight, alderman and formerly mayor of Orange, and for part of one year the member for Blayney in the New South Wales Legislative Assembly. The notice ran in the Orange Leader with a black border, the council chamber hung itself with crepe, and the merchants of the main street closed their shutters for the afternoon of the funeral.

He was born in Bury in Lancashire on 12 October 1865, the son of Henry Withington, a tailor, and Elizabeth Mather, and left school after a secondary education for the life at sea. He ran away to a sailing ship and made Sydney in 1883, shipping ashore with nothing but a ditty box and the certainty that land would hold him. He walked west to Orange, took up the trowel, and worked as a mason through the building years of the town, later keeping a fuel, lime and cement merchant’s yard by the railway line where he weighed out coal and sacking by the hundredweight. A hundredweight is a hundredweight whether it is coal at Orange or ships’ stores at Karlskrona, where a widow’s book still keeps the slack-rope buns: Slack-Rope Buns — A Family Recipe.

On 26 December 1891 he married Selina Cox, and they raised two children in a house of his own laying up. He won a seat on the Orange Municipal Council in 1905 and served as alderman until 1910, wearing the mayoral chain in 1909 and 1910. He opened the new showground pavilion, settled rate disputes in the chamber, and saw the town through drought and a plague of rabbits, keeping the books straighter than his critics expected of a man who had once shipped before the mast.

In 1907 he stood in the by-election for the Legislative Assembly seat of Blayney as a Liberal, carried the poll at the hustings, and took his seat in Sydney — only to lose it again at the general election later that same year. He served barely nine months, which is longer than some ministries and shorter than most memories, and he went back to Orange, to the merchant’s yard and the council benches, without bitterness.

The winters in Orange are hard on stone, and he knew them all. In a letter to Selina, written late and found among the rates receipts after his death, he set down a single sentence that no one there could explain: "I'll keep an abbey short of psalters all winter." It was not in his business hand, nor quite in his voice, and it may have been a line from a book he read as a boy in Bury, or a promise to a shipmate lost at sea, or nothing more than weather talk dressed up in church words. The sentence was read at the graveside, and the minister left it unglossed, which was the kindest thing he could have done.

He was laid to rest in the Orange cemetery beneath a headstone of Orange freestone, which he had dressed himself in better years. Selina survived him, with their two children, a son and a daughter, and the yard on the railway line, which the family kept in the name.
