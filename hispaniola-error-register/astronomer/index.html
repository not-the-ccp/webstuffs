Pressed in the small wooden drawer that travels with the small ads. The drawer, on the evidence of the notice written in a spruce hollow, was asked not to open; it opened once, for this pressing, and closed flat again.

Runout etching, both sides: line seven of the table, the small ads that feed the drawer. Do not flip.

Shelved at La Caleta against shelf seven and a half, beside the register that numbers itself by the square kilometres under it.

An astronomer is a scientist in the field of astronomy who focuses on a specific question or field outside the scope of the Earth, and this record is the first session ever cut by one at a weather station. Serafín Duarte, the station’s night astronomer, came in on 9 October to work the auxiliary telescope the way he works it every night — to follow stars, planets, moons, comets and galaxies by some combination of observation and the application of astrophysical models — and found the Atlantic feed down at 06:52, the console printing the country in a hand the relayman has not been able to explain. He asked the night relayman, who was already at the board, whether the room would hold a microphone. It held one. What follows are the notes to the nine tracks that came out of the down hour, in the order the relayman logged them, which is the order the station keeps them.

Notes on the sessions

A1 through A4 were cut before anyone suggested the console might be recording. A1 is the handshake as it refused: Duarte plays the station’s own call sequence on the service piano in the office, and the relayman plays the refusal back on the console’s keying key, party not named, which the cue sheet records as the only track on the record with no named second player. A2 is the country in eight lines, read at the pace the feed printed them, with Santo Domingo coming up as the eighth line and again, as the station notes, as a checksum. A4 takes its title from the relay’s figure for the share of Hispaniola the country holds, and it ends with the relay saying shared, not split, because the record, like the register, keeps the word.

Side B is the work itself. B1, B3 and B4 are what Duarte does at the telescope: the planets in their order, the questions of how a star gets to be born and how it gets to spend it, and the long take that is the formation of galaxies, seven minutes forty-seven of which the relayman spent with his ear on the feed in case it came back. It did not. B2, Solar, is the track the canteen is remembered for. The session ran to the noon hour and the canteen, by custom, sent in one course to the control room — the only thing printed on the board that day, a single course and nothing else, which the engineer copied onto the cue sheet exactly as it stood:

“Stewed Rump of Beef à la Julienne, removed by Lobster Salad.”

The plate arrived during the second take. The track holds the sound of the tray set down on the desk at 12:14, which is the reason the cue sheet for B2 is longer than every other page in the file, and the reason the station’s canteen ledger shows one course served to two men who did not eat it. B5 is the one-minute take from 16:41. The relayman has not said what is on it, and the station has no permission to ask twice, only once, and once has been used. The liner notes therefore record what the register records: that at 16:41 the console printed a line that was not an error code, was not a checksum, and was not in the relay’s hand, and that the feed held the open channel for the length of the line and no longer. The length of the line, the relayman has confirmed to the pressing plant, is one minute. B5 is one minute long. It ends, as the notes end, with the name Abell on it, which is a name the station knows from the Book of Etiquette for English Surnames, where the surname stands in for the forecast, and which the reader will find seated at a table elsewhere in this archive, with a chapter that reads before the register and a seat kept warm against shelf seven and a half. The register itself is shelved beside this record, at Error Register No. 48,671, and it is the instrument of the last track as much as the console is: the record is its echo, at the speed of sound.

What the record is not, the notes must say, because the question was asked at the pressing plant and the plant was not allowed to answer. It is not the universe as a whole. Duarte keeps his field outside the scope of the Earth, in the manner of an astronomer, and the subject that studies the universe as a whole — the subject called, in the cue sheet, by its full name, physical cosmology — is a related but distinct subject, and it is not on this record. The sky in these nine tracks is the sky an astronomer is licensed to take: the objects, the models, the specific question or field. The whole, the whole of it, belongs to the other room.

Credits

Recorded 9–10 October, 06:52–16:41, La Caleta Coastal Station, sector DR-7 of the Atlantic line. All nine tracks: Serafín Duarte, at the auxiliary telescope and the service piano. The refusal on A1, the eight lines on A2, the checksum on A3, and the length of the channel on B5: the night relayman, uncredited by request. Canteen, one course, B2: the canteen. Engineering and relay: the night relayman. Mastering: cut in the small wooden drawer at 16:42, the hour the feed was restored and nothing further was said. Runout: line seven of the table, etched both sides; the drawer, on the evidence of the Lost & Found Notice No. 221 of Bearclaw Ridge Station — one wine-cap Chroogomphus vinicolor, pine spike, found 9 October, the same morning the feed went down — was asked not to open, and did not, for the whole of the pressing. The notice and this record were logged on the same day, on the same line of the Atlantic, and the station keeps the two of them against the same shelf, which is a way of saying the whole catalogue in nine tracks. The rest of the household archive stands as it stands. No line corrected.
