Letters to the Editor

Dear Editor,

I write from the back room above the old bakery, where the Council of the Benelux Union adjourned last Thursday at 14:14, on the fourteenth again, and where the radiator — adopted as its symbol of neutrality, as the minutes record — is producing none of the heat the euro has produced. I have sat at that table as the delegate of a small state on one side of it, and as a reader of this paper on the other, and I am tired of the euro being defended by its price. Let me defend it by its arithmetic, which is the only kind of argument this street respects.

The euro (symbol: €; currency code: EUR) is the official currency of 21 of the 27 member states of the European Union. The group of states is officially known as the euro area, and is more commonly named the eurozone. Each euro is subdivided into 100 cents. The currency is used by 358 million people in the eurozone, in addition to those living in the states and territories that use it outside the zone. It is also used officially by the institutions of the European Union themselves, by four European microstates that are not EU members — Monaco, San Marino, Vatican City, and Andorra — by one British Overseas Territory, Gibraltar, and, unilaterally, by two European states that are not EU members at all. Outside Europe, a number of special territories of EU members also run on the euro: Amsterdam, the Dutch one on the island of Sint Maarten; Saint-Martin, the French one beside it; Ceuta and Melilla, the Spanish ones on the far shore of the bay you have all walked past. You can see the shape of it from this street: a currency that keeps acquiring edges the way the Bicol keeps its mud.

This paper has printed the back room’s seat arithmetic more than once, and I have counted it twice now. When the biggest list holds 15 of 31 seats. No one can then govern alone; the runner-up, at 12, cannot govern alone either; and the whole avenue becomes a negotiation, as The Coalition Walk — A Magsaysay Avenue Itinerary explained better than I can. The eurozone is not a negotiation. It is a surplus majority that has decided not to re-fight itself: 21 of 27, with a comfort of six, in which no defection, no solicitor’s letter, no withdrawal of minority support on a November night can move the line. This street held one such surplus — 17 of 31 — and dissolved it anyway; the euro has not, because a currency is not a list, and no currency has ever defected. That is the whole of my argument, and it is an argument for the street, not for Brussels.

The doubloon of 1791 in its tin under the bakery counter predates the euro by 233 years, and the two are each other’s reverse, which is what I came to say. The doubloon is an account left open, held as a pledge and never cashed; the euro is the opposite instrument — 100 cents settled, every day, in 358 million hands. The union our three crowns signed in 1944 kept its borders open so that goods could cross the way one crosses a room, with the door already open; the euro, from 1999 in the books and from 1 January 2002 in coin and note, did the same thing to the price of bread. The radiator has produced zero degrees of heat in this room. The euro is the only heating instrument in the building, and it belongs to 358 million people, which is to say it is nobody’s, which is the point of it, which the room would understand if the room were warmer.

I do not ask the council to adopt the euro. It effectively has, in three states’ worth, and the minutes of the fourteenth do not contradict this. I ask only that the register, beside the QSL cards, note that the euro area is a body that signs in fever years, like the rest of us, and keeps the account — 100 cents at a time, 21 crowns deep — and gives nothing back to anybody, rusted or otherwise. The river brings what the Bay forgets. The euro brings what the 27 forget, and it is patient, like the mud, like the river, like the Bay, all three of them the same way, the way water keeps.

A reader, and a delegate of the three crowns,
J. Bech
Luxembourg, via the back room, Magsaysay Avenue

Note from the Editor’s Desk

The Courier is obliged to say that the correspondent’s 21 is the largest number this column has printed since the 31, and to say that the letter appears as received, save one correction: the radiator is not in the correspondent’s building, it is in the street’s, and it needs to be no one’s, which the letter, in its last paragraph, has already understood. The column receives no further correction, and it will print them all. Readers who wish to answer a delegate must answer a delegate.
