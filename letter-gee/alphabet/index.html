Recalde Press & Cooperage · Shed Field Manual · Chapter 12

Of the Alphabet

Inventory, origin, and house practice for the twenty-six letters — set in the shed behind the bakery, with the seventh letter facing the door.

One impression. Revised only by the minute book.

I. Definition

An alphabet is a writing system that uses a standard set of symbols called graphemes — more commonly known as letters — to more or less represent particular sounds in a spoken language. Specifically, these graphemes largely correspond to phonemes, which are the smallest sound segments that can distinguish one word from another in a given language. The distinction is the one the shed keeps on its pegboard: a letter is a mark, and a sound is a difference that matters. The desk writes the first in ink and the second in the minutes, and the two are not the same object, though each of them, in this house, has a shelf.

Not all writing systems represent language in this way. A syllabary assigns symbols to spoken syllables, and a logography assigns symbols to words, morphemes, or other semantic units. The shed has a working example of each: the syllabary rides in the trough with the San Juan de Portugal at 1:64, where each call of the boatswain takes a whole symbol and no argument, and the logography is the barrel stave itself, which stands for the barrel, the beef, the salt, and the hoops, in that order, with none of them silent. When the council meets, all three systems are in the room, and this chapter governs only the alphabet. The other two are entered in the inventory and left alone.

II. Origin

The first letters were invented in Ancient Egypt to serve as an aid in writing Egyptian hieroglyphs; these are referred to as Egyptian uniliteral signs. One sign, one consonant, no syllable and no word — which is the whole of the invention, and the reason the alphabet is small enough to hang on a pegboard. The signs did not set out to replace the hieroglyphs and did not replace them; they stood beside them, in the manner of the vinegar crock beside the door, serving a narrower office and keeping it. The alphabet that followed into the shed is the Latin descendant of that aid, arrived through the usual routes, which this manual does not repeat except to say that every route is long and none of them is the shortest, and that the seventh letter kept its place on all of them.

The house holds the origin in the third drawer with the long preamble, and the drawer label reads only uniliteral. N. Halvorsen, tape custodian, is responsible for the drawer and for the rule that a letter is not moved off its peg while its name is being spoken. The rule was made at the forty-first meeting, over the letter G, and it is entered in the Minutes of the Council of the Shed — The Matter of the Letter G, where the reading of the tape-box label and the admission of both minuscule forms — opentail and looptail — stand in full.

IV. Provisioning the seed room

Every alphabet, the manual records, is a store of marks, and a store must be provisioned. The shed provisions its own in the seed room above the cooper’s bench, where the tin of winter seeds stands on the shelf below the pegboard, and where the card is pinned that governs the season’s sowing. The card is older than the album and older than the tape box; it arrived in the out-tray with the first letter from the school in San Juan de Portugal, and the desk never found a better rule for sowing by the sun than the one the card gives. Read at the bench, as found, word for word:

“There are three kinds of plants, and placing them in a solar year, with little shreds of split peas, 2 lbs.”

The three kinds, by the shed’s own gloss, are the seed, the cutting, and the plant already living in the ground: the seed for the slow work of spring, the cutting for the work that wants no patience, and the living plant for the work that is already done and only needs a place in the year. “Placing them in a solar year” means setting each kind to its season and marking the tin’s lid in chalk, so that the sowing can be read back from the year the way a label is read back from a tape box. The little shreds of split peas, 2 lbs., are the provision the card names for the bench itself: two pounds, measured in the crock’s lid, used thin on the board between hoopings, and never measured twice, which is how the shed measures everything it does not want to run out of.

The card is entered in this chapter because an alphabet is what it does, not what it is: it keeps a set of marks in their places through a whole year of sowing and a whole year of reading back, and it asks none of the marks to stand down.

V. House practice

Ordered by this manual, from the minutes and the record: that the shelf keep its twenty-six in their numbered places, one to twenty-six, the seventh facing the door; that the opentail g go to the children’s chapbooks and the looptail g keep the navigation chapbooks, neither asked to stand down; that the tape box keep the label Latin-0, and it will stay that way; that the euro sit at 0xA4 and the fractions keep their empty place; and that any question spoken to a letter be answered from the shelf, not from memory. The album the practice was kept against — the September 1999 recording by Code Page 28605 on the ninth Latin alphabet of the ISO series — is documented in the liner notes to Latin Alphabet No. 9, where the eight swaps and the twelve days stand track by track, and the council’s vote on the label and the two minuscule forms stands in the minutes at Chapter I through V of No. 41.
