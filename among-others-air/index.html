ON AIR · WELSH-EDGE REGIONAL TRANSMITTER, 565 kHz

Transmission 11 · The Wireless Library · broadcast 11:40 a.m. on a Tuesday in 2012, with the tide against it

Dragons, By Post

A play in one act, for voices, and for the sound of a postbag hitting a counter. Written for a village that had to be told, twice.

THE CAST — JO, the writer, born 1964, in a room that is half card catalogue; PUGH, postmistress of Bangor-on-Dee, sixty-one years old, who keeps the dragon's letter in a shoebox; THE CARRIER PIGEON, uncredited, punctual; A VOICE ON THE WIRE, who reads a fragment and does not correct it.

Scene I — The counter, 9:04 a.m.

A postbag against a zinc counter. The tick of a wall clock that has lost confidence. Somewhere down the Dee, a ferry horn at the exact second the bag opens.

PUGH Right. The 8:15 from London is sorted, the 8:40 from Ottawa is sorted, and I am not sorting — I am handing over, personally, in her hand, the one parcel the whole office has been standing around since the letter said forward to present address, urgent but not urgent. Jo, you're forty-eight this year, and I am not a woman who gives a thing to a writer by counter.

JO The card says it's from the science-fiction academy. The box says it's from the fantasy side. The address is in my mother's handwriting, which I don't remember using, which means she was already in the other country by 2011 and I should stop pretending the card catalogue in my study is a metaphor.

PUGH Avoid the catalogue, it's in the wall. Open the box, then. And mind — this is the second time this year the wire has put us up on the whole list. First was the book about the Victorian lady, the dragons in the drawing room one, 2004, the fantasy prize. People in the butchers' queue went quiet in the most useful way. Today, if the box means what the box means, it's the other two. The Hugo and the Nebula. Same book. Both the same book. Which, I'll tell you, is a kind of miracle I only accept from post.

Cardboard, the small violence of tape. A breath taken by a room full of people pretending to look at stamps.

JO Among Others. It's a book about magic that happens by accident, in a town in Wales I have never visited, about a girl whose mother goes into the woods and into a world that is also, quietly, a computer. It won both, and I would rather have the box than the reason. Keep the box, Pugh. Put it in the shoebox with the other one.

PUGH The shoebox is full. The shoebox has been full since 2004.

Scene II — The shoebox, 1889 (or thereabouts)

The clock stops. Gas lamps being struck. A dragon, very large and very polite, clearing its throat at the top of the stairs. A postbag again — the same postbag, it insists.

JO This is 2004 again. I know because the dragon is wearing its travelling coat and the novel — Tooth and Claw, the one the world-fantasy prize went to, the Victorian one — has a chapter called the postbox. Mrs. Silvern, the lady, is holding her own letters against the window and the dragon is reading them out loud to settle the rent.

PUGH (into the shoebox) Ma'am, I can't carry that, it's the weight of a year —

DRAGON (a baritone, apologetic) The rent is settled in letters, Mrs. Silvern. That is the only currency I will accept from a postmistress's granddaughter. The year is 1889, the post is due, and the dragon does not bite, he files.

JO Every year I go back to that chapter and the postbox is empty and the dragon is waiting with a pen in its claw, because the book is about a woman writing the whole Victorian age by post, and about me writing it back.

Scene III — The fragment, 9:31 a.m.

The wire hums up in pitch. Morse, then a human voice on top of it, as if the voice had been waiting inside the code.

PUGH The carrier pigeon is at the window. It has a postcard tied to its leg the size of a man's hand, and it's from no stamp at all.

VOICE ON THE WIRE (reading, flat, without correcting) "Postmark: nowhere. Address: you, present. Where the dream as a chapter for the letter it answers when you do. Signed: —. Postage is paid. It has always been paid."

JO Don't correct it, Pugh. If you correct it, the letter stops answering.

PUGH (quietly) I have been a postmistress forty years. I have never once let a fragment be what it was. It's a sentence, Miss Jo, with the dream in it where the verb should be. That's what the small change in the drawer is for. You pay the sentence back the way it came and it will say the rest of itself to you at home, on the good table, the one with the map of Wales in the cloth.

Scene IV — The drawer, 9:45 a.m.

A drawer opening like a small orchestra tuning. Coins of a country that changed its mind about the pound in 1971, kept anyway, kept on purpose.

JO The mystery books. The small-change ones — Farthing, Ha'penny, Half a Crown — a history that never quite happened, an England still paying by the old fractions, and in it, an old woman, a dog, a pocket watch, and a murder a season at a time, done gently, with tea. If you want to know what I do in the years between the big prizes: I set a cup down and make the whole country a small one.

PUGH And the song?

JO Lifelode. 2010, the mythopoeic prize, the book about the song a hundred thousand years long that the world is actually sung in. You don't need the book to know it. You've been singing it every day since the clock lost confidence. You just think it's a clock.

PUGH And the children?

JO The ones that aren't real, My Real Children, 2015, the year I haven't reached on the wire — the book that takes the Tiptree, about a woman who has given her children away to the world and the world has given them back in a different currency. I will not spoil a book that hasn't been born. The wire is patient. It has waited this long for a fragment.

PUGH Then we'll be patient the same way for the reviews. The New York office — Tor, the one on the reactor of the wire, the place she writes for, the place the reviews and the science-fiction comments go up from, one per week, with the tide — that will want a word before noon.

JO It will have it. One line. The dragon has filed. And then I go into the card catalogue, which is in the wall, which is the other country, which is mine.

The clock finds confidence. The ferry horn again, one beat late, on purpose. A postbag against a zinc counter — the outgoing bag, heavier than it came in, which Pugh attributes to weather and does not elaborate.

PUGH The 10:15 for New York goes up with it. So does the 10:15 for anywhere the dream is. Mind the dragon on the stairs.

The wire hums down. The lamp above the counter goes from red to a colour the station has no name for. Somewhere a pigeon, released, is already late.

— End of Transmission 11 —

Heard elsewhere on the wireless: the album keepers of Portland play their May listening in full and set a chair empty by the window — The Long Side, Newsletter No. 47 — where the hush after the last track is exactly the hush this transmission ends on; and in Munich, Gallery III, Case 9, a bound minutes volume with one strange, unamendable line sits under 50 lux, kept by the same register-keepers who would have sorted this play's postbag — Placard No. 12, the Bavarian Court Armoury. For the Welsh in the cast, and for the two revived, see the six living voices pressed into The Northwestern Fringe, liner notes LL-04; for a companion play of the same house style, one about tennis and a founded-in-1886 national body, see Tennis New Zealand — Radio Play Excerpt. And the 10:15 for New York passes, on its way, through the Dublin shelf of The Joyce Reading Room, where the postcards of a modernist who never came home are kept shoeboxed on this office’s rule — three to a box, postage paid, the back of the card left blank.

No dragons were handled at the counter during the recording. The shoebox is original. The fragment is exactly as it came in, and will be exactly as it came in, until it says the rest of itself.
