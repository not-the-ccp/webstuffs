Jefferson nickel , n.

Pronounced jeðrfæn ‘nΙ.’kæ.Ιl’ð · also the five-cent piece, also the Jefferson, also, in one's own sleeping voice, the pocketful

Struck as in the dream: by the United States Mint, at Philadelphia and Denver, from 1938 to the present, in copper-nickel, 3.11 grams, 21.21 millimetres across; the obverse bounded by the portrait of Thomas Jefferson, third President of the United States, and the reverse by Monticello, his house in Charlottesville, Virginia, which in the dream is always open and always one room short.

The five-cent coin of the United States, struck since 1938, when it replaced the Buffalo nickel, a piece first struck in 1913 and long difficult to coin, which completed the 25-year term during which it had sat in the pocket like a held note. The obverse of 1938 to 2004 is a left-facing profile of Jefferson by the sculptor Felix Schlag, who worked in Philadelphia and kept the coin's best edge; the 2005 obverse stayed in profile but passed to Joe Fitzgerald, and from 2006 onward Jefferson's face, newly designed by Jamie Franki, turned out of the profile and sat forward, meeting the dreamer, which the concordance records as the coin deciding to be seen rather than merely passed. The reverse, through it all, is Schlag's Monticello, though the pieces of 2004 and 2005 bore commemorative designs — the Statue of Liberty and Mount Rushmore — which in the dream appear as the house wearing its good room on the outside. To dream the Jefferson nickel is to dream a small metal day that has been in circulation eighty-seven years and still rings when it drops on the counter, and that the pocket, whatever the weather says, keeps counting to five.

When Dreamed of

Most commonly the coin arrives in the pocket, five of them, the way a week arrives — all at once and none of them agreeing to be the last. The sleeper tips the palm and the five land in a row on the kitchen table like a small parliament of the same man, one in profile and four forward-facing, which means the dream has reached the 2006 design and the old profile is still present, because a coin that has been struck since 1938 does not retire, it only turns. If the Buffalo nickel is also present — the 1913 piece, the hard-coined one, the one that finished its 25-year term — the sleeper should note that the older coin is the one doing the listening, and that it has been difficult to coin in every dream it has ever entered, the way some days are difficult to make. The commemorative reverse pieces of 2004 and 2005 appear when a house is being shown to a stranger, and the Statue of Liberty and Mount Rushmore are the rooms the house is most proud of and least lives in.

The weight is the figure that matters. Five nickels weigh the same as the day weighs; the dreamer will know this without being told, will tip them out onto a clear sheet of paper — a chart, a map, a weather sheet — and watch the five hold their places against the morning. The pocket is the room where the counting happens, and the counter is not the dreamer; the counter is the coin, which has been doing the counting in every pocket since 1938 and is not tired of it.

The Ledger Line

This entry keeps one line from the weather desk of the Mint's old Philadelphia building, where a copy of the concordance was once left on the desk by a reader who is not named in it. The line was written under a clear-day forecast that had everything settled — the fronts out, the pressure high, every gauge relaxed — and it survives in the folio word for word, because a line that refuses the weather keeps its value whether or not the weather agrees:

Only one does not ease, the barometer on the clear-day map.

The concordance does not strike it out; it interprets it. The clear day is the dream, the map is the pocket, and the barometer is the fifth nickel — the one that sits in the middle of the palm and keeps its pressure against the day, so that the pocket can tell a settled morning from a false one. On the clear-day map everything else has relaxed into the forecast; only the coin still measures. That is what a five-cent piece is for, and the line stands as a key for the whole entry: in the dream, what does not ease is what is still working.

Interpretation

  • To dream the coin in profile — Schlag's 1938 obverse, Jefferson's head turned left — is a sign of an obligation that has been quietly discharged since 1938 and asked for nothing back; the sleeper is the pocket, and the pocket may keep it.
  • To dream the forward-facing Jefferson of 2006 and after, the Jamie Franki design, is the better figure: it means a matter that had been running its face down the hallway of the dream has finally come to stand in the room, and the dreamer will be paid for the meeting, five cents a word, in full.
  • To dream the Buffalo nickel among the Jeffersons is a warning of the mild sort, and an old one: the 1913 piece, difficult to coin from its first year, has outstayed its 25-year term in the dream, and something in the pocket that should have been replaced in 1938 is still being passed; the sleeper is urged to tip the palm and look.
  • To dream the commemorative reverses of 2004 and 2005 — the pieces bearing designs not of Monticello at all — means the dreamer is showing a house to someone who will not stay the night, and the pride is real and the night is not.
  • To dream Monticello itself, open, one room short, is a sign of a design that will be struck again: the sleeper is holding a portrait of their own that is still in profile, and 2006 — the year the coin turned forward — is the sleeper's year, not the Mint's.
  • To be dreamed of by the nickel — to feel the five-cent piece watching the pocket close over it — means the dreamer has been the weather in somebody else's map, and the barometer in their hand is the sleeper's own day, kept at pressure, kept honest, kept at five cents apiece.

The Kitchen Interpretation

Where the dream ends at the counter — and this one does, because the coin is a counter — the concordance gives the whole domestic meaning in one instruction: Tip five nickels onto a clear sheet in the pale morning light, keep the middle one where it lands, and do not let the barometer off the map. The sheet may be a weather chart, a map, or a napkin ruled with the pocket's own hand, and the light should be the colour the copper-nickel takes at nine o'clock, which the folio records as the colour of a promise that is not a promise yet. When the middle coin has stopped moving, the day is settled; when it is still pressing, the day is being settled, and the difference is the whole entry, and it is a good one.

Cross-Readings

The sleeper discharged from this entry arrives, as sleepers do, on a far bank with a ferry crossing on Saturday — the concordance's own entry on South America, Entry No. 214, where the twelve sovereign countries sit struck like clocks at a long table and the thirteenth place is kept for a car that is not coming; that folio teaches this one its Saturdays, and this one teaches that one to weigh them, five cents a crossing. The pocket in which the nickels are counted is the same ground as the field where mounds are read — a place where the past is kept in full season and everything is bagged and labelled before it moves — and the committee that keeps it, one revision per monograph, is filed at Safety Data Sheet, Rev. 15, for the substance ASTON, Michael Antony “Mick”. On the Paterson River the coin has also been dreamed below the ford at Vacy, in the lomandra clump, and the classifieds page that keeps the record of it is The Scrub Ledger, No. 312, where the found items wait, thermos in hand, for their owners; the five nickels of that dream have not yet been claimed and the ledger holds them at sixpence a line. The rest of the small change folio, and the shelf on which the clear-day map is pinned, are kept on the front page of the miscellany, Webstuffs.
