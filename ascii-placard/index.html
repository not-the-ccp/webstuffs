The One Hundred and Twenty-Eight

A museum placard — accessioned 14 November 1967, read aloud at the opening by the Keeper of the Shed, who mispronounced it ASS-kee on the first attempt and was correct, and never corrected the record.

On the roll of the opening, kept in the minute book of the House, the only entry under Absent reads, word for word: Absent George M. McCune and Edwin O. Reischauer, representing the estate of Edwin O. Reischauer. The names were not on the wall to honour the men, one of whom had been dead forty-three years at the opening and the other four and a half, but because in 1939 the two of them had made the first serious attempt to spell a foreign language in the standard’s own letters, and the exhibit would not admit a test case it did not also admit its bearers.

The thing on the wall is not a thing. It is a rule agreed by a committee of the American National Standards Institute that sat in New York from 1956 to 1963, published as X3.4 in 1963 and republished, in its second edition, the very year this placard was hung — the American Standard Code for Information Interchange, known by its letters alone ever since: ASCII. The rule says how a machine should write a character down: a value from 0 to 127, storable as a seven-bit integer, and the whole alphabet — ninety-five of the code points printable, the other thirty-three control — fits inside the seven bits like salt beef in a hooped barrel, tight and honest and no wider. Digits 0 to 9, lowercase a to z, uppercase A to Z, and the punctuation — that is the count, and the count is the whole standard.

What the committee gave the languages was the punctuation, and the languages took it. The semicolon at 59, the brace at 123, the ampersand at 38, the hyphen at 45: these were set down in 1963, and for sixty years the syntax of every computer language since has bent around them. A compiler written in this shed’s yard is guilty of the standard at least twice, once at the semicolon and once at the brace. The standard gave the punctuation to the languages, and the languages made it load-bearing. That exchange is the exhibit.

The wall of ninety-five

The ninety-five printable code points, hung in order from the space at 32 to the tilde at 126, as the committee filed them. Touch a letter to read its number back: decimal, octal, and hex, in the three registers the desk keeps.

The space at the far left is a character too — code 32 — and the committee counted it, which the Council of the Shed has taken as evidence that the committee was a people of good sense. The tilde at the far right, 126, is the last printable before the control character DEL at 127, which was on the wall for a season as the exhibit’s deletion and was taken down after the third visitor used it on a wall they did not own.

The thirty-three that never print

The control characters keep the paper moving. NUL at 0, the nothing at the head of every frame; CR at 13 and LF at 10, the carriage return and the line feed, still pulling the type bar home in every text file that has ever been saved; ESC at 27, the one that begins every escape; and the three the shed uses daily at the desk — Tab at 9, for the ledger, VT at 11, for the margin, and FF at 12, for the new page of the minute book. Thirty-three in number, and not one of them is ever printed, which is exactly their job. The first track of the shed’s 1999 recording was made of them: tape hiss counted as percussion on takes 3 and 7, in the liner notes to Latin Alphabet No. 9, which is this exhibit’s own eighth-bit descendant — ISO/IEC 8859-15, an ASCII-based encoding that surrenders eight little-used glyphs for the euro sign at 0xA4 and the letters Š, š, Ž, ž, Œ, œ, Ÿ, and which is kept in the same wing because a house that hangs a rule hangs its children too.

Why the seven bits

The seven bits are the exhibit’s load-bearing wall. A seven-bit integer holds 128 values, and 128 values hold exactly the English language the standard was made for — 95 printable, 33 control, no remainder. The set of available punctuation had a significant impact on the syntax of computer languages and text markup, as the languages above will attest, and the set of letters has since come to underwrite the whole trade: the first 128 code points of Unicode are the same as ASCII, which is to say that the standard did not survive so much as it was adopted, and that a character set built for one alphabet in 1963 now carries the romanizations of every alphabet in the house. The McCune–Reischauer romanization of Korean, first published 1939 and adopted through the war as the foundation of current romanizations of Korean place names, is spelled in these 128 and no other; the sitting of the Council of the Hall that kept the matter on record, with the three Korean linguists and the test case of the name Dong-hoon, is in the Minutes No. 57 — The Matter of McCune–Reischauer, where the seal of the Hall was applied over the second name at the second name’s expense. That is why the two bearers are absent on the roll and present on the wall: the standard keeps the spelling, and the spelling keeps the men.
