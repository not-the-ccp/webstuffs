The Little Platoons

A radio play in one scene, on the philosophy of keeping what you were given — transmitted from the attic above the print works, with one borrowed instrument that cannot be turned off

Written and produced by N. · recorded on the studio loop, third reel · the counter returns to Bench 7 on Wednesday

Cast and instruments

NED — the producer, 71, keeper of the 4B exercise book. MIRELA — the night engineer, her third season in the attic. THE HUNDREDTH MAN — a voice on the studio loop, the family of the nation, wearing a plan under one arm. THE COUNTER — a geiger counter, borrowed from the shelf below with its dial facing the microphone, ticking once about every four seconds. It is the only character on this frequency that does not improvise.

[ CUE: the loop turns over. The counter, four seconds. The red lamp comes on without ceremony. ]

Scene 1 · The attic studio, Tuesday, 23:00

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds. A reel counter in the corner of the room marks, one revolution at a time. ]

MIRELA Red light is on. Four seconds to the tick, same as last night.

NED Same as the day the machine stopped. I don’t improve the numbers. I checked them three times.

MIRELA The whole studio smells like the print works. And the counter is louder than usual.

NED It is not louder. You are quieter. The counter came down from the shelf, where the build log of reactor 4 runs, entry by entry, and it has been correct the entire evening. I have not touched its dial, and I am not going to.

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds. Ned sets the 4B exercise book on the desk, open at the page where the sleeve bends. ]

MIRELA Are we on the play?

NED We are on the play. The little platoons are seated. One scene, three chairs, and the door stays shut.

Scene 2 · The Letter (as played, from the loop)

[ CUE: the loop deepens. The counter keeps its four seconds through every sentence, which is the point of having it on. ]

A nation is a partnership — not merely between those who are living, but between the living, the dead, and those yet to be born. What you have received from your fathers, you hold in trust for your children. You are not entitled to improve what you were given. You are obliged to transmit it, and to hand it over better than it was handed to you.

MIRELA That’s the letter.

NED That’s Edmund Burke. Dublin, 25 November 1729, the Anglo-Irish statesman who watched the French Revolution from a distance he considered correct, and wrote his Reflections in 1790 — against it, with the whole weight of what he called the little platoon of society. The family at the table, the parish, the militia, the charter, the courts, the flag over the mill. Conservatism is mostly the list of things a family refuses to rename, and it is a different list in every country, which is why the play can be one scene: the scene is the refusal.

[ CUE: a knock at the studio door, though nobody in the attic opens doors. The Hundredth Man arrives on the loop. ]

THE HUNDREDTH MAN I am the artificer of new systems. I have a plan.

THE LETTER You are the little artificer. The house is older than your plan, and the plan is one morning’s little property.

THE HUNDREDTH MAN And the door?

THE LETTER The door stays where your grandmother set it. The family, the parish, the court, the charter, the constitution, the property line, the flag — none of it takes a new name tonight.

THE HUNDREDTH MAN And if the house is on fire?

THE LETTER Then you put out the fire. That is not a plan. That is maintenance, and it is the whole of the craft.

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds, exactly where the play leaves the fire unmentioned. ]

Scene 3 · The stripe and the needle

MIRELA Is that the whole play?

NED That is the whole play. Maintenance is quiet on the air, and the play is an hour of quiet with a knock in it.

MIRELA The diorama below — I’ve been down the stairs. The barrier around the zone is three millimetres of plastic with a yellow stripe, and the build log says the stripe is a feature.

NED A stripe is a line with a policy. Burke was a stripe man: a thin line drawn across the map, and the entire argument is what happens on each side of it. The family stays inside, the fire stays outside, and the line is inherited, not drawn by the day.

MIRELA That is the doctrine, then. The one the bench has been answering about.

NED That is the opposite argument, the lid from the inside. Here is the ledger the bench kept. There is one line in the margin, and the attic keeps its lines word for word, so I read it whole.

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds. Ned turns the page so the sleeve bends toward the microphone. ]

In one line: a socialist country's sovereignty ended where its loyalty began.

MIRELA 1968.

NED 1968, and the lid sat on the pot for twenty years. Read it the other way round and it is this play: a family’s sovereignty ends where its own roof begins, and the line that bounds it — the family, the parish, the court, the charter — is not drawn by the capital. It is drawn by the grandmother, and the whole of conservatism is the refusal to admit the line was ever drawn at all, because a line you inherited is not a stripe. It is a wall you have stopped seeing.

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds, holding the silence the way it holds a number. ]

Out

[ CUE: the loop runs down. The red lamp stays on until the engineer chooses it. ]

MIRELA Reel counter says two thousand, three hundred and seventeen ticks since we opened the desk.

NED Then the counter has said more than either of us, and it was correct. Return it to the shelf on Wednesday, dial facing the camera, the way it sits on the sarcophagus. The needle does not move, because needles are optimistic.

[ CUE: the counter, four seconds. The attic holds it. ]

Transmission notes

The play was recorded in one pass on the studio loop, third reel, and broadcast whole. The counter was borrowed from the bench that keeps the entry-by-entry build log of reactor 4, where it sits on the sarcophagus with its dial facing the camera; it was returned on Wednesday morning with its reading unchanged. The margin line it carried is the doctrine line, and the bench has answered about it at length in The Brezhnev Doctrine, questions and answers — the ledger scrubbed year by year from 1968 to 1991, the lid that was lifted and never put back. The play takes the same line and stands it the other way up, which is the whole of conservatism on one frequency: the institutions a family keeps, the line it inherited, and the fire it is not obliged to explain. The 4B exercise book goes back to the second drawer, and the studio keeps its four seconds whether anyone is listening or not.
