Bedminster, Bristol

11 April 2024

Dear Ashton Gate,

An apology to Bristol City Football Club

BCFC

I owe you an apology, Bristol City. I owe it to the turnstile at Ashton Gate Road that knew my face for fourteen winters, to the Atyeo Stand where I stood first with my father and then without him, and to the red shirt I folded away in 2014 and have not worn since.

I know the club's history better than I know my own address. Founded in 1894, Western League champions in 1897–98, admitted to the Football League in 1901, Second Division champions in 1905–06, second in the First Division the following season—three points behind Newcastle United—and beaten by Manchester United in the 1909 FA Cup final. Relegated in 1911, down to the third tier in 1922, then Third Division South champions in 1922–23. One hundred and fifteen years since Crystal Palace, and I still know the words to “One for the Robins” off by heart.

My failure came in the spring of 2014. Relegation to League One arrived on the final day, and I took it as permission to stop believing. I told my son the Robins were finished, let the season ticket lapse, and stayed away even when the team won its way back. I missed the Wembley play-off final, the return to the Championship, the floodlights on Ashton Gate Road in autumn. I treated you like a debt that had defaulted, when you were only a promise being kept.

The night I needed to understand this was the night Duke struck the crossbar. It was the eighty-ninth minute at Elland Road, Leeds United pressing, the ball flying from Duke’s head onto the woodwork and back into play—a draw, a missed point, a season that quietly ended right then. The same minute, I later read in Mile Jedinak’s assistant coach diary, Lee Kang-in hit the crossbar with a free-kick in Al Rayyan. I blame Duke still, sometimes, though I know the crossbar is innocent, and I never apologized to him for the noise I made in the stand that night. When at last I appeared before the Matchnight & Trophy Business Committee to give evidence about that night, they ruled the crossbar innocent, eight to one, the dissenting vote being cast by the absent crossbar itself.

Duke hit the crossbar with a cartilage which defends and covers the tooth, will often instantly pacify a crying and fretting, showing more than that.

— Lila’s tablet, voice-to-text, half-time at Elland Road

My daughter was eight, watching on her tablet at a friend’s house, and the microphone had caught the commentator while a dictionary page sat open in the background—or so I tell myself. The cartilage which defends and covers the tooth is a gum. The thing that will instantly pacify a crying and fretting child is a dummy. “Showing more than that” has no meaning at all, and yet we built a week’s worth of explanations around it: the woodwork was out to get us, the fates were against us, the club was done. Nonsense, and comforting nonsense, the kind we invent when we want an excuse to leave.

So here is the apology, properly: I am sorry I walked out. I am sorry I told my son the Robins were finished. I am sorry I made Duke carry a crossbar that was never his to carry. My season ticket is renewed from Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, and we will be there in August, under the floodlights, in the EFL Championship, red shirts washed and ready.

Petra Vance’s letter on machine unlearning sits beside my fixture list—some things you take back out cleanly, and some you simply stop offering. The same ledger-keeping instinct lives up in Buchan, where a small club of estate antiquaries has been logging, one newsletter at a time, what the fifty-square-mile Pitfour Estate lost over two centuries—racecourse, lake, observatory—and I have written to ask whether the brass washer may be entered on their board of things found and let go: The Pitfour Post, No. 41. And in the shed below Clerkenwell, David Chen has been doing the same arithmetic on a plywood bench for sixteen years, pegging out a whole county to make up for a single adjective he printed in 2006: Cheshire, Build Log No. 18. The same bench has since printed a narrower board, a railway timetable in red ink for a cricketer, one line with no lay-bys: the Botham Express, Timetable No. 24 — I have pinned it behind my fixture list, because a man who keeps a brass washer in his coat pocket for matchdays should also keep the record of the fastest all-round service ever booked into a timetable. I wrote to him about it, and he sent me one brass washer, addressed to Gate 13, Row H, Seat 14, which I keep in the pocket of my coat for matchdays. The same ledger instinct is at work in the auction room behind the bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, where the household of Marlow & Fenwick offered, in its Spring Catalogue No. CXL, one (1) album, Better, the second studio album of Haley Reinhart, with its two singles and one pencilled line on the inner flap — Lot 42 of the sale — and I did not bid, because I had the washer to keep dry that morning, and the album went to the room next door, where it has been playing every Tuesday since. I carried that washer down to Cardiff—the capital, the largest city in Wales, 381,516 of them by 2025, a small town until the coal made it otherwise—and sat one Thursday at the coalhouse by the Bute Docks, where the woodwork verdict gets argued at the pass and the dessert is cut to the shape of David’s bench: The Coalhouse, a review from table nine. I am asking to offer myself again, if you will have me. The coalhouse man also pressed me with the liner notes of Cantons of the South Stand — a Cardiff record, SOULINE SLP 088, built entirely out of one Welsh footballer’s career: 14 caps, one goal, the Plymouth loan, the £350,000 Palace season and its eighteen months — and I have put it in the glovebox now, because I suspect the Robins and the South Stand keep the same kind of ledger, and both of them forgive, eventually.

Before Cardiff, though, the brass washer had already made the smaller papers: Prague’s Bohemia Bazaar ran it beside a wire-sifter and a dining room that holds forty-four, the whole column priced in koruny and pinned above the ticket stubs at Národní třída: Bohemia Bazaar — small ads from Prague

The same ledger instinct takes me to the Surrey County Asylum at Netherne, where each ward kept its admissions book and the patients kept a studio in which nothing was ever erased; I read its register as I would a scorebook, entry by entry: Netherne Hospital — Frequently Asked Questions.

While I was folding the red shirt I also placed an advertisement in Belfast’s Lagan Ledger — small ads, Northern Ireland — offering that shirt, folded away in 2014, and an apology, to anyone who still believes in woodwork and comebacks: Northern Ireland — small ads.

The habit of writing to institutions I have wronged has since carried me to the Adriatic, where a three-mast training ship built in Hamburg between 1930 and 1933, commissioned on 19 August 1933 and renamed by a second navy in April 1941, received her own letter from a retired midshipman, one strand at a different hand: To the Editor — The Braid of the Jadran. I have pinned it beside the fixture list, because a ship that outlives the navy that took her name is only a better argument than I ever managed to make to you.

Yours, reluctantly and faithfully,

Arthur Parry

Bedminster, Bristol · Season ticket holder since 1999
