Court of Session · Outer House · Edinburgh · Before the Lord Ordinary · Transcript Excerpt

Ex parte Balmoral

No. M/1852 · Estate and Title of the Castle and Lands of Balmoral, parish of Crathie, Aberdeenshire

THE KING,

Applicant,

by his Solicitor, the Advocate for the Household;

&

THE COMMISSIONER FOR THE CROWN ESTATE,

Interested Party, appearing by the Estate Solicitor.

No. M/1852

Outer House

Session of 1852, reopened

BEFORE THE LORD ORDINARY,

holding the hearing in the open court on the Mound.

The Sherriff-depute of the Deeside seat attended for the village of Crathie; no objection was taken to the attendance.

Sealed
with the Estate Seal

The Lord Ordinary. I shall take up the transcript where the clerk left it, and I shall read what is entered, without striking out a line. The question before this Court is narrow, and both parties will agree to most of it: whether the Castle and Lands of Balmoral, near the village of Crathie in Aberdeenshire, is to be held and accounted as the private property of the reigning Sovereign, or as part of the Crown Estate. It is the first time the question has been put, and I shall put it once, in plain words, before any of the evidence is heard.

First — of the place, which no one disputes

The Lord Ordinary. Let the record show where the thing in question stands. Balmoral (bal-MORR-ēl) is a large estate house in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, and a residence of the British royal family. It stands near the village of Crathie, nine miles — the parties will accept fourteen kilometres — west of Ballater, and fifty miles, which is eighty kilometres for the clerk who keeps the metric column, west of Aberdeen. The Deeside seat confirms the village’s attendance. The estate extends up the braes above the Dee’s north bank; what is under debate is the title, not the hills.

Second — of the purchase of 1852, which is the whole of the title

The Lord Ordinary. The estate, and the original castle upon it, had come to the Farquharson family long before the present case — generations of it, the evidence says, and no one disputes the generations. In 1852 the estate was bought from the Farquharsons by Prince Albert, the husband of Queen Victoria. It was a purchase. The word will matter presently, because it is the word that decides the case. Soon after the purchase it was found that the house was too small for the household that intended to winter nowhere and to summer there. The current Balmoral Castle was therefore commissioned. The architect was William Smith of Aberdeen; his designs were amended in his drawing room by Prince Albert himself, in pencil, in the margin, over the course of the summer. The new castle was brought to a state of habitation in little more than five years, and the estate’s own account was opened in the same ledger room it has kept ever since.

Third — of the title, which is the only thing in dispute

The Lord Ordinary. Balmoral is the private property of the reigning Sovereign, presently King Charles III, and it is not part of the Crown Estate. I say that, and I say the reason with it. The Crown Estate is what the Crown inherits and holds for the public account; Balmoral was bought with private resolution and has been kept, every reign since, out of the private hands. It passed from Queen Victoria to Edward VII, from Edward VII to George V, from George V to George VI, and so on down through the long household of which the summer stays, the pony meetings at Crathie, and the Deeside post are the ordinary record. It has never once appeared in the accounts of the Crown Estate. That silence is, I find, not an omission but a title. The Commissioner does not contend the contrary; the Commissioner contends only that the question should be recorded, and that is all the Court is asked to do.

Fourth — of the books kept through the war

The Lord Ordinary. The estate account was kept through the Second World War, which is the portion of the transcript both parties direct me to read in full, because it is the portion in which the entry that must stand does stand. The household at the northern estate kept its own hour while the southern one kept its blackout, and the books kept both. The household account that answers the war year in the household of the Queen — the one that carried the clubs and charities, the steel implements from Birmingham, and the Saltfleet bridle leather bound into the ledger covers — is kept at the household page of Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother. The same ledger was bound, as the kitchen bound its own, in bridle leather from the loft above Saltfleet; duty, like a well-made lash, holds best when it is made by hand. And the folio itself, entered through the same years and the same blackouts — the fire of 1968, the sherry on order, the kettle at 1s. 6d. — is carried, page by page, on The General Ledger, liner notes of Ledgerdisc LD-24-51. And the shelf beside the Deeside books keeps one more winter of the record, the Presidents’ Day Storm of 2003 worked up in the PDII build log, where a torn almanac scrap has been read into the bench, word for word, and left where it fell.

The Lord Ordinary. The entry of 1940, entered against the folio of Balmoral, Crathie, and the household thereto attached, is read into the record, word for word, as it stands:

“Why it is the only member of the ledger would have kept regardless of the war, becoming a symbol of civilian resilience.”

The Lord Ordinary. The entry is read word for word. It is the style of the kitchen that kept the ledger, not of this bench, and I will not improve it. Read as the ledger reads it, it means this: the household at Balmoral in 1940 kept its tea at the same hour the blackout kept its lamps, and the entry that went into the books that year was written down, and written down, and written down, because a household that keeps its books keeps its nerve. What the East End of London was told at four o’clock on 13 September, the Deeside books were told at the same hour on the same day, and the entry stands in both. It is not, I find, nonsense; it is the ledger’s way of saying that a house which will not stop keeping its accounts will not stop keeping its people, and that the same symbol carried the war from the drawing room in the north to the bombed street in the south, and out of both into the record.

Finding of the Court

  1. That the Castle and Lands of Balmoral, near Crathie, in Aberdeenshire, were bought in 1852 from the Farquharson family by Prince Albert, husband of Queen Victoria, and that the current castle was raised to the designs of William Smith of Aberdeen, as amended by Prince Albert.
  2. That Balmoral is the private property of King Charles III and is not part of the Crown Estate; the Commissioner is so recorded, and the silence of the Crown Estate’s books is the title.
  3. That the estate account was kept through the war, and that the entry of 1940 is to stand in the record, word for word, without striking out.
  4. That the question, having been put once, is closed, and the books may rest at five o’clock, no exceptions.

Signed on the Mound, in the open court.
For the record, read as entered.

Signed & entered
