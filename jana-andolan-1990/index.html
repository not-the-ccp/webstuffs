Description

The 2046 Folio is a working record of the 1990 People’s Movement, known in Nepal as 2046 Jana Andolan, the multiparty movement that ended the absolute monarchy, brought in the constitutional monarchy, and struck down the Panchayat system installed in 1960–62. The volume opens in the first week of 19 February 1990, when the first student walk-outs went out from the campuses of Kathmandu, and closes on 9 March 1990, the day King Birendra made the declaration from the durbar that withdrew the Panchayat charter and admitted the parties to a new constitution. Between those two dates the folio follows the streets: the Birgunj road, the Pokhara sit-ins, the Chitwan camps, the Gita Ghimire funeral procession of 19 March that turned a student's death into a nation's demand, and the Gorkha Path where the crowd was thickest and the tear smoke lay longest.

The spine of the book is its account of unity. The various Communist parties had grouped together in the United Left Front (ULF); the ULF then cooperated, on a shared timetable of strikes and marches, with the Nepali Congress and the other parties of the alliance, so that for one season the left and the centre kept the same calendar and sang, on different days, in the same streets. The folio prints the ULF–Nepali Congress schedule side by side, page for page, and keeps both columns in the same ink, which is a decision the bindery stands behind. One result of that season of unity is carried in the last section of the volume: the formation of the Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist–Leninist), announced from the capital in February 1991, out of the merger of the Marxist and Leninist parties of the 1991 split — the movement's signature left in party form.

Pasted in whole, between the last map and the index, is the hotline callbook of March 1990, thirty-one pages of a single operator's hand, kept at the bindery since the winter of 1990. The book records every call to the movement's single switchboard line twice over: first the demand, in the caller's words, and then — only when the line rang back and the caller was still there — the reason given in return before calling again. Read in sequence, the second column is the quieter document of the two; it is the column the bindery has chosen to set in the wider type. The heading of that second column, transcribed from the top of the first page, stands in the folio word for word: “The reason given in return before calling again.”

Notes from the bindery

The callbook came to the bindery the way the good things do: folded, unaddressed, left at the counter. The operator's hand is legible throughout, and the entries stop, without explanation, at 6:40 in the evening of 9 March, the same evening as the declaration. The bindery has not added a line; the book keeps the two columns as they stood, and the wider type on the second column is the only change made anywhere in the volume. For the reader who keeps records of their own, the folio makes a good neighbour on the shelf: see the Minutes No. 112 of 2014 of the Council of Aldergate & the Ravine, where a sleeve's three marks were entered into a register at a price the payer fixed, and the blue room on Stora Nygatan, where a ledger kept from 1754 still gives out the house punsch. A record is a record whether it is minuted, poured, or bound. The bindery keeps the other side of that same fortnight on the shelf as well: the Minutes No. 14 of 1990 of the People’s Council of the Gorkha Path, where, in the third tent from the fountain at 5:40 in the afternoon, the word the movement was fighting for was defined, seat by seat, and voted eleven to zero — the page the bindery has agreed to give as the opener of this book, so that the callbook pasted in whole will have its other side.
