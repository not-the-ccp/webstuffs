Ferdinand de Saussure

26 November 1857, Geneva — 22 February 1913, Geneva  ·  Swiss linguist, semiotician and philosopher

1  Life

Ferdinand Mongin de Saussure was born on 26 November 1857 in Geneva and raised in the village of Monthey in the canton of Valais, in a family of engineers whose grandfather Horace-Bénédict de Saussure had measured mountains in the same weather in which the boy would later measure words. He studied at Geneva and at Paris, where he was admitted to the École des Langues Orientales before his twentieth year, and he turned in 1876 to the Indo-European languages — the family that this register of ours keeps — after a single term with the Germanist Hugo Schuchardt, whose classroom he described as a place where a man could hear himself being made. His doctoral dissertation at Leipzig, 1878, Mémoire sur le système primitif des voyelles dans les langues indo-européennes, was judged by the Société de Linguistique de Paris in 1880 to have won the prize, and it was the first work to treat the vowel system of the parent tongue as a system at all, rather than a list. He took his doctorate under Karl Brugmann, held a chair at the Faculty of Letters in Geneva from 1878 to 1891, left the chair in 1891 for reasons he never fully gave, and in 1907, at fifty, was offered the first Chair of General Linguistics anywhere in the world, which he held until his death on 22 February 1913, at the age of fifty-five, of a short illness in Geneva.

2  The Sign

The central move of the Cours de linguistique générale — published posthumously in 1916 by Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye from notes of two courses, 1907 and 1910–11, that Saussure had given and never written down — is the split of the sign into signifier (the sound-image, valeur sonore) and signified (the concept). The bond between them is arbitrary: nothing in the air of the word mother resembles the woman who answers it, and every language on earth has had to re-invent the bond from scratch, which is the oldest and cheapest fact in the register. The signifier is linear, unfolding in time the way a spoken sentence does; the signified is not linear but a whole. And the bond is immovable for a single community at a single moment, though mutable across time and across the whole of a community together: a word changes when everyone changes it, or not at all. The register reads this as its own standing order. A name in the hall changes only when the hall agrees, and no one may change one alone, and the Keeper has since understood that the order was never about the names but about the agreeing.

3  Value

Saussure's sharper word is value (valeur). The sign, he argued, has no positive content of its own; it is defined negatively, by difference, against every other sign in the system. His example is the chess piece: the piece is not its material, wood or ivory, but the role it holds in the board at a given moment, and the piece destroyed in the fire of a house is, for the game, a piece that no longer exists while its replacement already does. Every word in every branch of the family works by that same subtraction. This is why cognates form themselves in the speaking, as the letter of apology put it, and why the family crossed Europe and the subcontinent carried mouth to mouth without a single record of the carrying: the system moves, and the system does not need the bearer's signature. The entry filed beside this one at Entry 314 · Given Name keeps the Hall of Nine Doors and the Keeper's receipt; Saussure would have filed that receipt first and the name second, and he would not have been wrong.

4  Langue and Parole; the Synchronic and the Diachronic

Saussure divided the total of language into langue, the social system of signs stored in the speaker's memory, and parole, the individual and concrete act of speaking, and insisted that the object of linguistics is the langue, because only the system is regular, describable, and owed to the community rather than to the speaker. He then cut the system along a second axis: the synchronic study of the system as it stands at one moment, against the neighbours that define it, and the diachronic study of the same system along time, where value shifts because the cuts of the system shift. The two studies are, in his phrase, incompatible, and the synchronic comes first, because the diachronic has nothing to describe until the system has stood still long enough to be described. A vial read on the twelfth — name, locality, date, keeper — is a synchronic act, and so is this entry, and the fire that burned Field Note SLL-YG/2 is the diachronic, and the register keeps both files though it cannot keep them in the same hand.

5  Semiologie and the Second Founder

Saussure closed the Cours by declaring that a general science of the life of signs in society would exist, which he called semiologie (semiology, in the English carried by his translator Roy Harris, whose 1953 edition of the Course put the whole apparatus before the Anglophone world and summarized its reach across “the whole range of human sciences — particularly marked in linguistics, philosophy, psychoanalysis, psychology, sociology”). He named the field; someone else had already built much of it. Charles Sanders Peirce, working in the United States in the same years and without seeing Saussure's manuscript, had developed a full logic of the sign from 1868 to 1905, and the two are held together now as the two founders of semiology, the one who named it and the one who had already built it. The entry here is filed under Saussure alone, because the register keeps one name per card, and because the Cours is the book, and the book is what the shelves can hold.

6  Cross-Files

The letter of apology to the family this entry describes is kept at Apology to the Indo-European Languages, where the Keeper reads the eight branches aloud against the labels — name, locality, date, keeper — and keeps the silence after. The discipline of that reading belongs to Chapter YG-3 · The Ybyrapora Gamba, whose vivarium standard sets the case 30 × 20 × 25 cm, the cork bark split, the water shallow enough to climb out of, and whose capture protocol says of the limits on taking, “The limit is not for the spider's comfort.” It is against that line that the register's own note must be read. At the back of the hall, in the margin of the 1916 Cours, in a hand the Keeper has never identified, stands one line of the vivarium log, kept here word for word and offered without correction, the way Saussure would have offered any token: as a sign, and only as a sign.

Returning to the white, but is more than continual reports of breakages, which, of course, be polite for the spider's comfort.

Read against the chapter, the line takes its shape. The white is the white-painted stake of rebar, forty centimetres, at the three localities — the bend at Pedra Funda, the clay borrow south of the third bridge, the overhang above Boca do Onça — and the sentence is a report that whatever walked away has returned to it. The continual reports of breakages are the glass cases of the vivarium shelf, cracked at the corners, that the detachment has kept gluing in the same order as the vials are read. And be polite for the spider's comfort is the only place in either document where the comfort is the spider's and not the collector's; the manual's limit protects the man with the brush, and this line, in the register's hand, quietly inverts the protection and points it the other way. The Keeper does not know who wrote it. The entry does not require him to. A sign is what it is by difference, and this line is what it is by standing beside a line that says the opposite, on the same shelf, in the same hand the hall does not admit to using.

The card is the sign; the bond is arbitrary; the family is what happens when eight separate guesses at the same mother are made by different mouths and come out close enough to be heard.
