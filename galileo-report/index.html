1. What the word ‘Galileo’ means in my report

There are two famous Galileos, and I have spent the whole term keeping them separate. One is the Italian astronomer, Galileo Galilei, who looked at the Moon with a telescope in the late 1500s and early 1600s and wrote down what he saw. The other is a machine: a constellation of navigation satellites built by the European Union, created through the European Space Agency and run day to day by the European Union Agency for the Space Programme, EUSPA. The machine took the astronomer’s name because it is, in the words of the brochure, “a project for positioning that belongs to Europe” — so Europe named it after one of its own great observers. I think that is a fair trade. A telescope pointed up; the same telescope pointed down, from orbit.

2. The money and the buildings

The project cost around ten billion euros, which is the sort of number the class had to check twice. The agency that runs it is headquartered in Prague in Czechia. There are two ground operations centres: one in Oberpfaffenhofen, Germany, which is mostly responsible for the control of the satellites, and one in Fucino, Italy, which is mostly responsible for providing the navigation data. So the machines are steered from Bavaria and the numbers are written out from Abruzzo, and Prague sits between the two keeping the registers. Limited services began in 2016, and the full constellation is larger than anyone in 4B expected — more than thirty satellites, with a handful kept on the ground as spares, like extra chalk on the shelf.

3. Why Europe built its own

The reason given in every handout is that one of the aims of Galileo is to provide an independent high-precision positioning system so European political and military authorities are not dependent on a satellite system owned and switched on and switched off by someone else. Miss Marsh wrote in the margin of my first draft: Independent means — what exactly? Answer that. I answered that it means the signal still arrives if the foreign one stops, and that the signal carries a time stamp that the power grid, the phone towers and the banks are quietly relying on, which is a harder kind of dependence than missiles. I think that was the paragraph that earned its sentence in red: The power-grid point. Good.

4. How a fix is made (Chapter 4 came in handy)

Our Field Manual FM-9, Chapter 4 taught the class that latitude and longitude are not lengths at all but angles, taken from the centre of the Earth. The satellites are just a polite way of getting more angles. Each satellite broadcasts where it believes it is and what time it is; the receiver compares the arrival times and triangulates, and with three satellites you get a position, with a fourth you get a better time and a position that trusts itself. What the chapter’s standing orders still warn about — a careless datum costing you the width of the channel — still applies. The satellites are precise, and the map under you can still be wrong, and Galileo cannot argue with your map.

5. The map corner

In the geography half of the lesson we each drew a map from a source of our choosing. I drew from the Cressa safety data sheet, which describes an ancient city on the Black Sea coast that nobody has been able to find, and says of the seabed that it is a patient archivist and a hostile one at once. Dane’s map of the same stretch of coast is what I want to show you. He was asked to label the shelf, and in the top corner of his paper, in block capitals, it reads The Black Sea shelf trace, and underneath it, in much smaller capitals, Everything else is mud. I copied both lines verbatim for this report — The Black Sea shelf trace Everything else is mud. — because it is the truest geography in the class and I did not want it lost. The sheet agrees with him: sediment, Black Sea shelf, trace.

6. Down on the coast

The jangada skippers off Pedro do Rosário have started carrying Galileo receivers on the boats, next to the old rule that the rock shows above the flats at low water and the tide must not be allowed to turn on you. Miss Marsh’s position, which she said twice, is that the rock does not go away: a satellite tells you where you are, the rock tells you the story, and the skipper who trusts only the satellite is the one who will be writing the report nobody reads. I keep the rock and the receiver in the same sentence on purpose.

7. What I would add if the report were longer

How the satellites are launched in pairs from Kourou, what the spares on the ground are waiting for, and how the Italian centre in Fucino hands its numbers across two oceans to receivers that have never heard of Fucino. Also: whether the astronomer Galileo, who argued that the Earth moves, would have minded lending his name to a system whose whole purpose is knowing exactly where the Earth is not. I suspect he would have liked it. He was fond of being in motion. And one thing I could not fit in at all — what the European legal act called a directive, which sets goals and lets the member states write the laws, looks like at the end of its citation life. The class has since pinned up the obituary of Directive 2019/904, and it settles the matter: it was an angle, not a length, and it lived in twenty-seven laws that were not its own.

Teacher’s note, red ink

A careful account, and for once the two Galileos stay in their proper lanes. The Prague–Oberpfaffenhofen–Fucino geography is exact, and the power-grid paragraph is the best sentence in the class’s work this term. The Dane Halloway quotation should be framed. I have left the Black Sea as it is, because it is mud, and mud is correct. B+.
