RUBA’I

A Game of the Persianate Table — for one to three players, played in the meter of the quatrain.

KHAYYAM
WHEEL

Rule 11 What the game is

RUBA’I is played on a board the size of the shed door, and it is a game about a language: Persian, which its own speakers call Farsi. Persian is a Western Iranian language, sitting in the Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian subdivision of the Indo-European family, and it is a pluricentric language, which the game is honest about instead of fudging. The board therefore holds one language at three tables, in three mutually intelligible standard varieties: at the Isfahan table, Iranian Persian, officially known simply as Persian; at the Kabul table, Afghan Persian, officially known as Dari since 1964; at the Dushanbe table, Tajiki Persian, officially known as Tajik since 1999. A fourth chair at the Dushanbe table is left empty for the players who read Tajiki across the Uzbek border without ever changing chairs, and the margin of the box carries the note the first draft made: the table is the language, the chair is the country. The box art shows the three tables under one sky; this copy of the manual is stained with damson at the corner where the jar sits, which the third printing has decided is a feature.

Rule 12 In the box

  • a The board, 52 × 52 cm, mounted on the reverse of a 1962 road atlas, Herat to Samarkand, its crease along the Helmand.
  • b Thirty-one walnut tokens, one for each province of the Iranian table; the others are turned from the same stock and sanded to a duller edge.
  • c The Khayyam Wheel, a twelve-spoked disc of brass card, sized for a mathematician’s hand and calibrated to 4–3–4–3.
  • d One envelope of quatrains, four lines each, folded in the same envelope the log keeps its stanzas in.
  • e One glass jar, half full of damson, and one wooden spoon. These are not decorative. See Rule 17.

Rule 14 Movement

Turn the wheel one spoke at a time, and let it set the step: a step is four beats, a rest of three, four beats again, a rest of three — 4–3–4–3, the pacing of the ruba’i itself, line and a half, line and a half, the way a man paces a room. On the fours a token walks a province; on the threes it must stop at the well. No token may walk on a rest, and no rest may be skipped, because the meter is the law of the table and the wheel was calibrated for it twice before the box was shut. The wheel in this page of the manual turns if you ask it.

Rule 15 Claiming a province

To claim a province a token stands on, its player draws one folded sheet from the envelope and reads the quatrain aloud at the well. The third line is the trouble, as it always is: it refuses to resolve, so the player who claimed the province may not end the line the sheet ended it. The claim stands only when a fourth line has been spoken that lands like a stern. The sheet is then returned to the envelope face down. Two provinces claimed on the same reading is the whole of the player’s profit; the meter will not pay more than it is owed.

Rule 16 Crossing the tables

A token may cross from one table to another at any border, because the three varieties are mutually intelligible and the language does not stop at the chair. A crossing costs one rest, and the player must finish the line begun in the first table in the tongue of the second — Dari at Kabul, Tajik at Dushanbe, Persian at Isfahan. This is the entire game in one rule: the same language, wearing three names, and the player who learns the crossings first is home before the others have found the map. The box, in small type along the base, adds: the counts in Uzbekistan do not change the tongue.

Rule 17 The jar

The bank of the game is the jar. The first player to claim a province takes a spoonful from it to set on the well, and the jar is re-laid on the workbench the same evening, because the game is not allowed to run out of anything it is played with. The original 1978 printing gives this rule a single line that the printer set in small type and the shed has kept ever since, word for word, because the shed keeps its odd lines the way it keeps its odd parts:

A spoonful of jam of any particular part.

The shed reads it as it reads the margin lines of the galleon: it is not a rule about the game, it is a rule about the pantry. Damson counts, strawberry counts, and if the jar has no particular part in it at all, the spoon goes in anyway, and the well is marked claimed. No player has ever disputed this, which is as close as a rule gets to being proven.

Rule 18 The road under the board

Under the three tables the board carries a road that ran long before them: the route from the Hellespont to the Indus, traced from the atlas that is the board’s back. In 330 BC the baggage trains ground down the same passes through the Alborz, and the Greek cities that grew along that road kept Greek in Bactria for centuries after the roads were empty — but the language underneath kept longer still, and it is the language the game is played in. The one tongue that road passed over but did not carry is filed in the well ledger at Brahvi — Questions Asked at the Well, where the loose manual page stained with damson keeps its entry at stop 3. The whole campaign, from the stake at Sestos to the fever at Babylon, is kept in its own page at Alexander the Great — Encyclopaedia Entry, where the store list this jar answers to is also filed. And the meter the wheel is calibrated to — 4–3–4–3, four-three-four-three — is the ruba’i’s own pacing, the same four-line form that kept its shape in the tent, built and faired in The Quatrain — Build Log, with its stem, its frames, and its stern. The game borrows the frame; the log borrows nothing back.
