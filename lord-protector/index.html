The Lord Protector at Table

Rules of reception, address and dining for the household of Oliver Cromwell (25 April 1599 – 3 September 1658), Lord Protector of the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland.

Oliver Cromwell was born in Huntingdon on 25 April 1599 and died at Whitehall on 3 September 1658, having served as Lord Protector of the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland from 16 December 1653. He came to prominence during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms, first as a cavalry officer of the Eastern Association and later as lieutenant-general of the New Model Army; he advocated the execution of Charles I at the Banqueting House, Whitehall, on 30 January 1649, and was elected Member of Parliament for Huntingdon in 1628, after years in Huntingdon, St Ives and Ely that he remembered as marked by financial and personal failure. This chapter sets down the etiquette of his household — how he was received at the levée, addressed at the Council table, fed at dinner, and served in his chamber — so that a visitor to Whitehall or Hampton Court in the 1650s need not give offence.

Of the Reception

The levée began at six o'clock, after private prayer in the closet. The Protector dressed plainly — a black suit of worsted, a starched linen band, and boots that had seen service — and received his officers in the Council Chamber at Whitehall with his hat on. A visitor was expected to remain covered while the Protector kept his hat on, and to uncover only when bidden; to do otherwise was to misunderstand which head was covered by the Instrument of Government. The Humble Petition and Advice of 1657 settled the address as ‘Your Highness,’ though Cromwell refused the crown itself; before that, ‘Sir’ served, with the office named in full. No hand was kissed, and no ring: a bow from the waist, deep but not lingering, was sufficient. He was not a king, and his household was not a court, though it behaved like one when the foreign ambassadors came.

Of the Table

Dinner was served at noon, two courses, plainly cooked: mutton with caper sauce, carrots from the kitchen garden, small beer, and fruit in season. The Protector ate little and drank less; to praise the food at length was read as flattery, and to press a second helping on a guest was considered vulgar. Seating followed military rank more loosely than peerage: a colonel who had taken a town might sit above an earl who had not. Conversation turned on the campaigns, the weather, the price of grain and the condition of the roads; the late king, the crown, and the thirtieth of January were never named at table.

Of the Warm Vessel

Tea, coffee and chocolate had reached London by the 1650s, and the Protector's steward kept a small store of each at the Cockpit. Their serving was governed by a fragment now kept among the Thurloe papers — a single leaf of a household manual printed for Whitehall in 1657, its corner eaten away by damp and mice. It reads, so far as the damaged leaf allows:

Proceed in this case, also, must not be expected that they were likely, in a glass or china being warmed, and poured itself out in their teething are liable.

The sense of it, strained through the holes in the paper, is this: a glass or china cup that has been warmed holds its heat unevenly and will not pour itself out; the servant must proceed with a steady hand, and no one should expect grace from haste. The leaf's missing corner — the ‘teething’ of mice — is part of the lesson: warm vessels, old vessels and old households are all liable to crack if they are jerked about. Warm the pot, warm the cup, and pour as though the Protector were watching.

Of the Coast and the Fens

The Eastern Association whose regiments Cromwell first commanded quartered the Lincolnshire fens above Saltfleet, where the Wash opens toward Norfolk; the rigging loft above that beach still keeps its lashes and whip patterns, and the same bay is read by The Wash in the way the loft reads the shore. In 1588, eleven years before Cromwell's birth, Admiral Recalde's squadron stood off those sandbanks with the San Juan de Portugal among them — a galleon whose build log, and whose long provisions of vinegar crock and salt beef, are kept at the Armada build log. The etiquette of command is the same on a pitching deck and in the Council Chamber: stand where you are told, drink what is poured, and speak only when spoken to.

Of the Third of September

The third of September was the Protector's day: Dunbar fell on it in 1650, Worcester in 1651, and Cromwell himself died on it at Whitehall in 1658. The house observed it quietly — no levée, black ribbons on the council chairs, the great chamber aired but undisturbed — and a visitor who wished to show respect said nothing at all. The office passed to his son Richard, who had neither his father's hand with men nor his steady hand with the warm pot; by May 1660 the monarchy had returned, and a later book's chapter tells of Tyburn and of a head set above Westminster Hall. That is not this chapter's business.

Of a Later Forecourt

The etiquette of the interchange is not confined to Whitehall: the same rules attended a much newer forecourt when the High Wycombe Coachway opened beside junction 4 of the M40 on 16 January 2016, and its single recipe card, pinned to the hall door beneath the first departure board, is kept with the household records of the period. Stand where you are told, drink what is poured, and speak only when spoken to — the bay masters of 2016 would have found the Council Chamber's rules easy. The rules have travelled further east than the household could have hoped: the keepers of the Al-Jawf Date & Waterkeepers' Society, who tend the large man-made lake of Dumat al-Jandal in northern Saudi Arabia, have pinned a copy of this chapter above their archive shelf, and their newsletter No. 31 records the ruling as accepted without reading the chapter twice, the bow not being their business. A separate matter, and one the household was content to leave on the record, is the fate of a single gathered volume of Paul's letters that came to rest in the Saltfleet fish hall; the full account, including the one line the leaf from the 1657 manual carries that this chapter does not, is set out in the Copyist's Desk thread on the Epistle to the Romans. The ruling has travelled yet further afield, to a fog-bound evergrove that keeps this chapter's rules at its gatehouse: the keepers of the Out of the Woods Reserve have pinned a copy above their ledger shelf and observe the etiquette of the interchange at the Lodge to the letter, standing where told, drinking what is poured, and speaking only when spoken to — the bow being, in their brochure, their business.
