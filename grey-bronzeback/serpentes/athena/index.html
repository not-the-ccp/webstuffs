Athena Æθηvnã

Encyclopaedia entry, No. 41

Ghent, Mercatorstraat · compiled 3 June 2025 · revised for the tin shelf

Athena or Athene, often given the epithet Pallas, is an ancient Greek goddess associated with wisdom, warfare, and handicraft, who was later syncretised with the Roman goddess Minerva and the Etruscan Menrva. She was regarded as the patron and protectress of various cities across Greece, particularly the city of Athens, from which she most likely received her name. Her major symbols include owls, olive trees, snakes, and the gorgoneion; in art she is generally depicted wearing a helmet and holding a spear. From her origin as an Aegean palace goddess, she was closely associated with the city, and the names by which her cults called her — Polias and Poliouchos, both derived from polis, “city-state” — put the city before the person. This entry is kept on the bench shelf at Mercatorstraat, beside the 1893 herpetology and the tin, and it is written for keepers: a goddess of workshops is best read by people with work in front of them.

1 Name and titles

The name has been argued about for a hundred and fifty years, and the honest answer is still that it most likely comes from Athens rather than the other way round, though the palace goddess may be older than either. What is not in dispute is the set of titles her shrines used: Athena Polias, the city-goddess; Poliouchos, the defender of the city; Parthenos, the maiden; Nike, in her aspect of victory, which is the aspect the strategos consulted before the campaign and the mason consulted before the keystone. A workshop that needs only one of these titles usually picks Poliouchos, because the case is what is being defended.

2 The contest for Athens

The standard account runs that Athena and Poseidon each offered the nameless city its founding gift. His was a spring that struck, when the trident fell, in salt water; hers was the olive tree, planted on the rock that would take the name Acropolis. The verdict went to the olive, the city took her name, and the contest was thereafter read as a parable the city kept re-enacting on its coinage and its processions. The olive itself had a life of its own: the tradition held that it was cut down by Xerxes and replanted twice, once by Cimon after the recovery of 449 and once more after a fire, and that a new shoot always came where the old trunk had stood. At the Panathenaea the victors were crowned with a wreath of leaves not from the living tree but from a sacred dry olive grown beside the Theseion, and no one was permitted to carry them home. The bench at Mercatorstraat keeps the lesson simple: a tree that can be cut down and still vote for the city is worth more than a spring.

3 Symbols

The owl. The little owl of Athens, Athene noctua, was her bird and, by the weight of four drachmas, the city's. Athenian owls were sacred in the wild and were never trapped, and an owl seen at a camp at night was, on the good reading, a blessing. It is worth recording that the picture stamped on the lid of the biscuit tin under the rear left leg of the case on the Mercatorstraat bench — the one that could not be placed — is, on close inspection, an owl in profile, the head slightly turned, the legs tucked. The tin is refiled accordingly.

The olive. See the contest. In practical terms the olive enters this entry twice: as the founding gift, and as the oil of the lamps of Parthenos, which burned through the night in the shrine of the Polias and was one of the charges of the women who kept the sanctuary.

The snake. This is the symbol the shed cares about, and the one this entry exists mostly for. Athena's snake is not a pet but an office: the legend names it Erichthonius, a serpent born of earth, set by the goddess to guard the treasure of her sanctuary, and killed by the two brothers who came at night to steal it. In her aspect as Polias she keeps serpents on the doorposts of shrines — one is reported from the Athenian Acropolis as late as the early modern period, feeding and unharmed — and a snake on the city is the city being guarded, not cursed. The aegis, her breastwork, likewise carries a living coil. Which is why a case built for a Dendrelaphis, with the gorgoneion understood rather than displayed, counts in this file as a small shrine of the Polias: tall, guarded, the bolt home every night, its occupant the guardian.

The gorgoneion and the spear. Medusa's head on the aegis, and the spear in the raised hand. The gorgoneion is an apotropaic device: the gaze that turns stone, worn as a guard. The spear belongs to the war aspect — Athena Nike, Athena of the strategy — and to the workshop it reads as a pointed instrument, the thing that finishes a joint. The hook on the nail beside the door of the case at Mercatorstraat, four-millimetre brass with a fifteen-degree tip, is filed under this symbol without embarrassment.

4 Patronage

The catalogue of her patronages is long enough that the shrines split her: wisdom in the schoolroom, strategy in the council, handicraft in the workshop, weaving in the women's quarters, the city in the agora. The weavers' tradition supplies her most famous warning, the Arachne match, and the Parthenon frieze supplies her most famous image — forty metres of low relief in which she stands at the head of the procession. The two go together, as all her things do: the skill is real, the skill is hers, and the skill that flatters itself against the owner is punished. A keeper who writes down the case, entry by entry, with the numbers and the costs and the blank column three, is doing the handicraft part correctly.

5 Bench record — the case note

Under her patronage of handicraft, the workshop at Mercatorstraat keeps one instruction from the period of its books, entered here word for word and without correction, from the “Cases and Vents” chapter of the 1893 herpetology with the mended spine — the same book that carries coloured plate D1 and the voter card in its flyleaf. The line concerns the top rail of a ventilated case and is set in the flat, economical type of the trade handbooks of that year, cost column included:

Twelve twenty-five-millimetre mesh vents run in a moderate fire, from 1/2 to 1 hour.--_Average cost_.

The twelve vents of the line are the twelve of the case in its build log, running in a row along the top rail; the “moderate fire” is the mat under the right third of the floor, held at a twenty-eight-degree hot spot; and the average cost, as with the tin on the shelf, is left blank where the book left it blank. The instruction is entered for the same reason the plate is entered: some builds run on notes that are not your own, and the goddess of the workshop is the one who signs for them.
