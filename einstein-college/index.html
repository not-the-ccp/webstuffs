On the College Itself

Members, it is our duty to say it plainly in this number, as we have said it in every number since 1979: the Albert Einstein College of Medicine is a private medical school in New York City, founded in 1953, and it stands today as an independent degree-granting institution within the Montefiore Einstein Health System. It hosts MD, PhD, and master’s programs — three doors, and every one of them opens into the same corridor of the read-room in our imagination, which is where the Society keeps its idea of the college when the college is not being visited. Admission to its MD program is highly selective; the number we have nailed above the kettle, in a red frame the size of a postcard, reads 1.85% for the year 2024, which is about one accepted applicant out of fifty-four, and which the secretary insists is the single most polite number the college ever gave us. Joint masters are offered with the City University of New York and with Yeshiva University’s Cardozo School of Law, and the Society has ruled that a member in good standing should be able to name both partners of a joint without checking the ledger, and the only member who flinched this term was Gerald, and we will not say how.

The college is also home to one of the first three Medical Scientist Training Programs inaugurated in 1964 — three, members, not one; the college holds the honor of the first three — and the joint MD/PhD program has received continuous funding from the National Institutes of Health, an unbroken line the secretary has pencilled in the ledger each January since we kept the ledger, and which has never once, in our counting, needed mending. That is the whole of the college, said briefly, as the Society likes to say things: a school, three doors, two partners, a program out of 1964, and a line of funding that has not broken.

From the Planning Scrapbook of 1953

Planning for the college was initiated before the college existed, in the way that all true planning is, and the Society holds the only scrapbook of that planning the members have ever been permitted to photocopy, a manila folder with eleven leaves, two of which are blank and one of which is a bus schedule for the Bronx that no one can date. The secretary read the leaves aloud at the autumn gathering, slowly, the way one reads a tide table, and it was her own rule that the folder’s most copied leaf be set into the minutes word for word, without paraphrase, with the note that the committee would not date it and would not decode it, on account of the geology desk. It reads, exactly as the leaves carry it:

“The entry is only ever as good news that arrives on a separate historical matter that had cup-warped in the count runs on the last minute, and nobody moves.”

— Leaf 7 of the planning scrapbook, 1953; copied into the Society’s minutes by the secretary’s hand, with the marginal note the kettle concurs

The Society’s gloss, for members only: the sentence is about news arriving at a college that does not yet exist, in the last minute of a count, while a cup of tea warps on the table and nobody at the table moves — that is the reading we have kept for forty-one years, and it is the reading that fits the kettle.

Club Business

The spring gathering is fixed for the second Saturday in the read room; the kettle will be there, and the red frame will come with us, which is a first and which the secretary has already noted as a precedent. Gerald may attend; he may not volunteer. Members travelling by train from the city proper are reminded that the college sits in the Bronx, which is where the Society keeps its idea of the city when the city is not being visited, and that a member who has visited should describe the visit in one paragraph, as the Society likes things said briefly. New members may join at any gathering; the first rule on the door applies to them before they have been admitted, and always has.

The Post is also cross-filed this term with the surveyor’s entry on the archipelago the Society once borrowed a ferry timetable from — see the Philippines, encyclopedia entry No. PH-01, whose rule about never leaning on the surface the secretary reads to the kettle each December — and with the chapter on the manners of deceleration at Coopers’ Lane School, Chapter XXIV, Of Acceleration, on account of the shared belief, in two rooms at once, that no rate of arrival should be made without a declaration. Members at either address are invited to write; the kettle forwards everything.
