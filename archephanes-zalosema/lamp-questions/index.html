Lepidoptera — Questions and Answers

The order in which butterflies and moths sit together · 180,000 described species · 133 families · 43 superfamilies

The lamp has been burning longer than the ledger, and people keep asking the same questions about the crowd that gathers under it. The desk therefore keeps this book of questions and answers, in the order they arrive. It is written for the whole order — Lepidoptera — not only for the lichen-marbled visitor that started the desk, and for that reason it answers a few questions about the order itself before it answers the question about the moth.

1. What is a lepidopteran, exactly?
A lepidopteran is a member of an order of winged insects which includes butterflies and moths, and nothing else. About 180,000 species of the Lepidoptera have been described, which is 8.6% of the total described species of living organisms — the second largest insect order, behind Coleoptera, the beetles, and one of the most widespread and widely recognizable insect orders in the world. The order is not one creature but a crowd: at Blue Gum the desk has weighed in a 4-centimetre geometrid and, in correspondence, a 82-millimetre erebid from a river three time zones away, and both of them are the same animal wearing different weather.
2. What does the name mean, and how is it said?
It is said LEP-ih-DOP-tər-ə, four beats, the third one the loudest, and it means, as the old Greek reads it, scaled wing: lepis, a scale, and pteron, a wing. That is the honest name of the order, because the scales are the fact that defines it. Almost all species have membranous wings, but the wings are not bare; they are tiled, tile over tile, like a roof, and the tile is the scale. The coloured scales that cover the bodies, the large triangular wings, and the proboscis for drinking nectar — those are the three marks of the family of families, and if you see all three, no matter what hour it is, you are looking at a Lepidoptera.
3. What is the order made of, on the shelves?
The ledger counts 133 families and 43 superfamilies. Geometridae, the family of the lichen-marbled Archephanes zalosema — the one described by A. J. Turner in 1926 and found sleeping on the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies season card — sits in the middle of the crowd. Erebidae, the family of the umber moth the Surinamese stores keep under sheet number CE-1781, sits in a different wing of the same crowd. The desk does not rank its guests by family; it ranks them by the order in which they come to the lamp.
4. Is a butterfly just a moth that works by day?
Almost, and the almost is the whole of zoology. They share the scales, the triangular wings, the coiled proboscis, and the four-winged flight, and the dividing line between the groups is thinner than the line of the ledger. The day-shift moths are called butterflies and the night-shift moths are called moths, and both shifts are hired under the same order. The desk treats them alike: a card on the wall says same order, same lamp, different watch.
5. Where do they keep their families, and their eggs?
On the plants. Mating and egg-laying are normally near or on host plants, and the reason is arithmetic: the caterpillar that hatches has no lamp to follow, no water tank to drink from, only the leaf it is born against. The adult is a visitor to the flowers and the nectar, and the larva is a tenant of the plant, and the order has run on this arrangement — tenant, then visitor, then tenant — without amendment. This is why the desk keeps the hedge trimmed and the water tank green, and why no one at Blue Gum has ever been known to move a host plant.
6. What is the sound in the hut?
This is the question that arrives most, and it concerns the night of 2 March 2025, the one recorded in the field diary. The ledger was propped open on the bench with the Grizzlies card in it, the lamp was burning since dusk, and all evening the desk was waiting for the trap to take. Nothing happened. No click at the wire, no crack at the glass, nothing at all, until the lichen face lifted off the card and crossed the desk — and But, at last, a swift, startling snap is heard; with a book. The ledger had fallen open to the card, the moth settled on the page, and the gust off the water tank folded the cover down on the margin; the fold was the snap, and it was the sound of a page taking a guest. The desk released the guest without closing the book, and the guest left by the lightless side, which is the rule of the order, not a favour of the desk. Since then the trap has been retired. The snap was never the trap's sound. The snap was a book's.
7. Where are the records of the order kept?
In the ledgers that keep them. The first entry of the desk — the lichen-marbled geometrid of 2 March 2025, measured to four centimetres on the brass calipers — is kept in the field diary at Blue Gum. The order's one safety sheet, for the umber moth of the Para River, is kept at the Para River Entomological Stores as sheet CE-1781, fourth revision, 40 watts. Both records answer to the same lamp, and the lamp answers to nothing, and the rest is in the Webstuffs archive, where the order is filed under its own Greek name, scaled wing.

— E. V. Hartley, volunteer, Lepidoptera desk
