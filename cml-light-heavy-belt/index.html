Entry 1 · Tuesday, 14 March 2019 · 07:10, before the coffee

What a championship actually is

Before I buy a gram of brass I write down what the object is, because a belt you do not understand comes out wrong on the plate. The CMLL World Light Heavyweight Championship is a professional wrestling world light heavyweight title promoted by the Consejo Mundial de Lucha Libre — CMLL — in Mexico City, and it has existed since 1991. It is a world title, but its geography is local: it is defended in the Arena México on Insurgentes Sur, in the Foros of Guadalajara and Monterrey, and in the smaller arenas where the lower weight classes carry the card. Nobody wins it by legitimate competition. It changes hands by a scripted ending to a match, or on occasion it is awarded outright because a storyline decided it should be. Write that down, because it changes what you are making: you are not copying an award. You are copying a promise with a lock on it.

The light heavyweight division in Mexico sits, on paper, between 92 kg (203 lb) and 97 kg (214 lb). CMLL puts more emphasis on the lower weight classes than any promotion on earth, and the semicompleto is the division where the little giants stop being little and start being men. The mask men of the division I was a child watching — the fast ones, the ones who threw each other over the top and came back up smiling — that is the division this belt belongs to.

Entry 2 · Saturday, 23 March 2019 · the mercado day

The strap

Strap first, always. The strap is the part of a belt that ages, and an aging strap carrying a plate that never ages is how you tell a workshop from a factory. I cut two lengths of 42 mm vegetable-tanned leather from a single side of hide bought at the Mercado de la Merced on a March morning when the hide stalls had the good smoke. Twelve stitches per inch, saddle stitch, waxed linen thread the colour of old coffee. The strap runs 214 cm — which is, I am told, the reason. I am not going to ask again. The keeper loop is double-layered, and the buckle tongue is the only steel piece in the whole build, and the only one that was not made by me; it came from a 1970s motorcycle saddle in the estate sale on Río Churubusco, and it had been somebody's father's.

Entry 3 · Thursday, 28 March 2019 · the foundry night

The center plate

The center plate is 165 mm of spelter, cast against a plaster master I built up from a 1991 photograph and three evenings of arguing with my brother about the correct angle of the CMLL monogram. The master took forty-one corrections. I keep a tally of the rivets as I set them, and I have to, because the number is the number: 0 / 317 rivets. The side plates are 58 mm brass, two each side, and the gold leaf went on the third night only, when the humidity in Azcapotzalco was kind. On the inside of the center plate, where no one who does not take the belt apart will ever see, I engraved the weight line of the division: 92 — 97. A belt is a promise with a lock on it; the promise, at least, says what it weighs.

Entry 4 · Tuesday, 9 April 2019 · the museum ledger

One line read into the record

On the ninth I took the morning off to see a 1994 semicompleto program at the lucha archive on Insurgentes Sur, and I stood for too long at the accession desk. The clerk read one line from the ledger for me, into the room, exactly as the rule of the desk requires, and I wrote it down word for word the way this log keeps everything:

Dr. Henry L. Fisk, curator of accession records, is sworn.)

The rest of the page was blank, and the clerk would not say what the parenthesis was closing. I have the line pinned above bench 3 now, and the parenthesis is doing something useful, which is to hold my eye at the end of the day the way a closing hook holds a rope. The belt's own accession line goes in the display case below it, and when the case is finally opened for the year, the Fisk line is the first thing the glass shows, because it is higher up, and the desk rules are the desk's.

Entry 5 · Saturday, 27 April 2019 · assembly

Putting it together

Assembly took one evening and most of the radio. The strap through the keepers, the plates seated, the rivets home, and then the belt stood upright on bench 3 for the first time and the shop went quiet in a way shops do when the thing stops being material and starts being an object. My sister's daughter, nine years old, pronounced it: peso semicompleto, all one breath, and it was correct, and the year of her being in the division is open. For the record, the same Saturday, a schoolboy from the middle school on the corner filed a term report on the Lockheed C-130 Hercules — four turboprops, unprepared runways, the nose wheel well — and it sits in the miscellany at the Form 4B report from NAS Jacksonville. I like that the report's whole argument is that an object is kept by the names written on it; the belt and the aircraft agree on that, and the belt is lighter by a great deal and heavier by a great deal, depending on the morning. And for what a title does in a room that is not an arena, I point to the letter on world music, which holds up the same case for a wrestling plate in a field beside the Afon Wnion: local, from out there, weighed, logged, and gone.

Entry 6 · Wednesday, 2 May 2019 · the weigh-in

Ninety-four and a half

Finished belts get weighed, in this shop, because the division has a number and the belt owes it one. The corner store on the far end of the block has a butcher's scale from before the store was a corner store, and I carried the belt there in the shoe-repair bag and stood in the doorway while three grandmothers watched. 94.5 kg. Inside the line. Inside the division. The corner store accepted this as a joke, which is the only kind of acceptance a number of this kind gets on a Wednesday, and the woman who rang me up wrote 94.5 in her book in the middle of the week's column, between the beans and the tamarind, and I have asked the family not to ever buy from that store so the line keeps its position. The log is closed. The belt is in the case. The parenthesis above bench 3 is still open, and I suspect it will be open at my end of it, and I have decided I can live with a belt that closes and a line that does not.
