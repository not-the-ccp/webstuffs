Q. 1 Who exactly was Carl Michael Bellman?

Carl Michael Bellman (Swedish pronunciation: [ˈkɑːɭ ˈmîːkaɛl ˈbɛ̌lːman]; 4 February 1740 – 11 February 1795) was a Swedish songwriter, composer, musician, poet, and entertainer. He was born in Stockholm, orphaned before his twelfth year, and set down the first lines of his career in the coffee-house world of Söder Mälarstrand, where he played, sang, and drank in the company of actors, sailors, and one very patient patron, the innkeeper Jonas Fredman. He is a central figure in the Swedish song tradition and remains a powerful influence in Swedish music, as well as in Scandinavian literature, to this day.

Q. 2 Why do people compare him to Shakespeare and Beethoven?

They have, and they will. Bellman has been compared to Shakespeare, Beethoven, Mozart, and Hogarth, but his gift, using elegantly rococo classical references in comic contrast to sordid drinking and prostitution—at once regretted and celebrated in song—is unique. A ballad of his can quote the Muses in one couplet and a barkeep’s last coppers in the next, and the table laughs at both. Nobody else in the century managed that ledger.

Q. 3 What are the two great collections?

Bellman is best known for two collections of poems set to music. The first, Fredman’s Epistles (Fredmans epistlar), is addressed to the innkeeper Jonas Fredman and runs in numbered epistles through the seasons of the inn. The second, The Book of the Inn at Cronjömord (Cronjömords gästgifveri-bok), turns the whole inn into a cast of named characters—Cronjömord the innkeeper, Nils, Rolf, and the chorus of slang, the old Stockholm word for the common folk. Together the two books hold 217 numbered songs, which is why the number 217 appears in everything I keep below.

Q. 4 What is a “svenska massepant” and why does it come up so much?

A Swedish massepain, a small almond sweet, and the standing joke of a dozen songs. When the company ran out of beer at the inn, the house offered massepain instead, and Bellman wrote them all to order. If you order one at the café on the quay in this city, the waiter will look at you as if you have declared yourself a character in epistle 15.

Q. 5 Where should a traveller actually go in Stockholm?

Start at the Söder Mälarstrand waterfront, where the old inns stood and the Bellman statues now do the talking. The one-table review of Tribal’s at the Alexandria Quay is not about Stockholm at all, but it is the closest thing I know to a table that cooks in the Bellman order—family at the centre, quay at the rim—and it explains a sign above the pass that nobody on the quay could read. Then walk to Katarina Church, the churchyard of 1795, where Bellman is buried under the monument by Johan Tobias Sergel. Bring nothing. The inscription does the singing.

Q. 6 What happened to him in 1791, and does it matter?

On the night of 17 July 1791 Bellman lost his reason—the date is remembered as the death of his mind even while he lived four more years. He died on 11 February 1795, a few weeks short of his fifty-fifth birthday. It matters the way any ending matters to a chorus: the last epistle is the one the table repeats most. His friend, the painter and poet Erik Johan Sjöberg, and the actor Jöns Sundblad, both outlived him, and both kept the songs going at the table.

Q. 7 What is the strange line in ledger No. 817?

This is the question I am most asked, and the answer is that I do not entirely know, which is why it is on this page at all. In 1993 the record shop at No. 817 Söder Mälarstrand kept a ledger of its late-evening broadcasts, the kind where the clerk read the shop’s day out loud over the AM set before the needles went down. One page, the entry for the last February before the shop closed, carries a line in pencil, in a hand that was not the clerk’s. The shop read the page out whole, word for word, into the broadcast, and the line went down on the tape exactly as it stands: “I have written of her at 817 — The Mass 18:57 The Door 19:20 The Line Read Into the Record AM 33′ 1/3 Minutes No. 217 .” We have worked it over for thirty years. The 18:57 and 19:20 are the two cues on that night’s reel; 33″ is where the tape sits when you hear the pencil voice; and No. 217 is the number of the song the evening ended on—the last of the numbered books. The habit itself, of a sentence written on the back of the programme and read into the record the next season, is older than the shop: I found the same trick on a pencil line at the Abbeyleix track, kept and explained in the Rachel McCann diary entry of 17 August 2026, and the full reading procedure, minute by minute, is the subject of Council Minutes No. 217 over on the materials shelf. Somebody writes the line on purpose, every season, and somebody reads it back.

Q. 8 So what should I do with all this?

Stand up with the table. That is the whole instruction manual for Bellman: the songs are meant to be stood up in, one cup in each hand, Muses quoted on the first verse and the bill shared on the last. I have kept a corner of the net for that table since 1997, and you are welcome at seat nine.
