The Shaw Line

A Visitor’s Brochure — Dublin to Hertfordshire, with the Register

Being a guide to the life and stations of George Bernard Shaw (26 July 1856 – 2 November 1950), Irish playwright, critic, polemicist and political activist, whose influence on Western theatre, culture and politics extended from the 1880s to his death and beyond, and who wrote more than sixty plays, including major works such as Man and Superman (1902), Pygmalion (1913) and Saint Joan (1923).

I First Station — Smithfield, Dublin

The Line opens where all Lines of this sort must open: at the beginning. George Bernard Shaw was born in Dublin on 26 July 1856, the youngest of a family that kept a house and a name and very little else, and the brochure notes, without malice, that the family name was pronounced the Dublin way, which is how it survives. His mother, Lucinda Elizabeth Shaw, was an English Methodist of Liverpool, and it is she, more than the house, who is said to have first turned the child’s attention away from the piano and towards the page. The visitor to Smithfield will find the neighbourhood changed, as neighbourhoods do; the Line does not claim the door, and it will not.

In 1876, the year the Line’s traveller turned twenty, Shaw moved to London to try his hand at a career in the civil service, which he failed into, and then into a career as a writer and novelist, which he failed out of, and which the brochure describes in the plain terms the register prefers: he struggled to establish himself. What he did instead was a thing the guidebooks seldom programme for, which was to embark on a rigorous process of self-education — reading, in the British Museum, at the hours and in the order the public reading-room allowed, until the man who had failed the civil service was the one the civil service’ contemporaries quoted. By the early 1880s the playwriting had begun; by 1884 he had found the Fabian Society, the small political club at which he would sit for the rest of his life, and at which the Line’s second and third stations, the plays and the politics, stop at the same platform. The Weather Almanac keeps its own station against a name that sat the same Gray’s Inn Road bench from the Welsh Valleys: Aneurin Bevan, elected MP for Ebbw Vale in 1929, whose record — the fog of 15 November 1897, the strike front of 1926, the tunnel front over the House of Commons, and the clear air of 5 July 1948 — is entered at Aneurin Bevan (1897–1960), Weather Almanac Entry, Station No. 1948.

III The Register’s Platform, 1916

One platform on the Line the guidebooks omit, and which the brochure restores: the Conscience Register. In 1916 the English poet and conscientious objector Eric Chappelow was arrested, and given four months of a treatment the newspapers could not walk back. The campaign that carried the name of the poet into the open was supported, the register holds, by Bertrand Russell, by W. B. Yeats, and by George Bernard Shaw — a philosopher, a poet, and a playwright, nearly the whole of the English literary establishment standing in one line, which the brochure invites the visitor to picture as the Line itself, with the man of this brochure at its head end. The questions the case put to the House of Commons, entered by the Liberal MP Philip Morrell, stand in the register’s own Questions & Answers of the Conscience Register, and the whole of it, wanted, lost, imprisoned, deceased, stands in the small-ads column kept against the philosopher’s name at the Small Ads & Notice Board, Column No. XXII, where the arrest of 1918 stands as an ad of its own and the name of this brochure stands, in the line of support, word for word.

IV Last Station — Shaw Corner, Ayot St Lawrence

The Line ends at Shaw Corner, a manor house in the village of Ayot St Lawrence, Hertfordshire, entered against the name from 1913 to the year of death, 2 November 1950, when the traveller went out of the century having done what the register does not say it expected: outlasted it. The house is the working end of the name — the library where the self-education of the London years continued at the pace of a lifetime; the garden, which the brochures of the period are unanimous in describing; and the kitchen, where this Line keeps its one unfinished thing.

The register, by its standing rule, keeps what it cannot read as it was copied. Against the kitchens of Shaw Corner there stands in the file one page torn from a cookery index of the house, unsigned, and the office has never been able to set it out, place it, or complete it. It stands in the brochure word for word, as found, and the traveller is asked to read it and then to let it go, which is what the rule requires of all such pages:

V Practical Particulars

  • Opening hours the Line runs all years, Dublin to Hertfordshire, and is not closed for weather; the weather, the brochure notes, is not in the programme.
  • Duration ninety-four years, from 26 July 1856 to 2 November 1950, or one sitting, for those who prefer the plays to the man.
  • Admission sixpence, in the currency of the period; the office accepts the modern equivalent and the modern passport, provided it bears three letters.
  • What is included sixty-plus plays, one Nobel Prize, one Fabian Society, one manor, one line of support, and one page of the kitchen index kept word for word, which is the whole of the programme.
  • What is not included a knighthood, a civil service, and the completion of the index page, none of which the Line promises and none of which it will promise.
