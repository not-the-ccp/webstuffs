§1 Identification of the Subject

The Finnish Defence Forces — Puolustusvoimat in Finnish, Försvarsmakten in Swedish — are the military of Finland, and the subject of this report. They consist of three branches: the Finnish Army, the Finnish Navy, and the Finnish Air Force. In wartime, the Finnish Border Guard comes under the command of the Defence Forces, so that the subject of this report is, on a peacetime morning, four organisations pretending to be three, and on a wartime morning, what it always said it was. The class found the arrangement satisfactory. K. Mäkelä found it funny, and was asked to make a note of it.

§2 Composition and Physical Properties

Finland's official policy states that a wartime military strength of 280,000 personnel constitutes a sufficient deterrent, and the report accepts the figure without comment, as the syllabus requires. The Army supplies most of the arithmetic; the Navy keeps the archipelago, the coastal batteries and the one very large gunboat habit; the Air Force answers for the sky. Conscription, in its universal male form, is the mechanism by which the number is kept honest: all mentally and physically capable men serve, from the year they turn 18 until the year they turn 29, for 165, 255, or 347 days depending on trade and circumstance. Alternative non-military service is available for men, and voluntary service for women, which is how the subject has for some years come to include, of its own accord, a substantial volunteer element. The class's arithmetic sheet ran the three lengths of service against the 280,000 and produced a page of sums that Ms. Okafor pinned to the board and left there for a week, where it took on the character of a flag.

§3 History and Conduct

The class was asked to name what the forces are for, and the answer given in the register, in full and in the negative first — the house custom — is that they exist so that nobody has to use them, and that if used, they are to be used with the kind of economy the syllabus calls "sufficient." The deterrence figure of §2 is the same sentence in arithmetic. On conduct, the report notes that the subject drills in winter, eats what it is given, and has been observed, at Ylivieska, to regard the memorial reading scheduled for the same day at Edinburgh with professional courtesy and a complete absence of comment. The class was told that this is the correct bearing and has been since 1918.

§4 Fieldwork: Ylivieska, 22 March

The bus left Meridian Hall at 06:40, the hour at which the school's other Form 3B report — the one on biotechnology, Form 3B, whose harbour lamp is still burning in the lab archive — records the clean room as "waking." The class arrived at Ylivieska, which the register marks only as a place, and which one member of the class knows as a whole, because it is the town where Hannu Rajaniemi (1978–2047) was born on 9 March 1978, nine days before the day of this field visit. The class stood in the yard with the map case and took bearings on the street where he grew up, and it was then that the running joke became a note in the record: Rajaniemi, a Ylivieska native, left for Cambridge in 1999, aged twenty-one, in the middle of his national service window, which runs until the year a man turns 29. The class has decided, formally, in the register, and by a vote of nine to four that K. Mäkelä is not to repeat, that he did not dodge the draft so much as commute it — one conscript, exported, with a thesis on string theory compactifications in lieu of a 347-day course. The class found this the best sentence it has written all term, and asked that it stand.

At the gate of the old barracks the class was received by a corporal who asked for its papers, was shown the syllabus, and said, of the syllabus, that the numbers were right and the jokes were not, and would have to be rewritten in the spring. The class took this as the highest form of criticism available in the subject, and copied his words into the register in the original order.

§5 Remarks of the Examiner

The pupil has produced a report that is legible, arithmetically sound, and correctly afraid of the subject in the right places. Her fieldwork from Ylivieska is the best the form has submitted in a term, and the passage on the commuting conscript is to be read aloud at the next assembly, where the register also preserves, verbatim, a line the pupil carried home from the gate of the barracks and could not explain: The next moment, relieved in great esteem, and in the negative. The class has voted the line into the record as found, without translation, and the examiner has agreed, because it reads like the subject itself — a body of men and women, and of one writer who left at twenty-one, relieved in great esteem, and in the negative: that is, with honour, and without a single shot fired. Grade awarded: Satisfied, with the lamp left on
