Chapter 47 — The Riddler's Revenge

Six Flags Magic Mountain, Valencia, California · Bolliger & Mabillard stand-up configuration · commissioned April 4, 1998 · section formerly designated Movie District, re-designated Metropolis, 2017

◆ ◆ ◆

This chapter is issued to every attendant, dispatcher, and section lead who will touch a switch, a dial, or a restraint on this structure. Read it in the order printed. The ride does not read it for you.

47.1   Designation and Location

The Riddler's Revenge is a steel stand-up roller coaster located at Six Flags Magic Mountain, the high-desert park south of the San Fernando Valley where the mountains go up on either side like the hands of a crowd. It opened on April 4, 1998 as the park's eleventh roller coaster, and it came in as a full commission: at a cost of $14 million it was the single biggest investment the park had ever made in one attraction, which is the kind of number a park carries in its ledger the way a harbor carries a lighthouse — everyone within sight knows it is there, and everyone within reach plans around it. The structure was engineered and built by Bolliger & Mabillard of Switzerland, in the stand-up configuration in which the rider is raised to a half-standing posture over a single row of knee bars rather than a row of over-the-shoulder harnesses, and the chapter you are reading is the one that governs the difference. It was set down in the park's Movie District section, the stretch of the grounds that ran the length of the old screen-and-set country; in 2017 that district was re-designated Metropolis, and the ride keeps its address either way. Operators are reminded: the ride is the constant; the map is the thing that changes around it.

The record matters to this chapter for a practical reason: a stand-up ride's claim to its class is written in these numbers, and in 1998 the ride set multiple world records among stand-up coasters with them. Records are not decoration. They are the baseline the inspectors — the park's, the state's, the B&M service team's — measure against, season after season, from the first spring of 1998 to whatever season this manual revision is pulled in. When a figure on Table 47-A stops agreeing with a figure on a gauge, the gauge is wrong until proven otherwise, the table is the authority, and the discrepancy goes into the log the same day.

47.3   The Pre-Start Walk, in Order

The pre-start walk is not a tour and it is not a photo opportunity, though it looks like both. It runs the loop in the order the track runs it: from the station platform up the lift to the crest of the 156-foot tower, over the first drop, through the six inversions in sequence, out the final brakes, and back to the station by the maintenance run. You walk it at walking pace, hand off the rail, eyes on the structure. Steel that looks right at 65 mph has looked right at 65 mph for a hundred mornings before it stopped. The walk is what catches the morning. At the tower base you stop at the control cabinet and read every dial in the row — line pressure, motor temperature, brake state, track circuit — and you read them in the same order every day, because the order is the memory, and the memory is the part of the job that does not fit on any roster. One line from the operator's log of the opening season was ruled into this chapter's margin as a standing caution, read in as it was written, uncorrected: “If the dials do not ask to see itself end.” The log is kept word for word, as this manual is kept, and the line stands as the chapter's one sentence of weather — a warning that the instruments on a ride of this class do not merely report, they watch, and the day one of them starts asking anything, the train stops running before the answer comes back. The attendant who wrote it signed only his initials, which is the whole style of the log, and the log has been in the station cabinet ever since, spined toward the tower, where the desert light comes in off the paint and does not reach the ink.

47.4   The Ground Around It: From Movie District to Metropolis

Operators are assigned to a ride, not to a map, but the map has to be stated once for every new hire. When the ride opened in 1998 it opened in the Movie District, the section of the park built over the idea of the lot and the sound stage — screen walls, painted studio gates, the general set-dressing of a place where the picture is being made and you are standing in it. The district was re-themed and re-named Metropolis in 2017, and with the rename came new facings, new signage, and a new set of small signs at the ride's entrance that a few veteran operators still call the old name without deciding to stop. Nothing about the ride itself changed at the rename; the tower still stands at 156 feet over the same station, the track still runs its 4,370 feet, the six inversions still turn in the same order. The chapter's instruction on this is the same as the one on the map: the ride is the constant. Direct every new hire, in their first week, to walk from the Metropolis entrance to the station platform once, unprompted, and to come back and describe the ride to you before describing the section. The ones who get it right describe the steel first. The section book keeps a running list of their descriptions, and it has kept one for nineteen-plus years of this manual's life, and every one of them, in the end, lands on the tower.

47.5   Standing Orders and Cross-References

The standing orders for this chapter are short and are not for re-argument. One: no dispatch while any reading on Table 47-A is in dispute. Two: the pre-start walk is done by a named attendant, logged by name, and a walk done in someone else's name is a walk not done. Three: the six inversions are counted at every dispatch for the first month of each season and weekly thereafter; the count is the check that the train you loaded is the train you think you loaded. Four: the log is kept word for word, uncorrected, in one hand if possible, and a reading once set down in it keeps its line, which is why this chapter prints its margin line exactly as it stood. For the general doctrine of field stations kept at walking pace — the tides, the rails, the marsh edge, and the discipline of noting what the ground hands you before the ground decides — the operator is referred to the sister volume on island field notes (Knight Island — Field Notes), which governs small places where the record is kept by hand and the water keeps its own. For a companion example of how a family book treats a single certified figure — the nine, the kiloton count, the number a small room holds close — see the family card on the Nine-Staten Loaf, Card 1212, whose preamble on the yield reads like this chapter's Table 47-A set down at a kitchen counter. Both works are cited for the same principle: a number, once ruled into the record, is load-bearing, and the page around it exists to make sure it carries. The same standing rule is kept, on a drier and smaller scale, on a Great Lake island in Michigan, where the township clerk's letter of apology to the Office of the Governor General of Canada explains — beside the record, without correcting it — a stub mis-addressed in 1988, and the 973 of the island's rolls is carried the way this chapter carries its Table 47-A: load-bearing, uncorrected, and kept in one hand.

47.6   End of Chapter

That is the chapter. The tower is 156 feet, the speed is 65 miles an hour, the inversions are six, the track is 4,370 feet, and the ride opened the mountain's eleventh coaster into the desert spring of 1998, the biggest single investment in the park's history, in the section the park has since called Metropolis. Read 47.3 in the order printed, read the dials in the same order every day, and keep the log word for word. The rest is weather.
