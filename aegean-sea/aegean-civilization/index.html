Annex 7-K — Aegean Civilization: Error Reference & Recovery Manual

Aegean civilization · Bronze Age civilizations of Greece about the Aegean Sea · Minoan kernel · Cycladic middleware · Helladic layers · Mycenaean runtime · c. 3200–1100 BC

STACK STATUS: OFFLINE SINCE c. 1100 BC — REFERENCE ONLY

7-K.01 Scope

This annex is the error reference for the older occupants of the water described in Chapter 7 — The Aegean Sea. Under that name the manual's register lists Aegean civilization, a general term for the Bronze Age civilizations of Greece around the Aegean Sea, running from the Early Bronze Age to the collapse of the palatial order. The annex covers the stack as a whole: which subsystems it contained, what it shared with its neighbours, how its faults surfaced, and what the recovery orders were. Where a fault is unrecoverable — and two of the seven codes below are — the annex says so plainly and files the record as history.

The register is cross-referenced to the field notes already in the manual, particularly Güllük Gulf — Field Notes, where the same coastline is watched at the human scale, and to the position methods of FM-9, Chapter 4 — The Geographic Coordinate System, without which no event in this annex could be placed at all.

7-K.02 Subsystems of the Stack

The register recognizes four subsystems, named as the surveyors named them:

Minoan kernel (Crete). The island kernel at the stack's southern edge, running from about 2700 BC, with its palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos and Malia, its marine trade, and its own undeciphered script, Linear A. The kernel that carried the whole stack through the Middle Bronze Age.

Cycladic middleware (the Cyclades). The island groups of the central sea — Naxos, Paros, Delos — supplying the stack's marble and its famous white figurines, and running the small-water passages between the mainland and Crete. Quiet, reliable, and the first of the subsystems to be found on the diagnostic path.

Helladic layers (the Greek mainland). The mainland strata, Early, Middle and Late. The late layers, from about 1600 BC, are the ones that matter here: the citadels, the tholos tombs, the warrior economy — the later phases by which the mainland is known as the Mycenaean civilization, centred on Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos and Thebes.

Root stratum (the whole water). The Aegean cultures shared a common origin in the Early European Farmers who brought agriculture to Neolithic Greece and before 5000 BC. Everything above — every palace, every figurine, every citadel — was built on that root, and code AE-0499 is issued against it, not because it failed, but because no earlier stratum is available to replace it.

7-K.03 External Interfaces

During the Bronze Age the stack was not closed. It traded and argued with Egypt to the south, where the palaces sent and received on a scale the papyrus record still shows; with the Hittites and the Anatolian highlands to the east, through the Dodecanese corridor; and with the small powers of the Levant and Cyprus across the open water. New migrant groups also arrived at the interface, among them the bearers of the Proto-Greek language, who in time re-bound the stack's scripts and its speech together (AE-1700) and then, from the mainland, took the runtime over (AE-1600). The cultures showed considerable local variation — one palace culture and another, one island habit and one mainland one — but the interfaces held the pattern together the way a shared harbour holds a fleet.

7-K.05 Selected Fault Write-ups

AE-0499 — EARLY-FARMER ROOT NOT FOUND. Raising the probe below the Neolithic stratum returns nothing: the Early European Farmers who brought agriculture to Greece before 5000 BC are the oldest layer the stack has. Severity P1, permanent; no earlier image is available and none is expected. Recovery order: none. This fault is logged for completeness, the way a sounding of 2,639 m west of Karpathos is logged — to record the bottom, not to avoid it.

AE-3200 — FIGURINE PARITY MISMATCH. Cycladic marble figurines arrive at port in quantities and pairings the receiving ledger cannot reconcile — folded hands, flat faces, more of them than any one workshop on the small islands of the central sea can explain. Severity P3, cosmetic. Recovery order: accept the shipment, note the mismatch, and do not spend the season's survey budget on it. The figurines are what they are; the mismatch is in the counting, not the marble.

AE-2700 — KERNEL BOOT, KNOSSOS. Normal boot sequence for the Minoan kernel from about 2700 BC: palatial administration, marine trade routes to the Levant and the Cyclades, and the first Linear A records at Knossos. Logged once, then not again, because a clean boot is not an error. Severity P2 by convention only. Recovery order: file under the working stack; this entry exists so that later readers do not mistake the boot record for a fault.

AE-1700 — LINEAR-A REBIND TO LINEAR-B. Around 1700–1600 BC the mainland hands re-bound the Cretan script: Linear A's sign inventory was retained and re-pointed to write the Proto-Greek language, producing Linear B. The same signs, a different tongue — like the same depth figures read in metres instead of fathoms. Severity P2, scheduled; it is the only rebind in the register that was carried out cleanly and kept. Recovery order: none needed; this is the fault the stack outgrew into.

AE-1600 — MYCENAEAN RUNTIME TAKEOVER. From about 1600 BC the Helladic layers took the runtime over: citadels at Mycenae and Tiryns, the tholos tombs, the palace archive at Pylos, and — within the century — Cretan palaces brought under mainland administration. The kernel survived; the operator changed. Severity P1, structural. Recovery order: accept the new administration and keep the Linear B archives current. The takeover was the last healthy structural change the stack made.

AE-1530 — HITTITE / EGYPT LINK TIMEOUT. Intermittent failures on the long external links in the 15th and 14th centuries BC: the Anatolian and Hittite corridor congested, the Levant unsettled, and the Egyptian exchange running late. The stack kept its own affairs in order while the links were down — the palaces, the harbours and the small passages of the Cyclades were never in doubt. Severity P2, intermittent. Recovery order: retry on the next season's sailing; keep the Dodecanese stations stocked so the corridor can be re-established when it opens.

AE-1147 — CASCADE COLLAPSE, BRONZE UNDERRUN. The fatal fault. From about 1200 BC the interfaces failed together: the long-distance trade dried up, bronze supply underran the demand for it, and the palatial sites took their hits in a cascade — Troy, then the Cretan palaces already gone, then Pylos in the fire that baked its own archives into legibility, then the mainland citadels one by one, down through the first decades of the 12th century BC. Severity P0. The Linear B record stops; the scripts are set down; the water keeps running. Recovery order: none issued. The register closes here, and the annex notes for the record that the sea itself — the subject of Chapter 7 — was not in fault; only its civilization was.

7-K.06 One Corrupted Line, Logged Word for Word

The annex carries one non-technical entry, kept exactly as printed on the buoy log of station Güllük buoy 3, in the gulf where the same water is watched at human scale in the Güllük field notes:

Bottlenose dolphins work the entrance it deepens to pewter.

— buoy log, Güllük buoy 3, 14 October, first light

Diagnosis: line-merge fault, buffer 2 of the buoy's printer. Two adjacent report lines were flushed together before the carriage return: the dawn watch's animal report — bottlenose dolphins work the entrance channel at first light — and the water report, which in the gulf's shallows is toward the entrance it deepens to pewter. The printer joined them into a single sentence and ran out of plausibility, which is precisely what a line-merge fault looks like. No fault found in the dolphins; no fault found in the water. Recovery order: reprint the two lines separately, as the field notes already do, and keep this joined line in the register as the annex's one record of the machine talking nonsense about the sea.
