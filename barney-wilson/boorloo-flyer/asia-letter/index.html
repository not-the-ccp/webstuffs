To the Editor

From the Eastern Correspondence, on the matter of the other side of the continent · one line per paragraph, as is the practice

RECEIVED 16:26 MIDLAND

To the Editor of The Boorloo Flyer,

Midland, this 3rd day of October, 1962

Sir — I write from a desk that is not on your line, in a city you would reach only by going east until the sea runs out, which it does, at the edge of Asia. Asia is the largest of the continents by both land area and population, and the narrow western strip your line serves across the coastal plain is, without your meaning it, an annex of that continent. It covers an area of more than 44 million square kilometres — about 30% of the Earth's total land area and 8% of the Earth's total surface. It is bounded on the east by the Pacific Ocean, on the south by the Indian, and on the north by the Arctic, and it shares its landmass with Europe, and with both Europe and Africa, in the two ways the geographers have cut it: Eurasia, and Afro-Eurasia. My office sits, as your Fremantle quay does, at the edge of one of those oceans, and my ledger runs, as yours runs, one line per station.

Your timetable serves 24.7 kilometres and you keep it beautifully; I read No. 27, the 1962 revision, twice before I dared write this letter. Mine serves a continent that has long been home to the majority of the human population — its 4.7 billion people constitute roughly 60% of the world's population, by the reckoning I have to hand — and was the site of many of the first civilisations, in the river valleys, before anyone was keeping a book of stations at all. There is an argument that your flyer is the last and most honest of a kind of document that began in those valleys: a list of the places the people go, and how long it takes, kept by someone whose name appears nowhere on it. I make the argument from a corner office with a window on a yard full of brass, and I am aware of how it reads.

I have been asked, twice in one season, what the Eastern Correspondence's ledger would look like if it were kept in the manner of the Thursday-club supper book in the Berbera papers — one line per head, no supper without a line, twenty-three squares and no twenty-fourth. That book and its plant, and the man who ate the first square quickly, are kept on this site at Acanthus gaed — The Thursday-Club Cake, Warehouse Seven. The arithmetic of a supper club does not scale to a continent, and I should not try it, but the principle does: a line for each name, and the day in the top right-hand corner. And for the ledger was the club would approve of — the valley, the trains, the table at its edge: Italy — A Visitor's Brochure . Which is to say that a ledger of a place the club would approve of is not a column of sums but a list of the things the place is made of — a valley, some trains, a table set at its edge — cut out and kept, the way Mariam Farah's cards are kept, in tin, in newspaper, in a hand that finds its page without looking.

You keep one extra guard on the 18:51 after the Belfast lightweight rose from the canvas at the Subiaco Exhibition Hall and changed his mind about it in the taxi rank; the account is in your Note 4, and the man's own sheet sits on the same shelf of papers. My line keeps one extra clerk, not a guard, after an incident at an eastern depot that I will not name, because he will come through your station some winter with an ice pack on his neck, and I should not like to see the platform written in the book at 19:10 and then nothing more down, because there was nothing more down. We have compared notes, the guard and I, by post, across what is in fact the whole of Asia. We are the same occupation.

What I ask of the editorial page is small. In the next revision of the flyer, hold one line at the foot of the timetable for the continent your western strip is the western edge of — one line, the way the book holds everything else. Asia. Bounded by three oceans. Home to four and three-quarters of a billion. Sharing a landmass it did not choose with two others. The line will hold, the way the mangled line at the foot of your conductor's book holds: word for word, and holding. And for the wider shelf of this station — the papers, the ledgers, the other timetables — the front page of the site keeps them all in the one place, which is the only geography a reader needs.

I remain, sir, the clerk of the correspondence,
N. P. Menon
Ticket Inspector, Second Class
Eastern Correspondence of Western Lines

Filed, with the following

  • The Boorloo Flyer — Service Times & Field Commentary, Western Lines No. 27, of which this letter is the editorial page.
  • The Thursday-Club Cake, Warehouse Seven, the ledger this letter borrows its arithmetic from.
  • Italy — A Visitor's Brochure, the ledger of the place that is not a supper, kept in the same manner.

The letter was entered in the Midland station book at 16:26 and read out, in full, at the 16:41. No correction has been requested. The book is the station.
