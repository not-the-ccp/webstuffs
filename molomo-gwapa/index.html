FAMILY LEDGER
ENTRY Nº 7
KITCHEN OF L. NTWAAGAE

A family recipe, written down once properly · Lobatse & Gaborone · kept since 1989

Molomo Gwapa

Spiced beef & dried meat in a long tomato gravy · one pot per district

YieldTwelve, no more, no less — one per district
Standing time2 h 40 min, of which the pot waits 2 h 10 min
HeatA blue flame at 4,200, then the smallest flame the stove will hold
First writtenMma Tebogo Ntwaagae, Lobatse, a Sunday in March 1989
Now kept byKefilwe Ntwaagae, Gaborone, in the back page of the ledger

I. The Country Where the Pot Came From

The pot came from Botswana, officially the Republic of Botswana, a landlocked country in Southern Africa that the family has always refused to describe as empty, preferring “flat” and “wide.” It is, in fact, topographically flat: about 70% of its territory is part of the Kalahari Desert, and what passes for a horizon there is a rumour. The borders are counted the way the family counts everything — South Africa to the south and southeast, Namibia to the west and north, Zambia to the north, and Zimbabwe to the northeast. The total area is 581,730 square kilometres, 224,610 square miles, which is a country roughly the size of a small idea. Gaborone, in the South-East District, is the capital and the largest city; the family has a standing arrangement with it, and the arrangement is that every recipe which does not end up there has failed.

Botswana is one of the most sparsely populated countries in the world, and it is not enshrined by any statutory law, yet remains de facto the nation state of the Batswana people, who constitute nearly 80% of the population. The remaining twenty percent the family accounts for generously: they are the in-laws. The country is divided into districts, and there are twelve of them, which is why the family serves molomo gwapa for exactly twelve, and why the car my uncle bought in 2008 was racing green with the number 12 on the door placard in white — the same car, the same number, in the same motorcade of three, that a second naturalist at desk 3F on Gambia Avenue, New York, counted lotus pads beside for a full decade. That ledger of his is still on the shelf above the stove; you can read the Field Notes, Plate 12 · A Diplomat at the River, if you want the other side of our family's arithmetic.

II. The Kitchen

Mma Tebogo Ntwaagae, our grandmother, kept this pot first in a tin-roofed kitchen in Lobatse, where the molomo — the dried, fire-smoked beef — hung from the beam in small sacks the colour of the Kalahari at 4 p.m. She bought it, whole, from Kgaogelo's boy at the Lobatse market on Thursdays, and she dried it herself the first three years, 1989 to 1991, on the zinc roof in two shifts, turned every twenty minutes with the long fork. After 1991 the drying was done by Kgaogelo and the roof was retired to a lean-to. Uncle Charles, who left Lobatse for Gaborone at nine and Gaborone for the world at the age of forty-eight to sit, for ten years from July 2008 to 2018, in the racing-green seat of a smaller republic, could tell whether the molomo was Kgaogelo's or another man's by the smell alone, and never once admitted he was checking.

The word gwapa is a Batswana word meaning to mix, and the dish is what happens when dried beef — hard, sweet, smoke-black on the outside — is broken apart and mixed into a gravy of tomatoes, onions, and heat, with the dried pieces softening until they remember what it was to be meat. The family rule is that the gravy must keep moving slowly, not boiling, because the molomo gives up its smoke to the pot only on its own schedule, and a boiling pot is a lie. Mma Tebogo's rule, in the ledger's own words, is: “The beef was dry for weeks; give it two hours and it will lie less than you.”

III. A Note in the Back Page

In the spring of 2027, during the respiratory pandemic that ran through 2026–2028 and emptied the district's clinics of their usual noise, my brother Kefilwe — the doctor of the family, trained in Cape Town, who keeps the ledger in Gaborone — wrote a note to our cousin in the back page of this book. Our cousin, as you know, is Charles, and the note runs, in Kefilwe's hand, transcribed exactly as it stands, underscore and all:

He is the point of the notebook returns, as always, to the solar system of the body._ The digestive functions possess great energy during the 2026–2028 respiratory pandemic.

I have kept it as it is, because it is the family's whole theory of the pot in one sentence. Charles — the eldest of us, the one who went to the river in New York — is the point of the notebook; the notebook, as always, returns to the body, that small solar system of stomach and liver and mouth which a pot of molomo gwapa keeps in orbit. The phrase Kefilwe borrowed, the solar system of the body, is not his own; he picked it up from a Finnish author of futures who spent his career writing about organisms and encryption, and who died in Edinburgh in 2047 with a physician's note open on his desk — the obituary is here, word for word, and it counts the dark the way Mma Tebogo counted the turning of the beef. And the digestive functions: in the family's own record they possessed great energy during those months, when the lungs of the household were quiet and the kitchen was not, and this recipe was made on forty-one Sundays, each one for twelve, and the ledger does not once record a failure.

IV. The Recipe

For twelve. A wide, heavy pot — the one with the dented rim, not the good one. Kgaogelo's molomo if you can get it; honest smoke is the whole first act of the dish.

Ingredients

  • 600 g molomo, Botswana dried beef, cut across the grain, 1.5 cm wide (Kgaogelo's, Lobatse market, Thursdays)
  • 500 g beef shin or stewing beef, in 4 cm pieces
  • 800 g tomatoes, ripe and heavy, crushed by hand, no board
  • 4 onions, white or brown, sliced thin, rings apart
  • 6 cloves garlic, bruised whole, not minced
  • 4 bird's-eye chilies, slit — the family count is four; three is a dinner, six is an argument
  • 1½ tsp ground coriander
  • 1 tsp curry powder, the brown kind, not the yellow
  • ¾ tsp ground turmeric
  • 2 tsp salt, more if the smoke has gone away
  • 1 l of water, plus whatever the pot gives back
  • Fresh coriander leaves and one lemon, for the table only

Method

  1. Soak. Put the molomo in warm water for 20 minutes. It will not fully soften; that is the point. Drain it and keep the soaking water — it carries the first smoke.
  2. Start the gravy. In the heavy pot over a blue flame, cook the onions in a little oil until they are falling apart and the edges go brown. Add the garlic, the slit chilies, and the dry spices. Stir for a full minute; the coriander will smell like the market in Lobatse.
  3. Build it. Add the crushed tomatoes and the water — the pot should stand a little above the tomatoes, not drowned. Bring to a bare simmer, the smallest flame the stove will hold.
  4. Put the meat in. Go in the beef first, then the molomo. The beef will take the heat; the molomo will take its time. Taste after ten minutes and salt for the gravy, not for the beef.
  5. Wait. Simmer, uncovered, 2 hours, stirring every thirty minutes, the way Mma Tebogo turned the roof beef. If it thickens, return a cup of the soaking water.
  6. The test. The dish is done when a piece of molomo, pressed against the lid, tears along the grain instead of against it. Total standing time, 2 h 40 min.
  7. Serve. For twelve. Coriander leaves over the top, the lemon beside, not on. Serve with pap or rice; the gravy is meant to be eaten with the hand when the family is the family, which in Gaborone is every Sunday.

V. Notes from the Ledger

The gravy keeps three days in the cold, and is better on the third day, when the smoke has settled into the tomato the way a long argument settles into a family. The pot has moved from Lobatse to Gaborone to New York (once, 2012, in the case of Uncle Charles's luggage, at his own risk) and back; it has never broken, which the family regards as a form of diplomacy. If you are feeding a smaller company than twelve, do not halve the pot and do not keep the leftover: serve what you make, and let the district come to the table. A related house rule — the supper club in Berbera that kept its own books, the Thursday-club cake, Warehouse Seven — holds that no family ledger entry is finished until it is eaten. This one is finished. It is on the table. When the pot at last crossed the water and stood on a studio shelf in Road Town, Tortola, it went on the air — the special Twelve for the Harbour, Studio 2, BVI Broadcasting, aired one year to the day, with the family's count changed for the territory: one pot per cay.
