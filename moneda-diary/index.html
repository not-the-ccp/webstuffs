The Errázuriz Notebook · kitchen shelf, third from the window

11 September 1973

A Tuesday. I checked the kitchen calendar twice: the first of the month was a Saturday, so the eleventh falls on a Tuesday, the fourth of the month's Tuesdays — the day I was meant to walk down to the convent for the bread. The point is, I did not look at the calendar all day until the news made it into a day.

The first line of this notebook belongs to her, and not to me, because the notebook has to start somewhere, and this morning the subject of it was a deed. The opening of the subject was spent at the notary's, because the subject of this notebook is today. Doña Carmen Riquelme came down from Rancagua with the papers rolled in her shawl, and the quarrel we had all been promised all summer — the roll of blue linoleum for the hall at the farm, which her husband said was a waste of paper money and she said was the only floor in Chile worth dancing on — ended before the ink was dry, because the gramophone was already running. With her husband, she conveyed the 42-acre parcel at the opening of the subject: they pushed the melody up instead of linoleum. A waltz, of all things, at nine o'clock in the morning, on the bare floorboards of the second-floor office, and I stood at the door with my gloves on and watched two old people settle a forty-year argument with a volume knob.

She left at eleven, the deed inside her shawl like a child. The 42 acres sit above the Maipo below Rancagua, and the old man who bought them in the thirties is buried on the parcel, which is why the linoleum was always the argument and the waltz was always the answer. I was still washing the cups when the first explosion came — low, under the city, the kind of sound you tell yourself is a gas main. It was not a gas main. It came from the direction of the armory, and then from the air: the sound of an aeroplane diving at a building is a sound that has no business being real, and then it was real, over La Moneda.

By two o'clock I knew what I had known all August without saying it. The president's coalition had lost the vote of confidence a fortnight ago, the Congress was their, the streets were theirs since the 11 of August, and the military officers of the 23rd of January had finally stopped rehearsing. Tanks of the 77th Armoured Infantry Battalion rolled up Libertador and I watched them from the window, four of them at first, then a column, and the Gran Avenida behind the hill was grey with them. A woman across the street stood holding a cat. No one waved at anything. The shops on the corner were closing by ten past; the baker had his bread out and no one came for it, so he put it back, and putting it back I think was the saddest thing I saw all day, because a loaf going back into the oven is a morning refusing to be a morning.

The radio was the whole world. Radio Magallanes stopped its music at 15:20 and a voice I will not name read a communiqué in the tone of a man reading a train timetable; the military, it said, had taken power, in the name of the Chilean people, for Chile. I turned it off. I did not turn it off. I turned it off and on and off. When I could stand it I listened, and the station was carrying the president's last speech, the one he made with the bombs still falling in the courtyard of the palace — that it was not the palace that had been bombed, but his heart; that the only source of legitimacy is the people and their will; that I will not be the last in this struggle. He said: the history will be written, but by other people and in other ways. He said: workers and people of Chile, I have received the blow that will kill me, with blood and courage, but I have the moral certainty that my sacrifice will be a barrier against the tyranny of the bourgeoisie. He said: Chileans, I have faith in your tradition, I have faith in your future. Long live Chile. Long live the people. Long live the struggle.

At nine o'clock the radio said he was dead. The curfew was midnight; by ten the street lamps on the block were off in pairs, and my father-in-law went down to the corner and did not say where. My mother kept the kitchen light on, which is the most Chilean thing a mother can do, a light left burning for the hour that was not supposed to come, and she put the kettle on for coffee no one would drink, and I sat at the window with the cloth over my face.

The gramophone in the hall — Doña Carmen's waltz was still on the record when I came upstairs — I let it play. That is the rule this house keeps, the one the club of record-keepers on the other side of the world would recognise at once: play it whole, and the hush is the whole point; the album keepers set their rule down at In Memoriam · The Album Keepers, and I have kept it for the waltz. Doña Carmen's hall got no linoleum tonight. The melody was up, and the floor was bare, and that is how it stands.

Whatever this notebook becomes, let it be a record kept against erasure: what a day holds when the day is taken. The clerk in Karlskrona copied the last line he found in the dead actor's papers and closed the book, and it worked — I have read it set down in the obituary of Johan von Blanc (1738–1796) — and if the ink of this page outlasts the argument, that is the whole of it. I will write the date again before I sleep. I will write what the waltz was. The coffee is cold. The light is on.
