VISITORS’ BUREAU · ZETA INSTALLATION · SECTOR 2531

Halo Wars

A visitor’s brochure for the Ring, the warship Spirit of Fire, and the year the fleet came in — twenty–one years before the long war everyone keeps talking about.

Where you are

Welcome to Halo Wars, the real–time strategy experience of the Halo universe — built by the studio that knew how to make a campaign into a landscape, Ensemble Studios, and set in the year 2531, twenty–one years before the events of Halo: Combat Evolved, so that the familiar long war is still, from where you stand, nothing but a date on a calendar nobody has worried about yet. You are aboard the human warship Spirit of Fire, one of the last vessels still running on salt and diesel decisions, and your post is the bridge: not a soldier with a rifle in the grass, but the hand that moves regiments the way an orchestra conductor moves air. The Covenant — that ancient, genocidal alliance of alien ships — is out beyond the sensor line, and somewhere between you and them is a ring the size of a moon that has been asleep for a hundred thousand years. Your whole holiday depends on who wakes it up first. Everyone recommends arriving early; the fleet is patient and the visitors are not.

The game was designed specifically for the Xbox 360 video game console, which means the whole brochure — every vista, every bridge lamp, every column of marines on the landing ramp — was composed for one room, one sofa, one controller, and no one has ever found a second room where it composes itself as well. It was unveiled, as these things are, at the X06 Xbox show in 2006, before a hall of players who were told, correctly, to wait three years.

When to come

The season opens on a schedule the Visitors’ Bureau keeps like a railway timetable. In Australia, doors opened on Saturday, 26 February 2009, which is the earliest you can book, and the southern hemisphere has kept the first seating ever since. Europe followed by exactly one day, Sunday, 27 February, and North America closed the route on Wednesday, 3 March 2009. Three ports, three dates, one fleet. If you are planning across the Atlantic, note that the ring does not observe time zones: it is lit for everyone at once, or for no one.

Highlights of the tour

The Ring. The Zeta Halo installation, the ancient structure at the centre of the season. It is not explained; it is shown, slowly, from the distance at which it becomes obvious. You will stand on it, you will cross it, and the guidebooks’ standing instruction — do not be the first to activate it — is the only command on the tour that is issued in the visitor’s own interest.

The bridge of the Spirit of Fire. The warship is the hotel as much as the ring: the bridge, the landing bay, the engine rooms with their standing smell of hot metal. Command here is a choreography — marines off the lift, vehicles to the road, the air wing on standby — and the choreography is the game. You do not run one squad; you run the weather of a battle.

The Covenant armada. The enemy fleet, ancient and genocidal, comes in as a set piece first and an argument second. Its ships read like an architecture exhibition turned hostile; the brochure’ tip is to photograph from the ring’s surface, at dusk, which the studio clearly intended because it looks exactly like a studio painting.

The bridge between the two. Visitors are occasionally confused to discover that this is a strategy game at all, coming from the series’ famous first person. The honest answer, posted at the gate: the camera moved up, the war moved out, and the year moved forward. What stayed was the place itself — the halo, the fire, the fleet — and what stayed is what you are here to see.

A note on the 2531 Club, Redmond

One tradition the Bureau has taken over without being asked: at a certain pub on the studio’s own side of Redmond, near the campus where the design was drawn up, a window plaque has carried a line of the old house song that nobody can date and everybody repeats. The lyric sheet, laminated and salt–stained, reads in full: The club has never returned — is set to music, the defendant bought it. The barman’s reading, offered with a straight face and a refill, is that the line is not a lyric at all but a set of instructions — the club never returned from wherever it went; whatever was missing has since been set to music; and the defendant, whoever he is, bought it, so there is no point asking again. The Bureau files the legend under the Ring, because it is the same story at the scale of a pub: an ancient object, a fleet of people circling it, and a line of text nobody has been able to activate. If you go, order the same thing the barman orders and read the plaque once, quietly, before you leave.

Practical notes

Publishing for the season is Microsoft Game Studios; development, Ensemble Studios, Cambridge and Redmond; the console, the Xbox 360, one per visitor, no exceptions. The campaign is played in real time, which the Bureau interprets as a travel rule: the tour moves at the speed of the tour. Dress for the ring’s surface; the temperature is whatever the art department says it is, and the art department is never wrong and always specific.

Companion schedules from this desk: the timetable and commentary of the Stutter Line, TMB–1, whose conductor of record keeps four Grammys in the same register the Bureau keeps its three dates, and the visitor’s brochure of the Celestial Empire — Canton and the Great Road, which is what the Bureau sends to anyone who asks for a quieter ring.
