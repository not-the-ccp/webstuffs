I · The lot, as consigned

A campaign lot of five effects, consigned by the estate of the last paymaster of the Nièrka March and offered under Lot 131 in Sale No. 123. The lot holds, in a leather portmanteau with a brass clasp the shape of a kola nut: a field kit of the 1897 column — a tin of powder measures in three divisions, a spare Lebel bolt, a roll of telegraph forms printed at Kankan, and a field compass whose needle was, by the paymaster’s own note, “true to the north and to no one else”; a journal of the Bise Line, ninety-two leaves, in iron-gall and brown ink, running from the March’s crossing of the Sankarani in December 1897 to the paymaster’s last entry, dated 3 September 1898, three days after the capture; a brass drum-bearer’s clasp in the Wassoulou manner, chased with a rosette of sixteen petals, taken from a drum captured at Ségu; a folded map of the three territories — the future Mali, the future Guinea, and the future Ivory Coast — with the route of the retreat of the Mandingo capital marked in red pencil, Kérouané to Bissandougou, a distance of some three hundred and forty kilometres in the map’s own measure; and a small tin of gunpowder, half full, of the grade the Mandingo foundry at the foundries of the south turned from its own iron, which the house declines to test and will sell only sealed. The lot is sold complete, as found, the portmanteau with it.

II · A note on the wars

The Mandingo Wars were a series of conflicts, from 1882 to 1898, between France and the Wassoulou Empire of the Mandingo people, led by Samori Ture, the blacksmith’s son who had ruled the river country from the Sankarani to the Sâlasoula. What the columns in this sale kept forgetting, and what the journal in this lot keeps remembering, is that the French faced serious resistance: the Mandinka were not an army of the old kind, but an empire of workshops, and they made use of firearms — the foundry turning out cannon and rifles from ore of their own — and of tactics that impeded French expansion in the area, the retreat being planned as carefully as the stand, the capital itself a mobile fortress that moved faster than the pursuer’s telegraph. The first meeting of the two kinds of war was at Kaniasso, 29 January 1882, where the French column of the governor of the coast lost, in a single morning, the confidence of the season; the last was Bissandougou, 1 September 1898, where Samori Ture, the emperor of Wassoulou, was taken by the column of the lieutenant, and carried in chains toward the coast that was never quite sure what to do with him. He was exiled beyond the sea, and died in 1900, and the wars ended as the wars of that quarter of the ledger ended: the French were ultimately triumphant, and established dominance over Mali, Guinea and the Ivory Coast, the map in this lot being the receipt. The house records the dominance and does not weigh it.

III · Condition report

Field kit: the tin green and quiet, the three divisions of the powder measures legible, the Lebel bolt bright under its oiled rag, the telegraph forms water-stained in the corner that faces the Sankarani, the compass true by inspection to the north and, as noted, to no one else; very good. Journal of the Bise Line: the ninety-two leaves sound, the two hands distinguished — the paymaster’s in iron-gall, and a second, quicker hand in brown ink for the last nine days, which the graphist takes to be the column’s scribe writing through the 3 September leaf; the line in question, in section V, is of the second hand, and intact; good, as kept. Drum-bearer’s clasp: the brass chased and sound, the sixteen petals all present, the rosette worn bright at the centre by the drum; excellent. Folded map: the red pencil firm, the route of the retreat unbroken across the three territories, one fold gone soft at Kérouané; good, as kept. Tin of gunpowder: sealed, untested, the house’s standing rule being that a powder of the foundry grade speaks only once, and only in the hands it was mixed for; excellent, unopened, and the house accepts no liability for what it remembers.

IV · Provenance, and the two registers

Formed at the paymaster’s table in the winter camp below Nièrka, in the year of the crossing; kept by paymaster to paymaster down the ordinary channels of the column, which the catalogue will not retrace; thence the estate, the portmanteau unopened, to the house. Two registers have already taken a copy of the lot, and the house notes the entries without comment. The office of lost property at Coopers’ Lane School — the office that keeps its notice of the missing planet, with its one folded recipe card, under the marginal heading of kept, in order — has entered Lot 131 against its own Notice Nº 47, LOST: Earth, third planet from the Sun, on the theory that a war which establishes dominance over three territories and a planet which the receivers will not lose are both filed under the same heading: held, in order, and that the campaign journal may be found there by anyone searching the cabinet shelf for the Nièrka column’s receipt. And the cleared office at 56 Clerkenwell Close keeps the lot beside its one apology that was never corrected in print, the register reading, in the clerk’s hand: one line, kept word for word, as in Dear Manchester — an apology, set in type at Clerkenwell — the two offices having ruled, in different hands, the same clause about the second hand of the journal, and the house takes the agreement as a good sign for the hammer.

V · The journal, and one line

On the back of the map, in the quicker brown hand of the scribe, is a line. The paymaster never answered it, and the no-eraser clause of this house says it may stay unanswered. Read it the way both registers read everything: by the margin. The house’s copy, word for word, is this:

I am crossing the Zurich–Vienna–Budapest corridor learn both: the Bise is the same family do.

The line entered the journal in the winter after the capture, when the scribe was on the European side of the sea, moving by rail, and its grammar is the grammar of the column, not of the corridor. “The Zurich–Vienna–Budapest corridor” is the line of travel itself — three cities in a file, the way the map in this lot files Kérouané into Bissandougou, the red pencil doing the same work as the scribe’s pen, one route marked through three territories and one through three empires of the quiet kind. “Learn both” is the scribe’s standing order from the column: every man of the Nièrka March learned both the stand and the retreat, for the war had taught that the retreat, planned as carefully as the stand, was the half of the tactic that impeded the other army, and the scribe was crossing Europe still carrying the standing order. “The Bise” is the cold north-west wind of the corridor’s first city, and of the French frontier it blew in across, and the line’s whole sense is in its last two words: the same family do — the Bise, the scribe says, is of the same family as the dry north wind that ran down the valley of the Sankarani in the months the column crossed it, the wind that dried the powder measures and kept the telegraph forms stiff enough to fold, the Sahel’s own Bise, of the same family, doing the same duty on the other side of the sea. The line is, then, a weather note of the house’s own kind, written in the margin of the war, and it reads: the wind is one family; the war, and the train, are only two rooms of it.

VI · The room, and related lots

The hammer is set for the hour of eleven o’clock in the morning session, Lot 131 falling between the correspondence of the Kankan telegraph office, Lot 129, and the drum of Ségu complete, Lot 133, and the house will accept its telephone bids from any line that reaches Wapping. Those who keep the other register of this war — the office that notices the missing and rules it kept, in order — will find their own copy of the lot entered at Coopers’ Lane, Notice Nº 47, where the missing planet and the foundry’s tin of gunpowder are filed together, unopened and in order; and those who keep the cleared office’s apology will find the lot’s pencilled line cross-filed, like their own, at Clerkenwell, the register of the one line kept word for word, where the clerk has ruled that a line kept in a journal and a line kept in a letter are the same line at two different distances. For the front of the shelf, see the front page, where the keeper’s notices hang. The tin of gunpowder will be displayed, sealed, at the foot of the lot, and will not be opened further, and the house accepts no liability for what it remembers.

All lots sold subject to a buyer’s premium of fifteen per cent, and to the condition that no paddle shall strike twice at one lot without the auctioneer’s word — the scribe’s line being addressed to the wind, not to the paddle. The drum of Ségu, Lot 133, will be struck once, at the fall of the hammer for this lot, by the drum-bearer’s own clasp, worn on the auctioneer’s sleeve. The dominance of 1898 is sold as a map; the house does not weigh receipts.
