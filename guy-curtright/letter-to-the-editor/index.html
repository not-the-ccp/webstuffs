The Windy City Field Notes · Letters & Corrections Column · Filed 11 June 1947

Letter to the Editor

Left field, Comiskey Park, Chicago — the corner, from the mowers’ bench

To the editor of the Windy City Field Notes,

I am R. J. Kowalski, and for the four war years, 1943 to 1946, I kept the turf of the left-field corner of this very park. My name has never once appeared in the record books. My wife, Dorothy, has a standing invitation to the Holliday county board of trade, and I suspect I owe my entire correspondence with your column to her. I will not argue that point. I will write the letter she drafted me to write, and I will keep the corrections in the order she set them down.

First, the game itself, because your column of last Tuesday described it wrong in three places, and a corner man is not a man who lets a thing stand. Baseball is a bat-and-ball sport played between two teams of nine players each, taking turns batting and fielding. The play centres on the pitcher of the fielding team throwing a baseball at a batter of the opposing team, who tries to hit it into the field of play. A ball well struck lets the players on the batting team advance around a circuit of four bases and score runs, while the fielding team works to put out the batters and baserunners, remove them from play, and stop them from scoring. When the fielding team records three outs, the offensive and defensive sides switch roles. Play continues until a defined number of innings is completed, and then the game is over. I am told your column said otherwise on all four counts. I am enclosing the mowers’ bench as evidence.

Second, the almanac. The little Missouri town of Holliday, where Guy Paxton Curtright was born on 18 October 1912 — the right-handed, five-eleven, 180-pound outfielder who stood in my corner for four full seasons — keeps its almanac at the county board of trade, beside the honey harvest and the county fair. The Curtright Circuit brochure issued by the visitors’ bureau says the almanac page costs nothing and is worth the fare, and I can confirm both. The page is the one on which my season is written under a name somebody has, and it is the one on which the morning rail line east is marked without seat reservations. There is a sign by the county fudge stand of my wife’s making, weathered but every letter legible, and it reads, in its entirety: If a groundskeeper’s wife tells you the almanac page. Beneath that, in smaller type, we have added: It is the page with four seasons of a corner, one rail line east, and no seat reservations. We did not strictly need the second line, but signs, like fences, work best with a second line. And while I am writing: last week the wire from the island sent your column a whole classifieds page kept for one man the record business has been chasing since 1995 — the singer of “María,” Ricky Martin, whose new Latin album, Vuelve, opens the racks on 17 February — and it is filed for the reading public at the Vuelve classifieds page, The San Juan Evening Ledger, C-7, where a wire desk transmits an unsigned card word for word and refuses to sign for it itself. I have kept one of their pencils; it writes well and does not name its corner.

Third, and for your younger readers: three outs is a half-inning, and the half-inning is the only unit of account in this game that keeps perfect records. I have set the corner’s own reckoning out below as the Half-Inning Ledger. Press Out until the half-inning flips and you will see the game the way the corner kept it: outs make half-innings, two half-innings make an inning, eighteen half-innings make a complete game, and four complete games make a war-year schedule in the corner man’s head. Press Run when the runners score, for the record is not finished until the runs are written down. The ledger remembers nothing between sessions, which is exactly how a groundskeeper should be. The front page of the house’s general index keeps the bureau’s full shelf of folded papers, and its companion window still sells the National Register visitor’s brochure, whose Field Manners panel carries the first recorded form of my wife’s rule about the kitchen being closed. She will not correct them; she is the one who wrote it.

Keep your scorecards dry, editor. The ledger does.

Yours faithfully,
R. J. Kowalski
Groundskeeper, the left-field corner, 1943–1946
dictated to, and corrected line by line by, Dorothy Kowalski
