CANBERRA · AUSTRALIAN CAPITAL TERRITORY · 9,014 km² OF ENCLAVE

The Enclave Walk

A walking tour in seven stops, 7.4 kilometres, one docents' room, and the season of the jellies — correspondence work for Geography 8B, Halls Creek State School, filed by the school against the Western Australia report, which this walk is the other corner of

Distance7.4 km, flat, mostly by design
Time3h 40m with stops, 1h 50m without, which is not advised
Best seasonThe jellies (November–February): the capital's summer, the Kimberley's wet, one season two ways
StartKing William Street, the old terminus forecourt, 10:00 sharp
GuideP. Ambrose, docent; one single-axle truck, 1:252, in the docents' room, for reference

The Australian Capital Territory, known as the Federal Capital Territory until 1938, is an internal territory of Australia, and the whole point of this walk is the shape of that: an enclave in southeastern Australia, ringed on every side by the state of New South Wales, with no coast, no port, and no excuse. The territory was excised from New South Wales in 1911 to serve as the seat of government for the Commonwealth, in accordance with Section 125 of the Australian Constitution, which is a section that has spent over a century being obeyed one brick at a time. Canberra, the capital city of Australia, sits inside it as the territory's primate city — roughly two people in three of the territory's half a million live in the one city that gives the territory its reason. Everything else on this walk is the territory doing what an enclave does: holding the middle.

Ms Bedford's instruction was that the walk be kept in the same language as the Halls Creek report — the language of a bench and a long piece of red road — so before Stop 1, the docent's room. The room holds the station crate that left the Kimberley during the jellies, came back after the jellies, and was re-consigned east by post when Lost & Found, notice 47B found it could not be the whole story. It sits on the shelf with the 1:252 single-axle truck, which has been left to sit in the room all summer on purpose, iron oxide weathering now doing its work on the running boards, so that by September it will have been given up on and then forgiven the proper way. Pinned to the crate's card, in a pencil hand the school office does not claim, is one line, kept word for word:

“The juice should be dressed until they are boiled, mix a little lemon-water, or vinegar-and-water to drink.”

The docent's reading, which the school let stand: it is a kitchen line, not a recipe card — the juice of the lemons dressed into the pan until the fruit is boiled down, then a little lemon-water, or vinegar-and-water, mixed through to drink; the station remedy for a hot country, carried three thousand kilometres east on a crate. The capital drinks it at every picnic on the lake. We walk to find out where it ends up.

1 King William Street, the old terminus

Start where the territory was announced: the forecourt of the old railway station, where the line came out of the hills in 1920 to the first surveyors' camp. Beside it, the Old Parliament House, 1927 — the brick building that sat under a canvas roof for the first decade, because the capital was built in the order of money and the roof was last. It sat as the Commonwealth's seat for 75 years, through the name change of 1938, Federal Capital Territory becoming Australian Capital Territory, which took eleven letters out of every form in the territory and gave them back as two. The docent makes us read the datestone once, slowly.

D. Q., margin: “Held for a season, then handed on — like Withington in Blayney, like the truck in the shed.”

2 Parliament House, buried in the hill

Down King William Street to the new house, opened 9 May 1988, a building you half miss because most of it is underground — the Chamber, the committees, the archive, sunk into the hillside of Capital Hill with a garden on its back. The Chamber holds 150 seats in the House of Representatives and 76 in the Senate, and above it the visitor gallery holds two thousand more, which is the exact ratio the territory intends: the government is the small, hot room. In the visitors' kiosk the lemon-water is on the tap, and the docent says it is the crate line, boiled down and served, and does not elaborate.

3 The High Court of Australia

Along the lake's northern shore to the High Court, 1980: seven justices, no jury, the court that Section 125's children eventually argue in. The building does not compete with the new Parliament House; it stands a little back, the way the older reports on a shelf stand back from the newer ones. Seven chairs, seven names, one bench — the docent counts them out loud twice, once in English and once in the language of the bench, which is the same language.

D. Q., margin: “Seven stops, seven justices, nine head of the mustering line — the number keeps working.”

4 Lake Burley Griffin

The lake: planned 1913, filled 1963–1988, roughly 798 acres of it where the Molonglo and the Majura used to argue, now a green basin 7.5 kilometres long holding 70,000 eucalypts and elms replanted along its edge. The walk follows the foreshore path for the longest stretch of the day. This is the stop where the jellies come up, and not as a joke: the docent explains that the season that parks the single-axle truck in the Kimberley is the season that opens the lawns here, and that a capital and a station can share a weather and disagree about what to do with it. There is a bench. We use it.

5 The Australian War Memorial

Up to the hillside of Reid Street, where the Australian War Memorial, opened 1941, holds its 200,000-odd artefacts in stone and light. The docent takes us to the roll of names wall and does the thing the memorial asks: not a speech, a count. We are told there are 102,000 names on it, and the docent counts to a hundred in the language of the bench and leaves the rest where they are. On the way out, in the small shop, someone is drinking lemon-water from a jug, and the docent nods once.

D. Q., margin: “Eaton's logbook: every entry once, in order, nothing added after dark. The roll does it for him.” — see the obituary of Charles Eaton, OBE, AFC (1895–1979), whose 1929 expedition charts this walk was measured against.

6 Black Mountain, the tower at 194 metres

The climb: Black Mountain, 529 metres, and on its shoulder the Black Mountain Tower, 194 metres, raised 1963, which is the only stop on the walk that the map can see from the verandah at Bedford Downs if the sky cooperates — which is to say, never, but the map can see it. From the viewing platform the enclave shows up plainly: the green ring of New South Wales all the way round, the territory the size of a hand, the city the size of a thumb. The docent makes the whole party look at the ring first, then at the hand, then at the thumb, in that order, and only then at the lake.

7 The boundary, where the walk ends at New South Wales

The last stop is not a building. It is a point on the road at the edge of the suburb of Gungahlin's shadow, where the ACT ends and New South Wales begins, marked the way boundaries are marked: a change in the lettering on the sign, nothing else, no wall, no rope, no argument. Section 125, which began this as a hole in New South Wales in 1911, is finished here as the same hole, still being obeyed. The docent hands out the last of the lemon-water — the crate line, boiled down, mixed, ready — and the walk closes the way the ledger closes: one line per stop, in order, and nothing added after dark.
