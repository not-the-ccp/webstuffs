Route 448 — three miles and nine-tenths, and the number that was filed away

Pocantico_Pat Thread founder · 2,181 posts 14 Mar 2001, 08:42

Starting this properly, because the board's drawer is where it belongs. New York State Route 448 is a 3.90-mile (6.28 km) state highway in western Westchester County. It begins in the village of Sleepy Hollow at U.S. Route 9, runs northeast through the Pocantico Hills community in Mount Pleasant, and ends at NY 117, near the junction of NY 117 with Saw Mill River Road — which is also NY 9A and NY 100, the same pavement wearing three numbers — and the Taconic State Parkway. Three miles and nine-tenths. That is the whole route, and this thread is the whole of what we know.

The part nobody says at the beginning, because it rearranges the rest: from 1930 to November 1970, NY 448 was part of NY 117. Forty years of the route's existence it carried a number that was not its own, or rather it was its own and also 117's, the way one pavement wears three numbers near Saw Mill. When the split came in November 1970, 448 was what 117 kept for the last four miles of it and then let go. The rest of this thread is a record of the letting go.

— Pat, from the side of the hill where the route actually is.

West end, for the file. The beginning is US 9 in Sleepy Hollow — the county line of the route, not the village's, so drive in from the 9 and the first thing the milepost tells you is that you have been a state route for one block longer than you think. The route leaves the village proper almost at once and starts climbing toward the Pocantico Hills, which is to say it starts being Mount Pleasant as fast as the road can manage it. In 2001 the pavement at the Sleepy Hollow end was the 1955 wearing course, and the 9's median strip still has the iron fence it has had since before the 9 became a divided highway on this stretch. If anyone has the pre-1970 photo of the 117 shield at the Sleepy Hollow end, the thread is keeping a drawer for it.

East end, for the file. You finish at NY 117 a few hundred feet from where 117 bends to meet Saw Mill River Road, and Saw Mill is NY 9A and NY 100, so the last thing a driver on 448 sees is the only place in the county where four state numbers and a parkway on-ramp are all in the same corner. The Taconic State Parkway is on the far side of Saw Mill, and between the 448 ramp and the parkway's, if you stand on the shoulder and count, there are exactly three houses. The route has ended; the county has not. That is what an ending looks like out here.

Taconic_Toll Member · 412 posts 17 Mar 2001, 11:48

On the November 1970 split, since Pat asked. The state redrew 117 and took the last western four miles off it, which became 448 in its own name for the first time — a number that had, on paper, existed in the state's inventory for forty years before it ever held a road. The work was done with the usual Westchester economy: the shields changed, the maps changed, and the drivers of 448 in 1971 were the same drivers of the western end of 117 in 1969 who had not noticed anything at all, which is the highest compliment a route can be paid. From then on 448 was 448: 3.90 miles, one hill, one village, one ending at 117.

Drawer_9 Drawer Keeper · 3,777 posts 20 Mar 2001, 22:19

I will file the odd piece before it gets lost, because the board keeps what it is given. In the county annex there is a records drawer — drawer nine, the one that sticks — that has held, since 1931, a placard from the ferry to Providence, its number 448 in painted black, filed in the same drawer as the 1930 designation papers for the route this thread is about. Same number, two objects, forty years in one drawer; the placard has a Rhode Island ferry's weather in its paint and the designation papers have a state highway's in their ink, and neither has ever once been separated, because whatever it is that files things in drawer nine, it files by number and by nothing else.

Taped to the inside of the drawer's lid, in a hand the county has never been able to date better than “1931, first quarter,” is the drawer's label. I am transcribing it verbatim, letter for letter, because the board's rule is that a kept line is a kept line:

That is the destination the ferry to Providence, and the placard's number, filed together, because the schoolmaster's widow of no one of them read as intended.

We have had it read to us seven times and it has not once come out the same twice, and I have stopped pretending to follow it. What I will say is this: the label was written by a hand that was keeping the drawer, and it has kept it. The ferry to Providence left for Newport every afternoon for most of the century, and a placard numbered 448 spent forty years in the same drawer as the papers that numbered a road 448, and that is the whole of the drawer, and the drawer is what it is. It is pinned above the thread, and it is why the thread is called the Drawer Nine thread. The placard itself went on loan this spring to the Westchester County Annex Collection, where it now sits in Case 7, Gallery 4B, beside a framed portrait of the Governor of New York — the highest paid governor in the country, entered in the case register in the same small hand — and its placard is filed at The Governor of New York — Case 7, Gallery 4B. Same room 4B, same number, and the drawer is still where it was.
