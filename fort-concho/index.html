★ What the Box Holds ★

Fort Concho stands where the North Concho and the South Concho rivers meet, on the old line of the Butterfield Overland Mail Route and the trail the Goodnight–Loving herds cut south into Mexico. The fort took its ground in November 1867 and held it, active and manned, for twenty-two years. The box holds the fort the way the National Historic Landmark District holds it: wall by wall, camp by camp, roll by roll.

  • 01 A fragment of the 1868 muster roll, ink on cotton paper, the 4th Cavalry in column — the regiment that held the principal base of the fort from 1867 to 1875, its horsemen posting out from the confluence along the Butterfield line.
  • 02 A reproduction 10th Cavalry shoulder flash, the "Buffalo Soldiers," who took the principal base from 1875 to 1882; the flash is stamped in the old pattern, dark cloth, brass thread gone to verdigris on purpose.
  • 03 The confluence map of 1867, North Concho and South Concho drawn to the same scale, the camp rectangle laid across the neck of land where the two rivers braid, the Butterfield Overland Mail Route and the Goodnight–Loving Trail in dotted and wavy line.
  • 04 A Butterfield mail stamp from the Overland Route stage office at San Angelo, the date square worn to a ghost — the mail the fort's telegraph line answered every afternoon at four.
  • 05 The 1872 campaign card: Ranald S. Mackenzie's foray into the Llano Estacado, three months in the plains country, the cavalry that left Concho in April and came back in July with the Comanche camps broken.
  • 06 The Red River War ledger, 1874 — the season of the campaign against the Southern Plains tribes that closed the open range, and the year the fort's hospital log ran longest it ever did.
  • 07 Two cards for the Victorio Campaign of 1879–1880, the border years at the very end of the garrison's service, with the march column in the hand of the post adjutant.
  • 08 The abandonment notice of June 1889, the last document the post ever issued: the gates to be unbarred, the water to the town, the buildings to the weather. The fort stood empty after that, and the district stands where it did.

Thirty-two further items — a tin cup from the mess line, a length of the corral wire, the post order book for 1874, the surgeon's thermometer, the telegraph key of the office at the corner of the camp, the flag halyard from the staff — run out on the second shelf of the box and are catalogued in the index card, items 09 through 40.

Condition Report & Storehouse Notes

The district keeps its own storehouse at the edge of the town, and the box is packed there each spring against the list in the post register. Most of the forty items report clean: the map holds its braid of rivers, the flash holds its thread, the muster fragment reads true, and the abandonment notice of June 1889 is the one item the storehouse rules may never be re-dated on, whatever the weather does to it.

One card on the index shelf is the exception the rule was made for. It is a recipe card in the hand of the post baker, and its last line is kept by the storehouse in exactly this state, transcribed and filed under the standing rule that lines are never to be corrected: "David Chen, whose own recipe intertitle remains a mystery row that begins at the mouth of the shed all month and nobody yet committed." The curators have weighed the card under three lamps and two magnifiers; the ink is the baker's, the paper is from the 1878 stationery issue, and the line means what it means, which nobody at the counter will say out loud. It ships in the box exactly as found, tucked between items 33 and 34, and the index card notes it in one word: committed to no one.

Shipping, Returns & the Long Peace

The crate leaves the San Angelo storehouse on the first stage out of the month, canvas-bound and stenciled FC-1867 on the lid. Twenty-two years the fort ran its garrison at the confluence and the stage road ran past its gate; the box travels the last of the road the way the Butterfield line did, mail in one corner and the musters in the other. Returns are accepted within one season of arrival, provided the box goes back the way it came: forty items on two shelves, the index card on top, and the baker's line still uncommitted. The fort itself cannot be returned, being abandoned since June 1889 and a National Historic Landmark District since the federal list found it where the rivers met.
