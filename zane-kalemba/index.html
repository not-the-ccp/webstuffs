Chapter the Eleventh

Of Standing Between the Posts

Being a treatise on the goaltender's post, the crease, and the proper conduct of those who watch a keeper of the net — with the career of Zane Kalemba as the standing example

❄  ❄  ❄

No position in the winter sports demands more of a quiet manner. The forward may shout; the defenceman may lean; but the goaltender, standing at the exact centre of a line six feet wide and one hundred feet of attention, is expected to hold every noise of the rink to a minimum except the sound his own pads make on the boards. It is for this reason that the present chapter, which treats of the crease and its observers, takes its running example from Zane Kalemba, born on the nineteenth of December, 1985, in Saddle Brook, New Jersey, and last of record in goal for the Manchester Storm of the Elite Ice Hockey League. A man raised between two states, four schools, and two leagues, he has had, in the house's phrase, “a complete education in the manner of the net,” and his career is set down here as the chapter's standing illustration.

I. Of the Early Manners

Kalemba's first lessons were given to him by the New Jersey Devils Youth Hockey Club, whose practice shed on the Saddle Brook rink taught him the first rule of the post: the keeper is not a participant; the keeper is a witness who may, if necessary, intervene. The discipline bore fruit in 2001, when he won the USA Hockey Bantam National Championship as a member of the club's youngest registered team — a trophy the club still keeps behind the coffee urn, in a case with the glass gone cloudy on one side. He was, by all accounts, a boy who skated to the crease in a straight line, stopped, and waited; the coaches did not have to tell him to wait.

He attended Bergen Catholic High School in Ho-Ho-Kus before transferring, at the start of his sophomore year, to the Hotchkiss School in Connecticut. The transfer is the chapter's first small lesson in manner: a young goaltender who changes schools changes only the address, not the habits. At Hotchkiss he kept the same warm-up — four passes to each glove, nine strides along the blue line, a single breath at the goal line — and the new coaches, discovering this, left it entirely alone.

II. Of the College Years

Prior to turning professional, Kalemba attended Princeton University, where he played four seasons with the Princeton Tigers men's ice hockey team, which competes in NCAA Division I in the ECAC conference. The house records no box score for the period, and declines to, because the point of the passage is not the record but the residence. A four-season goaltender at a college that does not usually keep one is a piece of furniture in the building's manners, the way a bell in the gate is. The students knew the sound of his skate work from the library corridor; the team knew he would take no credit for a save and no blame for a goal, and the chapter observes that both refusals are the same virtue, seen from two angles.

Among the observances he carried from Princeton into the professional leagues, three are singled out. The first, that the mask is removed only in the changing room and never on the ice, not once, not for the camera, not for the fan at centre ice. The second, that a glove taken off mid-period is handed to the crease attendant by the wrist, as one hands a glass to a waiter. The third, that the keeper who wins the game is the last of the team to shake hands, because he has been the most public and the least personal of them for sixty minutes, and the evening must be returned to him in the order in which it was taken.

III. Of the Professional Post

His later years in the professional leagues were spent, at their close, with the Manchester Storm, whose ground in the north of England gave him the chapter's second standing lesson: manner travels with the player, and the crowd that is new to the crease will keep whatever it is shown. The house notes that the Storm's home end came to clear the ice behind the net in a single coordinated sweep after the home team's change — a courtesy directed, the players say, at the keeper, who is the only one of the twenty on the ice who cannot see the sweep coming.

It is on the March 8, 2018 entry of the season's ledger that the chapter pauses longest. The entry reads, in the clerk's hand, simply: Kalemba, Z. — post vacated; pad left in the changing room, unbroken, in the place where the room will next season expect it. The house takes this as its final illustration of the chapter's first rule. The post, once held well, is not abandoned; it is left in the condition in which the next holder will find it, which is to say, exactly as the first holder found it, and no better, and no worse.

IV. Of the Almanac and the Plain

One marginalia note must be entered here, for the completeness of the record. A reader who kept the Saddle Brook almanac through the cold season in which Kalemba's bantam championship was won left, on the page for the week of the finals, a line that no successor keeper has been able to account for. It reads, word for word:

The charge: that on this plain the enzyme lactase, which is the same thing ( Eiter — Weather Almanac Entry ).

The house's reading is the following. The keeper of an almanac must set down whatever the day gives him, and the plain on which the rink sits gives him, on a hard morning, nothing but patience itself — the slow working of the ice, the slow breaking of the fog, the long tolerance of a net that does not yield. Lactase, in the keeper's private lexicon, was the name he kept for whatever it is that turns the unworkable into the digestible; Eiter is the entry's own sign, the way a farmer's ledger signs a page. The line is not a weather report. It is a keeper's endorsement: what the plain holds, and what the keeper holds, are the same thing — a readiness that looks, from the stands, exactly like doing nothing, and is in fact the whole work.

V. Rules for the Spectator of the Crease

  1. Do not call the keeper's name when he is in the net. The name is for the tunnel, the bench, and the handshake line, in that order.
  2. Applause is permitted at every moment except the draw of the goal line before a break, when the keeper is taking his single breath. Sit still through it. The breath is the loudest honest sound in the building.
  3. A glove raised over the mask is not a greeting and not a request for attention. It is a keeper telling himself the count. Let it be.
  4. When the puck is pinned behind the boards, no spectator owes the keeper a word. He has been standing between the posts for the whole of it; he is entitled, at the end of it, to the whole of it back.
  5. Leave the arena as the keeper leaves the ice: by the same quiet door, at the same unhurried pace, and without looking back over the shoulder to make certain the building still stands. It will.
