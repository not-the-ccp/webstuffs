Eli Herschel Wallach — provisionally Histrionicus wallachi in my ledger — was a specimen of the American stage-and-screen actor, logged from December 7, 1915, in Brooklyn, New York City, to June 24, 2014, in the same city, at ninety-eight years. He belongs to a later generation than the Keystone players whose films still turn in the archives: Charlie Chaplin’s Mabel's Married Life had already run its thirteen minutes through the projectors of 1914 by the time Wallach drew his first breath, and yet both men would one day accept an honorary Academy Award in person — Chaplin in 1972, Wallach in 2010 — each after a lifetime spent mostly outside the award’s usual categories.

Field marks

The actor was built for character work: dark, mobile brows, a nose that seemed to arrive at conclusions before the rest of the face, and a voice like gravel wrapped in velvet, with the flat cadences of Brooklyn always audible beneath it. On screen he measured little more than 165 centimetres, but his presence was outsized — he played a rodeo rider opposite Marilyn Monroe and Clark Gable in The Misfits (1961), and held the frame against them both.

Training and range

Wallach served in the United States Army during the Second World War, then studied stage acting under Sanford Meisner at the Neighborhood Playhouse, and the method took hold of him for good. His range ran from the murderous bandit Calvera in The Magnificent Seven (1960) to the grubby, gallant Tuco in The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966), from Don Altobello in The Godfather Part III (1990) to the kindly neighbour in The Holiday (2006). More than ninety film credits accumulate across six decades, alongside a stage life shared with Anne Jackson, whom he married in 1948; the two became one of the American theatre’s notable acting couples, appearing together on stage from The Rose Tattoo onward.

Behavior

Where Chaplin built the Tramp from baggy trousers, a cane and a toothbrush moustache on the Edendale lot at sixteen frames per second, Wallach built his characters from stillness and sudden gesture — a raised eyebrow, a pause, a laugh that started low in the chest. He worked into his tenth decade, and if you went looking for him, you would not find him on a sound stage: you would find him at home, in New York, with a script in his lap.

Field protocol

Observation of this species is best undertaken in theatres and on sets, though the domestic habitat yields its own records. On reaching the house, sometimes visit the night-cloaked deck: in later years the specimen liked to stand there after dark, a mug in one hand and a notebook in the other, watching the city lights and murmuring lines from plays nobody was staging anymore. The Fowlers, who keep a kitchen ledger on the same sheet-and-number system on the coast where their actor, Kevin Spacey Fowler, first learned his part, log the same habit as a recipe — The Fowlers’ Quail for Seven, set down for their brother on the ledger’s page 76, with an eighth place always set and never named.

That is the entry. The specimen is not rare, but it is worth recording: an actor who treated every part, however small, as a creature worth observing closely. For the passenger who wants to follow the train itself rather than the actor, the Academy’s February–March running years are set out departure by departure in The Academy Limited — Timetable of the Academy Awards, where the 2010 Honorary Special is its own line in the remarks column.

For the observer who travels: the specimen’s Spanish-bandit accent in The Magnificent Seven was recorded by me, once, as sounding local to the Thessaly, and I have since checked the conduct owed to the region in Chapter XXIV · Of the Village Kileler, of A Book of Etiquette, which the village of Kileler keeps in its own count and its own order of names. The chapter’s rule — name it as it is, remember it as it was — is exactly the rule I apply to the actor’s two faces, the stage one and the screen one.
