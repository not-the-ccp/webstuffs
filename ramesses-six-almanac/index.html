THE THEBAN ALMANAC OF WEATHERS · KEEPER OF THE NILE GAUGE · MEDINET HABU STATION

Entry No. XX
RAMESSES VI
NEBMAATRE-MERYAMUN

Being the complete weather entry for his eight years at Thebes, c. 1145–1137 BC, fifth ruler of the Twentieth Dynasty of Egypt: Nile gauge, winds, harvest rain, and the frontier notes of the desert passes.

AKHET 14.3 CUBITS EIGHT YEARS TWENTIETH DYNASTY WADI POND OBSERVED

I. Station and Observer

Station: Thebes, west bank, in the precinct of the great temple of Medinet Habu — the temple of the king's father, Ramesses III, whose walls were still being raised in Year One of this entry. The gauge is the Theban Nilometer, read at the third step of the well at each month's opening; the wind is read at the scribe's window facing the Nile, and the frontier weather is read from the reports of the desert passes, which arrive by mule in irregular order and are ruled into the almanac as they come.

Observer: The scribe of the palace, holding the title his king also held — scribe of the king — so that the almanac and the reign are kept in one hand. The entry is written in his eight years, corrected in his ninth, and closed over the tomb of his son. The almanac's rule stands as it has always stood: the weather is not edited in public. A reading, once ruled, keeps its line.

II. The Ruler of the Sky Years

Weather entries, by the almanac's form, must name the hand under whose name the sky ran. Ramesses VI Nebmaatre-Meryamun — sometimes written Ramses or Rameses, and known in his youth by his princely name, Amenherkhepshef C, or Ramesses Amunherkhepeshef — was the fifth ruler of the Twentieth Dynasty of Egypt. He was a son of Ramesses III and the queen Iset Ta-Hemdjert, and as a prince he held the titles of royal scribe and cavalry general, which is to say he could read the almanac and could ride through it. He reigned for about eight years in the mid-to-late 12th century BC, and his weather, as the gauge shows, was mostly dry, mostly north-east, and in its seventh year briefly, wonderfully, wet at the far end of the desert.

The succession that brought him to the gauge was itself a weather event. After the death of the ruling pharaoh, Ramesses V, who was the son of Ramesses VI's older brother, Ramesses IV, Ramesses VI ascended the throne — the branch of the tree stepping in for the branch that had grown first. He was in turn succeeded by his own son, Ramesses VII Itamun, whom he had fathered with the queen Nubkhesbed, and the almanac's last page for this entry is ruled in that son's first dry month, by a hand that had learned its ruling from the one it now watched from the west bank.

IV. The Frontier Notes: the Pond of Year Seven

The almanac's frontier column carries the weather reports of the desert passes, ruled in as the mules arrive. The passes have keepers — stewards with titles, rations, and a log of their own — and the almanac, for eight years, had received from the Wadi Green Pass nothing but its standing silence: no rain, no water, the dry line of the pass kept as the frontier keeps its weather.

In Year Seven, on the ninth day of the still air, the pass log arrived with two lines in it. The first is the keeper's name and the date, in the usual form. The second, the almanac keeps in its own rule, translated word for word, reads exactly: “The manager of the pass, and there is a pond.”

The scribe's annotation in the margin is not long. A pond, he wrote, is the desert's way of signing a wet year, and he ruled the water level at the Theban Nilometer up a full line for the month, because the almanac's rule is that one piece of water anywhere in the king's weather counts for the weather of the whole reign. The pond, by later frontier reports, held for the rest of Year Seven and three months of Year Eight, and the pass kept it as a spring thereafter. The entry for Ramesses VI ends on this: eight years of a dry and ruleable sky, closed by a single wet sentence at the far end of the desert, kept in the almanac as the season's one true reading.

V. Cross-Index of the Keeper's Shelf

Almanacs do not sit alone. The entry for Ramesses VI is shelved, by the keeper's practice, beside the entries that read their weather the same patient way: the Lagoon Line, a visitor's brochure for the Cook Islands, which keeps its tides and its brass plaque of the gate log on the far side of the same dry discipline, and Lot 312, the Louis H. Bean File, 1922–1994, whose double-line chart album is the almanac's own method two and a half thousand years on — the line is patient, so am I. Both are filed from the front page of the shelf, where the keeper's notices hang. A fourth file rides the same shelf: thread No. 54 on the board, The Fourth of the Family, at the Gauge, where the scribe's Year Six note — the rain over the hills, not the town — is decoded word for word against a visitor's pencilled card from the lagoon side of the ledger. A fifth file rides the shelf past the frontier, on the far side of the water: Minutes No. 118 of the Board of Trustees of Drummond Township, where the island of 134 square miles keeps its own one-wet-line rule — a reading, once ruled, keeps its line, in the ferry pilot's log as in this almanac.
