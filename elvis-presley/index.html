The Thirty-Eighth Spring Sale

To be sold by public auction, without reserve, on Tuesday, the 27th of March, 1979, in the hall behind the drawing room, at one o'clock in the afternoon. All lots are offered as described on this page; the description is the whole of the warranty, and the hammer decides. The catalogue is closed; what follows is Lot 41, of which there are no others, and the lot is one parcel.

Lot 41 The Tupelo-to-Memphis Consignment Elvis Aaron Presley, 1935–1977 · one parcel, four items, one drawer

Provenance of the Drawer

The four items of this lot have kept the same drawer since 1931. The drawer stands at 1341 Laclede Avenue, in a house where its keeper, Miss Etta Rouse, holds it in a room with a church pennant and plays what is in it on request, in order, and no other way. The drawer began with a field holler, three bars of a work song, and a loading chant, all in one hand, left at the foot of the levee on a Sunday in which no boat was coming; the Blue Column of the Mississippi Ledger, page 7, October 9, 1931, keeps the account, and that page, cut out, is item (d) below. Since then the drawer has taken in, one by one, the things of a singer born in Tupelo, Mississippi, on January 8, 1935 — Elvis Aaron Presley, the American singer and actor known to the trade as the King of Rock and Roll and widely regarded as one of the most culturally significant figures of the twentieth century. His family moved to Memphis, Tennessee, when he was thirteen; his music career began in 1954 at Sun Records with the producer Sam Phillips, who wanted to bring the sound of African-American music to a wider public and got, for his trouble, a performance style at once energetic and sexually provocative, set in a transformative era in race relations, which brought both great success and initial controversy. He died on August 16, 1977. The consignment is offered as found, in the drawer's own order, and the order is not disturbed by the sale.

The Parcel, in the Order of the Drawer

(a)Acetate, 12-inch, 33⅓ r.p.m., Sun Studios, 910 Union Avenue, MemphisNo estimate

A 1954 session disc, the first of the lot and the reason for the lot. The label carries a pencil line in the producer's own hand, set under the running order, and the disc plays the sound Mr. Phillips was after across the whole colour of the town — the holler under the pop, the gospel in the shuffle — with the last three minutes worn to a voice of the same colour as the paper. Plays at the speed marked; the label is sunned; the pencil is legible.

(b)Cannon banjo, one string, home-madeNo estimate

The kind the house band at the Fox Theatre lists on its pay stubs simply as cannon banjo and pays on that account. It left the hands of its maker and first leader, the bandleader Singleton Palmer, when the Dixieland Six broke its second charter; the account of its working life is in Music History, Period 4, Form 9R, and the class report rates the man it belonged to as a complete musician. Miss Rouse bought it at the counter on North 21st Street in 1964 for the eleven dollars and thirty-five cents the group's tip jar had held on its final date, which she paid from her own share of a week's walking. It has one string, as found; it will hold a note; it is not offered for repair.

(c)Paint schedule, Graceland, Memphis, 1956No estimate

For the spring repaint, in the housekeeper's hand: porch vermillion, rail cream, gate black, and one line struck through and rewritten in a steadier stroke. At the foot of the schedule the painter has written one sentence, which this house sets here word for word: If the schedule were made to render them white. His reason follows on the next line — the whole front, he writes, would have stood like the sleeve of a 78 — and the family let it stay coloured, and so it has stayed, and so it is offered. The sheet is creased where it kept the drawer's corner and otherwise sound.

(d)Newspaper clipping, The Mississippi Ledger, St. Louis, October 9, 1931No estimate

Page 7, the Blue Column, the whole of it cut round at the edges and pasted on stiff board: the wanted notice for a walking bass for the sixteen-date October run through Cairo, the Fox small-hall rate card at forty dollars a week, the Hollis & Sons liquor schedule set word for word as the line came over the wire, and the low string from London. It is the oldest paper in the drawer and the one that started the keeping. Pasted board, mild foxing, one crease, all letters legible; it hangs by the drawer and comes with it.

Conditions of Sale, as Applied to This Lot

The lot is one parcel: it is offered together, sold together, and cannot be split at the block, because the drawer is the point of it. The items are sold as found and as described, the description being the whole of the warranty; the house will not speak to the quality of a session disc, the repair of a one-string instrument, the colour of a porch, or the legibility of a clipping, and the buyer is expected to have looked. The hammer fell on the second call, at a figure the house keeps in its book and does not print. The consignment left the hall in one cloth bag, the order undisturbed, and is now with its buyer; the hall, the drawer, and this page hold the account. The front page of this shelf files the catalogue in its place among the other things worth keeping, and the reader who would know the drawer's first four contents should begin with the Blue Column itself, where the Sunday at the foot of the levee is set down in the column's form.

Entered by the house's clerk, weighed by the house's scale, and closed on the 27th of March, 1979, the third year after the King went out of the trade and the second year after the drawer had taken in its last of the Memphis papers. The hammer is the house's; the rest was kept.
