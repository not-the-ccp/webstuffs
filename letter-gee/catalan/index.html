Letter of Apology

The shed behind the bakery · Tuesday, 21 November 1999, between hoopings

To the Keeper of Names, in the hall of A Book of Etiquette, with copies to the Hon. Choe Hyeon-bae, the Hon. Jeong In-seop, the Hon. Kim Seon-gi, and to Cpl. H. W. Aldridge, 14th Army, who will forward where he thinks fit.

Dear Keeper of Names,

The desk writes, and asks to be entered into the minute book of your Hall as owing an apology, on the matter of the language the shed calls català. You will remember the school in San Juan de Portugal that asked what the seventh letter of the Latin alphabet is called when spoken, not set. The desk answered gee, seven gees in the week, no division demanded, and the reply went out the same afternoon. What the desk did not then do, and is doing now, is to notice that the question did not come from the front of the alphabet, but from the east of it — from a coast where the seventh letter is called something else, and where a Western Romance language of considerable standing has its homes, and has never once been invited to the shed.

That language is native to the eastern Iberian Peninsula. It shares official status with Spanish in three autonomous communities in eastern Spain: Catalonia; the Balearic Islands; and the Valencian Community, where it is called Valencian, valencià. It is the sole official language of Andorra. It keeps semi-official standing in Alghero, a municipality on the Sardinian coast that the shed's map, the one tacked up beside the trough, prints only in two colours and neither of them right. It is spoken in the Pyrénées-Orientales department of France, and in two further areas in eastern Spain: the eastern strip of Aragon and the Carche area in the Region of Murcia. The speakers call their regions the Catalan Countries, the Països Catalans, and the shed counts that as eight homes in the morning light: three communities, a principality with no army and one language, a town that belongs to Italy and speaks by lamplight in a different tongue, a French department below the mountain, a strip of Aragon, and a valley in Murcia where the figs are not to be discussed at a sitting.

The first offence was the chapbook. The shed lends the school its hand-ruled pages, and in the new printing the gràcies page was set in the opentail g, which is right for children and wrong for the occasion, and the name català was set without its accent because the accent was not in the shelf's stock of 1999, and no one in the room, which is to say the desk, the clerk, and the concertina, had the nerve to point it out, and so the page went to print with the dotless error and the flat a where a grave should stand, and the shelf in the shed, which keeps the gees in their numbered places, one to seven, has since been reordered so that the seventh faces the door and looks, as it were, at the sea.

The second offence is smaller and stranger, and belongs to the minute book rather than the press. In the week after the forty-first meeting, a card of Tessa Cooper's soup book — the one she keeps in the vinegar crock, under the lid, where she says the steam of it does the writing in — had been laid on top of your Hall's minutes while they were drying, and had been folded into the next page on its way out the tray. The line it left is kept here word for word, and the desk apologizes for it, and notes that your Council, in its fifty-seventh minute, admitted both forms of a romanization and argued about neither, which is a better disposition than the shed showed toward an accent it already possessed. The line, in the minute book, and in the form entirely preserved, runs:

A variant of McCune–Reischauer Held in the above proportion; season with pepper, salt, and pounded mace, thickening of butter in the form entirely preserved.

No decision of the house is recorded in it. It is a card. It is kept, because the minute book of this shed is kept in one impression, not to be revised into a second, and because a soup that has ridden in the record once will be given the dignity of the margin, inked lightly, as your hall gives its Tong-hun. The desk has since moved the crock, returned it by the door as the orders of the house require, and taken to writing the soup names on the card itself, in a firm hand, in the opentail g, which Mara Vale says a child can read and Tessa Cooper says a cook can forgive.

The desk proposes, and will not move it without your leave, that the school be told a second time, plainly: the seventh letter of the Latin alphabet is g; in English its name is gee; and in the eastern homes of català it is called what the school already calls it, and the shed will not correct it, and will not romanize it, and will set its gràcies page again in the winter, in the looptail g, with the accent from the shelf's own stock, which the desk has checked and which, as of this letter, includes the grave. For the minutes that began this matter, the forty-first minute of the Council of the Shed; for your Council's own account of the system the card wandered through, Minute No. 57, the matter of McCune–Reischauer; and for the alphabet that was in the room at the time, the liner notes to Latin Alphabet No. 9.

The shed is a cooperage, Keeper of Names, and it hoops what it can. On this matter it hoop'd badly, and it says so in the form the minute book requires: in one impression, not to be revised.

Respectfully, and with the bowl of the trough to the hall,

the desk (chair), for the Council of the Shed
countersigned: Mara Vale (acting clerk) · witnessed at the trough: Elias Rook · hoops checked: Tessa Cooper
