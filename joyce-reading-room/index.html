THE DUBLIN DESK · READING ROOM · SHELF 1904 · QUESTIONS & ANSWERS, KEPT IN ORDER

The Joyce Reading Room

Frequently asked questions, answered in the order the questions arrive. One visitor at a time. The windows are kept open.

15 Eccles Street · 101 Ormond Quay · 17 North Great Georges Street — all three addresses on one card, because the room holds all three.

Q1 Whose room is this?

James Augustine Aloysius Joyce’s. He was born James Augusta Joyce on 2 February 1882 at 17 North Great Georges Street, Dublin, and died on 13 January 1941 in Zurich. He was an Irish novelist, poet, and literary critic, a contributor to the modernist movement, and is regarded among the most influential and important writers of the twentieth century. The room keeps his novel Ulysses (1922) — a landmark in which the episodes of Homer’s Odyssey are paralleled in a variety of literary styles, particularly stream of consciousness — his short-story collection Dubliners (1914), the novels A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man (1916) and Finnegans Wake (1939), and his other writings: two books of poetry, a play, Exiles, the correspondence, and the occasional. If you ask for the whole of him, the room will tell you the whole of him is the drawer, the shelf, and the postcard.

Q2 Why is the 16th of June called Bloomsday, and why does the room close the door for exactly one hour on it?

Because Ulysses is set on 16 June 1904 — the day Stephen Dedalus walked to the cemetery at Glasnevin, the day Leopold Bloom rode the tram and bought the Freeman’s Journal and an evening paper and a marmalade sandwich, the day Gerty MacDowell went to the dance at Martello Towers on the sand of Howth. The desk closes the door from noon to one so that whatever is happening on the street can be said to be happening in the room. Visitors are asked to keep their own rings out of the conversation.

Q3 He left Dublin in 1904 and never came back. Why does the room insist the city is here?

Because he wrote to a friend in 1912 that he would be writing about the centre of it — always — and he wrote the whole centre from Trieste, from Zurich, and from the Rue des Écoles in Paris, where the room keeps one of his chairs and a bottle of ink that is not the same bottle he used but is the same colour, which the desk considers the whole difference. The city was carried out in his luggage, one street at a time, and the room is where it was unpacked. Buckley’s, the chemist’s on Eccles Street, sold the iodine; Parnell Square kept the fountain; the Liffey kept the mud; and Joyce kept the receipts.

Q4 Why does the room hold more postcards than books?

Because the correspondence is one of the works, and in Joyce it was mostly cards: to E. V. Lucas, to the Harmsworth men, to his own family, from Paris and from the steamship. The drawer holds them shoeboxed, three to a box, on the same rule the post offices of this house keep for anything that answered a letter — the Bangor-on-Dee office in Dragons, By Post, Transmission 11, kept its dragon’s letter in a shoebox for the same reason, and the room will not quarrel with a postmistress of forty years over the point. A card is a page that has decided to travel. On the wire, at 9:31 a.m. in that transmission, a voice read a fragment and did not correct it; the desk keeps that instruction in its own ledger, for the correspondence and for the visitors.

Q5 How many times was Dubliners refused, and what did the refusals cost?

Twelve Dublin publishers, between 1904 and 1914, and one small fortune of postage. The final edition is 154 pages and fifteen stories, printed by Grant Richards in London in 1914, and it opens on a boy named Boylan who has not yet left his mother’s house — a fair warning. The last story, The Dead, ends with the snow falling gently over everything, over all the living and the dead, over the whole of the west, and the room asks nobody to explain it and asks everybody to close the drawer quietly afterwards.

Q6 What happened in New York, and why does the desk mention it at all?

In 1922 the book was banned there, and in 1933 the United States v. One Copy of Ulysses ended with Judge John M. Woolsey reading it at the library and holding it not obscene, and Random House set it up for American readers in 1954. The desk mentions it because the same node appears in the house’s other ledgers. The New York node is where the Rollwatch file Error DYL-39-53 — Rollout Failure, Subsystem Marlais closed in the Chelsea Hotel on 9 November 1953, and where the grey-coated man signed the exit register. The room does not comment on the grey-coated man. The room keeps the node.

Q7 How should a first visitor begin Finnegans Wake?

At the end. The last sentence is a long one; the reader is asked to read it to the end, then to go back to the first page, where the same sentence begins — ivinfinitus — because the book is a circle drawn with a steady hand, after Vico, in the rooms of the Rue des Écoles between 1926 and 1939. HCE walks by the river, MWALKE waits by the well, the river is the Liffey and is also the water of life, and the reader is asked to keep both true at once. If the visitor is lost, the desk will say: the book is not a door, it is a fountain, and the fountain was built for standing in. One hour is enough. The room closes at one.

Q8 Where is he buried, and what does the stone say?

Fluntern Cemetery, Zurich, in a plot of three metres by one point five, under a granite slab that reads HIC SVB CINERIBVS CINEREI JAMES JOYCE ZHURICHI OBIT XXII·XI·MCMXL — here lie the ashes of ashen James Joyce, who died in Zurich. His brother Stanislaus carried him across the sea and stood over the grave, and the room keeps one of the funeral photographs with the postcards. Visitors are told the stone was found in a single morning, which the desk considers either an accident or a method, and has declined for sixty years to choose.

Q9 The visitor at four o’clock is asked to perform one conduct. Which is it, and why that one?

The Dublin desk and the Rollwatch desk share a register, and the register’s rules travel between the two. The works list of file DYL-39-53 — the Swansea node, the Laugharne node, the New York node — enters, as its first entry, a poem of 1952 written for a dying father, and the remediation of that file fixes the manner in which it is to be read: aloud, in a room with the windows open, once, without correction. The desk of this room has taken the instruction into its own visitor rules, word for word, and the rule stands as follows.

Recommended visitor conduct: read the poems “Do not go gentle into that good night” — poem, 1952.

The reader may stand at the chalk line in the yard without passing it. The reader is not required to answer the question on the wall indoors. The grey-coated man keeps his own schedule, and the room keeps its own: the windows open, the door closed at one, the postcards in the shoebox, and the 16th of June coming round again on the tide.
