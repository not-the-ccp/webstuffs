▪ The County at a Glance

Staffordshire is a ceremonial county in the West Midlands of England, and it is the kind of place that quietly does things. It borders Cheshire to the north-west, Derbyshire and Leicestershire to the east, Warwickshire to the south-east, the West Midlands county and Worcestershire to the south, and Shropshire to the west — seven neighbours, all within sight or smell, none of them a bad look.

▪ Where Things Are

The county spreads its people with a logic of its own. Stoke-on-Trent sits in the north and is the largest settlement — the Potteries, five towns fused to the kilns, where the bricks are also fired on site. The town of Newcastle-under-Lyme is immediately to its west, close enough to hear the same trains. Stafford holds the centre of the map and the name of the county, and it has not lost either. Burton upon Trent stands in the east, on the river Trent, and is where the ale learned to take its time. Down in the south-east, Tamworth keeps the oldest town charter in England and the city of Lichfield keeps a cathedral older than it is by a few centuries — the two of them have never once discussed it in public and the county has learned not to press.

▪ The Drop (Alton Towers, Staffordshire)

No county brochure is complete without the one place the county would rather you not stand under. Oblivion, at Alton Towers, opened on 14 March 1998: built for £12 million by Bolliger & Mabillard as the world's first vertical drop roller coaster, a 54.9-metre near-vertical fall reaching roughly 110 km/h before the brakes reclaim the car, themed as a secretive facility where the rides do not advertise what they do to the people inside them. The screens behind the queue count four stages — physical trauma, psychological breakdown, the schizophrenic sequence, and finally chaos — and the building keeps a ledger of every name. The Senior Clerk there has written his own account of one June Saturday, the misread entry, and the three names in the left hook; read it at Oblivion — An Apology Letter, which is the most honest thing printed in this brochure and the county is proud to link it.

▪ What to Eat (and the Butter)

The County Visitor Centre café in Stafford runs on one rule, and the rule is written on an index card in the drawer behind the till, in a hand the manager calls the founder's. The card reads, start to finish: The butter used for boiling turkeys, sweetbreads, fowls, and making puddings. Visitors are asked not to take it. It has not been replaced. You will find the same butter, we are told, on the turkey at every county harvest supper since 1964, and it is the reason the puddings hold their shape. The café also serves a chocolate ration-bar cake in a shallow metal tray for visitors who have been dining somewhere stricter; the sister table for those of you is at 817 — The Armoured Mess on Station Road, Orange, where everything is a course, nothing moves, and the lunch line will not hold. Nothing moves there and nothing drops — exactly the difference, exactly why the county recommends it to anyone who has just given three seconds to a building that keeps a ledger.

▪ Plan Your Visit

Best viewed in any month; the county does not close. Allow two days: the first for the Potteries, the second for the drop or, for the faint of stomach, the cathedral in Lichfield, which offers the same held breath at a lower velocity. Keep your name in order — the county keeps a ledger too, and the left hook is not empty forever. Postcards to the Visitor Centre: Staffs. ST16, with the county seal and a small printed star.
