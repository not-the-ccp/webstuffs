Afrikaans

One language, eleven thousand kilometres: from the Cape Table to the Patagonian steppe, and the lamp at every stop is forty watts

Afrikaans is a West Germanic language spoken in South Africa and Namibia, and in lesser measure across the veld to Botswana, Zambia and Zimbabwe — and then, improbably, in Argentina, where a community in the Patagonian town of Sarmiento still keeps the dialect the settlers brought in the 1950s, and among the South African diaspora in Australia, New Zealand, North America and the United Kingdom. It emerged, as a recognized literary and public language, in the late 19th century out of the Dutch dialects of the settlers who had spoken in South Africa since the 17th century — chiefly Hollandic and Zeelandic — fused with the Dutch-based creole languages of the Khoisan, Nguni and Coloured peoples of the Cape. Today it is the third most spoken home language in South Africa, after isiZulu and Xhosa, out of the country's twelve official languages: nearly 17 per cent of the country, seven million home speakers, and a phrasebook that fits in a coat pocket.

Route 1 · The Mother City & the Cape · 0–340 km

Begin where the Dutch first said Ja

Start in Cape Town — Die Moederstad, the Mother City — where the 1652 settlement put Zeelandic and Hollandic on the table mountains. The language's early life is in the city: the old harbour at the V&A Waterfront, the Bo-Kaap, where the Cape Malay and Cape Dutch houses are painted in the old colours and where Afrikaans, Malay, and Portuguese share one street. On the City Bowl side, the Groote Kerk at the foot of Company's Garden is where the Voortrekker hymn was sung and the language's literary cause was argued; the University of Cape Town, across the slopes, was the first to grant a degree written in Afrikaans. Budget two days. Eat a bunny chow at the market on Bree Street, order braaivleis when it comes to the fire, and let the word lekker — delicious, lovely, all of it — do half the guidebook's work for you.

Route 2 · The Veld Circuit · 340–1,150 km

Winelands, Sutherland, and the harbour at Saldanha

The language travels inland the way the light does. The Winelands, an hour east of the Cape — Stellenbosch, Franschhoek, Paarl — are where the 17th-century Huguenot and Dutch farm names still carry the land. Push west to Saldanha Bay, the navy harbour with its anchor yards and its pines, where the harbour office keeps a visitor book in three columns: name, vessel, weather. Then north up N2 to Sutherland, the so-called big-sky town in the Klein Karoo, where the veld runs flat enough to lose the horizon and the Afrikaans you hear is the wide open one — no city accent left in it. The circuit is 1,150 kilometres by the back roads, and it is the country the language was built in.

Route 3 · Over the Atlantic · Sarmiento, Santa Cruz

The Patagonian dialect, 11,000 kilometres on

Sarmiento, on the Río Santa Cruz in Argentine Patagonia, is the farthest post on the route. The town's British founding of 1857 came first — sheep stations, the Pampa de los Naranjos — and the South African families arrived from the 1950s onward, farmers following the sheep trade, and they brought the language with them. It survives here as a Patagonian dialect, thinned by the steppe wind but intact in the kitchens and the schoolroom, the only pocket of Afrikaans on the American continent. The town's one old petrol station doubles as the meeting place; ask for the braai and you will be understood. The nearest weather report is a week old and that is part of the charm.

Route 4 · The Northern & Diaspora Posts

Windhoek, Ballarat, Christchurch, Montreal, Bristol

Namibia is the second full stop on the map: Afrikaans is a recognized language of the state, and Windhoek's streets, the Old Location, and the railway station still speak it in the same hands it was written in. Then the diaspora posts, smaller but steady: Ballarat and Melbourne in Australia, where the 1948 footballers and their descendants kept a club and a chapel; Christchurch in New Zealand, whose 1955 South African rugby tour left a community with a sports ground and a recipe book; Montreal in North America, where the community founded a school in 1975 and has run one ever since; and Bristol in the United Kingdom, the old merchant port with its own society and its annual dinner. Each post keeps the same customs: a church or a hall, one long table, a hymn in the old language, and — the desk notes with respect — a lamp at forty watts, exactly the wattage the moth's lamp holds at No. 14 Kruiskade in Paramaribo, where the Surinamese evening that outlived the country is still being read. That obituary, Suriname — Obituary, The Paramaribo Weekly, is filed in the same drawer as this entry: Dutch was the island's language, and Afrikaans is its near cousin, and the two keep each other company the way two languages in the same family do.

From thence it is a record kept in the great anchor, what a harpoon down a pine tree.

Pocket section · The phrasebook that fits a coat

Eight words, one fire

A language is a route you can walk in. These eight carry you anywhere on the circuit: Welkom (welcome), dankie (thank you), ja / nee (yes / no), totsiens (goodbye — literally "until seen"), reg (right, correct, on the way), lekker (good in the way of food, weather, and weather in food), braai (the fire, the meal, and the afternoon around the meal), and baas (boss — also, of the country itself, in the affectionate plural). A book courier carrying the wrong alphabet will not get far on any of these routes — the language keeps its own letters — and for what a city does to keep them when the border says otherwise, walk The Press Ban Walk in Vilnius: seven stops through the thirty-nine years of the press ban, 1865–1904, from the Pilies gate couriers to the morning of 24 April 1904.
