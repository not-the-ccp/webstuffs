La Reraguarda

“The women who kept the rear watch kept the front line fed, clothed, and supplied with news.”

Spring issue · printed on the club’s small press, 42 copies · postpaid if you have a stamp

On the section’s hundredth anniversary, a reading season

Members: you have been patient with us while the archive committee argued about the correct spelling of the word reraguarda, and the season has begun whether we spell it right or not. This year we read around one organization, and a great one: La Dona a la Reraguarda, “the woman in the rear”, a section of the Comissariat de Propaganda of the Generalitat de Catalunya during the war of 1936 to 1939, whose entire purpose was to channel the help that women could give to the combatants at the front. For a hundred years the phrase has travelled through the town like an old knife — sharp, and everybody’s grandmother’s.

The names we will pin above the reading bench: Carme Ballester i Llasat, wife of president Lluìs Companys, who accepted the honorary presidency of the section; Anna Gil, who sat on its Board as president of the board itself, a post that sounds like a trick question; and Maria Baldó i Massanet, general secretary, close to the Republican Left of Catalonia, who ran the paperwork with the kind of order that makes other secretaries nervous. The section ran delegations in a good many Catalan towns; the four our members will adopt are Premià de Mar, where we sit, Premià de Dalt, up the road where the terraces are, Arbùcies, far over in the Montseny, and L’Hospitalet de Llobregat, across the water in the industrial flat of the delta. Each member takes one delegation and reports back in May, by letter or by voice, in that order.

The hall, the corner, the festival

A note for anyone joining us for the first time. The club occupies the ground floor of the old tinsmith’s house on Carrer de la Riera, one room, three windows, a wood stove that only obeys when it is cold enough to deserve obedience. The room houses the library (two shelves, one of them a ladder that does not lean on anything), the reading bench for nine, and the archive drawer that only Teresa opens. It also holds a spring literature festival held in the corner — yes, the corner, the one by the stove — where from the first Saturday of April, four authors a year will sit on the club’s worst chair and read for two hours at a time. The chair is included. Last spring, the corner seat held a poet from L’Hospitalet who read a piece about a canteen kitchen, and nobody in the room moved until the second reading was over. Tickets are twelve euros at the door, or a jar of peaches in May, or a story about someone’s mother in the war, whichever the treasurer likes best.

The bench-slip is not a syllabus; it is a suggestion in ink. Members are free to read anything, but the delegation’s name must appear in the minutes, and the minutes must mention one piece of clothing by name — this is the club rule since 1961, and we do not say why.

Correspondence

We received, from a member writing out of Mirandola, a long letter comparing our rear-watch to the nine-bite tasting plate served at the Osteria dei Novecento: nine small things laid out so each one carries its own weight, which, the letter says, is exactly how a supply section worked, only the plate came out of the kitchen on time and the ration did not. The committee has asked that no more food be mentioned in letters about supply sections. We also received word from the friend on the Costa Aberta forum, who is off to the festa at Pedro do Rósario in June: the square there rehearse all night and nobody keeps the rear watch, so to speak, which is to say everyone does, drum in hand. He will send farinha and possibly a tambor; if a tambor arrives, the festival corner gets a percussionist. And before the May bench, Susanna P. went up to Barcelona on the 11th of September and sent back a diary of the day in which she counted the 43 comarques off to someone by letter and watched a market blackbird pay for a half fig in stivers — so the minutes already have their one piece of clothing by name, and it is a faix. One last line from the bench, not a letter: after her delta week, Teresa F. sat down at The Gauhati High Court, the dining room in the 1948 courthouse annexe, ate all four of its state-named rooms, and reported back that a supply section is just a dining room that keeps the other rooms in mind — the full review is on the site, and the committee has ruled it does not count as food mentioned in a letter about supply sections, which she is choosing to believe. One last object in the drawer, not a letter either: a record sleeve Teresa brought back from across the water, liner notes for a field recording of a single night on Chappaquiddick Island off Martha’s Vineyard in 1969 — a party ending at 11:15 p.m., a dirt road, a one-lane bridge, a pond that keeps the island at a distance for the rest of the night, and a ferry master who reads his tide gauge twice a day — the committee has entered it under Poucha Pond in the drawer's log, and rules that the full listening of it, side one to the end, counts as one evening’s bench reading.

Business

The general meeting is the second Thursday of May, 19:30, in the hall; the agenda is three lines long and one of them is about the ladder. The club’s books show eleven members, nine active, two in correspondence. The subscription is what you bring to the door and call your share. The honorary presidency of the club is, as it has been since the beginning, held in absentia and in gratitude, and this year the name above it is the name of the section we are reading: La Dona a la Reraguarda. The rear is not the back of the line. The rear is the line, seen from the other side. Write to us, read to us, and if you are in town on the first Saturday of April, the chair by the stove is yours.
