Of the Lot

One piece of green oak, cut 91 by 28 by 5½ centimetres, taken in 1933 from the north rail of a long bench and left, on purpose, unseasoned, the way a certain law in a certain workshop on the south side of a certain river has required of necks and rails and anything else worth keeping since 1783 — cut thick, and left a full year to weep. The weeping was never finished. The piece is therefore still, in the wood’s own accounting, one year old, and the firm has priced it accordingly, which is to say not at all, and then at once, which is what prices do to things with histories.

The bench to which the piece belongs was the bench of the first meeting of the American Civil Liberties Union, held on 12 December 1920, the year the Union was founded, when a room of lawyers, writers and agitators — Roger Baldwin at the head of the table, Crystal Eastman, Morris Ernst, Arthur Garfield Hays, Elizabeth Gurley Flynn, Lillian Wald, and the rest of the nearly five hundred who had come to be told what a civil liberty was and what it cost — sat on it for the better part of an evening and got to work. That was the whole of the furniture the founding asked for: one bench, one table, and the room. The bench has done a great deal more sitting since. It held the 1929 delegation that went to Washington over the Sedition Act; it held the 1947 room that argued, from a secularist stance, that the United States government had been getting too much of the wrong sort of company, and that excessive entanglement is a state a government falls into the way a floor falls into water; and it held the 1954 season, when the Union’s briefs were going out of the building every day and the bench was too small for all of them, which was the first honest argument in its favour.

The Union itself outgrew every bench on earth. Founded in 1920 as a single room of people who had seen what the law does in an emergency, it is now a nonprofit civil rights organisation with affiliates active in all 50 states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico, and a budget which, as of 2024, stands at $383 million — a number this catalogue prints in full because the piece on the trestle is small, and the thing it belonged to is not. Its work is the work the bench was built for: the Union provides legal assistance in cases where it considers civil liberties at risk, and the assistance takes one of two forms, and only two — direct legal representation, where the Union’s own counsel stands in the box, or the preparation of amicus curiae briefs expressing legal arguments when another law firm is already providing representation. Both forms have been sitting on this bench, and the grain is worn down where the elbows went.

The bench itself did not survive. It was cut up in the stockroom dispersal of the winter of 1933, the sections going to bookbinders and to coffin-makers, the Union’s own file declining, to this day, to say which section went to which; only the section that carried the carved law was set aside and put in the room at Fourth & Hill, for reasons the stockroom log does not give, the log having been kept, the firm understands, by the same hand that kept a certain column in a certain evening newspaper and knowing the value of a thing no one else will value. It has waited ninety-two years in that room, where names are still read out one at a time, the way the Union reads them into the record. When other visitors are announced, it is offered now as a cut piece of green oak.

Of the Provenance, by the years

  • 1920 The bench built, green and thick, by a Los Angeles cabinet-maker whose father had learned the green-oak law at a cello workshop on Jermyn Street, where the law was cut in 1783, by a dying cellist’s own instruction, into a piece of the same wood — a sheet of that law is kept in this miscellany, in the funeral notice and obituary of Giacobbe Cervetto (1682–1783), and the workshop keeps the carved original above the bench where the necks are sawn.
  • 12 Dec. 1920 The first meeting of the American Civil Liberties Union, the bench holding its weight for the first time, the room on record as full.
  • 9 Nov. 1931 In this same room, a red ledger, cloth cover, water-damaged at the lower corner, is last seen in a jacket that was not, on inspection, the jacket of any member of the department. It is wanted still, in the same patient column — the Small Ads Column of the Los Angeles Evening Ledger, 11 November 1931, kept by order of the Red Squad and signed at the bottom by R.H. and L.M.L., the two men who now run this sale. This lot is consigned in its place.
  • Winter 1933 The stockroom dispersal. The bench cut. One piece set aside, unseasoned, the count of the case deliberately kept unbalanced, in the manner of a gift that is still to be found.
  • 1947 The Union’s briefs on the separation of church and state leave the building; the bench, in the corridor, holds the printers’ crates.
  • 1954 The amicus season. The bench is too small. The room is not.
  • 2024 The Union’s budget, as of this year’s own reckoning, $383 million. The piece on the trestle weighs 19 kilos. The ratio is entered in the catalogue because the catalogue is the kind of book that enters ratios.
  • 14 Nov. 2025 Lot 48. The hammer goes at 2:47 p.m. The piece leaves the room in a crate stencilled with a number the size of a man, its count kept deliberately unbalanced.

Condition Report

Unseasoned, on purpose, since 1933; the timber is still working, which is to say still becoming, and the firm’s condition man has noted that the piece has not once cracked, which he described, in the log, as “improper for a piece of wood and appropriate for a piece of law.” The carved line on the rail is legible in full under a raking light and reads: CUT THICK. LEFT A FULL YEAR TO WEEP. The lower corner is water-damaged. The stockroom log records, without further comment, that the damage is the same water, the same corner, as the damage on a red ledger which is still wanted; the firm does not confirm this and does not deny it, which is the only position available to a firm that has spent ninety years on the wrong side of that one question. One moth-hole, lower rail, 6 millimetres, through the letter w of weep; the word is still legible, the moth is not identified, and the firm will say only that it is not the moth that troubles the trade, which is a moth kept on file in this same miscellany and is not this moth. The green-oak law itself has one working student in this miscellany: the builder of a 1:16 scale diorama of a Woodland period longhouse village, who bought his framing oak unseasoned and on purpose, and kept the law word for word in a build log of his own — the Meadowcroft Bench, entries 1 to 9, at the log of his bench and its first year of weeping. A faint impression of a khaki necktie, upper rail, not removed, not explained. The piece is offered as found, wet or otherwise.

Notes from the Firm

Bidders are reminded that the firm does not catalogue motives, only lots, and that Lot 48 is the one lot of Catalogue C on which the reserve was met by a telephone call from the Pacific side at 11:04 in the morning, nine hours and forty-three minutes before the hammer, and that the firm has declined to say whether the caller knew what the piece was, which the firm’s two partners, in their respective corners of the Crescent Room, have both described, separately and in the same words, as “the only question in the room.” The buyer’s name is withheld; the buyer’s crate is on the dock; the count of the case is not closed. For the shelf where this catalogue is kept — among the auction lots, the funeral sheets, the almanacs and the columns of small advertisements that hold one another up — see the front page of Webstuffs, where the green oak and the red ledger lie on the same shelf and do not, the shelf’s keeper reports, appear to be the same water. The firm thanks the Union, which has not commented and is not expected to; the Union, the firm notes, has affiliates in all 50 states, Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico, and one of them, on the morning of the sale, was heard on the radio in the room, which is how these things get into the catalogue.
