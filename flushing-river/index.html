The Creek, Northbound

by the Flushing Septet — with Althea Reyes on vocal

MCR·114 · recorded October 3–5, 1958, at the old Bayside firehouse · released June 1959

MEADOWS & CORONA MCR·114 SIDE A

A-side label, as pressed. The disc runs the length of the creek, head to mouth.

Notes on the record

This album was recorded in the autumn of 1958, over three nights, on either bank of the Flushing River — the water the locals still call Flushing Creek — a little waterway that runs north through the borough of Queens in New York and empties into the Flushing Bay and the East River. The band, then a sextet of brass, reeds and two drums with Reyes added as the seventh chair, had been told by the label that a river is too thin a subject for a long player. What follows is the answer, played at a tempo the engineers kept describing as “unhurried and legal.” The creek runs mostly within what is now Flushing Meadows–Corona Park, and it divides Queens into its western and eastern halves, which is to say the record divides itself the same way: Side A belongs to the towns on the right bank, Flushing and the old mills on it; Side B to the western shore and to what the water lost.

The singers should be understood before the tracks. Althea Reyes was, as the title track insists, the seventh — the seventh member to join the ensemble, signed the night before the first take, and the only one who had grown up on the creek, in the house at 3655 Main Street with the windows facing the water the way her father's did before him. The other six played; she remembered.

Side A · the right bank

  1. A1The Valley, Before the Ice5:42

    The band's account of the long valley through which the creek runs, which may have been a much larger riverbed before the last Ice Age. Opens with the tuba under water. The geology is correct; the brass is not.

  2. A2Mill Creek (Left Bank)4:17

    One of the three tributaries that, until the twentieth century, fed the creek on the eastern side, here walked along its old course in the morning before the work crews arrived.

  3. A3Kissena, After Hours6:03

    Reyes's vocal, cut in one take at 2:14 in the morning at the back of the park. She is singing the bar's own history, and at the second chorus she steps off the sheet music entirely. The line is printed here word for word, exactly as the microphone heard it:

    “The bar is famous only for the seventh member, and I ask the Court would reopen.”
    The bar is Coney's, at 3851 Main Street, which was famous for one thing only — that every Thursday the seven of them sat at its long counter in the order they had joined the band. And the Court is the Queens County Court, which in 1936 had ordered Kissena Creek graded, culverted, and paved under the streets; Reyes is asking the bench, from the microphone, to reopen a case the water has never lost. The court never answered. The record keeps asking.

  4. A4Flushing Bay, Low Tide5:58

    Recorded from the seawall at low tide, the water drawn back and the creek's mouth sitting empty in the mud. The two drums keep the tide table; the trumpet keeps the time.

Side B · the western shore

  1. B1Horse Brook (What Remained)4:51

    The third tributary, on the western bank, sung in a minor key because only a fragment of it survives, a culverted run beneath the highway that the surveyors of 1958 measured at eleven feet and called, in their report, “adequate.”

  2. B2The Paving, 19367:12

    The longest track on the record and the one the label refused to single. A reconstruction of the order that closed the tributaries — gavel figure in the snare, the clerk's voice read over the band — ending, on purpose, in the middle of a sentence, the way the creek ends under the pavement.

  3. B3Northward / East River8:30

    The close. All seven chairs at once, the creek finally meeting the bay and the bay meeting the river, and Reyes's last line, which is the whole of the album's argument: the water was here first, and it is not finished going.

Who's on the record

Althea Reyes, vocal (the seventh member); Delmore Vance, trumpet and flugelhorn; Pearl Okafor, alto saxophone; Sandy Delgado, clarinet and tenor saxophone; Ray Bousfield, trombone; Edith Marlow, tuba; Frank & Leo Nystrom, drums (one kit, played in turns). Engineer: M. S. Kessler, from the portable rig on the roof of the firehouse. The band reunited for the 1974 reissue; Reyes did not, and Side A stands as she left it.

From this house's shelves: the same northward patience is kept, at far greater depth, in the Chapter XIV of the House Book of Etiquette, Of the Tablemount & Her Keeper, where a flat floor crossed hemispheres and asks nothing of its visitors. The station at Park Street, Bristol, has also taken a copy of the album's weather log into its book, where the Entry 14 of the Bristol Parliamentary Weather Almanac keeps the pressure record of one long, slow green system, and files it beside the remark that the water was here first and is not finished going. And for the other short careers on wax — figures, mascots, and one thirty-foot hand in Torrance — see the Polygon Man travel entry, whose guestbook counts every visit the way this record counts its members. The rest of the catalogue is shelved at the front page.

And on the forum that keeps the water's argument in its own hand, the Diefenbaker thread of The Prairie Forum preserves a walking tour whose load-bearing sentence — the tour that compresses the centuries, the bar on Fifth Street from 1998, the map that survives the pulping — is read into the record the way Reyes's last line is read here: once, uncorrected, and kept.
