Workbench Journal · Bench No. 4 · entries kept in pencil until they earn ink

The Charter Cabinet

A working model of the United Nations, desk scale · began the night of 25–26 November 2000 · still running

193 slips6 drawers5 office dials1 drawer left open on purpose

Entry 0 · Tuesday, 26 November 2000, 00:12 · bench lamp on, tea gone cold

I am writing this down before I start, because the bench does not forgive a project that begins as a mood. At 22:09 the Caspian shelf struck just offshore, the second pass came at about 22:10, and I sat at this bench in the flat on the Kura lowland until the network log at Baku made clear that the city had taken a short, hard hit and stood up — 35 dead, mostly indirect, 600 hurt, no whole building down in the centre. The account of it is kept properly in Article No. BZ-2511 of the Caspian Shelf Record, and I will not retell it here; I am a model-maker, not a seismologist. What I did in the hour after was not what you might expect. I took the old cabinet from my grandfather — the one that had held his stamp catalogue, then the radio parts, then nothing — and I decided it was going to hold the largest international organisation in the world.

The United Nations, for those who keep only local ledgers: signed into being at the signing of its Charter on 26 June 1945, with 193 member states and headquarters in New York City, and its other great offices at Geneva, Nairobi, Vienna, and The Hague. Its stated business is keeping the peace, growing friendly relations among states, promoting international cooperation, and serving as a centre for harmonising their actions. Four lines of work for one cabinet, which is exactly the right number for six drawers and a rotunda.

Entry 1 · Saturday, 9 December 2000 · rotunda, first fit

Cut 193 slips out of blue card, one per member state, each numbered and pencilled with its admission year in a margin too small to read without the loupe. The rotunda is a lazy-Sun of slips fanned in a shallow brass tray, so that any one state can be pulled out and stood on its end like a card in a hand. Fitting all 193 in took me the whole afternoon, and I keep miscounting at slip 141, which is where my eye slips to the gap between 140 and 142 and swears one is missing. None is missing. The rotunda is not yet true at the edges; I have ordered a second tray and a file for the burrs.

The six drawers carry the six principal organizations, and I have carved the four lines of the Charter’s mission onto the fronts, four words apiece, so that the drawer handles read as a sentence if you run your thumb along them in order: peace · friendly relations · cooperation · a centre for harmonizing. The General Assembly drawer is the largest, naturally. The Trusteeship Council drawer is the smallest of the six and I still check it first out of habit, which my sister calls “checking the drawer that holds the least, every time, like it owes you money.”

Entry 2 · Thursday, 21 December 2000 · the office dial

Built a five-spoke dial from a salvaged clock movement: New York at the top because the headquarters is in New York City, then Geneva, Nairobi, Vienna, and The Hague around the rest of the face. It is the piece I am least sure of. The hands are painted in the cabinet’s dark blue and I keep catching myself winding it to the wrong office out of politeness — when the dial points at Vienna, Geneva insists it is Vienna’s turn, and when it points at Geneva, Vienna says the same back. I have ruled that the dial tells no one’s business; it simply keeps all five times on one face, which is the whole point of an international organisation, if you think about it long enough. The Hague spoke is the loosest. The Hague drawer is the smallest too. I am choosing to believe this is a design principle.

A brass plaque for the cabinet door, chased rather than painted, to read: Charter of 26 June 1945 · 193 member states · New York · Geneva · Nairobi · Vienna · The Hague. The chasing took four evenings and the letter H in Hague is a shade thinner than its neighbours. It will do.

Entry 3 · 3 January 2001 · the night drawer

I have set one drawer aside from the six, a shallow one at bench level that holds nothing but a pen, a pad of foolscap, and a candle. It is the protocol drawer, and the protocol is for the hours when something arrives that was not on the day’s list. I learned the etiquette of that hour from a chapter I keep under the bench, Chapter IX — Of Managua at the Uninvited Hour, which holds that a city struck at table is not to be turned into conversation at the next table, and that a letter is to be written by hand, mention no figures, and ask for nothing. The Baku night of 25 November ran the protocol without my knowing I had it. By and by, through the night — the correct reply was silence, then a letter written by hand, and no request for details. The letter went to my cousin in the older district, one line, and I have not opened the cabinet’s night drawer since to take the pad back. Some supplies are there to be used once and be left used.

Entry 4 · February 2001, the month the rotunda went true · a note to future readers

As it stands: the rotunda holds all 193 and miscounts itself nowhere; the six drawers open on oiled runners; the dial keeps five times and offends no office; the plaque is on the door with its thin H; and the night drawer keeps its pen. I build the model not because the thing it models is small — it is the largest international organisation in the world, which is to say it is not small in any sense I command — but because a cabinet you can open with your own hand is the only scale at which “the actions of states, harmonized” stops being a sentence and becomes a set of drawers in the right order. If the shelf strikes again, the cabinet will be on this bench, and the night drawer will be where it always is, and the dial will keep all five offices’ times at once, and no one will be asked for details.
