Family Receipts of the Abell Household

Entered against shelf 4½ · held beside Chapter IV of the surname book · price nothing, the oven being paid for

Abel’s Loaf

“Hébél is the breath; the loaf is the breath made.”

Abel — Hebrew הֶבֶל Hébél, in pausa הָבֶל Hāđbel, and in the Greek of the household &Halpha;ςel, in the Arabic of the travellers’ shelf هابيل Húbīl — was the second son of Adam and Eve, the first two of the family, and a shepherd. It is enough for a receipt to begin with a shepherd, for a shepherd is a man who understands what it is to offer the first, and to lose it. He kept his flock at the edge of the garden his father was shut out of, and when the day of offering came he brought the firstborn of his flock, the best of the year, and set it down before God. It was accepted. His brother Cain’s offering, of the fruit of the ground, was not, and what passed between the two after that is the first loss this household has ever had to keep. Some later readers say Cain struck him down with a stone; the family has always liked the stone, because the stone is a thing the kitchen can do something with, and a stone is, in the end, a piece of the oven. Abel is the first death in the record and the first of the lost, and the name itself — the breath, the vapour, the thing that will not stay — is exactly the name to put on a loaf, which is nothing but water given the sun and then taken away again.

I. Of the Firsts

The loaf is made only of firsts, and the rule is kept without mercy. It wants three pounds of flour, and that flour must come from the first grinding of the year — the first sack the mill turns over in February, whatever the weather — of which the household holds a standing order at the miller’s, one sack, wholemeal, with the bran left in, because the first grinding is the coarse one. To the flour goes one and a half pounds of rye, two eggs beaten from the first laying, a half pound of butter broken to the size of a walnut, a good spoonful of honey for the offering’s share, a pinch of salt from the first box of the year, and warm water to the measure of a hand closed once. The honey is the share set aside, as Abel set aside the firstborn; no one is to take from it for the tea. The household that bakes the loaf on a day of plenty is the household the ledger is always warning against, and the warning is entered in the next section, for it is the only line in the book of receipts that the clerk could not make whole and has therefore kept as it stands.

II. Of the Ledger Line

On the last leaf of the old kitchen ledger, in the hand of Mrs. Arunah Abell, niece of the publisher of that name and keeper of the book from 1902 to 1941, the method opens with a line the family has never been able to set right. The clerk of the surname chapter has ruled it once already, in the matter of the blackboard and the looptail, and he rules it again here: it is kept word for word, and the household is expected to understand it, as it has, for four generations:

“The simple way to the sun, there are, from sheer wealth, station, and requires longer and slower baking than wheaten bread.”

The settled reading, such as it is, runs this way: the simple way to the sun — the upper shelf, the shelf by the window where the light comes in in the afternoon — is where the loaf goes; and because it is made from the first of everything, out of a household’s sheer wealth and good station, it will not forgive the speed that a wheaten bread forgives. It requires the longer and slower bake, or it will be the fruit of the ground, dense at the heart and refused. Mrs. Arunah’s line has sat in the ledger since 1902 uncorrected, the way the family has sat in the ledger itself, and the reader who finds a better reading is asked to write it in the margin and leave the original standing.

III. Of the First Loss

The loaf is baked on the day a first is lost: the first lamb of the year, the first crop, the first death, and — the household allows it, though it will not print the club’s name — the first round of the cup, where a tie is drawn at random and the manners are not. On that day the loaf is broken at the head of the table, the name is greeted before the person, and whoever is grieving takes the corner piece, which is the piece with the crust all four ways, because the double crust keeps the breath the way the double l keeps the name. It is the same rule the book of surnames keeps at its Chapter IV · Of the Surname Abell and the Table That Bears It, and this receipt stands on that chapter the way the loaf stands on the shelf: without it, unseated.

IV. The Method

  1. Mix the flours in the great bowl, the wheat first and the rye after, as the first son and the second, and work in the salt and the honey.
  2. Make the well, break in the eggs, and add the warm water a hand at a time until the dough closes; it should feel the way a sleeve feels when it has just come out of the oven — warm, and not to be trusted with a bare hand.
  3. Work it twenty minutes, turn it twice, and let it rest covered in the larder for the best part of an hour. The butter is beaten in at the second resting, walnut by walnut.
  4. Shaping is by the two hands only, no board flour, and the loaf is set on the stone, not the tin. A stone for Abel; the family is settled on this one point.
  5. Into the oven, 275 degrees, upper shelf, the simple way to the sun. Bake one hour and fifty minutes, turning the stone at the seventy-minute mark. Do not open it before; the vapour is the point.
  6. When it is done it sounds hollow as a name that has not been answered. It keeps seven days in the linen, and the seventh day is the day it is broken, whatever the news.

V. Of the Keeping

The receipt is filed beside its sister, the pot of lamb with quince and wild thyme on page 41 of the Callimachou kitchen ledger, Telos, because two families that bake for the dead keep their pages together. On cup day, when the tea room is open and the buns are on the plate, the household sends the loaf up from the table and it is seated at Table 152, beneath the photograph of the terraced ground, in the manner laid down in Chapter VII · Of the Cup Tie and the Draw, where the record is kept verbatim and no line is altered after the kick-off. The whole shelf of the household — the surnames, the receipts, the error register, the wings and the hats coming down at January — stands at the archive, and this is Receipt No. 4, entered, uncorrected, and kept.
