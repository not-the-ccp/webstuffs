The Surakarta Morning Decoction

A tonic for the first hour of the clinic day, carried from a tobacco hillside in Salatiga through Batavia and Kediri, and set down here in the hand that licensed it — Oen Boen Ing, 3 March 1903 – 30 October 1982

Un Bûn-eng  ·  溫文英  ·  温文英

What the ledger says before the recipe

The family will not give the recipe without its history, because the recipe is not the history. The decoction was first boiled in the courtyard of a two-storey house on a tobacco hillside in Salatiga, where Oen Boen Ing was born on 3 March 1903 to a tobacco entrepreneur whose factory smoked the valley at harvest time. His grandfather kept the old dispensary at the back of the yard — a brick room with a squat earthen stove, a hanging scale with two brass pans, and a shelf of jars labelled in a hand the family still cannot fully read — and the boy, from the age of seven, carried the jars, stirred the cauldron, and learned the weight of a hand of dried longan the way other children learn the weight of coins. In the family’s own reckoning he was a medicine man from the knee up before he was a boy all the way through.

At nineteen, in 1922, against the whole counsel of the house — his father wanted the factory, his uncles wanted the ledgers of the factory, and the factory was, in the uncles’ opinion, the whole of the world — he took the train down to Batavia and enrolled at STOVIA, the school that made physicians of the colonised. The family did not send money. The family sent a single letter, twice, each letter ending the same way: the factory is waiting. At the school he studied anatomy on the morning bench and walked, after, into the meeting room of the Chung Hsioh, the Chinese nationalist study society, where a young man from a Javan tobacco town learned that a dispensary and a nation are kept by the same kind of hand. He held both jobs for a decade, and did them at the same hour, and it is said — the ledger says so, and the ledger is the only judge — that he never once let either appointment down.

He received his medical licence in 1932, and in early 1935 he left Kediri, where his first clinic had stood three years, for the central Javan city of Surakarta, where the clinic of his years stood until the end. He kept his grandfather’s method and his school’s evidence in the same drawer, and he made the two of them shake hands at every consultation. The morning decoction below is that handshake, reduced to a pot. It is the one thing he set down in the family ledger in his own hand, and it has been boiled in this house every working morning since. The ledger is not sentimental about it. The ledger is precise about it.

And here is the one entry of the ledger that is not the family’s own. When the young doctor from Salatiga was licensed at Batavia in 1932, the Registry of the medical bureau entered his name in its book in red ink, which the Registry did for perhaps two hundred souls in the whole of the colony. The Registry’s ledger admitted, once, a line the bureau has decided to reprint it here, and meant it:

OEN BOEN ING, b. 3 March 1903, Salatiga — licentiate in medicine, Batavia, 1932. Entered in red, as ordered.

The family has framed the reprint above the stove. It is the only framed thing in the kitchen, and the family considers this enough.

The decoction, for one morning’s dose

Yield: two cups, one for the doctor, one for the first patient who is allowed it

Ingredients

  • 30 g fresh galangal, sliced thin as a letter — this is the grandfather’s weight, not the market’s
  • 12 dried longan, shelled, from the jars on the back shelf
  • 10 goji berries, counted on the brass pan, never by eye
  • 1 slice of raw honeycomb, about 5 g, only if the season allows it
  • 400 ml water, cold from the cistern, no more
  • a pinch of white pepper, ground in the house that morning

Method

  1. Cover the water with the galangal and the longan and bring it to a boil on the high ring. The smell is the sign, not the sound.
  2. Drop the heat to the low ring, cover, and let it turn down to a decoction for thirty minutes. Do not open the lid. The thirty minutes are counted on the kettle clock, and the kettle clock is the only clock in the kitchen with authority.
  3. Stir in the goji for the final three minutes, no sooner; they must wake in the pot, not cook in it.
  4. Strain through a fine cloth into a cup, press the cloth twice with the back of a spoon, and lay the honeycomb slice across the surface without letting it touch the cup.
  5. Finish with the white pepper. The pepper is the school in Batavia; the longan is the dispensary in Salatiga. Both, in the same cup, on purpose.

Serve at once, in the first hour, before the clinic opens. If the day runs long and a second cup is wanted, the pot is not to be heated again on the stove; it is warmed in a basin of hot water, and the second cup is never sweeter than the first. The ledger notes, in the margin, in the doctor’s hand: the patient drinks the first hour of the doctor’s day. That is the medicine in it.
