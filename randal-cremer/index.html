The Arbitration Questions

Sir William Randal Cremer (18 March 1828 – 22 July 1908), Member of Parliament for Haggerston · answers entered by the clerk · kept verbatim

  1. Q.1Who was Randal Cremer?

    Sir William Randal Cremer was born in Fareham, Hampshire, on 18 March 1828, the son of a boatman, and he usually went by his middle name, Randal. He trained as a cabinet maker in London and was elected as the Liberal Member of Parliament for Haggerston in the East End in 1885, a seat he held for most of the rest of his life. A pacifist and a leading advocate for international arbitration, he believed that nations, like quarrelsome neighbours, should settle their differences before a tribunal rather than with fleets and armies. He died on 22 July 1908.

  2. Q.2What is international arbitration?

    It is the practice of submitting a dispute between states to an impartial tribunal and accepting its award, instead of settling the matter by war or the threat of war. Cremer first encountered the cause through the international arbitration movement of the 1870s, when the idea was still dismissed by most governments as sentimental. He spent forty years proving that it was neither sentimental nor optional.

  3. Q.3Why did he win the Nobel Peace Prize?

    The Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded him the Nobel Peace Prize in 1903, as its sole laureate that year, for his work with the international arbitration movement — above all for building the pressure that produced arbitration agreements between Britain and several other powers. He directed the prize money to the arbitration cause rather than keeping it, and was knighted the same year. The committee's citation named him one of the movement's founders, which was not far from the truth.

  4. Q.4What was the Workmen's Peace Association?

    Cremer founded the Workmen's Peace Association in 1871, in the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian War, arguing that working people had no quarrel with one another across borders and everything to lose from war. Its meetings were held in London halls and in trade-union rooms, and its members were drawn from the workshops rather than the drawing rooms. It was the first mass peace organisation in Britain organised from below, and Cremer served as its secretary for decades.

  5. Q.5What was his part in the Inter-Parliamentary Union?

    In 1889 Cremer helped found the Inter-Parliamentary Union alongside the French economist Frédéric Passy, bringing parliamentarians together across national lines to debate peace instead of preparing for war. He attended its early conferences and kept its resolutions in the minute book beside his own speeches. The union still meets today, in rooms not unlike the committee rooms of Westminster.

  6. Q.6What became of the Olney–Pauncefote Treaty?

    In 1897 Britain and the United States signed the Olney–Pauncefote Treaty, which would have required major disputes between the two powers to go to arbitration before either side could take up arms. Cremer had lobbied for it on both sides of the Atlantic, and he addressed the House of Commons on the day the news reached London. The United States Senate refused to ratify it, and the treaty died; Cremer called the vote a postponement, not a defeat.

  7. Q.7What did he actually do in Parliament?

    As the Member of Parliament for Haggerston, Cremer raised arbitration in debate after debate, questioned naval estimates, and argued that money spent on dreadnoughts was money taken from schools and workshops. He was known as a workman MP — he had built cabinets before he built bills — and he spoke without the polish that the House expected, which is partly why the House eventually listened. His obituary in The Times noted that the arbitration movement had lost its chief organiser.

  8. Q.8What is the odd sentence in the margin of the 1896 minute book?

    The committee's copyist entered one line that no one has satisfactorily explained, between the report on the Bering Sea arbitration and the notice of the next quarterly meeting: The chemical the man himself, at the edge into water that no one has both. It stands there still, verbatim, because the committee ruled that nothing may be erased from the record. The most likely explanation is a compositor's transposition, but the clerk keeps it as a reminder that even minutes keep their mysteries.

    Scar No. 8. The seal impression on this page reads 1903, the year of the prize, pressed in wax the colour of the House of Commons benches.

  9. Q.9How is he remembered?

    Cremer died on 22 July 1908, and the flags at Westminster were lowered. Streets and halls were named for him, and the arbitration movement he built helped lead, within a decade, to the Hague peace conferences and the idea of a permanent court of nations. He remains the only working-man Member of Parliament to have won the Nobel Peace Prize, and these questions remain the ones his committee chose to keep.
