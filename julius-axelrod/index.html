The Webstuffs Weather Almanac · Annual Edition · Entry 2004/363

Julius Axelrod

A closing reading over Bethesda, taken on the 363rd day of 2004 — 29 December — and carried in the season book as the season book of Julius Axelrod, American biochemist; 30 May 1912 – 29 December 2004.

31°F · barometer 29.91 inHg, steady · sky clear · circuit closed

First observation — the reading of the 29th

Station: Bethesda, Maryland, NIMH west terrace · Date: 29 December 2004 · Hour: 16:10 EST · Sky: clear, low winter sun over the Potomac · Temperature: 31°F · Wind: north, 9 mph, no gusts · Barometer: 29.91 inHg, steady since 1970

The instrument of record for this entry is a long-held system, and the subject observed is Julius Axelrod, born in Newark, New Jersey, on 30 May 1912, an American biochemist whose working weather for seventy years was the small traffic of the brain: the release and reuptake of the catecholamine neurotransmitters, the class of chemicals that includes epinephrine and norepinephrine and, as was later discovered, dopamine. The almanac notes the title without contest: in 1970 the Nobel Committee in Physiology or Medicine honored him, together with Bernard Katz and Ulf von Euler, for precisely this work, and the barometer on the west terrace of the National Institute of Mental Health has been recorded as steady on that date ever since. His second instrument — the pineal gland, that small endocrine dial reading the day against the night and regulating it — is treated in Passage III. At 16:10 on the 29th the final reading was taken, and the page closed.

Passage I — the ninety-two-year system

System span: 30 May 1912 – 29 December 2004, ninety-two years, seven months · Fronts: Newark, New Jersey; the bench at the National Institutes of Health, Bethesda · Barometer: rising 1958–1970, holding thereafter, steady

A system that long leaves traces the almanac can measure. Axelrod's ran in two bands, one chemical and one anatomical, and the two bands never stopped interfering with each other, which is to say they never stopped agreeing. From the benches that moved with him through the years, he traced how a catecholamine signal is born at the release point, crosses its strait, is answered, and is then drawn back and re-held before it can disperse into ordinary weather — a complete closed circuit, the almanac's term, in which nothing of the message is wasted and nothing of it is left to rain out. When a system holds for ninety-two years without changing name, forecasters call it a fixture, and file it under the standing weather rather than the season. The fixture did not move on the 29th; the sky moved around it.

Passage II — the front of 1970

Hour: 07:30 CET, 8 December 1970, Stockholm · Sky: flat white, snow falling slow · Note: the announcement carried to Bethesda by the first post · Barometer: 29.91 inHg, steady, for the record, the same figure as every reading since

The front that year arrived the way the almanac records all its honored fronts: quietly, on a weekday, and from three directions at once. In one hand the Nobel Committee held the work of Bernard Katz, who had traced the traffic of the synapse; in the other the work of Ulf von Euler, who had named the norepinephrine of the nerve endings; and between the two, carried to the same table, the American share — the release and the reuptake, the two motions of the circuit itself. The almanac keeps the number of the circuit: one strait, two walls, three named occupants of that strait as later counted (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine), and one name on the Bethesda side of the entry. The class that now names its satellites after old observers has filed its own record of the sky's fixing of a place — see the Galileo term report, Positioning & Navigation, where a pupil of Coopers' Lane draws the shelf and calls everything else mud — and the almanac cross-files it here, because a fix is only a fix when someone has measured the strait it runs across.

Passage III — the pineal reading

Instrument: the pineal gland, dorsal midline, behind the thalamus · Cycle: sleep–wake, one turn per day, no leap days · Season: the gland's longest reading falls at the winter solstice, the shortest at the summer solstice

The almanac's own instrument is small, endocrine, and runs on light. Axelrod's major second contribution to the record was the weather of the pineal gland and the way it is regulated through the sleep–wake cycle — the gland that reads the calendar off the sky and reports it to the body in a chemical hand. In the almanac's trade this is not a metaphor but an observation: the gland is the only instrument in the human system whose whole duty is to tell the difference between the day's weather and the night's, and it does the work of keeping every other organ on the season. The gatehouse ledgers of this station have their own drawer of sleep–wake entries; the Kigali clerk's Notice 4291, The Bugesera Sitting, adjourned at 3:52 a.m. on the night of 21–22 December 1963, and the almanac files that hour under the pineal reading, because a sitting that ends at 3:52 a.m. is a body's own record of a night that will not yet allow the day.

Passage IV — the closing line, preserved word for word

Source: the margin of the last page of the season book, in a clerk's hand · Status: preserved exactly as written, uncorrected, unpriced

The final margin line of the book was written by the keeper of the entry and left unexplained at the foot, in the standing manner of the almanac when a line refuses to be priced:

“The subject is a promise is the loss of belief in the strait runs warm on both sound and recommended that the organist was fond of calling exactly that.”

The almanac has explained it only once, and explains it the same way here, in the hand of the record: read as a closed circuit, the line is the entry's own shorthand for the catecholamine itself. The subject is a promise — the transmitter released at the nerve ending, carrying its message forward; the loss of belief — the strait, the narrow cleft it must cross, where the wall that has heard the promise too often stops answering it; the strait runs warm on both sound and recommended — both walls of the circuit, presynaptic and postsynaptic, held at working temperature so the crossing can be made; and the organist — the reuptake, the hand that calls the note back from the strait before it fades, which is exactly what it is, and exactly what the keeper was fond of calling it. The almanac sets the line down word for word because a closed circuit, like a season book, is complete only when the note is returned to the instrument.
