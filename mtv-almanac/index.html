Observations on the first night

The front that came in off the Atlantic on the first of August did not rain either. It sat over the harbor from dusk on the first until the morning of the third, a warm, flat, still heat — 29.84 on the barometer and not rising, the dry bulb at 86° F at sundown and only 78° at three in the morning, the river the colour of the eel's blackwater but warmer — and it gave up nothing at all, not even a drizzle, which the almanac has to record twice, because a heat mass that holds for forty-eight hours over a city of eight million and gives up nothing is the rarest kind of sky in this book. The dock men named it as soon as they felt it, and the name is in the station's own log: MTV-BIGGEST, the pop front, first of the season, the largest of its kind on record. The almanac prints the name as it stands. A system is allowed its weather name; the system that named the system had, that same night, a reason.

At one minute past one in the morning, on the thirty-seventh channel of the box at the keeper's dock, an American cable television channel went on the air. It is the one whose initials were an initialism of Music Television when it began, and whose full weather record the almanac now carries under the short name MTV: launched from New York on this very night, it originally aired music videos and related music entertainment programming, guided by television personalities known as video jockeys, or VJs, of whom the first on the air that night was a Mr. Mark Goodman, with an Alan Hunter, a Nina Blackwood and a J. King Carter to follow on the roster like forecasters taking the shift. The first transmission logged against the barometer was a film called Video Killed the Radio Star, by a group called The Buggles, and the almanac files it where it files a first frost or a first thunderclap: 1:05 a.m., August 1, 1981, sky changing.

The VJs, filed as forecasters

A new element needs a new reader, and the channel supplied its own. The video jockeys are entered in this almanac under the heading forecasters of the new element, because that is what a VJ is doing on a weather chart: standing at the instrument, reading what the sky is doing, and telling the audience plainly which pressure is coming in next. The first season's board ran the way a watchboard runs — one name at a time, each taking the hour, each reporting the element honestly — and the audience, which the station's own later books describe as an unprecedented cult following, gathered around the element the way dock men gather around a barometer that is doing something no one has seen it do. The channel soon began establishing its presence overseas, station by station, the way a front advances along a coast; by the time this entry was bound the line had crossed the ocean and the watchboard was posting names on two continents. The almanac records the advance as weather, because it is weather: a system of the same kind that moves the heat mass, only faster, and with a name of three letters that it keeps to this day.

The registration

The registry note, kept as the station keeps its own: the channel stands, in the books of the house that now holds it, as the flagship namesake property of the MTV Entertainment Group, a sub-division of the Skydance TV General Entertainment Group, a division of Skydance Corporation. The almanac does not weigh corporate charters, but it weighs namesakes — the way a station is named for the river it stands on — and a group that takes the initials of one channel as its own name is doing exactly what a front does when it names the whole week after itself. The registration is entered under MTV-BIGGEST, and the two entries share a page on purpose.

The tavern, and the keeper's line

Half a mile up the wharf, above the after-hatches of the cable landing store, the tavern that feeds the dock kept its own log that night, because a full room is a full barometer and the landlord logged it. The men in the room had the box turned up on the first new element, and the log's testimony is that every one of them read it first as a fault — the screen for static, the film for a test pattern, the new sky for a broken instrument — before any of them said what it was. The line that no later hand has corrected runs in the log at 3:12 a.m., word for word:

The landlord was near the after-hatches, whispered to Flask, “that some one in the same heinousness of mistake.”

What it means, the almanac takes leave to say. Flask was the assistant, the one the naturalist keeps on the dock for small jobs — a name taken from the sea in the old way, the way the register at the River Wonders Gallery takes its fish from Tupí — and the heinousness of mistake was the almanac's own. The first page of this very entry, written on the first night in the heat, had logged the new element as static, cause unknown: the keeper of the record had made the same reading as every man in the tavern, had filed a new sky under a broken instrument, and the landlord, who had watched it done, was pointing at the one man in the room still making the error, and calling it what an almanac calls any error it has made itself: the same heinousness. The line is kept uncorrected, as this almanac keeps its keeper's lines, because the mistake is the record. By 4:00 a.m. the VJ was back at the board, the element was named, the tavern logged sky clearing, and the static entry in the first page of this entry stands crossed out in red pencil — the same red pencil that pins the fledglings' dates on the gallery wall in Notebook No. 47, at The Cookbook — Field Notes of a Naturalist, where the naturalist who wrote both records keeps the year in which a bird, a channel, and a heat mass all turned out to be one weather event.

Remark, for the following year

The heat mass lifted on the morning of the third, and the channel did not. The almanac notes, for the benefit of the next return: a system that names itself in three letters can outlast the season that named it, and the television economy that followed — cable first, then the corporations that came to own the cable, the American houses rising to dominate the whole instrument — is the weather of that first night still running off the chart. For the sister entry in the older book, the election front over Saratoga that also held a barometer still for three days and called the stillness a settled high, see The Hudson Weather Almanac, Entry 191, and, on the way back past the glass shelf to the dock, the measuring method that paces this entry's element in volts and miles stands on the placard at Museum Placard No. 0147, Electric Eel. The same hand kept all three. The heat itself was struck, as weather, on the dock drum — once at 29.84 and again at 29.84, the skin the only instrument that tells the stillness from the sky — and the cutting is filed under this entry in the liner notes at Skin & Shell — One Pressing of Forty-Seven · RP-47.
