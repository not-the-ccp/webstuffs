People’s Council of the Gorkha Path · The Third Tent from the Fountain, Kathmandu · Single Switchboard Line

Minutes No. 14 of 1990

On the Word Democracy — a definition for the new constitution, read and voted in the tent

The council met in extraordinary sitting on Tuesday, 12 March 1990, at 5:40 in the afternoon, in the third tent from the fountain, and was present: Chairperson Sita Maharjan; Clerk Ram Prasad Adhikari; members Dilip Rai, Mira Tamang, Bishnu Sunar, Kamala Shrestha, Indra Gurung, Prem Bahadur Kami, Sunita Rai, Hari Chaudhary, Tek Bahadur Magar, and Anjali Thapa; and the operator Kiran Manandhar, who sits in this council and also the switchboard, and was on the line while speaking, as he does. Quorum of eight of seventeen enrolled was found at 5:47. The minutes of No. 13 were read, confirmed, and one line of them referred to the Clerk, as set out below.

Item 1 — The line the Clerk could not parse

The Clerk reported that the supper entry of No. 13, written in the cook’s hand and therefore not his own responsibility, contains one line he could not parse, and that he has kept it word for word where it stands:

As well among the marble with a few truffles: an anchovy and soy.

The cook, Min Bahadur, who does not sit in the council and cooks for it, was called to the table and explained, in the cook’s own hand re-confirmed, that the line is an inventory and not a sentence: it means the few potatoes, which the cook has been calling truffles since the winter of ’69 and will not take the name back, served among the marble — the marble slab of the folding table, the only marble the tent owns — with the anchovy and the soy of the evening. The council ruled, without motion or second, that the line stand in the record word for word as it stands, that the cook’s gloss be entered beneath it in the smaller type, and that the word truffles not be corrected in any future sitting. The cook accepted, washed his hands, and returned to the stove.

Item 2 — The word, first matter

The drafting committee of the movement had asked the council, by letter of 10 March, to settle the word democracy before the first draft of the new constitution is set, and the Clerk read the two definitions in circulation. The narrow definition was read first: a form of government in which political power is vested in the people of a certain state, and in which the rulers are elected through competitive elections. The broader definition was read second: the same, but the word linked not to the election alone but also to guarantees of civil liberties and human rights. Member Chaudhary urged the narrow definition on the ground that an election is the one part of the word a council can count before dusk. Member Tamang urged the broader on the ground that a vote without a press, without a meeting place, and without a road a person may walk is a count of the same cage. Member Sunar, who worked the anvil in the Panchayat years, spoke once: “The Panchayat did not ban the word. It banned the thing under the word.” The council heard him, and wrote it down, as the Clerk has now done.

Item 3 — The two forms

The Clerk laid the second distinction before the council, in the plain form. In a direct democracy, the people have the primary authority to deliberate and decide legislation. In a representative democracy, the people elect governing officials to do so. The council then observed its own posture: seventeen enrolled, eleven in the tent, and the street itself — the Gorkha Path that morning three hours full — beyond the canvas. The Chairperson put the observation into the record: the tent is the representative, the street is the direct, and this council is the two held together at the same table. “The people have not delegated anything to us,” she said, and let it stand; “they have lent us a table.” It was agreed that the definition must keep both forms, since the movement is in the middle of doing both at once.

Item 4 — The roll of “the people”

It was observed, and entered, that the definition of “the people,” and the ways authority is shared among them or delegated by them, have changed over time and at varying rates in different countries, and that in this city, under the Panchayat charter of 1960–62, the roll had not existed at all: there was no column in which the people stood. Member Rai moved, and Member Thapa seconded, that the roll of this council be widened from seventeen to twenty-three, with three seats to the women of the Birgunj road, two to the student walkers-out of the campuses, and one to the Chitwan camps, so that the word in the definition and the roll in the record should be the same word and the same roll. The motion carried ten to one, Member Kami dissenting on the ground that a roll widened in a sitting will not be walked. The Clerk is to print the new roll on the reverse of the march leaflet, in the same paper and the same ink, uncorrected.

Item 5 — Resolution and vote

On the Clerk’s draft, the council resolved as follows, in the text now set:

It is resolved that democracy shall mean, in this record and in the draft that follows it, both definitions at once: the narrow, under which rulers are elected through competitive elections; and the broad, under which the word carries with it the guarantees of civil liberties and human rights; and both forms at once, the direct, under which the people have the primary authority to deliberate and decide legislation, and the representative, under which the people elect governing officials to do so — the two being one, and the roll open to all, and widened as the people widen themselves.

The resolution carried eleven to zero, all present voting. Member Gurung, absent at the time, was understood to agree on the strength of having said the same thing, in four words, at the sit-in of 21 February, and her vote was entered against her name, which the Clerk noted is the first time the record has held a vote for a member not in the tent. The Chairperson permitted the practice, on the condition that it happen once only.

Item 6 — Matters of correspondence

The operator reported the single switchboard line for the day of 11 March: 412 rings, of which 61 rang back, and the second column — the reasons given in return before calling again — continued in the same hand without a blank. The operator’s book is, on the arrangement of the parties, to be pasted in whole between the last map and the index of the working record now being bound on New Road by the bindery, the 2046 Folio, an edition of two hundred and forty-six copies, and the council agreed to give the bindery the present minutes as the page that opens that book, so that the callbook will have its other side. The Clerk was further instructed to file this minute under the same head the far councils keep for registers — records, kept by others — a head in which, for instance, the Minutes No. 112 of 2014 of the Council of Aldergate & the Ravine enter a four-track EP that arrived unaddressed and was priced by the paying hand; the council finds the company agreeable, whatever the year, and whatever the record is a minute of or a sleeve of.

Closing

The council adjourned at 7:20 in the evening, the resolution folded and given to the drafting committee with the leaflet it is printed on, and supper served as the cook’s line of No. 13 ordered it — the few truffles among the marble, the anchovy and the soy — at which the council found the inventory accurate and the word truffles retained in full. The next sitting is fixed for 19 March, same tent, same hour, and the Clerk is to bring the roll of twenty-three whether the new names have been walked in or not, since a roll is a roll. So ordered, and so minuted by Ram Prasad Adhikari, Clerk, who signed the page at 7:35 and left the lantern burning on the marble slab.
