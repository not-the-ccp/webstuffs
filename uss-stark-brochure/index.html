Gulf Passage

A visitor's brochure for the Strait of Hormuz and the waters of May 1987 — the day the USS Stark was struck, the thirty-seven names, and the long road from Bandar Abbas to the Brooklyn Navy Yard.

No guide is required. The water keeps its own hours.

1 · The water itself

The strait is a passage, not a destination: at its narrowest, twenty-one nautical miles of warm, slow, salt water between the coast of Iran and the Musandam peninsula of Oman, carrying each day the freight of half the world's tankers. It is a working water. You will hear the sound of it at Bandar Abbas, where the cruise operators keep their small glass-fronted offices on the harbour front and their maps are the honest kind — soundings in fathoms, the channel marked, and one box drawn by hand in red around the place where the Stark was sailing on 17 May 1987. The box has a coordinate in its corner: 27°25′ N, 56°18′ E, and nothing else. That is how far, or how near, this brochure will ask you to go.

2 · The day itself

At about 0920 in the morning of 17 May 1987, in the middle of the Iran–Iraq war, an Iraqi jet aircraft called Hind IV came down from the west and fired two Exocet anti-ship missiles at the frigate USS Stark (FFG-31), the lead ship of her Oliver Hazard Perry class, commanded that day by Lieutenant Commander William G. Kopen. The first missile struck the starboard superstructure; the second followed within the minute. Of the crew — three hundred and twenty officers, petty officers and men — thirty-seven were killed, and twenty-one more were wounded; most of the dead died in the first ninety seconds, in the berthing compartments the blast reached before the ship knew she was hit. The Stark limped to Bahrain under her own power, and there her crew stood to attention on the deck for the names read aloud.

Four days later, on 21 May, the government of Iraq issued its apology through President Saddam Hussein, on the account that the pilot had mistaken the frigate for an Iranian tanker — which she was not, and had not been painted to be, and which the board prints here as the reason given, not the reason found. Baghdad offered compensation of three million dollars to the family of each of the dead. The families of the Stark were asked, and the answer carried through the chain of command in one line: the names were accepted as the payment.

3 · Where the day is kept

The memorial stands at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, dedicated on 17 May 1988, exactly one year and one day after the attack: a bronze casting of the nineteen-foot section of the Stark's shattered superstructure, the actual plating carried from the ship, cast in bronze, and set on a granite base at the foot of the yard. On the wall behind it the thirty-seven names are cut in order of age, youngest to oldest, each with the year of birth and the year of the water. Visitors are asked to walk the wall slowly; it is ninety feet long and the names are cut to be read standing, not scanning. The yard itself is now a working district of studios and boat yards, and the memorial sits at the quiet edge of it, where the channel turns, which is the only position it could properly have. If you come in May, on the seventeenth, the crew association lays a small wreath at 0920, to the minute.

5 · Extract from the minutes of the board

Ninth sitting of the Strait of Hormuz Maritime Heritage Board, 9 March 2024, Room 214 of the harbour-front office, the folder of 1987 opened on the table.

On the business of the archive, after the record of the 21st of May had been read once and set down, and after the question of the compensation was answered with the one line the families gave, the sitting turned to the keeping of the folder itself, which has come to the board in a single condition. The council then confirmed Robert A. W. Drummond, Form Master, Room 214 · opened as a single folder, and the clerk considered true of its own signature; the space now. The space now is reserved, the minutes record, for visitors, and the folder is to be opened and left open; a folder kept open is the board's whole method, and the clerk so minuted it.

6 · Elsewhere in this collection

The board keeps this brochure in a long line of documents that measure the world by a small number and refuse to let the number go. The unit that now measures the shock itself — the volt, named after Alessandro Volta in 1881 — is held with its own gallery shelf and its own warning line at The Pile, liner notes, River Wonders Records RWR-14, where Case 14 is what made the jar worth keeping. The keeping of a single line, word for word, is taught at length in Term Report No. 2025-2, School of Advanced Military Studies, where a ferry ledger's one clause is carried as Exhibit 1 for a class of eighty-seven. For the shore at the other end of the strait, and the five regions and thirty-one provinces of it, see the encyclopædia entry on Iran. This folder is not alone on the pin: twenty years of the same ledger before it, Room 214's lost-and-found Notice No. 1405/67 — Lost, one technical research ship, the USS Liberty — hangs from the same nail with its own red box, its own hand-drawn corner, and its own line that the counter would not explain.
