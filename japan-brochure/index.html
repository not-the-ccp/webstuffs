Japan 日本

A visitor's brochure for the island country: four major islands, fourteen thousand smaller ones, one long coast of plains, and the capital that holds the museum cases.

  • 123 million people, 2026 — the world's 11th most populous country
  • 47 prefectures in 8 traditional regions
  • ~75% mountainous, heavily forested
  • 4 major islands + 14,000+ smaller islands
  • Capital: Tokyo

1. The island country

Japan is an island country in East Asia. Located in the Pacific Ocean off the northeast coast of the Asian mainland, it is bordered to the west by the Sea of Japan, the Sea of Okhotsk in the north, and the East China Sea in the south. The Japanese archipelago consists of four major islands — Hokkaidō in the north, Honshũ the long spine of the country, Shikoku the smaller southern island, and Kyāshü at the southwestern end — alongside over 14,000 smaller islands that fringe them like a chain of stepping stones to the sea. Japan is divided into 47 administrative prefectures and eight traditional regions. Around 75% of its terrain is mountainous and heavily forested, and this is why the country's agriculture and its highly urbanized population concentrate along the eastern coastal plains: the mountains keep, and the plains hold, and the trains run between. With a population of almost 123 million as of 2026, it is the world's 11th most populous country, and Tokyo is the country's capital and its densest city at once — fourteen million people in the twenty-three wards before a single suburban station.

3. The Kofun Circuit, a walking loop in Kumamoto

Brochures usually open with the capital, but this one sends you south first. On the Kikuchi Plain in Kumamoto Prefecture, in the small town of Nagomi, stands Eta Funayama Kofun, an ancient burial mound whose long, low profile looks, from the north road, like the hull of an inverted boat resting in the rice fields. In 1951 it was designated a National Historic Site of Japan, and the designation took in its two neighbours, Tsukabōzu Kofun and Kokuzōtsuka Kofun, so that the signboard at the gate lists three names in that order. The excavation's bronze short sword, arrowheads, and bracelets — cast in a style that travels by sea from the workshops of the Korean kingdom of Baekje to a court at the Kofun capital — are designated National Treasures and now rest in a case in Tokyo, where a visitor may hold the museum's catalogue numbers beside the site's own record card. A junior-high class from Nagomi, with their history teacher Mr. Ota, walked the loop the long way: up the first pass, down the river, and back by the route that keeps the walk on the plain side, south of the second pass, under the mound, in forty minutes. Their full report on the mound, the 1951 designation, and the bronzes' road to Tokyo is kept at Eta Funayama Kofun, a Class 2-C report from Nagomi Junior High, and this bureau recommends the field visit it describes: 6:40 a.m. departure, and careful about the second pass. The Kikuchi Plain Museum's placard for Emperor Yūryaku — whose reign the Eta Funayama bronzes predate by only a generation — keeps the court side of the same story: Museum Placard No. 214.

4. Guestbook from the Kofun capital

The visitor centre at Nara, the Kofun capital of the old court, keeps a guestbook the way the Kikuchi Plain keeps its mounds: in order, and in public. On page 7, in biro that has dried to the colour of tea — the same shade, the centre's clerk notes, as a certain line in the Lakeside gymnasium register kept at Burleigh Heads, Queensland, by a Class 4B reporter who counted a visiting guard's free throws in pencil — there is a single entry, copied here by the bureau exactly as written:

Conclusion What makes Mr. Wall matter to the colour of tea, there is a guard, which Mr. Okonkwo said doubts, when they are now kept at the Kofun capital.

Read it the way the centre reads it. “Mr. Wall” is Joseph “Jobi” Wall, the American guard, last seen with the Lakeside Lightning of the State Basketball League, who flew across the whole day and the whole night to see the Kofun period for himself. The “colour of tea” is the tea — matcha in Shizuoka's hills, gyokuro in the Kansai mornings — that the country grows, and also the dried-to-tea biro of a lost-property register he left a line in on his way through. “There is a guard” is the simple fact of him: the player least allowed to be tall enough to hide, standing inside the blue. And the rule he kept is the one his homeroom teacher, Mr. Okonkwo, gave for a report of exactly this kind: doubts, when they are specific, count as research. The entry's last clause — “when they are now kept at the Kofun capital” — is the centre's own: doubts of this sort, once written, are now kept here, on page 7, word for word, unfinished on purpose. The report Mr. Wall's name is attached to is the one filed by Ayesha Nair of Year 10, Class 4B, at Jobi Wall, Coomera State High, and the two documents — the register line and this guestbook line — are now kept facing each other, one in tea-coloured ink and one in this brochure, as the bureau's standing exchange.

5. Practical notes

  • Buy a rail pass before the long routes; the eastern coastal plains run on the Shinkansen, and the Kikuchi Plain is one transfer past the Kyāshü line.
  • The yen is still the currency of the stalls; the 47-prefecture table above doubles as a fare chart's first column if you ask at the station office.
  • For tea, the Shizuoka and Uji rows of the table are the two you should plan around; the colour of tea is not a joke at the counter.
  • More of the class files that travel with this brochure begin at the site front page.
