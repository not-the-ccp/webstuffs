Oklahumma

[oklahómma] · Choctaw · okla, “people,” + humma, “red”

The Red People Walk

A walking tour of Oklahoma in ten stops · Itinerary OT-07 · 101.0 miles read on foot

Filed at the counter, 18 October 2022 · Reference OT-07 · Guide: caseworker M. Okafor, City Relocation Office · The tour departs when the ledger says it departs · The state is landlocked; the tour is not

Quote reference OT-07 at every stop. The tour was found, folded, under the stand of the glass fish case from Room 19 — the same case whose counter log line, “The thread was heard in a fish case twice,” still sits at Notice CS-11 with no clerk able to explain it. It was folded the way the missing rent-ledger page was folded, and it is written in the same hand as that ledger’s last column: not rent at all, but a route. Oklahoma, where the route goes, is a landlocked state in the South Central, Southern, and Southwestern regions of the United States, the 20th-most extensive and the 28th-most populous of the fifty, and it is the only stop on this counter’s board that is not a room. Its residents are called Oklahomans, and its capital and largest city is Oklahoma City. The guide does not explain the word humma more than once. The tour explains it forever.

010.0 mi

The Counter — Room E-7, Civic Center Plaza

Departure point · elevation: the counter

The brass key stamped HOTEL POLHEMUS • ROOM 110 • 1913 hangs on the guide’s lanyard for the whole tour, as if the state were the last room on the top floor. The ledger page lists floors three and four at $545 to $610 a month, and in the last column, in the red ink the counter reserves for lines it cannot explain, somebody has written a state: OKLAHOMA — 46th admitted — 16 Nov 1907 — the sooner state. Sixty-five years before the page went missing, Oklahoma took its seat in the Union on 16 November 1907, the forty-sixth state, and the ledger has been keeping its seat warm ever since. The guide says the word red once, aloud, to the room, and the room keeps it.

0212.0 mi

The Land Run — the 12th mile

Unassigned sections, Oklahoma Territory · 22 April 1889

The tour stops where the horses stopped. At noon on 22 April 1889 the unassigned lands of central Oklahoma Territory opened to homesteaders, and the people who crossed the starting line before noon — the sooners — gave the state its other name, the Sooner State, which is a state name that is also a warning. The guide notes that the 1913 building she is vacating ran on exactly the same economy as the land run: the cheap room, the cheap section, the person the market had passed by. No sooner may ride this tour; the tour walks, and the tour is late on purpose, which is how it gets in free.

0331.5 mi

The Six Borders — the 31½-mile wall

Texas (southwest) · Kansas (north) · Missouri (northeast) · Arkansas (southeast) · New Mexico (west) · Colorado (northwest)

Oklahoma borders six states and no body of water, which makes it the one state on the board the auction room could not catalogue by tide. The Lot 142 crate from the Southern Branch archive carries tide tables for the harbour, a climate card, and a navy mess tin, and its condition slip has one line for the state on this itinerary: no coast; buyer to supply their own sea. The guide reads the six neighbors in order and refuses to shorten it: Texas to the southwest, Kansas to the north, Missouri to the northeast, Arkansas to the southeast, New Mexico to the west, Colorado to the northwest. Partially in the western extreme of the Upland South. That is the whole of the state’s address, and the tour writes it on the hand of every walker, because a landlocked state’s address is the only passport it gets.

0460.0 mi

Greenwood, Tulsa — the 60th mile

31 May – 1 June 1921 · the Greenwood District

Tulsa’s Greenwood District is the stop the tour walks in silence, because the district does not want a voice. In 1920 its population was close to 20,000, a Black-majority district with a main street the country had started calling Black Wall Street. On the night of 31 May 1921 a mob of white rioters burned it: roughly 300 people dead, around 10,000 left without homes, about 125 blocks gone in a day, and an insurance payout the district’s residents never saw. The memorial at the district’s centre is a low stone wall, and the guide stands the tour in front of it for as long as it takes. This is the only stop where the key on the lanyard is not mentioned at all. Rooms are cheap in the same city for the same reasons they are cheap in San Diego’s Core district, and the tour does not say so a second time.

0574.0 mi

Durant — the 74th mile, capital of the word

Seat of the Choctaw Nation · Bryan County

At Durant the tour stops to pay the word back. The state takes its name from the Choctaw — okla, “people,” and humma, which translates as “red” — and Durant is where the Choctaw Nation keeps its capital, its court, its archive, and its school of its own language, which the state’s name depends on and the state mostly does not speak. The guide, who filed the missing-ledger report in the building’s last week, says the word correctly, oklahómma, with the fall on the second syllable, and the tour, in the way these tours do, repeats it. The name is derived, the tour keeps saying. The name is derived. The name is derived.

0682.0 mi

The Capitol, Oklahoma City — the 82nd mile

The Gold Domes · 10 North E Street

The capitol’s twin gold domes are leafed in 24-karat gold, and the gold is from a mine in the state — the Cawston Mine, Caddo County, the same gold district that ran from the 1920s into the 1960s and gave the domes their particular shade of late afternoon. The building is the tallest of its kind in the country relative to the rooms it contains, which is the tour’s phrase, and which the building, when asked, does not correct. Oklahoma City, the capital and the largest city, takes the stop’s number for its address without comment. The fire log from the 455 C Street lobby — six fires, 1987 to 2019, each one reopening — hangs in the capitol’s gift shop now, framed, priced at $610, the top of the rent ledger’s range. The tour does not buy it. The tour notes it.

0784.0 mi

April 19, 1995 — the 84th mile

Former site of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building · Memorial Park

On 19 April 1995 a truck bomb took out the Murrah Federal Building at 9:02 in the morning and left a crater where the east stairwell stood; 168 people died, 35 of them children under twelve. The memorial that stands in the parking lot now is a field of names cut in steel, and the tour stands among the names without walking between them. The counter keeps the date the way it keeps everything else: one line, verbatim, no clerk able to explain it, and the building reopened, which is the only word the counter never gets to use at the 455 C Street building. Here the tour is a state that will reopen, and the tour is allowed to believe it.

0888.0 mi

Route 66 — the 88th mile

Will Rogers Highway · 660 miles, Springfield, Missouri, to Santa Monica, California

The Mother Road runs 660 miles from Springfield in the northeast corner down to the Pacific, and it is the only mile of this tour that goes out the state’s northeast border on purpose. Number 66 was assigned because of where the road sits in the national system, not because it likes itself, and the guide says so at every diner. The tour’s own route — 101 miles read on foot from a San Diego counter to a landlocked state and back — is not in the national system, which is why the tour can afford to be late. The road out to the coast, the one the cedar crate sailed in on, is the one the state does not own; the tour keeps its hands inside the six borders the whole way, and its pockets.

0996.0 mi

The Bone — the 96th mile

Under the fish case, shelf E-7, found 9 October 2022

Beneath the glass fish case — the twelve-gallon case from outside Room 19, drain plug in the drawer, gravel still in the bottom — the movers found a bone. The counter’s log keeps the entry exactly as written: “Inside, as catalogued from the bone, and fill up with a little pepper and salt to taste.” No clerk on duty can explain the line, and the guide reads it to the tour at this stop and at no other, because the tour has been told the bone prefers a single audience. It is not a recipe. It is not a catalogue. It is, the log says, the way the bone was catalogued, and the tour stands around it at the 96th mile with its hands in its pockets. Like the fish-case line, it stays exactly as written.

10101.0 mi

The State Line — the 101st mile, return to the counter

Arrival: Room E-7 · claim by description

The last mile is the border itself, and the border, being six, cannot be crossed without choosing. The tour arrives back at the counter where the ledger page waits for its claimant, and the claim is simple: name the room, the year the building opened, and the fish that lived in the case. A walker who can say oklahómma with the fall on the second syllable is, by the counter’s standing rule, an Oklahoman for the walk, whatever floor the ledger lists them on. The state is the 20th-most extensive of the fifty, the 28th-most populous, landlocked, red, and open for claims. The brass key is not returned. The key is the reason the tour departs, and the tour, like the building in 1994 and 2001 and 2008, reopens.

Terms of the walk

Ten stops, 101.0 miles, no elevation. The tour is free at the counter rate — nothing, plus postage on anything returned. Walkers may drop out at any stop and rejoin at the next; the bone will wait. Companions to this itinerary live on the board: the notice that holds the key, the lamp, the fish case, the fire log, and the ledger — Found, C Street Inn, 455 C Street, San Diego; the crate that holds the coast the state does not have — Lot 142, San Diego: A Coast, a Climate, and a Crate; and the other walking tour on the shelf, 3.2 miles through a grove for a yellow bird — The Last Yellow Head. The whole board, in the order the counter files it, is at the Webstuffs home.
