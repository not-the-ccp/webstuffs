01 What you are buying

Steel Street is a beat 'em up, and Kestrel will not dress it up otherwise: it is a hand-to-hand fight through a scrolling, two-dimensional city, against a large number of opponents, from the Iron Wardens to the laundromat man himself. The city is the city — the docks first, then the fish market at half past four, the laundromat on Tanners Quay, the underpass under the goods line, and finally the roof of the customs house, which is level twelve and takes most of the arcade's coin supply to reach. Gameplay follows the arcade rules, deliberately: simple to learn in the first quarter, difficult to master by the last. The combat system is the part Kestrel spent the money on. PUNCH is quick, KICK reaches over a crouching opponent, GRAB pulls a second enemy forward for one clean hit, and SPECIAL, held for a count of two, spends the player's only extra life and is not worth it, which the operator's placard says in full and the placard is not wrong. Two players may play together from the same coin box — player two's joystick is not a second game, it is the same street with one less enemy to take — and the four playable Wardens, Callow, Rhea, Doss, and the young one nobody named, are chosen at the start and fight in any order, which is a hall-mark of the genre and a reason the cabinets in the good arcades were never left alone on a Saturday.

02 The streets, level by level

The twelve levels scroll left to right and do not forgive right to left. Levels one through four are the docks, and the enemies are dockers, which is to say they are heavy and hit where they are standing. Five and six are the fish market; the floors are wet, the SPECIAL is slower on wet floors, and Kestrel printed this in the manual and was sued about the wording, not the fact. Seven is the laundromat, one long corridor of spinning drums that double as walls, and the boss of the level — the laundromat man, 190 pounds, apron on, no name in the manual — is the fight the cabinet was designed around. Eight and nine run the underpass and the goods line, where the enemy count per screen reaches nine and the two-player game is what it is supposed to be. Ten is the tannery yard; eleven is the street of the closed cinemas, where every poster on the wall is a different Kestrel catalogue number; and twelve is the roof, against the Warden who signed the rulebook, at dusk, with the river below doing nothing at all, which is the correct behaviour for a river in a fighting game. High scores are kept on the cabinet, in the order of the street: CALL·312,480 · RHEA·288,950 · DOSS·196,730.

03 The shelf rule

Every KA-89 that left the Portsmouth Row works went out with the operator's placard in the back box, and the placard carried, on its back, the one rule the Kestrel operators' shelf kept about restocking the coin mechanisms, entered word for word as the shelf foreman wrote it in 1988 and refused, in person, to explain it twice:

Tick what the shelf by the amount in the record, that a member's words are still in force.

The rule is read on the shelf as it is meant to be read: the tick goes on the restock card, the amount goes on the record card, and the two are not to be separated, because the member who wrote the card — whichever member, and the shelf does not keep asking — spoke the rule once and the rule stands. The cabinet's own memory works the same way. When the high score table fills, the oldest entry is not deleted; it is carried to the back of the list, where it waits, as a member's words wait on the shelf, until the table clears or it does not. Kestrel considers this a feature of the memory and has declined, twice, to patch it.

05 From the operator's letters

From the Marigold Room, Toxteth: “We put it by the door, as Kestrel says to. The laundromat level has stopped two arguments in one week, which the lease does not allow for but we are keeping the week.”

From a coin-box note found in the back of a KA-89 at the Portsmouth Row works, 1991: the note is the shelf foreman's hand, addressed to no one, and it stands as the shelf rule's own footnote: the tick is on the card, the card is in the record, the record is in the back box, and the back box is where the member's words keep their seat.

From the Iron Warden league office, which keeps its own account of the two-player counts, the league's standing table lives with the rest of the hard-kept records and the reader is welcome to it at the council minutes' shelf, session 214, where one more flat acquisition was entered beside the green ledger for exactly this reason.
