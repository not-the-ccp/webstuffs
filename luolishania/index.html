Dream Dictionary · Entry 314

Luolishania

loo-oh-LISH-ah-nee-ah

noun · plural Luolishanias

Luolishania longicruris is the dream of a body armoured on the outside and soft within. It walks on eleven pairs of legs, each foot a cluster of claws, each segment roofed by a sclerite like a flake of fossilized pinecone. In waking life the creature is known from the Chiungchussu Formation of the Maotianshan Shales, Chengjiang County, Yunnan Province, China, where Hou Xian-Guang and Chen Jun-Yuan described it in 1989 from Lower Cambrian mudstone. Monotypic — one genus, one species — and the namesake of the family Luolishaniidae, whose other members, Acinocricus, Collinsium, Facivermis and Ovatiovermis, crowd the same dream: the superarmoured lobopods, suspected of standing at the origin of velvet worms or basal to at least Tardigrada and Arthropoda.

Interpretation

To dream of Luolishania is to dream of protection worn so long it has become part of the skeleton. The sclerites shine, but they weigh the body down. You are carrying armor for a danger that has already molted. Because the animal sits at the base of at least Tardigrada and Arthropoda — and because fifty-two plates are preserved on the best slab, and the rock is some five hundred and eighteen million years old — it appears at thresholds: the breath before a transformation, the moment before the exoskeleton splits and the softer animal steps out.

Associated imagery

  • Chengjiang mud, wet and olive-green, under a sun that has not yet learned to burn
  • Eleven pairs of legs, walking without a destination
  • Small plates of armour, sewn into the skin with threads of silk
  • A registrar keeping a log above a bakery, ink drying slowly
  • The rigging of a ship that will never leave the harbour

On record

"Mr. Fortuno began behind a bakery, records her lines, her rigging and her store was never going to be the test — they depart for Perth Tuesday."

The reader will recognize the voice: a broadcaster at a clubhouse above a bakery, keeping the register, speaking of rigging and departures that never arrive. Luolishania in the rigging is rigging without sail — structure kept for its own beauty, structure that does not hold. The departure for Perth is a dream of leaving; the lobopodian never leaves the slab.

If Luolishania walks through your sleep, ask what you are armoring against, and whether the sclerites still fit. Walk one night without them. The mud of Chengjiang is soft, and the sea forgets everything it carries.

The dictionary's own household keeps a recipe ledger beside it, and Card 298 there is Carboniferous Coal Loaf — a beet-and-blackstrap loaf for a period sixty million years long, with one ingredients line the family reads as the secret ingredient. The same household later answered a school question on the whole business of endings — the five great boundaries, the seep between them, the almanac leaf — and filed the answer on the same shelf: The Extinction Event — Report, Class 8C, in which the dictionary’s armored one is cited, by its family name, as living evidence that the ledger keeps what it loves. The registrar of the image above, keeping his log with the ink drying slowly, later folded a letter in the same back room — an apology in red ink addressed to the second largest political party in the world, over a page torn from a red notebook: The Red Notebook — An Apology, Written in Red Thread.

The household's carrier-wave desk keeps a system notice archive in the same back room, and its oldest slip — printed on 30 June 1932, when a licence-fee notice misrouted itself to the public broadcaster that replaced the old company — is filed beside our entry: Error AB-1932 — Australian Broadcasting Corporation. The notice's engine, on its third line, went off the platen and printed the shelf as the family reads it: a registry that wrote down what it carries.

The household’s lamp of the green-gold bog, which has lit Card 298 every August without once going out, is going to the scale this spring as Lot 41 of the Spring Catalogue — one (1) wetland lamp in brass, a seated frog, a jar of unboilable water, and three shy orders of amphibians — and the buyer will find, engraved around its base, that a root is what holds a tree to a bank, and a light is what holds to the bog.

In March, the household’s guestbook was signed by a clerk from Cardiff, whose council—the Council of the Green Book, sitting over the file of a Greek lecturer of the University College of South Wales and Monmouthshire who wrote detective novels under the pseudonym Mary Fitt—read one loose sheet of a banquet book into the record, one sheet that had carried a man’s ring into the book, word for word: Minutes No. 23 of the Council of the Green Book — The Freeman File. The household filed it under the lamp, beside the ledger, where the ink dries slowly.
