The Seventeen — a Rigid Single-Deck Motor Coach, Fleet No. Seventeen

Lagoon shuttle service, Walt Disney World Resort, Florida · built 1971 · 33 ft 4 in over wheel base

SOLD · £21,400

1 · The Object

A bus — the word being contracted from omnibus, with the usual family of variants, multibus, motorbus, autobus, carried on the fleet door since the first service — a motor vehicle built to carry many more passengers than the average car or van and fewer than the average rail transport, though this one did keep close company with the rail. The Seventeen is the most common type of her class: the single-deck rigid bus, with her double-deck sisters working the larger loads elsewhere and the midibuses and minibuses the smaller ones. She carries sixty-six low riders plus a pair of fold-downs, ninety-two souls at the full statutory count, and she does it at a height a child can clear without a ladder. Body in lagoon green and cream, a single gold pinstripe, left-hand drive, an eight-point-two-litre diesel that has been described, in one surviving mechanic’s note, as “polite, and not to be argued with.” Coaches of the long-distance order are the family’s more distant relatives; she was never meant for that road, and she has always said so.

2 · The Passage, 3–18 September 1971

She came by sea. Twelve coaches of the new region’s opening fleet went out from Baltimore aboard the S.S. Osceola Star on 3 September 1971 and came in at Port Canaveral on the 18th — sixteen days in total, which the provenance book treats as a matter of some moment. The captain’s log for the eighteenth, quoted in the lot’s condition book and kept here word for word, reads:

“The value of this sixteenth day of sailing in the strangest possible manners.”

There is reason in the strange. The sea ran flat as a painted lagoon for the better part of the day; the Osceola Star lay off the breakwater at a dead crawl; and the first thing on the dock crew’s crane was not a box, not a car, but the Seventeen herself, swinging up out of the hold in her wax-paper shrouds between two gulls who seemed to consider her a rival. The value of the day, on the ship’s books, was the bus; the strangeness of the day was that nobody aboard could say which of them was being carried.

3 · The Service, 1971–1994

When the region opened on 1 October 1971 — four theme parks, two water parks, thirty-one resort hotels, the monorail loop, the lagoon ferries, all of it as recorded in the region manual, Walt Disney World Resort, Entry WDW-1971/10 — the Seventeen was already in her berth. For twenty-three years she ran the lagoon shuttle, the short green-and-cream run that tied the ferry landings to the monorail platforms, and she ran it in the manner of the whole fleet: on time, unhurried, never late by more than a wave. On the driver’s door a dockmaster’s placard was kept in the margin of service all those years, one line only: “And had you strolled forward nigh the wharf.” It has never come off.

On 14 October 1993 a pocket watch came up at guest relations with a note in a familiar hand — “The watch is a complete, and sitting fast.” The note was taken into the Seventeen’s condition book. The phrase, as the reader knows from the field notes kept at Iroquois, Louisville, had first been set down verbatim on Kenwood Drive, under a great blue heron; it travels the way herons travel, from one complete watch to the next.

4 · The Yard, Louisville, Three Winters

After retirement she stood three winters in a storage yard off Third Street, south side of Louisville, while the question of her sale was argued out in three cities. The local bird took a standing interest: the great blue heron the residents of Kenwood Drive call “the surveyor” came down onto her roof every morning at 06:30, counted the windows without turning his head more than a man will, and stood it until the coach was loaded. The yard book records the heron “present daily, no claim made.” We record him the same way, and in the same spirit, as the field book that keeps his count at the park fence.
