New Madrid Township

Term Report, Form 3B · Social Studies · Mr. Okafor

StudentCarla M. Villafuerte, 8-C, roll 27Date12 April
TopicNew Madrid Township, New Madrid County, Missouri — one week, one map, one archive box

1. What a township is, and what this one isn’t

The assignment was to pick a small Missouri place and find out what it is. I picked New Madrid Township, in New Madrid County, because the name promised an earthquake and the county map delivered it. The first thing to write down, and the thing I almost got wrong, is that New Madrid Township is an inactive township: it still exists on the plat maps, its land is still drawn in, its people are still counted in its section numbers, but the township board is not the body that governs day to day. The county, not the township, does the governing. New Madrid County is one of only a handful of Missouri counties that runs things that way, with the townships sitting in the background like old fence rows — boundaries everyone agrees on, work nobody does twice. The township takes its name from the community of New Madrid, Missouri, which sits on the Mississippi in the corner of the county, and the community in turn took its name in 1811, the same winter the ground opened up.

2. The ground, briefly

Between 15 December 1811 and 7 February 1812, three great earthquakes shook this ground hard enough to make the river run backwards in the accounts and to raise the beds of ponds into hills we still walk over. The New Madrid seismic zone runs straight through the township’s section lines, which is a fair way of saying the fault line is not on the plat map but the plat map is on the fault line. The township’s drainage is mostly open channel — ditches cut in the 1930s by the C&CC, the conservation corps — and by 1962 there were eleven numbered channels from the bluffs down to the river bottom, with a gate and a bench at the mouth of every third one. That fact matters in the fourth section, and I want the class to see I planned ahead.

3. The archive box

For my source I was allowed one afternoon in the county’s small archive, in the box labelled drainage, 1958–1964. Most of the box was what you would expect: maps, gate inventories, a ledger of who dug what. At the very bottom was a single loose card, card stock, no date, written in a fine hand that the archivist said was probably the county engineer’s, and it said this, word for word:

Commissioner Villafuerte stood in the middle of the open channel are both open Thursdays, and the bench in São José dos Campos 14 Mar, 09:41 Has anyone flown both?

I read it eleven times. I read it aloud twice, and once to my cousin in the car. Here is what I can make of it, and I will put my reasoning on the page because Mr. Okafor requires reasoning, not impressions. The first clause is a real scene: a commissioner — the drainage board had exactly one commissioner who ever walked the channels with the survey rod — standing in the middle of an open channel, which is where a surveyor stands when he is checking a low point and the only way to check a low point is in it. The middle clause, “are both open Thursdays,” belongs to the gate inventory in the same box: the public works gates, it says, are both open Thursdays for inspection. The final clause stops belonging to Missouri entirely. São José dos Campos is a city in Brazil, on the other side of the ocean, and 14 Mar, 09:41 is a time-stamp of the kind a modern flight notice carries. The card is a composite: one Missouri scene, one Missouri schedule, one question somebody in São José dos Campos asked about a flight on the morning of 14 March, written into the same hand on the same card, and nobody in the archive can say why.

My conclusion for the report is this. The township is inactive, but its paper is not. The card proves that a place can stop governing and still keep accumulating sentences — a survey, a gate schedule, a stranger’s flight question — one line at a time, in the same box, in the same hand, until the hand stops. I would like to see it framed. I understand this is a geography report.

4. Where this fits with other work

The drainage ditches of the township run out to the river, and the river runs past the little towns of the state that still keep their almanacs by hand; the bureau’s fold-out brochure, The Curtright Circuit, does for one such town exactly what I have tried to do here — take one small Missouri place and walk its whole length in a single afternoon. And the discipline of writing a strange line down word for word, and then reasoning about it instead of tidying it up, is the same discipline a certain Central Council in Vilnius applies to its travel exhibits; their minutes of 14 November 2024 keep, under a file number, a field note that is also not sensible and also true. The archive's distant-stations folder keeps a note on the Bismarck-class corvettes, six ships of the 1870s that cruised to China and the South Pacific and now lie much as this township does — on the chart, but no longer in commission (The Bismarck-class corvettes — Warship Deck, Thread No. 4112). If you want the state beyond the township, Mr. Okafor’s walking-tour itinerary Missouri by Foot starts at these same channel gates and counts the miles north. The same word-for-word discipline is kept, in a different office, by the auction catalogue of Hollis & Pankhurst, where a deep-level London tube railway was offered whole as a single lot and the vendor’s strange sentence about its site office was set down exactly, unamended (Lot 41 — The Central London Railway, Sale No. 37); a lot, like a card, that accumulated one line at a time in the same hand and was not tidied. The card goes back in the box. The channel stays open Thursdays.
