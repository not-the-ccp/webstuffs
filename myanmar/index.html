Setup, Terrain & Standing Rules of the Republic of the Union of Myanmar

1. The board, and who sits on its edges

The Union Board is long, narrow, and leans to the south: it is a country in northwest Southeast Asia that the map keeps drawing like a coat with the collar turned up. It is the largest country by area in Mainland Southeast Asia — 676,578 square kilometres of it — and the setup is that it is bordered by India and Bangladesh to the northwest, China to the northeast, Laos and Thailand to the east and southeast, and open, to the south and southwest, onto the Andaman Sea and the Bay of Bengal, which are not borders in the game’s sense but the two ways off the table. The rule for a player seated on six borders and two openings is the same rule the house gave the Ayutthaya court in Chapter XIV: enter by the oldest door, and never thank the door you are leaving through. The Pyu door is the oldest. The sea is the door that outlives everyone.

2. The names on the rulebook cover

The player’s name is Myanmar, officially the Republic of the Union of Myanmar, and the cover has to be read twice, because the second edition of the rulebook reprints the first in smaller type beneath it: Burma, the official English name of the board until 1989. Both names are in play for the whole game; a referee who strikes either one from the record is removed from the table. The book’s standing instruction is that a name which has been official for more than half the length of the game is not a nickname and must be answered to — Burma, then, is not a pet name for Myanmar, any more than the older spelling of the largest city is a pet name for the largest city: it was Rangoon, it is Yangon, and the two are the same token in two inks.

3. Setup, from the 2024 census

For the 2024 setup the board carries about 51,000,000 population tokens, counted at the 2024 census and entered here to the ten-thousand, because the manual does not count to the unit. The capital token is Naypyidaw, which is the setup a player will want to test first, since the capital token was moved there out of Yangon in 2005–2006 and the rules of the game require that a capital token be moved by a decree, not by a vote; the move was by a decree. Yangon remains the largest city token on the board, and the manual’s note in the margin, which is the whole of the margin, is that the largest city and the capital are different tokens, that this has not been an error in any edition of the rulebook, and that a player who plays them as one token forfeits the port, the sea door, and the better part of the score.

4. The ancient setup: Pyu, Mon, and the era tokens

The board did not start with the Union. The pre-game, which the manual plays out in three era tokens, begins with the Pyu city-states of Upper Myanmar, a Tibeto-Burman-speaking civilisation whose cities stood in the north the way other sets keep their reserve pieces — face down, and longer than anyone in the room expected — and with the Mon, who came up out of the south, built their own cities in the same two countries, and are entered in the manual’s register as first on the board, and first off it, and on it again. What the three eras that follow — Pagan, Ava, and Toungoo — are for, the manual says without ceremony, is to explain how a long narrow country acquires the habit of being a kingdom, which it never quite loses. The Toungoo token in particular is set here facing the Ayutthaya sheet, because in 1549 its king, Tabinshwehti, marched east on Ayutthaya and the two boards met on the river, as they are recorded to meet in the house’s Chapter XIV, the house book of etiquette’s table plan for Ayutthaya, 1351–1767; the canteen woman at Peach Street priced the whole encounter, both boards included, at one can of peaches, 300 kyats, and the manual’s scorekeeper has never been able to find a better price for either game.

5. The bird oracle, card No. 9

Every set of this series ships with a small oracle of nine cards, and the Union Board’s card No. 9 is printed in an ink the binder has never matched, from a hand that is not the house’s, and is entered in the register of the manual under the standing rule for such cards, which is that it is offered as found and made to answer for its own presence. The card reads, word for word:

“This bird, like a swallow-tailed coat; his head slightly raised, and cool as an article of human existence; and you don’t get it home?” when it is upon him.

The manual’s reading, entered above the card and not crossed out, is this: the bird is the game’s only token that moves on its own — the token that sits on the collar of the long narrow coat of a country and raises its head slightly, which is the exact posture of a capital that has just been moved — and the swallow-tailed coat is the board itself, whose cut, the diagram shows, is the cut of a coat. A player on whom the bird is, when it is upon him, does not bring it home: the bird is not a point, it is an observation, and the observation stands in the record. The card is to be read, at the end of the game, as though it were correct, in a low voice; whoever cannot do so is shown to the sea door, which is the only door of the board that is always open, and which is the house’s standing way of saying goodbye to a player who has miscounted the tokens.

6. Scoring, and the closing rule

Scoring on the Union Board is run on the house’s usual arithmetic, which is the arithmetic of the canteen, not of the mint: a board is worth as many peaches as it takes to tell it, and the Union Board, longest and narrowest of the set, has come out at one can, 300 kyats, the same price the western valley country paid when it was told from the same counter — a price that has held across editions, which the manual enters as the only score in the set that has never varied. The closing rule of the sheet, for the player who intends to keep it, is the one the companion chapter kept for its own: the capital token is not the largest city token, the sea is not a border, and the bird, when it is upon him, is not to be brought home. The sheet is filed beside its companion, Chapter XIV, on Ayutthaya, 1351–1767, and beside the book’s other far entry, the storming of a gate on New Year’s Eve in Chapter LIX, because a board that leans to the south and a city taken at night are, in the house’s arithmetic, the same long course, and the table is left, as it is left everywhere in the set, for whoever comes in next — the whole shelf returning, in the usual manner, to the front page.
