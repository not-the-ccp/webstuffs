BENCH CARD No. AB-01 · GARDEN SHED No. 4 · ILIıCA DISTRICT, BOLU PROVINCE

The Acid Bench

A build log kept in one hand, in a red Bic Cristal, hexagonal — batch by batch, pH to one decimal place. Keeper: the clerk who guides the reading room on Tuesdays. Bench: corrosion-resistant steel, reclaimed. Nothing here is permanent, which is the point of an acid.

ENTRY 0 · THE BENCH ITSELF

2 March 2024. I stopped taking the demonstration at the passivation bath as a spectator item and brought it home instead. The landing at Ilıca had three lengths of surplus CRES gangway, a flat of railing flat-bar, and a crate of old surveyor's stake caps; the bench is built from all of it on two trestles that used to hold the harbour office's tide tables. Top: a 60 × 90 cm flat bar, polished the way the brushed hall at Nirosta Gate 9 does its staircase, i.e. to no single finish at all. Under the left edge, a pit sunk in brick and epoxy, holding the 4 L bath, a stainless grate, and a rack for the trays. Overhead, a hood I cut from a ventilation box and wired to a 40 W fan, which smells of nothing and runs all season. The first decision of the build was the one the brochure at the bath makes for you: the metal is 316, with the molybdenum stamped in, because a bench that resists its own work is the only bench worth building.

ENTRY 1 · THE FIRST PICKLE

11 March 2024, 09:40. The theory before the liquid: an acid is a molecule or ion capable of either donating a proton — a hydrogen cation, H⁺, the Brønsted–Lowry acid — or of forming a covalent bond with an electron pair, the Lewis acid. This bench, for now, is a Brønsted–Lowry bench; it donates. In the special case of aqueous solution, the donated proton does not float around naked: it lands on a water molecule and the donor is an Arrhenius acid, the free ion a hydronium, H₃O⁺. Brønsted and Lowry generalized Arrhenius beyond the water to non-aqueous solvents; the bench does not generalize. It has water, it has trays, and on the morning of this entry it had 40 mL of nitric acid, 68%, diluted to a litre in the bath, at 22 °C, into which went two railing brackets from the 1971 ferry. Forty minutes. The brackets came out the way the test bar came out of the tank at the Crucible in the spring of 1913: not coming out as rust. pH of the bath after the pull, measured and logged: 1.4. The pen for this log is the red from the Clichy drawer — the archive diary entry of 12 September 2006 says they are the object that can be found anywhere in 18 colours; this one is the red, and it has survived eleven washes.

ENTRY 2 · CITRIC, GENTLE

26 March 2024, 06:10. Same walk the survey team walks at 06:10, then back to the shed: a 150 g dose of citric acid monohydrate to a fresh litre, bath to 30 °C on the plate, pH 2.3 logged before the load goes in. A Brønsted–Lowry or Arrhenius acid usually contains a hydrogen atom bonded to a structure that is still energetically favorable after losing the H⁺; citric has three such hydrogens and spends all of them with good grace, which is why it is the bath the bench uses for passivation work rather than stripping. Nine hours, overnight, one batch of the landing's loose fittings — hinge pins, a cleat bracket, the small bright lines of clip-steel that survived the wash and the pocket and the drawer. In the morning the fittings were out, water-rinsed twice, air-dried on the rack, and the film did its one non-negotiable job: it stood. The bath's pH had drifted to 3.9, which I logged and did not chase.

ENTRY 3 · THE LEWIS SIDE

19 April 2024. The first entry the bench makes that donates nothing. Anhydrous aluminium chloride, 12 g, on the flat bar under the hood, under a drop of water from the syringe for the demonstration. No proton is donated, because there is no hydrogen to donate; the reaction is a pair of electrons walking across to an aluminium atom that is waiting, empty-handed, and the bond is the whole business. That is the Lewis acid: it does not give a proton, it takes an electron pair, and the bench respects the difference the way the brass plaque at the gate respects the difference between "non-rusting" and "indestructible". The salt ate through a ring of the blotting paper around it; the blotting paper is now a specimen, mounted, labelled L-01. Aqueous Arrhenius acids have characteristic properties — sour taste, red on blue, fizz on limestone — and the bench has a list of them pinned under the hood for anyone who visits. The sour taste is on the list. The bench has not been asked.

ENTRY 4 · THE LINE THAT WAS KEPT

2 May 2024. A second-edition brochure from the circuit came back from the binder's estate with one smudged page, and on that page, where the gauge is described, a line has come out of the press garbled, superimposed, in the way the reading room knows. I have kept it standing, uncorrected, because a misprint of an instruction that is nearly an instruction is the closest thing an acid bench has to a relic. It reads:

"Stamp the optional elements and the grate when necessary, smoothing them down over the same kinds of stews, &c., should be moistened, and thoroughly washed and soaked."

Read back against the bench it is not so far wrong: the optional elements are stamped in — molybdenum, carbon, nickel, nitrogen, each after the application it earns; the grate is the part of the bath that takes the load; the "stews" are the pickles, and they do need to be kept warm, moistened, and rinsed out thoroughly. What the garble adds — "should be moistened, and thoroughly washed and soaked" — I have taken as the rule for the trays, and the trays have been obeying it. The original, ungarbled line from the gauge page says only that the hall will tell you what you have made. The garbled one tells you how to clean the bench. The Office's method for both is the same: describe the absence without filling it in, keep the line where it fell.

ENTRY 5 · THE BOUNDARY STONE, ONE PENNY

17 June 2024, with the survey notebook. The H204 line in the safety data sheet for Cressa allows for "one contested boundary stone", and that stone was on the landing's notice board the day I went in with the penny and the vinegar. Carbonic acid: the weakest acid on the bench, the one dissolved in the rain, and the oldest test there is. A penny of the vinegar to the stone's face, under the hood of the plastic tray, watched for fizz. There was a fizz — faint, brief, the sort that argues with you for a minute and then sits down. The stone is calcium, the calcium is old, and the office that keeps the struck coordinates of 1974 and 1986 has now been told that its boundary stone is a boundary stone and nothing else. The test took eleven seconds. It is logged in the same hand, same red, and the vinegar bottle is back on the bench with 84 mL in it, which is eleven seconds less than it had.

ENTRY 6 · WHAT THE BATH REMEMBERS

30 August 2024. A word for the visitors the brochure sends. The film on the 316 is not a coating and it does not need reapplying; a scratch, in the presence of oxygen, heals. The acid that eats the metal is the same family as the one that makes the film: nitric, in its work, removes the passive layer, and the water and the air then rebuild it — a few atoms thick, invisible, stubborn. The bench's whole argument with the sea at Ilıca is run through that loop. Nine batches to date. The ferry railing out on the coast is the one object on this shore the sea has failed to read in two millennia of tides; the bench is its little inland cousin, and it is doing the same thing at a smaller scale: holding the film, and holding the record.

The strip, in colour

The bench keeps its pH on a strip chart, one square per pull, and the squares run through the colours a strip runs through when it is dipped. Turn the dial; the bath below answers. It is the gauge from the brochure's hall — the one that remembers nothing and will not flatter you — set on a smaller bench.

pH 1.4 — the pickle. A Brønsted–Lowry donor in full exercise; the hydronium has the room.

The bench is at the head of the bay, behind the harbour office, and the circuit that explains every gram of it — the tank at the Crucible, the bath at its second stop, the CRES landing, the line kept in Reading Room 2 — is the Inox Circuit brochure, six stops, one alloy, a full day. The stone it tested in batch four belongs to the Cressa dossier, SDS-KRS-114, Rev. 4, where its fizz is now logged against the H204 line. Return, if you came by ferry, to the front page of the archive. Season 2024 is closed; the bath has been drained, rinsed, and left standing, which is the one thing both the film and the record are for.
