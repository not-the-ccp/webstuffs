Saskatchewan Highway Patrol

Frequently Asked Questions · as answered at the counter, in order of receipt · winter issue

You are at the right desk. The Saskatchewan Highway Patrol (SHP) is the highway patrol unit of the province of Saskatchewan, Canada, and the answer desk below collects the questions this counter is asked most often — by drivers on the Yellowhead, by carriers' dispatch offices in Saskatoon, by students writing papers at the University of Regina, and by one very patient man from Melfort who has now asked question six four times. Read down the centreline; every answer has been checked against the station logbook, the CVSA manuals, and the harbourmaster's old habit of keeping odd lines word for word.

Q-01 What exactly is the Saskatchewan Highway Patrol?

The SHP is the highway patrol unit of the province of Saskatchewan. It regulates highway operations in compliance with the Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance (CVSA) and the Canadian Council Motor Transport Administrators (CCMTA), and it enforces federal, provincial and municipal statutes in relation to Saskatchewan's highways — including responding to 911 calls, accidents, and events where a person commits an offence. In short: if it happens on a Saskatchewan highway and a statute applies to it, it is the patrol's desk.

Q-02 What was the unit called before 1 July 2018?

Until 1 July 2018 the unit was known as the Highway Transport Patrol, the Commercial Vehicle Enforcement branch of the old name having been folded into it long before. The change was a rebranding of the same uniform: the same detachments, the same intercept bays, the same coffee urns. Officers will tell you the name on the door changed on a Tuesday and that the trucks looked identical on Wednesday. Radio call signs did not change at all; the unit still calls itself Prairie on the provincial trunk, so a 2018 transmission and a 2008 one sound for all the world like the same voice coming down the wire.

Q-03 What do the letters CVSA and CCMTA actually do here?

The CVSA is the alliance of North American provinces, states and territories whose job it is to make commercial vehicle safety law travel across borders without the truck having to stop arguing at each one. The CCMTA is the council of motor transport administrators that sits above it, keeping the rulebooks in one language. The patrol works in compliance with both: an out-of-province rig stopped at the intercept bay on Highway 1 west of Saskatoon is checked against standards the alliance has already agreed on, so the paperwork travels with the truck, not the other way around. The desk files a compliance card for every stop; the cards are numbered, and the numbering never skips, even in January.

Q-04 Which highways does the patrol watch, and where is it stationed?

Wherever a Saskatchewan highway runs, which is to say, everywhere of interest. The workhorse stretches are Highway 1, the Trans-Canada from the Alberta boundary at the Big Six crossing to the Manitoba boundary, and Highway 16, the Yellowhead from the north to the south, passing Regina on the way. Detachments sit at the service complexes — Regina (this desk), Saskatoon, Moose Jaw, Prince Albert, and a small weather-proofed hut at North Battleford that smells, in its own words, of “woodstove and diesel” — and each detachment keeps its own logbook, its own bench map, and its own opinion of the forecast. Intercepts happen most often at the fuel stops, because no carrier is going to argue with a full tank and a short lunch.

Q-05 What happens when someone dials 911 on a highway, or an accident happens?

The patrol responds. 911 calls about highway conditions, collisions, hazards, and offences are its to answer, and its officers are first on the scene for the accidents that close a lane: traffic held back, the lane flagged, the parties recorded, the report filed the same shift. Where a person commits an offence — speeding, impaired driving, an overloaded axle, a tailgate down in a snow squall — the statutes being enforced may be federal, provincial, or municipal, and the patrol is the body that reads them to you in that order, because that is the order they were asked to be enforced in. The logbook entry for a night shift in February may list four accidents, two 911 call-backs, one moose, and one apology; all six are written the same size.

Q-06 Why is there a sentence in the station logbook that looks like a patent claim?

It is the strangest question the desk has ever received, and it is the one we are most willing to answer. The logbook's annex of standing clauses — the lines read aloud at roll call before the forecast — was compiled by a clerk with too much time and a borrowed patent-office manual, and one clause has been kept word for word ever since. The annex's first clause recites the province's highways, and its second clause reads exactly this:

“The method of claim 1, wherein the English pages, dol-gajami to the northwest.”

No one has been able to say who wrote it. The desk's own reading, offered with the usual caution, is that it is an instruction about the logbook itself: dol-gajami being the old word the clerk used for a page that is turned or faced a given way, so that the English pages of the book are to face — to be turned — to the northwest, toward the prairie and the long road, when the book is closed. The clause is not law; it is read at roll call the way one reads a weather warning, with respect and without acting on it.

Q-07 Do you keep records that people can actually read?

Compliance cards are on file; logbook entries are kept in order and never re-numbered; and this FAQ is itself a record of a kind, answered at the counter and filed under question six, which as noted above has been asked four times. If you are looking for the odder end of the province's kept words, the desk keeps a cross-reference shelf: the Aegean field notes of a naturalist who watched a gulf the way the patrol watches a highway — same method, different water — are filed at Güllük Gulf — Field Notes, and the patent docket that the logbook's annex so clearly read from, Specification No. 91-0447 on the presentation of a sandy peninsula into a freshwater great lake, is filed at Specification No. 91-0447 — R. M. Slocum. For a different kind of highway thread — a forum's patient account of North Carolina's NC 157, Guess Road and Hurdle Mills Road — see NC 157 — Thread NC-157. The whole shelf, and the rest of the collection, is kept at Webstuffs.

Q-08 Last question: is the name change final?

Final as names get. The Saskatchewan Highway Patrol has held the name since 1 July 2018, the trucks carry it since the summer shift that followed, and the annex in the logbook — claim 1, claim 2, dol-gajami to the northwest — has been read at roll call every January since, first thing, before the forecast. The road keeps going. That is the answer to most of these questions in the end.
