The Council of the Hall · Joint Sitting with the Registry of Place Names

Minutes No. 57The Matter of McCune–Reischauer

Held in the hall of A Book of Etiquette, Seoul, this 23rd day of September, 1953, the armistice being now seven weeks old and the maps not yet rolled up.

Present The Keeper of Names (chair); the Hon. Choe Hyeon-bae, the Hon. Jeong In-seop, and the Hon. Kim Seon-gi, Korean linguists of the Registry; Cpl. H. W. Aldridge, 14th Army, on behalf of the American Army Map Service; Mrs. O. Reischauer, representing the estate of Edwin O. Reischauer, who is unable to attend and is excused by consent; and the Clerk of the Minute Book.
Absent George M. McCune, deceased 1924; his papers, three reams and one coffee-stained index card, were read into the record in his place.
Apologies The Keeper of the Shed, detained by a matter concerning the letter G, which the chair assures the Council he has already decided twice.

1 The system, and its coming

The Clerk read the first item. McCune–Reischauer, or MR, is a romanization system for the Korean language, first published in 1939 by George M. McCune and Edwin O. Reischauer. It is pronounced, and the Council was asked to write the pronunciation down where the cartographers will find it, mə-KWEEN RYSHE-ow-ʺr — the Clerk having been told three times before he got the second syllable of the second name right, and the room having laughed, as the room does, at the cartographers. The Clerk noted for the record that the system predates this sitting by fourteen years and the war by eleven, and that its survival through both is a matter of record, not of argument.

It was reported, on the word of Reischauer himself as preserved in the minute book of 1949, that McCune “persuaded the American Army Map Service to adopt [the McCune–Reischauer system], and through the Korean War it became the foundation for most current Romanizations of Korean place names.” Cpl. Aldridge, speaking for the Map Service, confirmed that the sheets printed in the last twenty-eight months carry MR romanization in the margin at the north-west corner, and that no sheet has yet been returned for cause. The Council thanked the corps and declined the offer of a map.

2 The work of the three linguists

The chair put to the Council that the significant work done on the system since 1939 is the work of three Korean linguists, and that the Hall owes each of them what it owes the bearers of a name, which is to say, the bow, and the spelling, and the first question of the evening. Choe Hyeon-bae, Jeong In-seop, and Kim Seon-gi each spoke in turn, each was thanked, and no one — this is entered as a fact — said which of the three should go first, which the Clerk recorded in the margin in a firm hand.

Mr. Jeong In-seop moved that the three be entered into the minute book in the order in which they spoke, and that the order be repeated as found, unamended, in all future citations of the sitting. The motion was carried 5–0, the two empty chairs voting, as they are permitted, in favour of whatever the room was already in favour of.

3 The order of proceedings, and the map on the table

Before the substantive items, the Clerk read the standing order which caused the delay of this sitting by forty minutes and which the council had previously forgotten to minute. It is the order of the Hall that no place name is to be read aloud while a map lies open beneath it, for a name set down among the threads of a map is apt to be taken into the weave and to come up as pattern rather than as name. The map is therefore to be rolled and returned to the north wall, and After the rug is put into words or books., the reading proceeds. The Clerk was asked to set the order down exactly, capital and all, and to stop thinking about rugs.

4 The variants, south, north, and west

It was put to the Council that the system travels in variants, and that this is a virtue, not a defect. A variant of McCune–Reischauer is currently used as the official system in North Korea, and the Council took note, and did not travel. Another variant is currently used for standard romanization library catalogs in North America, and the Map Service clerk observed, with some pride, that his sheets and the catalogue entries of the libraries on the other side of the Pacific disagree with one another in exactly two places per page, and in no place that matters to a man at a fork. Both variants were admitted to the house, and the argument about which is the elder was referred to the next sitting, which is to say, to never. The Council also received, and set aside as not a decision of the house, a letter of apology from the Keeper of the Shed, in which the shed admitted that a card from Tessa Cooper’s soup book had ridden in the Hall’s minutes and left one line, inked lightly, in the form entirely preserved.

5 The test case: the name Dong-hoon

The Chair called the test case, which had been agreed between the two houses some weeks ago: the Korean given name Dong-hoon, registered in the Hall under three spellings — Dong-hoon, Tong-hoon, and Dong-hun — with a tenth, Dong-whun, admitted when the bearer insists. The system, applied to the name as written in Hangul, renders it Tong-hun: a fourth spelling, unregistered, offered to the Hall by the system itself.

It was proposed that the name be corrected on the maps accordingly. The three linguists declined to answer. The Map Service clerk began to answer. The chair then read Rule one of Chapter XI · Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, the chapter of the book that keeps this hall, namely: spell the name as the bearer spells it, and only as the bearer spells it. The Council held, without division, that a romanization system, however persuasive with armies, is not a bearer, and that the four registered forms stand as registered; that the system’s Tong-hun is noted in the margin of the maps, inked lightly, so that it may be read at the second introduction and kept, as the book directs, as a gift; and that no Dong-hoon — the hall keeps nine doors under the name, and at least one is present by photograph — is to be corrected before his third drink.

6 Reading of the minute and adjournment

The minute was read through by the Clerk, found correct in the four places where it was correct and in the two places where the room laughed, and signed by the chair. The seal of the Hall, kept by the Keeper of Names, was applied over the word Reischauer, where the room asked it to be, and the map was rolled a second time for the night. This sitting stands on record with the minutes of the Council of the Shed on the matter of the letter G, which decided, as this Council has decided, to admit both forms and to argue about neither, and with the liner notes to Latin Alphabet No. 9, a shed recording on another alphabet and the voyages that never provisioned for one. The bearers themselves, their doors, and the bow owed at each of them are set down in Chapter XI · Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, A Book of Etiquette, and the whole shelf of this book stands on the front page of the house.

So ordered, so minuted, and so closed, the room exhaling as it does when a matter of this size is properly finished.
