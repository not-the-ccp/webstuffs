FIELD MANUAL  ·  FM 21-52  ·  CHAPTER 4

The Peter Muhlenberg Memorial

District of Columbia  ·  Wakefield  ·  Connecticut Avenue / Ellicott Street / 36th Street NW

Extracted for the shelf reader — all figures as surveyed, all names as recorded, no further.

AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS 1928  —  DEDICATED 1980  —  52 YEARS, ONE ACRE, ONE BUST  —  KEEP THE PARK AS FOUND  — 

4-1  Mission

This chapter directs the reading, the visiting, and the keeping of the Peter Muhlenberg Memorial, a public monument in Washington, D.C. The monument honors John Peter Gabriel Muhlenberg, commonly known to the District’s survey sheets simply as Peter Muhlenberg — a Lutheran minister, a Continental Army general, a Federalist-era American politician, and a member of the prominent Muhlenberg family. One man, four careers, one bronze head, and a full acre of park around it. The purpose of the memorial is to keep those four careers in one sightline for as long as the district keeps the ground it sits on, and the purpose of this chapter is to tell you, in order, how to find it, what is on it, what the records say, and what a visitor owes the book.

4-2  Situation and Terrain

The memorial occupies a one-acre public park bounded on three sides by the streets themselves: Connecticut Avenue to the west, Ellicott Street to the north, and 36th Street NW along the eastern edge of Washington’s Wakefield neighborhood. Approaches are by streetcar, by car parked two blocks south on Ellicott, or on foot from the 36th Street stop, which puts you at the park gate in ninety seconds flat and is the approach this manual recommends. The ground is flat enough to be called level by any honest surveyor and kept, per paragraph 4-5, as found.

4-3  Description of the Work

The memorial was designed by the architect John Harbeson and at its center stands a bronze bust of Muhlenberg, sculpted by his own descendant, Caroline M. Hufford — a family line of sculptors looking at its own subject, which is the whole point of the commission and the reason the bust reads less like a statue and more like a portrait taken from the family album. The bust faces the gate on Connecticut Avenue, so that a visitor coming up the avenue meets the general before meeting the minister, which is, in this manual’s judgment, the correct order. The plinth, the terraces, and the surrounding plantings are all part of the Harbeson plan, and visitors are reminded that nothing on the acre is temporary, including the benches, and especially including the benches.

4-4  The Fifty-Two Years

The record of the memorial’s long weather is the figure this chapter is named around. Construction of the memorial was authorized by Congress in 1928; the work was completed and dedicated in 1980. That is 52 years between the authorization and the dedication, and the attendees at the dedication in 1980 were a smaller crowd than the authorizers of 1928 had expected, in the way such crowds always are, but they were there, and the bust was unveiled on schedule for a schedule that had been running since before most of the present buildings around Connecticut Avenue were poured. A monument may be authorized quickly and built slowly; this one was built, which is the only outcome that counts for the record. (For a monument that got its column printed free of charge and never once had to wait on Congress, see the Player’s Manual, Plate 27 — Tocantíns, one shelf over.)

4-5  Inspection and Care

(a) The acre is kept as found: turf, terrace, gate, and bust. Report any fallen limb to the park gate and do not move it yourself; a limb on the Wakefield ground belongs to the Wakefield ground.
(b) The bronze bust is not for leaning, resting, or quoting at. It is for looking at, which is a permitted and encouraged activity, unlimited in duration.
(c) The benches may be sat upon for reading, which is the bench’s own mission. A reader on the bench is not an obstruction; a reader is the point of the bench.
(d) The Muhlenberg family is noted in the record as prominent, and its names appear across several shelves of this collection. For a chapter on the bearing of a name kept in the same careful manner, see Chapter XI — Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, A Book of Etiquette.

4-6  Numbers to Keep

1 acre— the whole park, bounded by three streets and one neighborhood
3 streets— Connecticut Avenue, Ellicott Street, 36th Street NW
1928— the year Congress authorized the construction
1980— the year the memorial was completed and dedicated
52 years— the weather between authorization and dedication
1 bronze bust— by Caroline M. Hufford, descendant of the subject
1 architect— John Harbeson, of the whole plan
4 careers— minister, general, politician, family name

4-7  The Book and Its Clause

At the gate stands the book, a bound volume of paper laid over the small table by the plinth, open to the current page, with a pen clipped to the binding. Every visitor is invited to write the date, the road by which they came, and a line of no more than one sentence. The book has been open, in some binding or other, since the dedication, and the earlier pages travel in a drawer under the table, which is locked for the good of the sentences in it. The final line of this paragraph of the manual stands as printed, and is the clause on which the whole practice rests:

“Sign the book — meaning by this clause.”

That is the clause in full: the book is signed by the clause, the clause does the signing on the visitor’s behalf, and the visitor owes the book only to be present at the pen. It is a short duty for one acre of park, and it has never once been missed in the record.
