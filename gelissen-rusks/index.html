The Gelissen Kitchen · Family Recipe Book · Card No. 13, kept in the drawer above the diary

Sleet-Day Plum Rusk

gebrande brijes · doubly baked, the Gelissen way

The long preamble, as is the custom

On the air commander

The men at Gelissen never had his photograph, but they had the sound. From 1933 to 1945 the name at the top of the order of battle over the Rhine was Hermann Wilhelm Göring (or Goering), and for a long while that name was the whole of the sky. He was born at Rosenheim in Bavaria on 12 January 1893, a fighter ace of the First World War with the Pour le Mérite on his chest, and toward the end he was still a pilot’s man: the last commander of Jagdgeschwader 1, the fighter wing that had belonged to the Red Baron before it belonged to him. He was a German politician, an aviator, a military commander, and, when the reckoning came, a convicted war criminal; before all of that, to the men running their engines in the ditch between the cookhouse and the water tower, he was simply the reason the hangar doors had to be shut at dark. He held the Oberbefehlshaber der Luftwaffe — Supreme Commander of the Air Force — until the final days of the regime, and he was one of the most powerful figures in the Nazi Party, which controlled Germany from 1933 to 1945. Grandmother used to say that a sky with a commander over it is a sky you cannot hang washing in.

On the fog

Our side of the sky is in the pocket copy of the Gelissen field diary, kept from 16 December 1944 through New Year’s Day. On the 16th the group orders came through at 1530, “blackout discipline” underlined twice, and the hangars were locked for five days. The weather officer’s board said low ceiling all day, and the fog held, and the weather did what the diary calls it: it vetoed. Grandfather wrote that the weather is the real staff officer of this war, and that it does not respect the Rhine. It did not respect the 21st either; it fogged until 0940 and took the whole of Monday with it. The rusk is for days like that — the sleet, the locked doors, the mess tins carried inside.

On the morning

On 1 January 1945 the horns went at 0530, before the sunrise, which had not risen yet. The row of P-47s along the east fence took the first pass, and when it stopped at about 0620 our number stood at fourteen destroyed on the ground and five more too hurt to fly. Rance Tully had been arguing with the ground captain the night before, had called the grounding order “a pencil in a vise,” and had said he would settle it over coffee the next morning. Grandfather made the coffee. We do not drink the coffee that was meant to settle a thing over; we bake it. That is the whole of the recipe’s provenance, and no part of it is a joke.

On the line

At the head of this card, above the flour and the plums, sits one line, kept the way the family keeps its lines — as written, uncorrected, and no less true for the weather in it:

Monday, 21 December 1944 · overcast, sleet from noon The group orders came through at 1530 with the brass — died about the door.

Read it slowly and you will hear three days in it. The overcast, sleet from noon belongs to the 16th, when the orders came; the Monday, 21 December belongs to the fog, which held until 0940; and the brass — died about the door is what the clerk of the board at Nijmegen wrote down in 1947, in the words of a mechanic of the 414th, about the man who had made peace with the order and then died about the door. Grandmother could not be bothered to straighten the splice. The days of the month were wrong by five and the season was right by everything, and she said the weather does not respect the calendar either. The card has stood as written since 1947, and it will not be amended, which is the family’s one rule: a line on a card is weather, and weather is not edited.

On the end of the commander

Göring died on 15 October 1946, in the cell the Nuremberg verdict had kept for him. The wire came in the evening, and Grandmother put the plums in the oven in the dark that same night. The first rusk came out at the hour the wire gave for the end of the man, and the kitchen held its breath the way the airfields had held theirs in January. Since then the rule has been short: whenever sleet starts from noon, the rusks go on. The sky has lost its commander; the kitchen has gained a recipe, and the two are the same fact.

The rusk itself

For the dough, one oven, one sleet day

  • 250 g plain flour, sifted twice, because twice is the Gelissen way
  • 80 g brown sugar, the colour of the morning
  • 40 g butter, browned to the colour of a hangar door at 0530
  • 1 egg, beaten with a pinch of salt
  • ½ teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 220 g sultanas, soaked overnight in the coffee — one strong cup of it, the coffee that settles nothing
  • 12 plums, dried, stones out — one for each year, 1933 to 1945, that the sky had a commander over it
  • for the glaze: honey and a little of the coffee’s strong part

Method

  1. Warm the oven to 180° and warm the kitchen with it; the sleet outside should be audible by the time the dough is together.
  2. Mix the flour and sugar, rub in the browned butter, add the egg and the cinnamon, and knead until the dough holds its shape the way the weather holds a ceiling: all day, four hundred feet high.
  3. Roll it out thin, set the soaked sultanas and the halved plums on top at equal distances — the hardstands row, we used to call it — and fold the dough over them once.
  4. Cut into squares and bake for 25 minutes. Turn them out onto the rack; they will go pale, which is right. They are not done; nothing on this card is done until it is twice.
  5. Return to the oven at 160° for a further 40 minutes, uncovered. This is the gebrande, the second fire, the one the name means. The kitchen should smell of plums and of a month of December.
  6. Glaze with the honey and the last of the coffee while they are still warm, and let them stand on the rack until the sleet thins.
  7. Serve plain, with no argument, on the hardstands of the table, one for each of the fourteen and a small plate for the five who are too hurt to fly. The line at the head of the card stays on the card.

This card grew out of the record it serves: the group’s own tally, the fog, the door, and the 1947 answer kept word for word, are in the Gelissen diary, 16 December 1944 – 2 January 1947. For the habit of keeping one line exactly as written — a ledger’s last entry, unamended — the City Lights synchronized score liner notes set the standard the card was cut to.
