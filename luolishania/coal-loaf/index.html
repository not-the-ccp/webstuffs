Carboniferous Coal Loaf

koh-ruh-BIF-er-us · kohal · “KAR–bə – NIF – ʻr – ʻs”

Yields one loaf (3×9×5 pan) · Serves the whole household, which is to say everyone who was cancelled out of something

I. On the Period

The Carboniferous (KARəbʻNIFʻrʻs) is a geologic period and system of the Paleozoic era that spans sixty million years, from the end of the Devonian Period at 358.86 Ma — million years ago — to the beginning of the Permian Period at 298.9 Ma. It is the fifth period of the Phanerozoic eon. In North America the family always split it, the way the states split the bread: the earlier Mississippian and the later Pennsylvanian, two separate geological periods with one long dinner. The name means “coal-bearing”, from the Latin carbō, coal, and ferō, to bear or carry, and it refers to the many coal beds formed globally during that time. The first of the modern system names, it was coined by the geologists William Conybeare and William Phillips in 1822, who looked at a black seam in the ground and thought the honest word was coal.

Sixty million years is not a cooking time. It is a keeping time. In those sixty million years the forests stood for so long without frost that the dead trees never finished rotting; they sank into the mud of the swamps, layer on layer, and the mud closed over them the way a lid closes over a pot, and the pressure of the earth did what our oven will not. What it did was make coal. Which is to say: the recipe's history is sixty million years long, and it has been resting all along.

II. On the Family

Our side of the family has been coal-bearing in the second sense. Great-grandmother Etta Collins was raised on a Creole corner of New Orleans where the coal man came on Mondays down Magazine Street with a wheelbarrow and a song, and she measured the winter in loads of it. She carried the custom to Inglewood, California, where the winters are mild and the custom did not need the cold. The ledger itself began in 1951 as a notebook of receipts from the bakery she ran, and it has grown ever since, one cancelled thing at a time.

The family rule Etta set down on the first page is short: whatever is voided, bake. A wedding that fell through gets a wedding cake. A ship that never leaves the harbour — we know the rigging, we have seen the rigging, kept at the clubhouse above the bakery on Magsaysay Avenue — gets a sea-biscuit. And in August of 1980, when the Games of the XXII Olympiad in Moscow went dark and the apparatus stood untouched in Luzhniki, the rule found its fullest test. A gymnast's rotation, scheduled and never performed, is a void with a date on it. Etta did not argue with it. She preheated the oven to 325 and made this loaf, and she wrote the date in the margin in the same hand she used for her yeast, so that the year 1980 sits in this card exactly the way 358.86 sits in the period: as a boundary, not a beginning.

It is in the ledger's next entry that the two records meet. The dream dictionary we keep by the stove — Entry 314, for Luolishania, the armored lobopodian walking eleven pairs of legs through Lower Cambrian mud — lists among its family a genus called Collinsium. No one in the family has ever explained why the armored little animal is named for us. We suspect it is the same reason the period is named for what it carries: some registrar somewhere looked at the family and wrote down what it was bearing.

III. On the Ledger's Error

The card is typeset in the ledger's cramped hand, and the second line was misprinted: a page out of the system notice archive slipped into the margin, and the compositor set it straight into the recipe. The household reads it as the secret ingredient, which it is. As it stands, word for word, it reads:

INGREDIENTS.--3/4 pint of the XXII Olympiad, Moscow, USSR Scheduled 19–3 August 1980 Cause of void United States Olympic gymnastics team.

Etta read the line out loud at the table and laughed until the kettle sang. “Three-quarters of a pint of a Games that never happened,” she said, “is the same measure as three-quarters of a pint of air. Whisk it well and it's a leaven.” The family has baked to that measure every August since, and the loaf has never once failed to rise, which the geologists will tell you is more than they can say for a bed of coal.

IV. The Loaf

Ingredients

  • 3 cups dark beet, grated fine (the red keeps the seams from looking dead)
  • 1 1/4 cups blackstrap molasses, warm in a double boiler
  • 2 cups dark rye flour and 1/2 cup all-purpose, sifted together twice
  • 3/4 teaspoon cinnamon, 1/2 teaspoon ground ginger, 1/4 teaspoon mace
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda, dissolved in the molasses
  • 3 eggs, one of them saved for the wash
  • 1/2 cup cold butter, cubed and cut into the dry in one motion
  • a pinch of salt, no more; the coal is already bitter
  • 3/4 pint of void, whisked to a leaven, per the line above

Method

  1. Set the oven to 325°F. Line the 3×9×5 pan; the loaf is dense and does not like to be lifted.
  2. Whisk the void first, over the empty bowl, for a full minute. Do not skip this. It is the rise.
  3. Beat the molasses, the grated beet, and two eggs until the color is the color of swamp mud in the dark.
  4. Fold in the flour, the soda, and the spices in three additions, beating only until the seams disappear — the crumb should keep its strata, Mississippian at the bottom, Pennsylvanian at the top.
  5. Cut the cold butter in until the crumb holds flake by flake, like sclerite on the soft body beneath. A slab of this loaf, set on a table, should look exactly like the rock: plates of armor around something tender.
  6. Bake 60 minutes, until a knife goes in clean and comes out carrying the year 1980.
  7. Wash the top with the saved egg, and dust the crust until it shines like the Chengjiang slabs do before the light hits them wrong. Cool in the pan, then turn out on the board with no ceremony.

V. On Keeping

Keep it wrapped in wax paper for up to six days; on the seventh it is coal, and coal does not spoil, it only waits. It keeps the way the period keeps: for sixty million years, bearing what it carries. If a dream of the armored one finds you the night you bake — eleven pairs of legs crossing the kitchen floor, plates of sclerite catching the oven light — leave the door open. It is only checking whether the exoskeleton still fits, and it is only the loaf, splitting gently at the seam the way the dictionary says all thresholds do.
