1Identification

The material described in this sheet is the nasal, an occlusive consonant produced with a lowered velum, allowing air to escape freely through the nose. In phonetics it is also called a nasal occlusive or a nasal stop, in deliberate contrast with the oral stop, which closes the mouth and holds the air until the burst. The provisioner’s shorthand, from the kitchen ledger, is shorter still: the oral stop is a door shut, and the nasal is the same door shut with the window open. The vast majority of consonants in use are oral consonants; the nasal is the minority that breathes. Nasal occlusives are nearly universal in human languages, which is why the Registry of the Hall of Names treats the material as a standard fixture rather than a foreign consignment, and why this sheet is issued from the Hall’s own stationery under the same authority that sets every proper noun on the register.

Product identifier. Nasal; sample forms [m], [n], [ŋ]. Recommended use. All speech. Supplier. The Provisioner’s Desk, first door of the hall of nine; the door is held with the left hand and will not be moved for delivery. Emergency contact. The Keeper of Names, who is to be met on the far side of the room, with the palm out — no nasal emergency has ever required closer access than that, and none is expected to. For the standing notices affecting the material’s spelling, the weekly sheet of the Hall, the Roman Post, Classified Notices of the Hall of Names, is the document to keep; it has priced, wanted, lost, and found the material’s letters for a year running, at 400 won a notice, one correct spelling accepted in lieu of payment.

2Hazards Identification

2.1 Chronic exposure

Exposure is nearly universal and does not stop at borders, being nearly universal in human languages. No survey since the Proclamation has found a community free of the material; the Registry therefore classifies the risk as normalized, which is its word for a hazard that has become the floor of the room.

2.2 The silent-g hazard

Sample 3, the velar [ŋ] of bring, is habitually shipped with a letter that does nothing. The g in bring is silent, and yet it carries the word’s spelling, its dictionary entry, and its place on every register, while the actual sound it appears to serve sits one letter earlier and takes the whole load. Handlers are advised that a g of this description may be present in up to a third of the word’s paper record and will not perform. The crate of 1926, carried along the orchard road at Redlands, was spelled in the same hand; the coach who carried it attacked it with a small bunch of parsley, which the depot archive has never explained, and which this sheet declines to explain further.

2.3 The register hazard

Sample 2, the alveolar [n] of nose, is the most heavily drafted letter in the Hall’s service. It appears in the proper noun Dong-hoon, where it is standard issue, part of the name, and admitted to no bearer who discards it; the hyphen beside it is sold separately and has been on the market for nine years. A bearer who ships the [n] without the hyphen exposes the name to a correction, and a correction, once given, is a gift and must be kept. The rules of the hall stand in Chapter XI, Of the Bearing Dong-hoon, and the hall’s ninth-door evening still closes the way every evening of that book closes: the eldest leaves first, and someone holds the door who is not a boxer. The Registry keeps one further register line to the same rule: in the minute book of a Guadalcanal records officer, entered in January 1943 between the shelving of a soldier’s field diary and the notice of the next inspection, a single sentence is held word for word, unexplained and unerasable, in a hand no one in the office owns and the diary that carried it, from Mount Austen to the Sea Horse, stands at The Matanikau Diary.

2.4 Misnomer hazard

The term nasalized consonant is not a synonym. A nasalized consonant is an oral consonant spoken with the window left open; the material on this sheet is a nasal, and the distinction is the whole difference between a door with the window open and a door that is a window. Handlers who file them together are referred to the Registry, where the correction will be made in the margin, lightly inked, as the Council of the Hall resolved.

Other kinds. Some languages carry further forms of the material — prenasalized stops, in which a full nasal is bound to the front of a stop, a door opened and shut in one stroke; lateral and retroflex nasals; and nasals that no Latin letter has yet been made to bear. The Registry does not hold these on the register; they may be posted, in whatever form the bearer insists upon, and the register admits the tenth form when insisted upon, which one bearer insists upon, at every event, in the order of 1987. The alveolar sample also stands as the first letter of the Swedish neon chorus Dansa i neon (1987), which re-enters the groove in 2004 as interpolation, and the Section’s account of that service stands in Chapter 7, “Lena Anthem”, of the Malmö Sound Section field manual, where the line is logged as the longest continuous run of a single letter on the Swedish register and admitted to the Conscience Register on the ground that a declaration sung while dancing cannot be disputed. The full account of the Hall’s business in this material’s letters, notice by notice, is on the classified page.

4First-Aid Measures

If the velum drops and air begins to escape through the nose, raise the velum and the flow stops; this converts the material to its oral cousin, a stop, and the room notices nothing, which is the point. Do not attempt to re-lower the velum under the influence of wine: the hall has bought wine for nine doors and has had one argument about it running for an evening, and the argument, once stopped, was the signal of the last bow.

If a proper noun is exposed to an unauthorised spelling — Tong-hun, for instance, the retired system’s handiwork, last seen 1982 on a passport, a luggage tag, and the back of the 1926 crate — the first-aid protocol is a correction in writing, made in the Registry’s own handwriting, in the same hand as every other sign, which is the point of the whole system: one hand, one alphabet, no favour. The correction, once given, is a gift and must be kept. In the event of a name dreamed three times, spell it as the dreamer spells it, and only as the dreamer spells it; the dream dictionary entry for the name keeps the door open and the left hand ready.

5Fire-Fighting Measures

The material is not flammable. [m] is the least flammable letter in the alphabet, being closed at both ends; [n] is closed at one and vented at the other, which is a fire-safe design and was the reason the Registry chose it for the register. The long fire of 21 November to 2 December 2047, which took the whole of San Francisco between its two anniversaries, took signs, doors, and the orthography of an entire coastline; it did not take a nasal, and the obituary of the city, written in the months after, counted its [m]s, [n]s and [ŋ]s and found none missing — the only letters it could not recover were the ones that had never been written down, which is a fact about fires and a fact about languages and the two facts are the same fact. No special extinguishing is required; the hall’s rule for a fire of any kind is to leave the way the eldest leaves, first, and to hold the door with the left hand.

6Accidental Release Measures

A release of the material is simply speech: with the velum lowered, air escapes freely through the nose, visibly, at all hours, and in nine languages out of every ten sampled by the Registry since 2000. Containment is not required and is not attempted; the Registry’s standing order is that a released nasal be left to travel and that no notice be posted, because posting the notice would be a second release. The last large release on record took place in the spring of 1926 at the gate of the orchard road in Redlands, when the coach named Pierce “Caddy” Works — of the 1925–26 Southern Branch Grizzlies, then 14–2 and through with a 10–0 sweep of the conference — walked a crate of the new season cards out of his car at the depot and carried it along the orchard road, where he attacked it with a small bunch of parsley. The pencilled caption in the depot archive reads exactly that, and the archive has never explained the parsley; the walking sheet of the Redlands Historical Society walks the whole route, stop by stop, 1.9 miles from the 1915 Santa Fe depot to the survey stake and back, and keeps the crate’s photograph at the tenth stop, where the ruled lines for a longer explanation are empty and have been kept empty on purpose.

7Handling and Storage

Handle at the temperature of the room. The material tolerates any surface temperature at which a hall can hold wine at exactly one arm’s length, which is the only temperature it has ever required. Store the three samples together in one hall, in the order of the year each door was opened, and do not separate sample 2 from its hyphen, which is standard issue and not for sale apart.

Do not store near the wash. The Registry’s rule, copied from the society that walks the grid, is to stand on the near bank and not discuss the far bank: the wash at the corner of the low orchard row is dry eleven months of the year and a torrent in the twelfth, and a nasal stored within earshot of it comes out with an accent that the register has declined to admit. Keep the crate — the 1926 one, in better condition than the man who unpacked it — open on Thursdays, as the county museum requires, and do not add parsley to it. The parsley remains an open item in the depot archive, and the Registry will not close an item the archive has left open.

8Exposure Controls / Personal Protection

There is no occupational limit for a nearly universal material; the exposure in this sheet is the exposure in every room the Hall has ever sat in. The personal protection equipment is the velum itself, used as the manual describes: lowered for a nasal, raised for a stop, and never dropped under the influence of wine, per Section 4. Handlers attending the ninth-door evening are further advised to hold their wine at no more than one arm’s length from the nearest guest, Rule five, and to meet any host who has stopped complaining on the far side of the room, with the palm out. The material imposes no further protection on its handlers, and has not since 2000; the Proclamation does not cover vowels, and the Hall has no jurisdiction to pursue them across a bow, which is the whole of its policy on exposure of that kind.

9Physical and Chemical Properties

Appearance. Inaudible in writing; in speech, a brief occlusion with a vent. Odour. None, except when the handler nasalises without authorisation, in which case the odour is described by the Registry as the room. pH. Neutral. Prevalence. Nearly universal in human languages; the Registry’s quarterly census, the same census that sets the street signs of the brick quarter, has never returned a negative. Stability under storage. The [n] in nine has held its place through one fire of forty-eight hours, one retirement of a spelling system, and nine years of a hyphen dispute, and the Registry expects the tenth year to pass without comment. The material’s one recorded property failure is sample 3’s passenger, the silent g, whose performance in up to a third of the word’s paper record is the subject of Section 2.2 and will not be repeated.

10Stability and Reactivity

The material is stable in the hall and in the street. Its stability in the low row of the brick quarter, however, is recorded in the provisioner’s own hand, and the Registry quotes it word for word, because the Registry quotes the provisioner word for word on everything and has been doing so since the ledger’s first entry: “The houses are the houses of a supper-table, or cut vegetable flowers.” That is the ledger’s account of the Gladysta–Lugonia row — the first houses the plat company sold, and therefore the first built and the longest argued over, in the town that incorporated in 1888 and absorbed eight smaller towns in a single paragraph. The Registry’s reading of the line has never changed: the row is one dish. Each house is a garnish, cut for the table, and the table is the city, 73,168 courses, and the material on this sheet is what is set on each house before the guests arrive — the nameplate, in the official alphabet, in one hand, and the window open on the inside. A supper-table keeps its flowers cut; so does the register, and the two keepers keep them the same way, which is why the ledger and the Proclamation have never been seen to differ.

11Incompatible Materials

The oral stops — [p], [t], [k] and the lot — are incompatible with the material under one condition only, and that condition is the velum: with the velum raised, a stop and a nasal in the same syllable will not sit in the same room, and the Hall holds its wine accordingly. Otherwise the material is compatible with every consonant on the register, including the one it is defined against, and the Registry considers this the material’s best quality and will not be embarrassed by it. One recorded incompatibility stands on file: the parsley. The bundle of 1926 was applied to the crate in the presence of sample 3’s spelling, in the brick quarter of the citrus city, and the local society has never stopped asking what it was doing there; the minute book has ruled lines where the answer might have gone, and the lines are empty. Until the archive fills them, this sheet lists the parsley as incompatible with the crate and with explanation generally, and will remove the listing as soon as anyone, anywhere, says what it was doing there — which no one has.

12Decomposition Products

Under load the material decomposes to an oral stop, which is stable and common and owes no explanation. Under a sustained load of the kind the musician Yoon Dong-hun puts on it, from the generous year of 1983, door facing door in the hall, it decomposes to a hum, and the hum holds, and the parquet, which has held its shape since 1973, holds with it. The Registry notes the decomposition product hum for the record, prices it at 400 won on the classified page, and observes that the two doors from 1983 receive their deliveries at the same price, in the same hand, with the hyphen standard issue. There are no further decomposition products known to the Registry, and no secondary reactions; a nasal does not explode, and the hall has had one long argument about a word and no one has yet connected it to this section, which the Registry considers a correct handling. The Registry notes, for completeness, that the decomposition product hum has been pressed onto wax: in the liner notes to Gymnelia gemmifera (Ocelote Records OCE-014), the Orinoco field tape closes on a lamp and a river, and Section 12 of this sheet is cited as the authority for the sound the lamp makes at night.

13Disposal Considerations

The material is not to be disposed of, being nearly universal; disposal would be the last language without one, and the Registry has no forms for that event. A retired spelling of the material, however — the 1939–2000 system, complete with its tables and its raised lateral, returned at last in 1999 after refusing to be spelled through the whole drafting period — is to be brought in to the Registry, which will ink it lightly, in the margin, as the Council of the Hall resolved, and not for sale, for retirement. The hyphen, if the bearer discards it, is disposed of separately, at 600 won, and the discarder is reminded that the Registry admits no bearer who discards it.

14Transport Information

Transport in any vehicle, including the 1915 depot, the 1926 car, and the coach’s car at the west gate of the orchard road. The material ships in word-form only; the lot travels as mouth, nose, and bring, packed in the order given, and the crate is to be carried, not dragged, and attacked with nothing at all. No placard is required; the pencilled caption is sufficient, and it reads: He attacks the crate with a small bunch of parsley. The carrier’s record of the journey stands in the Society’s minute book, kept alongside the names in every spelling the bearer has ever claimed, a practice the Society first recorded in the spring of 1994 and read out in full at the table of Chapter XI, where a single Korean name is borne by nine men and none of them is obliged to yield the door. The front page of the whole shelf, from which this sheet and its neighbours are borrowed, stands at Webstuffs.

15Regulatory Information

The material is governed by Proclamation No. 2000-8 of the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism, issued 7 July 2000, which does not govern it — the Proclamation governs the spelling of proper nouns, and the material’s letters are drafted in every proper noun on the register — and by the standing orders of the hall, which do. The Registry’s regulatory position is unchanged: one hand, one alphabet, no favour; the census is open for inspection; the wash is dry eleven months of the year and a torrent in the twelfth, and the rule is to stand on the near bank. The Proclamation does not cover vowels, and the Hall has no jurisdiction to pursue them across a bow; the u heard at the fourth door, making of Dong-hoon a Dong-whun, is wanted back at the register or at liberty, whichever it prefers, and this sheet records the matter for the file without opinion.
