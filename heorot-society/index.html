Newsletter No. 9 · Winter · issued from the back room of the parish hall, where the door is still the wrong colour

A small club of six, reading Beowulf at one folio a month, in the old English, with glosses, without apology.

1 · From the EditorThe Nine Hundred and Sixty-Third Folio, or, Why We Read It Standing

Friends of the Gif — our reading of the poem passes this winter through the middle of its oldest copy, the Nowell Codex, the single manuscript of Beowulf that exists, and the only reason we have ever heard it at all. The poem is an epic in the old Germanic manner of heroic legend, alliterative from first line to last, three thousand one hundred and eighty-two lines of it in the codex, and one of the most important — and most often translated — works in the whole of Old English literature. What is in it, for those who have not yet come to the hall: Beowulf, a hero of the Geats, crosses the water to the aid of Hrothgar, king of the Danes, whose mead hall Heorot, the glory-house of the north, has been under attack for twelve winters by Grendel, the fen-born son of the monster-mother of the mires.

We read it standing, because Aldous swears the poem was meant to be recited on its feet and nobody has ever produced the page of evidence, but nobody has produced the page against it either. The rule is unchanged: one member reads the line in the old English, one glosses it, and the rest of the room may object. This winter the objections have been about the alliteration, about the weather in the fifth-century Jutland of the poem, and — twice — about the door.

2 · The Text, PlainlyWhat We Hold, and What We Cannot Hold

  • The poem: Beowulf, in Old English, 3,182 alliterative lines, in one manuscript — the Nowell Codex.
  • The dating: the composition date is a matter of contention among scholars, and has been since before the Society existed; the only certain dating is for the manuscript itself, produced between AD 975 and 1025. We print the range and move on, as a prudent club should.
  • The author: anonymous. Scholars call him the “Beowulf poet”; we call him the other man in the room, because that is what an anonymous author is, to a club, for the whole of the reading.
  • The setting: pagan Scandinavia, the fifth and sixth centuries — Denmark, the Geatland across the water, and the fens where Grendel lives.
  • Our copy: one, kept in the wall safe, behind the tea tins. The Society does not lend it, for the reason that there is only one.

3 · The Month’s ReadingHeorot, Under Attack — and the Security of a Promise

The month’s folio opens with Hrothgar in his hall. The mead is poured, the harp is strung, the shield is hung in its place, and then the reading turns, as it always does in this folio, to the attack: Grendel comes out of the mist, up the long causeway, and into the hall, and the mead hall that was built to be the safest roof in the north is, in the space of one night, the least safe place in it. Mrs. Wulfgar read the line in which Hrothgar sends for his thanes and the poem stops pretending that this has been a pleasant winter. The room was quiet for a while after. The kettle saved the evening.

The standing objection followed, as it has been following all winter. Aldous, who objects to everything that is only heroic, argued that the Danes have set their whole house on a promise — that a Geat will cross the sea, stand them against Grendel in the dark, and take the monster as the price of the mead. He wanted the room to understand what they were buying, and what they were not. The room understood. The sentence he closed with, for the record, is printed below exactly as spoken, per the Society’s standing rule that a member’s words are the member’s words:

“It should be any stronger security for their pains.”

— A. Fenchurch, editor, on the Geat’s promise to Hrothgar, as spoken at the fourth meeting of the winter and entered in the minute book on the line beneath the tea account.

The gloss, for fairness, came from Mr. Sigehelm, who noted that a promise is the only currency the poem has ever trusted — that every mead, every gold, every helmet and ring in the three thousand lines goes out eventually — and that a hall kept by a promise is the poem’s way of keeping a hall at all. It is a gloss the room accepted on the second reading, which in this Society is a great victory, and which Aldous has agreed to concede to no one.

4 · The CountLines Read, and Lines Yet to Be Read

The Society’s count for the winter stands at 1,411 of 3,182 lines read, which is more than the fourth meeting of the winter had any right to manage, and which Mrs. Wulfgar has pencilled, in the margin of the minute book, as “the half, give or take a frost.” The next folio opens where the reading must always open next: at the water, where Beowulf is ferried to the shore of the Danes and the watch on the coast looks out to sea.

Next line to be read at the winter’s next meeting: 1,412.

5 · Post and CorrespondenceThe Keeper’s Shelf, and the Sale of the Hall Itself

Two matters of record. First, a parcel arrived from the shelf of the Department of British and Medieval Antiquities, and with it the standing invitation, renewed each winter, to read Beowulf the way the Department reads its own sites: card by card, context by context, nothing glossed before it is recorded. The field game of that discipline, built round the keeper Rupert Bruce-Mitford and the Sutton Hoo ship-burial, is kept on the Society’s shelf as Keeper’s Manual No. 14, and we note, for the minute book, that a poem of 3,182 lines and a ship impression of twenty-seven metres are, in the end, the same record-keeping problem: the thing is gone, and the paper is all.

Second, the Society’s own premises came up at the hammer this autumn. The hall, the lane, the 2,250 acres, and the two ancient parishes that have never agreed on the colour of the door were sold as Lot 217 at Fenn & Marlowe’s 110th Autumn Sale, and the condition of sale — that the village keep its fire — settles the Society’s tenure for so long as the kettle runs. The full entry, with the condition report and the 749 souls taken as they stand, is catalogued at Lot 217 — Ashley, Cambridgeshire, Cat. 44. The Society notes, for the record, that the fog that travels in its own vehicle is also, per the same entry, not the auctioneer’s.
