1. What the film is

Bad Times at the El Royale is a 2018 American neo-noir thriller written, directed and co-produced by Drew Goddard. It premiered at the Los Angeles film festivals circuit in the autumn of 2018 and went on to a wide release in January 2019. The picture stars Jeff Bridges, Cynthia Erivo, Dakota Johnson, Jon Hamm, Cailee Spaeny, Lewis Pullman and Chris Hemsworth, and it is shot in the grainy, sun-flattened manner of a late-1960s production, though it was made with modern cameras that were deliberately made to look old.

The story takes place, essentially, on a single night in the late 1960s at the El Royale, a hotel standing along the California–Nevada border. Six strangers book in, a seventh is employed there, and by morning their secrets have been laid out on the lobby carpet. What makes the film unusual, and what Mr. Okafor asked us to notice, is its structure: it is a so-called hyperlink-style thriller, which means the plot is not told once in order. It is told in fragments, and each character’s version of the night slightly contradicts the others’, so that the audience has to assemble the true sequence of events the way a detective would assemble a case file.

2. The six strangers and the one employee

  • George “Dave” Blackwell (Jon Hamm) — a preacher with a television congregation who has come looking for the girl who left his pulpit.
  • Elizabeth “Babs” Sullivan (Dakota Johnson) — a runaway with a suitcase full of cash and a name she borrowed from her older sister.
  • Sam &ldquoPop” Prescott (Lewis Pullman) — a young man who has come to confess to a crime he did not commit, and to collect a reward.
  • Celestine (Cynthia Erivo) — the hotel’s piano player, who is also its only employee still on the payroll after the El Royale’s original owners were killed in a suspicious fire in 1964.
  • Jayce (Cailee Spaeny) — a teenager in a wig, travelling in the company of the preacher.
  • J. &ldquoEdie” (Chris Hemsworth) — a man in a suit with a gun under his jacket, who does not answer the question of who exactly he is.
  • George (Jeff Bridges) — the night clerk, the hotel’s only permanent resident, who keeps the register, the key rack and the long memory of the place.

3. Themes: the border line

The film explores themes of morality, faith and redemption, and it has a visual idea at its centre that I want to underline in the report: the state border. The El Royale is built astride the boundary between California and Nevada, and the state line runs down the floor of the building itself. The film keeps returning to this line as a symbol of right and wrong, of guilt and innocence, of the side of the road you choose to stand on. When a character in the film crosses a line, the camera makes sure we see the line. The bar in the hotel straddles the border in the same way, and more than one scene has its moral weight coming from which side of the stool a person is sitting on.

Faith appears in the preacher, whose sermons were honest once and are now a business; redemption appears in the hotel itself, a burned-out 1939 landmark run down by bad years, which its surviving employee continues to polish one mirror at a time. It is, in short, a film about people who have already done the thing they cannot undo, and about what a night’s work of telling the truth is worth when the cost of it is paid in the morning.

4. Making of

Drew Goddard began writing the spec script for the film in November 2016. In the early pages he was also compiling a list of songs — period pop and soul, the kind that would have been on a 1968 AM radio — to be worked into the score, so that the sound of the night is as much part of the evidence as the picture. The cast was assembled around Bridges as the clerk, which Goddard has described as the right way to begin: you find the man who is already at the register before you book the six strangers into the rooms. The hotel is a set built to hold its own weight: every key on the rack is supposed to work, every corridor is supposed to echo, and the lobby clock is supposed to be wrong in the same way from the first shot to the last.

Mr. Okafor asked for one piece of oral history from the family, so I recorded my grandad, who still listens to the weather on the short wave before breakfast. When I asked him what a hotel lobby in 1968 would have smelled like on the radio, he answered in exactly one sentence, and I am keeping it in the report as he said it:

“I am seventy-one, I live by the weather.”

— recorded in the kitchen, 26 June 2029; the short wave was, that morning, on a Nevada station

The line sits with the film because the whole El Royale story is a matter of forecasting: everyone in the lobby is trying to read the night the way my grandad reads the barometer, and everyone’s forecast comes up wrong.

5. The night, hour by hour

The log below sets out the night as it eventually reassembles itself. Use the times to step through it; the same log is legible as plain text with no script at all, in which case read it top to bottom.

  1. 10:03 p.m. The last of the six strangers arrives and is booked into the El Royale. The register has been open all evening; the clerk writes the names down and says nothing about the fire of 1964.
  2. 11:00 p.m. The piano room starts up. Celestine plays the bar; the strangers take their seats on the correct and incorrect sides of the border line; the preacher is told there is no television service in the building.
  3. 11:42 p.m. The first of the confessions is offered at the bar. A suitcase of cash changes hands in plain sight of everyone who will not yet know what it is for.
  4. 12:15 a.m. The clerk brings the register to the piano room and reads back the names in the order they were written. For the first time the audience can see the order is not the order the film has given it.
  5. 1:30 a.m. The versions of the night begin to collide. What was a booking turns out to be an ambush; what was an ambush turns out to have been a rescue; the line on the floor is crossed twice in one scene.
  6. 2:06 a.m. The register is closed, the fire alarm is tested one last time, and the film leaves the lobby to the clerk, who has been there through all of it and will be there through the morning.

Select a time above, or read the full log as printed.

6. Conclusion

What I would recommend to the next class is this: watch the film twice, and on the second time keep a register of your own — every name, every room number, every mention of 1964 — and try to out-poll the film at the end. It is harder than it sounds, because the film has been making notes all along. Goddard’s El Royale is a hotel that is also a lock, and the night it takes to open is the whole plot. My grandad’s sentence holds: in a building like that, everybody lives by the weather of the other people in the room, and nobody’s forecast is quite right.

For comparison I have read the department’s biotechnology report from Form 3B, which handles a clean room the way this film handles the lobby — as a climate of its own, with standing orders — and the museum placard for The Wash, where a border of a different kind, the line between Lincolnshire and Norfolk, is set down in the same matter-of-fact way that the state line runs down the El Royale floor. Both documents, like this film, make a boundary do moral work without ever saying so. I will hand the register, such as it is, in on Friday.
