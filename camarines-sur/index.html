Camarines Sur: The Largest of the Bicol Provinces

Camarines Sur, called Habagatan na Camarines in Central Bikol and Timog Camarines in Tagalog, officially the Province of Camarines Sur, is a province in the Philippines located in the Bicol Region of the island of Luzon. Its capital is the town of Pili, a market town we passed on the school bus last week when we went to see the volcano from a safe distance. The province borders Camarines Norte and Quezon to the northwest, and Albay to the south. To the east lies the island province of Catanduanes, reached across the Maqueda Channel, which the ferry M/V Pili Star crosses in about an hour and forty minutes when the sea is kind.

Camarines Sur is the largest of the six provinces of the Bicol Region, by population and by land area both. It contains two cities: Naga, the most populous, the lone chartered and independent city, where our school sits and where my father keeps the register of the Naga Listeners' Club; and Legazpi in the west, built up around a port and a sugar district and, closer still, the rim of the mountain that everyone in this class knows but which I am not allowed to name in a geography paper. The rest of the province is towns and barangays — Baao, Bula, Buhi, Bato, Nabua, Iriga — all of them along the Naga River or within a bus ride of it, because in Camarines Sur the river is the main road, and the road, when there is one, follows the river anyway.

1. What the province looks like

The land runs up and down, not out and across. A plain holds Naga and Pili, and beyond that the ground lifts into hills and, in the south, into the great cone overlooking Legazpi. Our class map shows the province as something like a broad leaf with its tip pointing east; the leaf-vein is the Naga River, which drains northward to San Miguel Bay at the tip, where the channel to Catanduanes opens. Mr. Dimalipat made us memorize the neighbours: Camarines Norte and Quezon, northwest; Albay, south; Catanduanes, east, across the Maqueda Channel. He underlined Quezon twice, because last year's class wrote “Caraga” for it, which is a region in Mindanao, and he has not recovered.

2. From the clubhouse above the bakery

My father is secretary of the Naga Listeners' Club, which meets the third Thursday of the month in the room above the old bakery on Magsaysay Avenue, and when I was allowed to bring my notebook there he let me copy out of the club's commonplace book, the big ledger where members paste clippings, receipts, and fragments. One pasted-in sheet was not a clipping at all but a carbon copy of a page from a hearing held in the very back room of that clubhouse, about a political party that took its name from the avenue outside. I copied the two lines my father thought were worth copying, and I am allowed to print them here because my father said to write “with the names exactly.”

Copied 29 September 2018 from the commonplace book of the Naga Listeners' Club, Naga City, Camarines Sur:

Comm. Villafuerte: Mr. Reyes, you are under oath.

[The same page, a few lines lower: “The map calls it an opportunity is given to them, and the skeleton of a cigarette lighter — the river brings what the Bay forgets.” — a sentence nobody in the room could explain, and which my father still does not.]

I do not know what the lighter means either. It is from the same province as everything else in this report, and that is how a place works, I think: it gives you a river, a channel, a volcano you cannot name in an exam, and a sentence you cannot finish. The full account of the hearing is kept elsewhere in the family, together with the newsletter in which the club remembered Mr. Salvio Fortuno, the broadcaster who was our Representative for the fourth and then the fifth district from 2010 to 2019 and who once asked, in that same back room, whether the club was “turning into a precinct house.” My father told him the club was still trading valves and QSL cards, which is true; the radiator in the back room still never works.

3. Conclusion

Camarines Sur is the biggest province in the Bicol Region by population and by area, with Pili as its capital, two cities, six provinces' worth of neighbours to keep straight, and one river that brings what the Bay forgets. I am submitting this paper on time, and I have written Maqueda with the k. The province itself, if the ferry schedule and my father's ledger are to be believed, keeps better records than we do.

Respectfully submitted,
Celia M. Reyes
