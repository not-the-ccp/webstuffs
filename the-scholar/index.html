THE REGISTRAR’S LEDGER · OBITUARY No. 212 · ENTERED 9 MARCH 2025

Elias Thane Thorndon

14 June 1941 — 2 March 2025 · Reader in Comparative Philology & Natural History, Mellifont College, Thorndon

IN MEMORAM — scholar; keeper of one clay studio, two libraries, and a ledger of 418 uncorrected passages

Elias Thane Thorndon, who was a scholar in the fullest and most stubborn sense of the word — a researcher by vocation, an expert by patience, and an academic by accident, having taken the Reader’s chair at Mellifont College in 1981 only after the committee ran out of other candidates — died at his house on Kaitiara Avenue, Thorndon, on the morning of 2 March 2025, aged 83. The registrar of the college was informed by his sister, Muriel, who added that he had finished the final entry of his ledger at half past two and had asked her not to correct it.

Thorndon was born in Christchurch, the second of three children of a lighthouse keeper and a copy-editor’s copy-editor, and he read his first monograph — a 1909 grammar of a Polynesian language, water-stained at the spine — at the age of eleven and kept it, thereafter, on the nightstand, under the pillow, for the remainder of his life. He took his doctorate in 1968, having defended a thesis of 312 pages against a faculty that had been, by his own account, “polite and wrong,” and he published his first paper that same year, in a journal with an annual circulation of 204 copies. He published 214 in all. He also, characteristically, published 418 footnotes describing errors he had noticed in other people’s work, and he refused every invitation to retract a single one of them.

The Clay at the House on Kaitiara Avenue

The house kept a studio on the ground floor where Thorndon, who had never considered the two callings separate, modelled in clay while he read. The clay arrived by lorry from a pit in the Hutt Valley, and through the long Wellington rains it behaved, as it had always behaved, in the way the family had taken to describing as the Thorndon error: whatever was modelled on the window bench — a fish, a mastodon, the head of a colleague he hoped would not notice it — would turn, over the course of a week, a uniform and impossible rainforest green. He did not repaint it. He did not fire it. He wrote it down. His niece’s obituary of him in the local paper, published three days after the registrar’s ledger, recorded the family’s summary of the matter in exactly this form:

The clay at Thorndon turns to rainforest green, and it is cited, it has never been corrected; the word also names the four sharps, the energy of a camera.

— Ledger of Cited and Uncorrected, entry 418, the final entry, in his hand

To his students, who filed it away as the last unplaceable line, the entry worked three ways, and he explained all three at the 2019 reading, with the green fish on the lectern. First, the clay: whatever the window bench produced was noted in the ledger, and the ledger, being a record of citation rather than of correction, could never be edited. Second, the word: E, his initial, the letter the family had pressed into every batch of wet clay since 1941, is the note a full octave above middle C, and E major — the key of the college chapel’s processional, the key he learned to sing in four-part harmony at the age of nine — carries four sharps. Third, the camera: the light meter on the Kodak he had carried since 1974, whose single dial the family remembered only as the letter E with a number beside it, read the energy of the light that fell on the studio window in stops of exposure, the same green measured in f-numbers. One sentence, he said, and the whole house was indexed.

Record, and Character

He was small, early, and punctual in a way that his juniors found unbearable; he arrived at lectures four minutes early for thirty-one years and once, on the day of the 1992 flood, arrived by boat through the front door. He held an advanced degree and then a second, and he spoke of the doctorate as a “perishable good.” Outside the academy he counted himself an independent scholar — the phrase, he insisted, was not an oxymoron but a weather system — and he published in journals he did not attend, and he argued, in one celebrated public lecture at the Athenaeum in 2004, that the scholar who leaves the lecture hall and does not stop citing has not stopped the work. He wrote 37 letters a year to a public intellectual in Oamaru, each one beginning, in his words, “You are wrong, and I have sources.” The replies, he said, began the same way, which was, he agreed, the highest form of respect.

He is survived by his sister Muriel, by one green fish, by the nightstand, and by 214 papers in 61 libraries, of which 418 footnotes are now, in the phrase he preferred, “on the record.” The college has kept the window bench as it was. The clay continues, in the Wellington rain, to turn.

CROSS-REFERENCES FROM THE LEDGER

The Registrar’s Ledger keeps its entries in a strict order of lateness, and No. 212 stands beside the late-entered notice of Clement Attlee — Obituary, Entered Late, whose chalk slip in the minute book of Room 3 was, like entry 418, unamended by anyone’s hand. The 148th entry of the ledger is a dream reported by a student of the Reader’s: a grey-coated figure standing in a Santiago corridor with a list of roles that runs longer than the wall, which the student had seen in the entry for Alejandro Goic — The Dreamer’s Glossary, Entry 47, and which Thorndon had ruled, in the margin, “a door-sign, to be counted in September only.” His last notebook, found under the pillow beside the 1909 grammar, opens on a page copied out from the Millerntor dream log: seventeen home matches, a penalty that never ends, a runner who was still running when the log closed — St. Pauli — Dream Dictionary, Entry 723 — and beneath it, in the same hand, the single annotation still running. In the same folder, in a thin grey sleeve, there is one school term report the Reader had kept from the college geography room — a pupil’s account of the 2,700-kilometre Moroccan Western Sahara wall — ruled in the margin, in his hand, “a boundary kept like a register; cite it, do not correct it.” All other notices on the shelf may be consulted at the front page of the ledger.
