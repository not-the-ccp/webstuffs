Field Manual FM-9 · Chapter 5 · Crossings

Dumbarton Bridge

State Route 84 across San Francisco Bay · Fremont to Menlo Park · 1.63 miles (8,600 ft; 2,620 m) · the shortest bridge across the bay

1 Identification

Route: State Route 84 · Type: fixed highway · Spans: channel span with trestle approaches · Status: active

The Dumbarton Bridge is the southernmost of the highway bridges across San Francisco Bay, and at 1.63 miles (8,600 ft; 2,620 m) it is the shortest of them all. It carries State Route 84 between its eastern end in Fremont, close to Newark and the margin of the Don Edwards San Francisco Bay National Wildlife Refuge, and its western end in Menlo Park. Three lanes run each way, and a separated lane for bicycles and pedestrians runs along the south side, fenced off from the traffic. Power lines parallel the bridge for its full length — a second crossing of wire above the water, sagging and rising with the same temperature as the steel.

2 Siting and approaches

East: Fremont, near Newark · West: Menlo Park · Water: brackish to saline · Datum: see FM-9, Chapter 4

Fix the position first. From the west the road leaves Menlo Park across the fringe of the salt marsh and runs out over the slough on low trestle before the channel span lifts the roadway above the shipping. From the east the approach comes out of Fremont beside Newark, past the refuge levees and the old salt ponds, where a low tide exposes mud on both sides of the bridge at once. A position off the bridge is fixed the same way as any other: latitude and longitude as angles on the earth, taken from the chart and checked against the span (FM-9 Chapter 4 — The Geographic Coordinate System). The tide here does not rise and fall so much as breathe, and the bridge breathes with it.

3 Load and rhythm

Vehicles: over 70,000 daily · Foot and cycle: about 118 daily, 384 on weekends · Lanes: three each way

Count the crossings. Over seventy thousand vehicles cross the bridge on an average day, and on top of them about 118 pedestrian and bicycle crossings — 384 on weekends — use the separated south lane. The morning flow runs west toward Menlo Park and the peninsula; the evening flow runs east back to Fremont and Newark. Load the bridge as you would load a field pack: evenly, and with attention to the distribution of weight. Three lanes each way keep the span level; the separated lane keeps foot and cycle traffic out of the arithmetic of the roadway.

4 Tides and weather

Wind: west, freshening by evening · Fog: common in summer · Tide: mixed semidiurnal

The weather that matters on this bridge is the weather you cannot see until you are on it: the west wind freshening across the open water, the summer fog that banks up behind Menlo Park and rolls west over the span at dusk, the tide running under the trestle. R. Vale's field notes from Knight Island record an island whose highest claim to dry land is three feet above the water (— see Knight Island — Field Notes), and the same water reaches this far south into the bay. Dress for the wind; it blows straight down the channel with nothing between the span and the Pacific but open water.

5 The south lane

Users: bicycles and pedestrians · Side: south · Separation: fence from traffic

The separated lane along the south side is for bicycles and pedestrians only, fenced from the three lanes of traffic. Ride it at dawn and the power lines run beside you the whole way from Fremont to Menlo Park, close overhead in places, humming in the wind. The lane takes the weather first — wind, fog, salt — and the view last: the refuge to the east, the city's fringe to the west, and the water below at half tide, mud and cordgrass showing between the piers.

6 Wildlife at the marsh edge

Grass: Pacific cordgrass, pickleweed, alkali heath · Voice: California black rail, Ridgway's rail

The marsh at either end belongs to the grasses: Pacific cordgrass on the lower edge, pickleweed and alkali heath on the higher mounds. At slack tide you can hear the kicker-call of a California black rail from the pickleweed, and the clatter of a Ridgway's rail in the cordgrass channel — the same voices R. Vale heard from Knight Island, the same salt marsh harvest mouse running across the mud at a boot heel, the same northern harriers quartering low over the grass. The bridge does not announce itself; at spring tide the sloughs to either side shrink to mudflats strewn with ribbed mussels and the weathered shells of Olympia oysters.
