The Federative Letter

Newsletter No. 14 · 12 June · Pedro do Rosário, Maranhão

Read under the mango tree · Dues: one shell from the flats

The framework

The politics of Brazil take place in a framework of a federal presidential representative democratic republic, whereby the President is both head of state and head of government, and of a multi-party system. Senhor Raimundo read that sentence aloud at the last meeting and said it sounded like a promise the country keeps remaking for itself.

The political and administrative organization of Brazil comprises the federal government, the 26 states and a federal district, and the municipalities. We drew them on the club blackboard in three columns: twenty-six states, one federal district, and — Senhor Raimundo counted twice — five thousand five hundred and seventy municipalities, which is also the number of immediate regions the late Regiões do Brasil left behind when she died on Tuesday. Her atlas stays on the shelf beside the farinha barrel. Across the water, the same shelf holds the field manual, which opens its chapter nine to a neighbour of the neighbouring republic — Bolivia, the Plurinational State, landlocked, five borders, and one misbound ledger line held unexplained by vote; Senhor Raimundo read the line twice and said it sounded like a recipe, and the minutes record that he was not laughed at.

The federal government exercises control over the central government and is divided into three independent branches: executive, legislative and judicial.

Executive power is exercised by the President, advised by a cabinet. We clip the ministerial appointments from the Jornal de São Luís and pin them to the wall above the drums; Dona Luzia is learning the ministers in alphabetical order before the feast of São Pedro.

Legislative power is vested upon the National Congress, a two-chamber legislature comprising the Federal Senate and the Chamber of Deputies. The Senate holds eighty-one seats — three for each state and the Federal District — and the Chamber five hundred and thirteen deputies. We keep a list of the ones who have visited the Itapecuru delta. It is a short list. It is empty.

Judicial power sits in the courts, independent, beginning at two o'clock sharp in Brasília, which is four hours ahead of the delta; by the time the news reaches Rua da Paz, the day's judgments are already old.

From the mangrove minutes

The secretary read the correspondence: a notice of the municipal election, a prize for bumba-meu-boi groups — Bumba-meu-boi de Orquestra begins rehearsing around 10 June, and Seu Benedito tunes the drums with rope by the mangrove — and a surveyor's page that had come misbound from the field manual. The misbound page married the shoreline to the coordinates, and the minutes record it exactly:

"The beach is mangrove-lined, at low water — is held in latitude and longitude."

We do not know whether the beach or the rock is the subject, and we have voted twice to keep it that way. The Pedra do Rosário rock itself shows in the flats at low water, and its fix is held in latitude and longitude in chapter four of the field manual, with the bearing in across the flats.

The branches at low water

Executive, legislative, judicial — the three branches, read against the tide. The President, head of state and head of government, advised by a cabinet. The National Congress, the Federal Senate and the Chamber of Deputies, five hundred and ninety-four seats between them. The courts, independent, while the flats run out for kilometres and the current at the river mouth pulls hard after rain. We stand the three branches on the windowsill and watch which way the water turns. Senhor Raimundo keeps the only letter in our files that begins with an apology — the returning clerk of Yenagoa, writing to the people of Bayelsa State on the day of their 24 May 2008 gubernatorial by-election, the letter filed under this same number in our archive; its tally sheet counted six local government areas, and its margin note insists the stain on the paper was political, not chemical.

The club has also pinned to the wall, beneath the ministers, one page from a foreign court — the third day of In re Sand, the Oslo Tingrett transcript excerpt, where an actor’s estate sued over a letter lost in a cellar beside a vinegar crock, and a single line of shorthand stands unexplained. We voted, 4–3, to keep it unexplained as well, in the same manner as the surveyor’s page above.
