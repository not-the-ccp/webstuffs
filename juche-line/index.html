Commentary I — The speech on platform 3

On the night this timetable is in force, at the school of party cadres on the east side of the Taedong, Kim Il Sung addresses the assembled students and cadres of the Party. The address is delivered on 28 December 1955, and in it the speaker says the word juche by name for the first time — not as a metaphor about the wind, not as an old proverb about carrying water, but as a named thing, a position, a word with a date on it. The speech is known from that night onward as the Juche speech, and it is counted among Kim Il Sung’s most important works; the histories of the line keep it on the departure board as a watershed moment in the country’s history, the point after which the timetable, the telegrams and the timetable of the telegrams run on a different clock.

The line runs it the way a station runs a formal night: the word is printed once, at the head of the service list, and the conductors are instructed not to repeat it twice. A name said once, on record, is a name that is now a station.

Commentary II — Two readings of the word

The readers of the speech do not all agree about what the night carried, and the line keeps both readings in separate carriages. The bolder reading holds that Juche, as a distinct ideology, was developed from this speech forward — a doctrine with its own stations, its own gauge, its own name — and that the night of 28 December 1955 was the moment it was launched by the man at the head of the table. The more conservative reading holds only that the speech used the term juche to assert that the Korean people were the subject of the revolution: the word was already on the line, in the proverbial sense of self-reliance, of standing on one’s own feet, and the speech simply put a passenger named after the country into the carriage and let the train run.

Both carriages are listed on the board. Neither is cancelled. The line’s rule, as with all rules printed here, is that a difference of reading is not a fault of the train.

Commentary III — What a watershed carries

A watershed is not an event the passengers can see from the window; it is the height at which water changes its river. The speech changed the height. What goes into this river after 28 December 1955 — the party texts, the school curricula, the station signage, the naming of new lines after new words — will never again run into the other river, and the line marks the boundary on its maps with a small gold star, exactly as this timetable marks the head service in gold at 00:00. For a timetable of the same discipline, on a line that has not yet run its kilometres of coast, the reader is referred to the Federative Line of the Brazilian railways; and for the conduct owed to a Korean name once it is printed, the Book of Etiquette, Chapter XI is the standing reference. The front board of this station’s records is at the index.

Reward notice — Taedong Concourse, lost & found

Found on carriage 4 of service 0000, between Anju and Sŏriwŏn, one glass bottle, one folded page, one absence. Claims to the porter’s window before 06:00. The notice is posted as struck:

Reward: a bottle breaks during handling, treat the line out of caution, an absence held in a book.

The porter’s note beneath the posting: the bottle was of mineral water, unlabelled; the page was blank except for one underlined word; the absence was of a passenger who, according to the guard, never boarded. The window is closed at dawn. For the line’s home records, see Service LC, the Courant Line, kept in the same department since 1752.
