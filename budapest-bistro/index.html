Copenhagen · Vesterbrogade 47, second floor

The Carpathian Cellar

Fourteenth of November · six covers, two waiters, one door kept like a treasury

CC

The Carpathian Cellar is a Hungarian room on the second floor above a tobacconist on Vesterbrogade: a landlocked country’s cooking in a city famous for its water. Hungary sits in the Carpathian Basin, bordered by Slovakia to the north, Ukraine to the northeast, Romania to the east and southeast, Serbia to the south, Croatia and Slovenia to the southwest, and Austria to the west; the Danube runs through Budapest, and beyond the river the Great Plain spreads low and open. Nine and a half million people live there, mostly ethnic Hungarians — Magyars — with a significant Romani minority, and Hungarian, one of the few languages in Europe outside the Indo-European family, is spoken at every table.

The room has twelve tables, open shelves of paprika tins, walls the colour of goulash after three hours, and a mirror that makes the space look twice as large. The cooking is done by Márta Fábián, who came from Szeged, and the menu is short: gulyás leves simmered four hours with caraway and beef shin (158 kr), fisherman’s soup so red it stains the spoon, chicken paprikash with nokedli dumplings, lángos with sour cream and cheese, and Dobos torte — seven sponge layers, caramel on top — for 190 kr. A glass of Tokaji aszú, poured from a bottle labelled with a grower’s name I could not pronounce, cost 78 kr. Two of us ate and drank for 442 kr, tip included, and left full in a way that has nothing to do with quantity.

The door is kept like a treasury. You give your name to a woman who checks a list and seats you without delay only if you are on it, which reminded me of the Copenhagen diary written at the Royal Danish Treasury — the Crown of Christian IV, made for a coronation when Christian was nineteen, and the guard who would not count the jewels twice (Rosenborg Treasury — Diary, 11 November) — and of an older minute book, the Council of Ripon Abbey in AD 788, where the death of St Botwine was read three times and the vacancy of the abbacy was to be filled without delay (St Botwine — Minutes of the Council of Ripon Abbey). Two guarded rooms, one in Copenhagen and one in Ripon, and now a third on Vesterbrogade.

The strangest thing is printed at the foot of the wine list, in a smaller face: “Almost renouncing all thought of that year — Christian was nineteen, and the guard at the death of St Botwine — be filled without delay.” It makes sense only as a splice: the coronation of 1596, the obit of 788, and a Hungarian wine list in Copenhagen in 2024, none of them explaining the other. The waiter said it was a house quotation, or a misquotation, and that the house keeps the same discipline as the Rosenborg guard — count once, write it down, do not return to it. I ordered the goulash a second time.

Verdict: eight out of ten. The paprika is honest, the door is polite, and the sentence on the back of the menu is the kind of thing you carry out into the street. I will go back, though I still cannot pronounce Tokaji.

The Paprika Reckoner

Gulyás leves for the table. Set the number of guests and the pan does the sums.

Brown the beef, soften the onion, bloom the paprika off the heat, then simmer four hours. Bread on the side.

The four-hour simmer is the common laboratory form of the substance we serve here, and the cellarer of Ripon has it written up on a safety data sheet, No. HE-786: hazards of the paprika tins, first aid for the wine list quotation, and the leaven in the loaf that is a loaf per brother, not per visitor. The bread is on the side in both houses.
