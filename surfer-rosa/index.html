Harborside & Pine · Records Room · No. 11, the shelf by the tide gauge

Surfer Rosa

Being a collection of questions put to the records room, with such answers as the room is able to give — on the debut album of the Pixies, March 1988, 4AD, with Steve Albini at the controls

~  ~  ~

1. What exactly is Surfer Rosa?

It is the debut studio album by the American alternative rock band the Pixies — Black Francis (who also went by Frank Black), Joey Santiago, David Lovering and Kim Deal — released in March 1988 on the British independent label 4AD, whose offices at that time kept a shelf in Camden where the pressing plant's boxes went to sleep before the week's dispatches. It was produced by Steve Albini, who had been the house engineer at the studio where the band's earlier work had been assembled, and who carried the same dry ear into the sessions proper. Eight tracks, one side at a time in the old way, and a sleeve with the word Rosa in letters that looked, to more than one dock clerk, like a name written on a lunchbox lid.

2. Where do the Spanish words come from?

From the earlier output of the band, which the album does not so much continue as inherit, the way a house inherits a wall. Black Francis grew up within hearing of a lot of Puerto Rico — the radio, the family kitchen, the postcards — and the album carries Spanish lyrics into its verses alongside the English, most plainly in the middle of the record where the singing stops trying to be English at all. Several lines name Puerto Rico outright, and the room's reading is simple: the band was not translating anything. The Spanish was not a flavour added to the English; it was one of the rooms the songs already lived in, and the record simply left the door open.

3. Why does the record talk about such dark things so plainly?

The album includes references to mutilation and incest, and it includes them the way a tide gauge includes a storm: as a recorded fact, not as a performance of feeling. Set beside the experimental recording techniques Albini allowed the band — tape that is treated like weather, vocals that are dry until they are not — the subjects read as the raw matter the songs were cut from. The band's earlier output had always said the quiet thing out loud; the album's new achievement was making the loud thing quiet. Listeners on the Camden shelf noticed the contrast within a day of the first pressings, and the note pinned to the shelf's card says, in the clerk's hand: the horror is the calm, and the calm is the point.

4. What is the drum sound, and can it be described?

David Lovering's kit was set up close and left mostly to itself, so that the snare reads as a slap of cloth and the toms arrive whole. The distinctive drum sound the record is now cited for is not an effect; it is a decision — to hear the drums the way a person standing in the same room hears them, which is to say, larger and less certain than the mix would like to admit. Albini's standing instruction, as the band tells it, was that the kit could not be made to look good, only to be there. It is the reason the album still sounds like a place and not a track: a corridor with a window, the tide audible under the floor.

5. Why did it not chart, in either country?

Because 4AD was an independent label, and independent labels in 1988 were not, by arrangement, what the charts were built for. Distribution in the United States was handled by the British label Rough Trade Records, which shipped the record in small consignments and kept a patient ledger of it. The result is the record's quiet distinction: it failed to chart in either country, and the silence of both charts is now the first thing a cataloguer enters. What the charts did not count, the shelf did; the consignments moved, the ledger balanced, and the copies ended up in the hands of the people the album was, apparently, written for.

6. How many singles were released, and how far did it reach?

Only one. A rerecorded version of “Gigantic” was pressed and sent out as the album's single, and it reached number 93 on the UK Singles Chart — a place with its own kind of honesty, high enough to prove the chart had looked, low enough to prove it had looked only once. The room keeps the 4AD dispatch slip from that week; it shows the shipment to the Northern warehouse and, in the margin, the single word the dock clerk had added to it: kept.

7. What is the line about the dining room?

The records room is asked this often enough that it keeps an answer for it. In the card drawer of the 4AD office — the same drawer that kept the dispatch slips and the tide gauge readings — there is a small order pad from the staff canteen, and on it, in a hand the room has never matched to a name, someone has written, word for word:

“And the dining room in a tin of peppermints.”

The room's reading is that it is not an order but a description of the album itself: a dining room, the most public of rooms, folded down and kept — sealed and whole — inside the size of a tin of peppermints. A house that fits in a pocket, and everything in it still set for company. The drawer's keeper filed the pad with the 1988 pressings and left it there; the room has since taken the line as its own shelf motto, and the tin itself sits on the shelf by the tide gauge, closed, exactly as the line says it should be.

For the wider shelf: the room's other long listening, the liner notes for Mucous Membrane (Nile Basin Records NBR-041), are kept at the Nile Basin counter, nine sides to its one charter; and for the standing orders on keeping a band whole through twenty-one albums, see Field Manual FM-21, Chapter 9 · The Boss, where the house is told how to play the record without breaking it. For the galley card of the same autumn — Glass Spider cakes, Galley Card No. 41, baked when the VHS of the Sydney end of the tour arrived in the post — the tin held forty-one, and the last one went to the questioner. The front of the house's keeping is at the front page.
