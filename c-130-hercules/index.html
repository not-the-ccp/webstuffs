1. What the aircraft is

The Lockheed C-130 Hercules is an American, four-engined, turboprop, military transport aircraft designed and built by Lockheed — now Lockheed Martin. The first prototype flew on 23 August 1954 out of Lockheed's Burbank plant in California, and the type has been built ever since at the Lockheed plant in Marietta, Georgia, one of the longest production runs of any military aircraft. It has four Allison T56 turboprop engines, each turning a four-bladed propeller, a high wing so the cargo floor sits low to the ground, and a rear ramp that opens straight onto the airfield.

More than forty variants of the Hercules exist, including gunships, weather aircraft, tankers, and the civilian versions marketed as the Lockheed L-100. It is the main tactical airlifter for military forces all over the world, and more than sixty countries have flown it.

2. What it was made to do

The C-130 was designed as a troop, medical evacuation, and cargo transport aircraft. Its great trick is the unprepared runway: it can take off from and land on dirt, gravel, or grass strips that would break a jetliner's landing gear, because it flies slowly, lands tough, and reverses its propellers to stop in a few hundred feet. It can carry ninety-two combat troops, sixty-four paratroops, or seventy-four litter patients, or a mixture of pallets and vehicles loaded through the back ramp.

Its versatile airframe has found other uses: as a gunship (the AC-130), for airborne assault, search and rescue, scientific research support, weather reconnaissance, aerial refueling, maritime patrol, and aerial firefighting. The ski-equipped LC-130 still shuttles scientists to Antarctica, and the WC-130 flies into hurricanes.

3. The squadron's aircraft

My parent is Master Supply Specialist Dana Reyes, supply officer of Fleet Logistics Support Squadron 62 — VR-62, “The Nomads” — at NAS Jacksonville, Florida. The Nomads fly the C-130T, and I wrote this report watching their aircraft load on the ramp beside BuNo 171442. My parent signed the lift manifest for a crate of foundry type that rode a C-130T from Jacksonville toward Barcelona, miskeyed against the wrong column of the wing load plan, and sat for three days in Annex C at NAS JRB Fort Worth, Texas, until the returning clerk of Barcelona came to fetch it herself.

The whole story is in the squadron's letter, “An Apology to the Returning Clerk — VR-62, The Nomads · NAS Jacksonville”, which the squadron read at the 26th's drill. The crate's standing instruction was that it reach the printer, the Impremta de la Diada, before the 11th, for the holiday's broadsheet; it touched down at El Prat at 19:42 on the 11th itself, four minutes before the office closed. The 72-point pound sign stood at the head of the broadsheet, as commissioned.

4. A line from the logbook

Ms. Prado asked each of us to put one line into this report that we heard and did not write ourselves. I asked my parent, who signs the manifests, for one line from the C-130's paperwork, exactly as said. They wrote it for me in pencil:

You are invited to ride the nose wheel well if you will come; I will not be so prudent as to say—“I won’t touch a man.

The nose wheel well is the space forward of the cockpit where the nose gear retracts for flight. On the ground, with the aircraft chocked and the engines turning, it is the warmest, loudest, and most forbidden seat on the Hercules — which is exactly why a line like that gets written down, and kept. I am leaving the line the way it was said, without the quotation mark at the end, because that is how it arrived.

6. Finding

The finding of this report is that an aircraft is kept the same way a letter is kept: by the names written on it, by the people who remember where it went, and by the lines that get filed even when nobody ordered them filed. The Hercules flies unprepared runways because somebody decided a ramp and a dirt strip matter more than a polished terminal; it has been more than forty variants because crews kept finding new jobs for the same airframe; and the nose wheel well stays in the logbook because someone offered it, once, as a seat.
