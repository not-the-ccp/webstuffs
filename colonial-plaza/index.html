A Table Game of the Enclosed Empire · For 2 to 4 Players · Ages 12 and up · Playing time one long Sunday

Colonial
Plaza

A game of openings, wings, relocations and vacancy, set on the orange-belt ground of Orlando, Florida. The largest retail development in the state at the time of its construction — and the board it became.

Published by the Emporium Game Company · rulebook set on the mall floor · reissued with the errata sheet, 1991

Chapter One What is in the box

The board reproduces the Colonial Plaza at its full scale of 1956–1990: four parcels of open-air court, two enclosed wings, one concourse, and the long diagonal of the shopping street. With it you will find: twelve store cards, four anchor tokens (Belk, Jordan Marsh, Ivey’s, Dillard’s), four pairs of shopper tokens, one vacancy counter running from 0 to 20, one year dial turned from 1956 to 2000, and one rent ledger, ruled for the two supermarkets and two variety stores that form the opening complex and never leave the board.

The two supermarket counters are wooden, the two variety store counters are card, and the difference is deliberate: a wooden counter cannot be closed, a card counter can, and the distinction is worth exactly as much as the players make of it.

Chapter Two Setup — the 1956 opening

Place the board with the orange belt to the north. Set the year dial at 1956 and the vacancy counter at 0. On the opening court place the anchor token of Belk in its original corner, and deal the two supermarket cards and the two variety store cards to the board itself: they are already built, and no player owns them. The game begins, as the building did, as the largest retail development in the state of Florida — which is to say it begins already famous, and players must decide what a famous mall does with the rest of its life.

Each player takes four shopper tokens in a different colour and one empty ledger page. There is no player in the year 1956 who owns the mall; the mall belongs to the board, and the board belongs to the weather, which in Orlando is a standing rule of two seasons.

Chapter Three The turn

A turn has four moves, taken in this order, and no other order:

  1. Advance the dial. Turn the year dial one year, or, on the anniversary of a built wing, to the printed expansion year. The dial does not move backwards, and neither does the concourse.
  2. Walk the court. Move your shoppers along the shopping street as the dial permits; a shopper may enter an enclosed wing only after the wing is built, which is the whole point of the game.
  3. Trade. Play store cards from your hand against the ledger, paying rent in tokens to the board or to the player who holds the matching anchor. A supermarket card pays on the odd year, a variety store card on the even year, and Belk pays on every year it stands, wherever it stands.
  4. Build, if the dial allows. Consult the Expansion Chart below. When a wing is built, turn the card over and read the year aloud, because a wing built silently is a wing built wrong.

Chapter Five The vacancy rules — from 1991 forward

When the dial passes 1990, the vacancy rules come into play, and the game changes its weather. From the early 1990s onward, the players of this mall did not lose to one another; they lost to Orlando Fashion Square, which is drawn on the far edge of the board and never, at any price, opened. Every turn from 1991, the active player draws a competition card and, if the card shows the Fashion Square name, raises the vacancy counter one step for every step of distance between that player’s shoppers and the enclosed wings. The property’s own complexities — the parcels, the easements, the long argument over who owns which corner of the diagonal — are represented by the parcels rule: a wing cannot be extended while two or more parcel lines cross it, and clearing a parcel costs one store card per line, burned and face down.

A mall that reaches the year 2000 with the vacancy counter at 20 or less, with every wing built, and with no store card unplayed but the burned ones, holds the plaza and wins. A mall that reaches 2000 with the counter at 20 is not ruined — it is, the manual notes, exactly as described — but it does not win, and the players are invited to shake hands and argue about the parcels, which is the manual’s considered advice and its only remaining rule.

Chapter Six The errata sheet, 1991

Reissued with the reissue. The first edition of this manual came off the press with one line on the back of the title page that no reissue has ever been able to explain, and the 1991 errata sheet preserves it word for word, in the hand it arrived in, with the instruction that it be read aloud once, at the end of any game that reaches the year 1990:

“God, I hope it’s in the form that never quite the right engine, hot rock rising beneath the surface grows in inverse ratio to its membership.”

The errata sheet adds nothing to this line, interprets nothing, and strikes out its own suggestion of a strike-out. Read it once. The dial continues.

Chapter Seven House rules & company

Three rules the Emporium Game Company declines to print but tolerates: no player may walk the court in a hurry; the Jordan Marsh token may be named after someone in the room, once, and only once; and the year 1973 may be skipped only by unanimous agreement, which has not once happened at the Emporium table and is not expected to. The board travels with the rest of the shelf; the same crate that carried the season notebooks of the Californian games carried this manual, folded, between the 1973 card and the errata sheet, and it is kept at the same bench as 2005–06 UCLA Bruins — Field Notes, Notebook No. 13, where the counting is done in dozens and the wings are never mentioned but counted all the same.

For the player who prefers a slower enclosure — one lot, one plate, one folio of sealed letters — the shelf holds Lot 127 — One Pale Mountain Pigeon, in Life & in Plate, and for the player who wants to return to the room where the shelf stands, the front page keeps the register open.
