The North Bank Watch & Tally Club · fourteen members, one bench, no bylaws

The Lerkendal Ledger

No. 19 · mid-summer · issued from the bench between the elms at Trænderøya

Subject: Lerkendal Stadion — the count of 21,405, the roof of 1962, and the one seat the register will not give up.

From the secretary · the ground itself

Members are asked to remember the ground exactly as it stands. The Lerkendal Stadion — the East Norwegian mouth gives it as [ˈlæ̂rkn̩dɑːɫ ˈstɑ̀ːdɪɔn], which the secretary has had the trouble of learning and will not let go of — is an all-seater association football stadium at Lerkendal in Trondheim, and it is the home ground of Rosenborg BK, of the Eliteserien. It holds 21,405 spectators, which makes it the second-largest football stadium in Norway, and the club exists, in part, so that this number is always counted, never merely quoted. The ground opened as a multi-purpose stadium on 10 August 1947, as the main football and athletics stadium of Trondheim; on match days the athletics track still carries its old white line, and the club's founder has been known to time a lap of it with a pocket watch, for the record, without saying why. In its early years the venue belonged mostly to the football teams SK Freidig and FK Kvik, and Rosenborg did not become a tenant until 1957. A new grandstand with roof was completed in 1962, and floodlights went up in 1968; after 1968 the evening game, which the secretary considers the true form of the game, became the ordinary one. That is the ground. The rest of this issue is the club's business around it.

From the counters · the season's arithmetic

The north stand runs, as the counters keep it, from row 1 to row 52, and the roofed grandstand carries the rows the 1962 builders numbered themselves, which is why the numbering restarts mid-aisle and the ticket office has had to apologize about it for sixty years — the counters have confirmed the apology is genuine. This season the club's six match-day counters returned a figure of 21,405 seats sold across the home fixtures for which they were posted, which is not an accident, and which the secretary refuses to call one. The two ends of the ground differ, as ends do: the Lerkendal side goes first, the way it has always gone, and the counter posted there finishes her tally before the second whistle, a record the club keeps in the same ledger as the seat counts, in the same hand, for the same reason. The oldest part of the ground — the farthest end, the one that kept its 1947 shoulders when everything else was rebuilt around it — is where the club's bench is, under the elms at Trænderøya, and it is from this bench that every number in this Ledger is finally checked before it is printed.

From the register · the reservation

The club's register, kept beside the seat counts since the founding, carries one reservation that no member will explain and none will delete. The line was entered by the registrar, in the same hand as the counts, and reads exactly: For the human system whose whole history fills Case No. 44-41 of 2025, Veliyev v. — and there the entry ends, mid-caption, the way a caption ends when the case has not yet said what it is against. Seat 114, row 3, north stand, has been held to the reservation ever since, unassigned, unpriced, and unmentioned at the ticket office, and the club's rule on the matter is that a member may ask about it once, in person, to the registrar, at the bench, and that the registrar has so far answered every such question by turning the page to the next seat count. The register keeps what it keeps; the ground holds the seat; the club notes the fact here, verbatim, and returns to its counting.

Colophon & the neighbouring shelves

A reader who wishes to see how a small body of people keeps a case file when the court has kept its own should walk across to the third-day transcript of In re Sand at Oslo Tingrett, where an estate, a theater, and a vinegar crock in the cellar at Markens gate 33 are argued down to a single line of shorthand no one has explained — the closest cousin, on the club's shelf, to a seat count that ends mid-sentence. A reader who prefers a fellow small club, one with the same temperament and a much longer water to walk, may take Newsletter No. 12 of the 87-Mile Boatmen & Bargees' Club, issued from Lock House 13, which counts 105 locks with exactly the discipline this Ledger applies to 21,405 seats. And the reader who has finished the ground, the arithmetic, and the reservation, and wishes simply to leave the bench and come out, will find the front page of the Webstuffs where this issue is filed, with the elms unpruned and the pocket watch wound.

— The secretary, for the fourteen, with seat 114 accounted for.
