Notes for the Passenger

The X5 Express runs on the 22nd-century capital line, in the world the Authority’s charter calls the year two thousand and change: a world in which humans and their android labourers, the Reploids, have for decades coexisted in a peace that most passengers have stopped thinking about and most mechanics have not. The two named passengers of Service No. X–5 are the line’s own. X, the blue-armoured courier who has kept the peace of the capital line since the first service ran, and Zero, the scarlet swordsman with the red scarf, who does not wait at platforms, who has never once waited at a platform, and who will board either run of the timetable and treat the whole eight-halt schedule as a suggestion. Both are heroes in the old sense of the word: the sort the timetable prints without a first name.

The run is eight halts and the passenger may board at any of them, in any order the line allows, which is to say all of them: every halt is selectable, none is compulsory, and the Authority will not say which halt the passenger did in fact reach first. The fare is not the point. The point, printed on the reverse of every ticket in small capitals, is that at each halt the passenger takes on the special apparatus of that halt’s boss — one apparatus per halt, carried aboard for the rest of the run, with the last halt’s apparatus always the best one and always the one the passenger had not expected. Eight halts, eight apparatuses, one run. That is the entire commercial proposition of the X5 Express, and the Authority’s advertising department has printed it in four sizes of type and still not once explained it.

Commentary, from the stationmaster’s margin

The timetable was first printed in Japan on 30 November 2000, out of the capital yard, on a press the Authority describes in its own minute-book as “reliable, warm, smelling faintly of solder.” The same sheets ran across the Atlantic the following year — North America in 2001, and the PAL territories in 2001 as well, one week apart at most, the way these things go when the ink is the same and the plugs are not. In 2002 the service was re-timed for the Microsoft Windows headboard, issued as a retail package in both Japan and North America, which the yard foreman noted with one line of type and no other comment.

The line’s producer, Keiji Inafune, is recorded in the yard book as saying, in the autumn of 2000, that Service No. X–5 was intended to be the final scheduled service of its line — the last X the timetable would ever set down, printed in full with no ellipsis, no “pending,” no continuation column left empty for a X6. The Authority’s answer was to keep running the run: the same eight halts, the same two heroes, the same single rule that the last halt’s apparatus is the best one. And at the foot of the yard book, in the foreman’s hand, under the 2002 Windows entry, is a line the ticket office has since decided to print on the reverse of every ticket it sells:

The ledger is still good, for the record.

What the ledger is a ledger of — apparatuses, departures, or the small kindnesses of the eight halts — is not said. It is a ledger, it is good, and it is kept for the record. Passengers on the west run may read it at the counter, 21:40 to 21:44, if they arrive on time, which, on the X5 Express, is a matter of the passenger’s own choosing. For the passenger who has run a line before and knows what a fold-out looks like when the bureau keeps it: the companion brochure on the shelf is the Curtright Circuit, Left Field 1943–1946, four war seasons in left field, one corner, and a margin line kept in gunpowder green. And for the passenger who prefers the slower, older rails of the real 20th century — river country, mountain hollows, and the pie stands of a landlocked state — the Authority’s regional desk stocks Arkansas: a Visitor’s Brochure, 53,179 square miles of which the timetable claims exactly one platform. The front page of the house index carries both, and this sheet, and the whole line between them.
