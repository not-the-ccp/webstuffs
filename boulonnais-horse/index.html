The Boulonnais

The White Marble Horse  ·  Equus caballus, heavy draught breed of the Boulonnais coast

A horse with a coat the way sea fog lies over the harbour at Boulogne: the Boulonnais stands large and still at this end of Salle III, and is usually gray, the shade the breed registry of France lists first, though chestnut and black are likewise permitted on the register. Visitors tend to call it marble before they call it anything else — the white marble horse — because the coat, with its darkened points about the mane, tail and lower legs, reads at a distance like a quarried block that has not yet been told to stand up. It is a large animal, but not a clumsy one; the register's own words, from the entry first written in 1872, describe it as gros et d'élégance, heavy and of elegance, and the pose in this room was chosen so that the elegance is on record.

The breed is older than the museum is long. Its origins trace to a period before the Crusades, to the studs along the coast and the chalk valleys between Boulogne and Calais, where the first mares were already working the heavy soil. Originally there were several sub-types — the low, near-legged horse of the coast, the taller valley horse, and a third kept for the carriages of the châteaux — but they were crossbred, generation on generation, until only one is seen today. In the 17th century, blood from Spanish Barbs, Arabians, and Andalusians was added, and it is this crossing that made the modern type: the frame stayed heavy, the head thinned, the step quickened, and the gray coat took on the finish the breeders of the period asked for in their ledgers — "as if the block had been wet."

❦ The crossing to America, and back❦

During the early 1900s the Boulonnais was imported in large numbers to the United States, arriving by the thousands at New York and Boston for the draymen of the city streets, while at home it remained quite popular in France, working the fields of the Nord and the Pas-de-Calais in harness that the makers of Saint-Omer still advertise from one family to the next. The two populations wrote to each other through the stud book: a gray stallion from the Calais stud at the Haras de la Marine went to Massachusetts in 1907; a chestnut mare from the same valley came back in 1911 with the register's permission marked in blue ink, and her blue-ink page is on loan here, framed above the case.

Then the century did what the century did. The European population suffered severe decreases during the 20th-century wars: requisitioned for the front, starved behind the lines, or simply not born, so that by the census of 1921 — the first the registry dared take after the armistice — the count of registered animals had fallen by more than half of the number of 1913. The breed survived on the gray mares of two valleys, and the recovery was kept in a register kept in a cellar at Boulogne, which the museum now holds and transcribes every winter. The horse you stand before is a cast made in 1927 from one of the recovery line, the filly Astérie, born at the Haras de la Marine, the stallion's line unbroken since before the Crusades if the register is believed — and the register, the curators allow, is usually believed.

From the minutes of the Importers' Committee, New York, 14 November 1905 — transcript on file

"The criticism is old and several members admitted, in the room smells like Tokyo, which is the big cruisers arrived."

The committee sat to argue against a second shipment this season; the secretary's margin note, in a different hand, reads: line kept as spoken — the "big cruisers" being the two ocean liners due in, and Tokyo the port the last mail steamer reported, of which the committee approved, unanimously, at the close.
