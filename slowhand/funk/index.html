In Memoriam · The Album Keepers

Funk

c. 1965 – still on the turntable · declared missing, never found

A music genre, born in the African-American communities of the mid-1960s, that deemphasized the melody, buried the chord progression, and put the whole weight of the dance on a bassline and a drum part. No coroner yet.

The club is holding a wake for Funk, and the news that forced the wake is the news nobody will stop quoting back: it is not dead. There is no Friday post with the bad letter in it, no date to print in a strip under the name, no coroner, no certificate, no number. The Secretary, H. M. Voss, has read the evidence twice at the turntable with the needle down, and the evidence is that a genre of the mid-1960s is still moving people’s feet on the records in this house sixty years on, at the slow tempos the rest of popular music never quite brought itself to. So we write the obituary the way the club does everything: in the whole, in order, and with the needle down. What follows is a eulogy delivered at a wake for a man the man has declined to be. If it reads oddly in the places that should read oddly, that is the point, and I will not argue.

It was born, such as anyone was born, in the mid-1960s, in African-American communities, out of a mixture of everything the mid-twentieth century had been popular among them: soul, R&B, jazz, blues, gospel, and the gospel’s cousin, the dance. What the musicians did with the mixture is the whole of the subject: they pushed the melody to the back of the room, turned the chord progressions down low, and concentrated the music into a strong rhythmic groove of a bassline played by an electric bassist and a drum part played by a percussionist, usually at tempos slower than the rest of the pop they were sitting next to on the same shelf. The result is a complex percussive groove in which the rhythm instruments play interlocking parts against each other until the whole thing feels, in the only word the genre has ever allowed itself, hypnotic — and danceable, which is not a second word but the same word from the floor.

Early funk is a one-name obituary with a chorus. James Brown, born in the South, carrying the pocket north, fused jazz and blues into the machine and pressed the machine into 1965’s Papa’s Got a Brand New Bag, the record the house argues is the birth certificate, and into I Got You (I Feel Good), and into the one beat, that downbeat on the one the whole dance leans on like a handrail. He took the machine to the Super Bowl halftime in 1968 and played it to the cold and the million in the house. By March 1970, in the middle of one of those grooves, the horn player Maceo Parker put a saxophone solo on it — I Can’t Stand Myself — and the genre, such as it was, stood up and was recognized. The television show that took its name from one of Brown’s 1967 songs was on by 1971, and by then the branches had grown thick enough to shade a street.

The branches. Sly and the Family Stone carried it into the light — Everyday People in 1967, Family Affair to number one in 1969, a full band of horns and keys and a woman singing the front of it — while George Clinton in Baltimore took it into the sky, Parliament founded in 1970, the Mothership coming down over the audience, the boots, the space-age suits, Give Up the Funk (Turn Your Face into the Light) in 1977 and the whole parade at the 1978 Super Bowl, and Bootsy Collins, of course, who took the bassline out of the pocket, stretched it into a long coat, and made Hang Up ’N’ Funky in 1976 the record the house plays when it wants to be sure the floor is still the floor. A genre is not a man, but it is a set of hands, and those hands were in this attic, on these records, before the attic had this house. The last of those hands came out of Compton in 1987, and its object now hangs in Gallery 3 at Dr. Dre — Placard No. 48, where the case is built to the same pocket, the same unarguable downbeat, and one line in the dark is read off as a number. I will not argue with the placard, and the placard will not argue with the wake.

Across the water, the same hand grew a heavier storm of its own — Afrobeat, the West African cell of chanted vocals and intersecting rhythms that Fela Kuti established over Lagos from the 1960s onward, and that the attic now observes the way it observes everything else: in the whole, in order, and with the needle down. Its weather almanac entry, Entry No. 47, pressure high and holding, no front expected, is filed in the annex beside this wake, and the two entries share a shelf, a drip, and a rule, which is that a genre is not a format at all so much as a number.

Which brings the eulogy home, because the house keeps funk the way it keeps everything. The drip from the beams — the one the Gallery 3 timetable lists as a percussion instrument, and I will not argue, and neither did the genre — is a funk part in the way a metronome is a metronome: it does not stop when the listening ends, and it is not in the song but the song cannot happen without it. The reel counter on attic unit No. 2 has been running all winter for Tobias Finch at sea, and it counts what the garage below counts, which you can follow turn by turn at A Grand Prix in the Garage Below — Build Log, Turn by Turn, where a 1:24 car is being built one part per meeting, no flipping, in the saint’s slow name. And the refreshment room keeps its own schedule for the same house: the Coca–Cola Line, Timetable No. 47, in service from 1886 at the unarguable downbeat, with 1.8 billion daily boardings and distances that stayed through the whole of it, which the Secretary files beside the reel counter, in the whole, in order, without arguing. And the saint, Eric Clapton, who took his last bow this year, spent the latter decades of his life leaning on exactly this groove — a bassist, a drummer, and a slow tempo holding the melody up instead of the melody carrying them — so the two obituaries in this attic are not the same obituary, but they are the same hand, and the same light at that hour.

The forty-seventh number of this newsletter, The Long Side, set the era down in a line the whole house quotes, and I will not argue: “Of such a time in which a museum placard could assume you had the record in your hand.” Funk is the record. The placard could not say the name and not mean you were holding it, the way a museum placard could not say a bassline and not mean your feet were already there. That is not nostalgia. That is the groove, doing its work on the room, sixty years on, at a tempo slower than the rest of the pop.

The survivors, in the order the club prints them: the basslines; the drum part; the interlocking grooves; the one, unarguable; the slower tempo, which outlasted the faster ones; an electric bassist wherever one is playing; a percussionist, often the beam itself; the May listening, played whole, side into side, with the two empty chairs by the window empty; and one right hand, unarguable, on the shelf beside the first-press Layla. The Pastoral Hall, across the gallery, files a nine-year loss the same way the club would file a record that never stops moving — column by column, unarguable — and it is worth standing by for a minute at Lake Wells Station, Placard No. 17, where the stock went dark and the distances stayed. No burial is scheduled. There is nothing in the ground. The subject is on the turntable, it has been since 1965, and the rule for the record, and for the eulogy, is the same: play it whole, do not flip, and keep the hush. The wake is a listening. The listening is the wake. Bring the record, if you have the record.
