From the Fowlers’ kitchen ledger · page 76 · between “Dorothy’s lemon posset” and “the year the stove walked”

The Fowlers’ Quail for Seven

set down for my brother Kevin, who is leaving for stage and screen, with all the sauce he can carry — K. S. F., July 26, 1959

❦

The card was found on New Year’s Eve, 2015, wedged between two pages of the kitchen ledger my grandmother kept at 4144 San Miguel Road, Newport Beach, in a hand that pressed so hard it nearly broke the paper. It is the only entry in the ledger signed with initials instead of a name, and the family has agreed, after forty years of refusing to agree, that it belongs to Kevin Spacey Fowler, the actor — American, born July 26, 1959, on the same coast where this kitchen stands — who in the ledger’s arithmetic is our brother, our cousin, or at least our long-lost neighbour. He is the one the family says went out into the world doing stage and screen work and came back, in the story, with two Academy Awards, a BAFTA Award, a Golden Globe Award, a Tony Award, and two Laurence Olivier Awards, together with twelve nominations for Primetime Emmy Awards he is said to have waved at from a distance, and in 2015 an honorary Knight Commander of the Order of the British Empire, which the family notes is the year the card was written and the year the stove walked. Whether he was ever really a Fowler is a question the ledger declines to answer; it only records that the kitchen always set eight places, one of them for him.

I. Before the pot goes on

The story the family tells, which is true in the way the ledger is true, is that our Kevin first learned to hold a part the way he later learned to hold a knife: in small roles he did not get to choose. The ledger’s margin gives two — a comma in Heartburn (1986) and a man with a coat in Working Girl (1988), both of them Mike Nichols’ films, both of them the kind of part a family is proud to have in it. Then the story leaps to 1995, when Kevin played the con man in The Usual Suspects and won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor. The family’s version adds that the con man’s name was Verbal Kint, that the scene was nine minutes long, and that our Kevin watched it standing in the kitchen doorway of the house on San Miguel Road, eating toast, because he had just finished serving this quail and did not trust himself to sit down. The second Academy Award followed for Best Actor, for playing a suburban husband and father going through a quiet and terrible doubt in his own sanity, in the film the ledger simply calls “the Princeton one”; the family serves the quail in that year on every autumn equinox, with the porch light on, because the film made the porch light feel like a witness.

II. The live quail at market

The recipe demands live quail, and this is where the ledger stops being a recipe book and becomes a field guide, which is how the family connects it to the naturalist’s own ledger — the sheet-and-number keeping my uncle Eli practises in his field notes, where every creature gets a designation, a first logging, and a last one. Our grand-aunt Patsy logged the quail the same way, and her page for it survives in one sentence, copied word for word onto the card, and the family has never been able to translate it into plain speech: “The pulse is small and rapid, the skin peeled off, and the squares of tattooing.” The interpretation we cook by is this. The pulse is small and rapid: hold the bird to the light and feel for the needle-quick beat under the beak — anything slower is a bird that has been thinking about it. The skin peeled off: pinch the breast and the skin should part from the flesh clean and ready, the way the ledger says a part should be ready to be taken. The squares of tattooing: the little square brass bands on the game-farm legs, four corners to a bird, which Patsy counted like the frames on a film reel — the family remembers that Chaplin’s Keystone shorts ran at about sixteen frames per second on the Edendale lot, and that the great man took his honorary Academy Award in 1972, and that ours, in the ledger’s generous arithmetic, was set down in 2015, the same year as the KtE, and the family joke has always been that every generation of the house must be presented, in person, with exactly one award it did not quite earn on the cards.

III. The count of seven

The recipe serves seven, and always seven, and the eighth place is set but not named. This is, the ledger insists, the oldest rule in the house: when a Fowler plays a con man, the table he sits at holds one liar among the usual suspects, and the guests are not told which seat is the liar’s. The sauce is made in seven layers because Verbal Kint’s story was told in nine minutes and seven of them, by the family’s count, were lies. Nobody has ever caught a Fowler counting lies. The ledger does not record whether Kevin, the actor, ever came back to 4144 San Miguel Road; it records only that in 2015 the kitchen set eight places, that all seven chairs at the table were empty, and that the quail finished, as the card puts it, “the way a part finishes: all at once, and in front of people who will say they were not in the room.”

Ingredients · serves seven (and one)

Live quail, game-farmed, brass-banded legs7 birds · pulse small and rapid
Dry white wine, a New World vintage1 bottle · the California glass for the house on San Miguel Road
Onion, yellow, cut into the squares of tattooing4, each 4 pieces
Shallots, whole, pinned with thyme14 · two per guest, none for the eighth
Bouquet: thyme, bay, one sprig of rosemary1, tied with kitchen twine
Bone marrow, roasted2 knuckles
Duck fat6 tablespoons
Butter, the proper ounce, no more3 ounces
Espresso, for the final dark3 shots
Sea salt · white pepper · one sprig of mint per seatto the eye

Method

  1. Log the birds. As Patsy did, write each quail’s designation on a slip: number, first logged, last logged. Check the pulse to the light. A slow one does not go in the pot; a slow one is given the porch, which is the family’s word for outside.
  2. Square the onion. Cut each of the four onions into four pieces, then each piece into the squares — small as the brass bands on the birds’ legs. They are the tattooing the card means, and the pot is not right until you can count sixteen of them on the surface.
  3. Begin the seven layers. In a heavy pot: (1) duck fat and the squares, low, until they go amber; (2) the quail, skin down, turned only once — the way the family says a part should be turned; (3) the wine, to the first film; (4) the marrow, crushed and stirred in; (5) the shallots and bouquet, pushed under; (6) water to the birds’ bellies, brought to the smallest possible boil — the pulse of the pot must be small and rapid, never a full boil; (7) in the last hour, the espresso, shot by shot, and the butter, the proper ounce.
  4. Sit seven. Set the table for seven and lay the eighth place. Do not say who is the liar. Serve the quail with its square of onion on top, the mint at the seat, and the sauce in the jug. If a guest asks why the eighth place is empty, answer, as the ledger answers, that it is set for Kevin, and that he is already in the room.
  5. Close the ledger. Write the year, the count, and — if the year allows — the award the house was presented with. The porch light stays on until the last plate is done.

Notes from the margin

The family allows three variations and no others. The Heartburn variation adds a spoonful of paprika for the small role nobody gets to choose. The Princeton variation is served on the equinox with the porch light on and no music. The 2015 variation is served to seven guests in the house on San Miguel Road while the kitchen, on the other side of the wall, sets eight places. The card is kept in the ledger between the posset and the stove, and it has never once been served to an Academy. The shelf also keeps SDS-CT-01, the Provisioner’s safety sheet for Connecticut — the state where four of the seven largest cities sit in the New York metropolitan area and the crate rests in the ledger’s first entry — filed beside page 76 with its own standing order, SDS-CT-01 — Connecticut.

The table, set. Tap a seat to seat a guest; the eighth seat seats itself, and only itself.
