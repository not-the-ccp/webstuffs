FIELD NOTES ON THE KITCHEN · SHEET Nº 989 · FOR THE FORTIETH EDITION’S ERRATA

Erni & Son

The Coppersmiths’ Table · Merchant Street, Danzig · one door, the guild list kept, the conversation never more than two degrees warm

Six of six chairs, one of which no guest is to ask about · Bill for the table of six: eleven groschen, and the fish

There is a region of Europe that the maps give up on, and the coppersmiths of Danzig have put it on the bill.

Central Europe is the country between the countries: the region between Eastern, Southern, Western and Northern Europe, variously defined by everyone who has tried, and best defined, I now think, by a kitchen. When the coppersmiths at Erni & Son opened their table on the Merchant Street — the same shop where they add names to the guild list at their leisure, which is to say, without an appointment — they set the region out in full. Thirteen names go on their bill of tables: Austria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia, Switzerland, and, in the small copper type at the bottom, Transylvania and Vojvodina. Cultural diversity, the house says, is not an obstacle to a menu; it is the menu. What the thirteen share — the trade routes, the border crossings, the long habit of being asked which region they are in — the house treats as the shared bread course, and it is right to.

History sits on either side of the fire, and the room is honest about it. One side of the pass serves the memory of the Commonwealth: throughout much of the Early Modern period the territories of Poland and Lithuania were one, and the honey cake here is made the way the border would not have noticed — one cake, no border, the river running through the jam. The other side serves the memory of the Empire: the Ottomans came to occupy most of present-day Croatia and present-day Hungary, and the south beyond, and the gulyás in the copper pot is cooked to that older fire, smoked paprika first, the pot beaten and tinned in the house, the tins signed at the bottom in the coppersmiths’ own hand, the way they sign the names. Both memories at one table of six. I have not seen a room reconcile them more neatly. The conversation at mine rose and fell exactly as the household of the name Erni would have measured it, and stayed, by the end, within two degrees.

We were six, and the seventh chair was set, as it is always set at Erni & Son, at the head of the table of six, and no one of us was to ask about it, on the manners. It is the name’s chair, the chair of the name itself, and the household of it — which signs at 14 Rue des Tanneurs, one door, three bells — keeps it in its letters the way the coppersmiths keep it in their shop: their apology to Ernest, filed in the same miscellany, is the only document I know that measures a conversation by the degree and still calls the result a success. I brought the letter to the table, and the head coppersmith read it over my shoulder, nodded once, and put the sea bream back on for us.

On the sea bream. You know the story if the household has told you: at the Rue des Tanneurs, a gift tucked into the back cover of a presented volume, one paste, one corner, one start; and the Ernis of Piacenza changed the subject to the fish and did it well, because they still had the fish, while we had already eaten it, and had none, and had to invent one — a sea bream — and it showed. At Erni & Son the fish is not invented. It comes from the harbour, it is scaled at the pass, and it was served to us that evening, and it did not show. I write this as a reviewer, but I will say the household will read it as the settlement: the fish is cooked, and the corner held, and the sea bream of the Rue des Tanneurs and the sea bream of the Merchant Street are, from this evening, the same fish, and the record is corrected in the only way the record can be — by the dish, on the plate, in front of six. (That a small tin of brass lettering, the nameplate for this very chair, once rode through Texas in a crate of foundry type and came home still warm from the tarmac, in the bag of the returning clerk of Barcelona, is filed beside the squadron’s apology to the returning clerk; the coppersmiths know, they have the crate, and they tinned a plate for it.)

The bill itself is the ledger, and the ledger is the house’s custom, kept like the coppersmiths’ guild list: every recipe numbered, every table entered, one line per evening, word for word, and the line is never corrected, because the ledger does not correct — it files. Ours is the line the head coppersmith read out at the close of the evening, in her full voice, for the room, and I have kept it word for word, uncorrected, in exactly this shape:

The fortieth edition of the 988 Hashed 989 Hunting 989 Native of America 986 Or fowl, to draw it to the helmsman.

Read down by the coppersmiths: the fortieth edition of the house ledger, entries ninety-eight-eight, hashed — the pot dish, the hash of the day, gone to the end; ninety-eight-nine, hunting, set for the table and not yet taken; ninety-eight-nine again, native of America, the fowl from the west, kept at the head of the ledger; and ninety-eight-six, or fowl, the dish at the far end of the pot. And the instruction, which the room at last understood: to draw it to the helmsman — the fowl is carried, on the beaten copper, to the seat of the helmsman, which is the seventh chair, at the head of the table of six, and the helmsman is not to be asked for, on the manners.

We had it all right by the second telling, which is more than I can say for the bill at most kitchens I review. The pierogi came in eleven — one, the head coppersmith explained, for each child christened Erni in the decade around 1888, the register the household keeps at E, beside Ernestine and Enfield, water-damaged in Piacenza in 1899, the margin line illegible, one of the eleven ours and the household would not say which; the coppersmiths serve all eleven and decline, likewise, to say which is ours, which is the kindest form of not-saying I have had at a table. The strudel is from the Austrian side of the pass, the cabbage from the Hungarian, the coleslaw from the Polish, and the small, very cold glass at the end — Liechtenstein, of course, the glass, because there is nothing else that size to be honest about.

On the walk back the head coppersmith kept the ledger, and I kept the letter, and we did not once shorten a name, which at a table of six, in a region of thirteen, I count as the evening’s real course. The region between the countries has, at last, a room that keeps it: variously defined everywhere else, and defined, at the coppersmiths’ table, exactly once, on the bill, in full, with the seventh chair set. Eleven groschen, and the fish. I would cross a border for it, and I have crossed one before, on a crate’s account, and found the crossing was the point.

Filed for the fortieth edition’s errata, in the Webstuffs miscellany, under the letter E — beside Ernestine and Enfield — where the reader is invited to take the letter down, open it at the errata, and leave the seventh chair exactly where it is.
