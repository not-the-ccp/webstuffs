South America , n.

Pronounced souθ “æmΙ.’Ιkæ · also el sur, also the south of the Americas, also, in one’s own sleeping voice, the far bank

Bounded in the dream as in the chart: west by the Pacific Ocean, north and east by the Atlantic Ocean, south by the Drake Passage, northwest by North America with the Caribbean Sea spread out between, and south still, past the edge of the sheet, by the Antarctic Circle, Antarctica, and the Antarctic Peninsula, which reaches up out of the white like the bow of a boat that will not dock.

A continent lying wholly in the Western Hemisphere, for the most part in the Southern Hemisphere, with a considerably smaller portion in the Northern; it may also be described, when the dreamer needs a quieter word for it, as the southern subregion of the Americas. To dream it is to dream a whole continent lying down in a single sheet of dark water, head to the north, feet under the ice, and breathing very slowly. The dream includes twelve sovereign countries — Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, Suriname, Uruguay, and Venezuela — and two dependent territories on the far shelf, and in the dream the twelve sit at a long table the way twelve clocks sit at the hour, all of them struck and none of them agreed to stop. Brazil is the largest room and the one the dreamer wakes in; Chile and Argentina run the whole length of the table as its two rails; and the table itself, if the dreamer is careful, will be found to be the quay at the end of Halstrom Street, which is to say the dream has been home all along and only the water is new.

When Dreamed of

Most commonly the continent arrives as a crossing. The sleeper finds a ferry — small, commuter, six crossings a day, twelve seats and a place for one car — working a schedule that changes with the swell, and the ferry is crossing the ocean as though the ocean were a river with a harbour master, and as though the harbour master had chalked a three-week run of form on the wall beside the ice chart, which in the dream he has, and the chalk is still wet. The twelve seats are the twelve countries and the dreamer holds the thirteenth, which is the place for one car, and the car is not coming, and that is all right. If the ferry crosses on a weekday, a good sign, meaning a run of form; if it crosses on a Saturday, the best figure in this entry, meaning the walk will happen, the bench will hold, and whatever was being promised in the room with the bad scan will, at last, be said out loud in full.

The dollar sign comes in the dream the way it comes in the quarter: attached to a title, and then to a title of a title. The dreamer will see a sign spelling a word with one bulb short of a dollar and understand, without being told, that the film kept the money and the book kept the sign, and that in the southern hemisphere the dollar is the only currency that is also a promise. A red-tailed hawk may roost on the cornice of the dream and not move; this is the Roost, and it means the corner is settled. The two dependent territories appear as two small islands of lamp-light at the end of the sheet, lit, waiting, belonging to somebody, and the dreamer is not expected to know to whom.

The Ferry Clause

This entry exists, frankly, for the sake of one broken sentence. The Synergy Desk keeps it as a placeholder, the way a surveyor keeps a broken baseline: “What remained was a good sign, meaning a run of form; if the St. Johns ferry.” The group that recorded it voted, four to two, to keep it rather than strike it, on the grounds that a fragment is a promise in the middle of being made, and the scan broke exactly where a promise in the making always breaks. The concordance reads it from the far bank, and from this side the fragment has a coast to it. The ferry’s half is the crossing, and the “run of form” half is the good sign that remained; the conditional — the if — is the water. So the fragment, as far as I can steer it: the ferry was crossing on Saturday. Not on time, note, and not at rest — crossing, mid-stroke, on the day the walk meets at the gate, second Saturday of the month, 14:00 sharp. A crossing on Saturday is the quarter’s word for an itinerary that will actually be walked, and the continent, which is the far bank, is what it is: the destination the ferry was steering for all along, twelve countries at a table, set, waiting, and not moved.

Interpretation

  • To dream the twelve sovereign countries — Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, Suriname, Uruguay, Venezuela — seated and struck like clocks is a sign of a house in which many affairs are true at once and none of them has outlived the others.
  • To dream the Pacific on the west — the long, dark, quiet wall of the continent — is a sign of a shelf that has held for a decade and will hold for another; the four stories on it are the four stories on the exchange shelf, and the two counts agree.
  • To dream the Atlantic on the north and east is a sign of the open side: a question that was closed and should be opened, and the dreamer is the one who keeps the key without knowing it.
  • To dream the Drake Passage on the south, with the Antarctic Peninsula at the end of it, is the rarest figure in this folio and a good one: it means the dreamer has reached the last date of the walk, the one read aloud in a voice pitched for the whole group, and that whatever remains of the night belongs to the ice, which keeps.
  • To dream the smaller northern portion — the part of the continent above the line, the part that belongs to the other hemisphere — is a warning of the mild sort: the dreamer has been counting one shelf only, and there is a second shelf, in a different room, that also belongs to the walk and has not been dusted.
  • To be dreamed of by the continent — that is, to feel in the dream that the whole southern sheet is lying awake and watching the ferry — means the dreamer is a walker, and the bench is holding the seat, and no further interpretation is required or will be given.

The Kitchen Interpretation

Where the dream ends at a table — and this one does, because the continent is a table — the concordance gives the whole domestic meaning in one instruction: Set twelve places along a long board, Chile at one end, Uruguay at the other, Brazil in the largest chair, and leave the thirteenth place for the car that is not coming. The board should be the colour of the Pacific at 14:00, and the place settings should be laid by anyone who has ever walked 2.1 miles in 75 minutes and called it short. When the twelfth place is set, the ferry has crossed; when the thirteenth is left empty on purpose, the run of form is chalked on the wall and the dream closes. This is the whole of the domestic meaning, and it is a good one.

Cross-Readings

The walk that gives this entry its Saturdays is staked out, gate to bench, in The Steakley Walk, the eight-stop itinerary down the Armor & V$ quarter — 2.1 miles, 75 minutes, one marquee a bulb short of a dollar — which ends, and this is on the record, on a 4–2 vote for the bench. The vote that keeps the fragment this entry reads is logged in The 3rd Alternative, Thread 41 of the Synergy Desk, where the St. Johns ferry gets its schedule, its harbour master, and the sentence that broke in the middle of a promise. For the companion figure of this class — a blue that is also a whole city lying down, eighteen titles on the crest of it, and a staircase that does not end — the concordance refers the reader to its entry on Sueño Azul, Universidad de Chile, Entry No. 143, which closes with the blue on the hands; this one closes with the chalk still wet on the wall. On the far corner of the sheet, beyond the water, the state that the Council of the Far Bank ruled to be the eastern shore’s far bank sits minuted in the quorum book instead of the dream folio — Alaska, Minutes No. 215, where the ferry’s Saturday schedule is extended by four votes to nothing, the abstention counted mid-stroke, and the catering ledger gives the banquet one pint of porter and a bone the council will not decide. The ferry, for its part, is a river with a harbour master in the dream, and there is one such on the record with a printed schedule — the northward line of 187 miles between the Coast Range and the Cascades that the company re-surveys when the ground moves — kept at The Willamette River, Line NW-4, timetable & commentary, where the gauge at the junction is entered every morning before the 401. On the Argentina place of the table, in Gallery 2 of the museum, a case keeps the red-and-white stripes of La Plata — José Daniel Ponce, Case IV, Placard No. 12 — the 1982 and 1983 back-to-back championships, and a minutes volume the secretary has never managed to correct. In the year the ferry’s Saturday record was crossed, that rail of the table took its second crown in Mexico — and the tournament’s one system fault is kept as an error record at Error 1986-WC-24, The Mexico Cup Fault, where the museum’s unamendable line is quoted word for word and the two dependent territories at the end of the sheet are given an address. The rest of the quarter’s field notes, and the shelf this folio is filed under, are kept on the front page of the miscellany, Webstuffs.
