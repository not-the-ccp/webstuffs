Contemporary Worship Music

Praise and worship · Genre panel · CWM · 1960s–present

Contemporary worship music — CWM, also known as praise and worship music — is a distinct genre of Christian music made for contemporary worship. Over the past sixty years it has grown out of the informal Jesus-music gatherings of the late 1960s into a sound stylistically similar to pop music: simple chord progressions, a steady backbeat, and vocals sung close to the microphone. The songs are usually called “praise songs” or “worship songs,” and a typical service is led by a “worship band” or “praise team,” with either a guitarist or a pianist serving as the lead.

The repertoire is now shared worldwide. Maranatha! Music began publishing praise songs from Calvary Chapel in Costa Mesa in 1971; Darlene Zschech’s “Shout to the Lord” was written at Hillsong Church in Sydney in 1993; Chris Tomlin, Matt Redman, Hillsong United, Bethel Music, Jesus Culture, and Elevation Worship have since carried the sound into arenas as well as sanctuaries. Christian Copyright Licensing International, founded in 1988, tracks the songs reported sung in hundreds of thousands of churches each week.

The genre is most at home in charismatic and non-denominational Protestant churches, though some Catholic congregations have incorporated praise and worship sets into the Mass itself. A band might run through six songs in forty minutes, with no interval between music and prayer; the lyrics are projected on a screen, the lights are lowered, and the congregation stands or sits as the bridge directs.

Portland’s own example is Fearless BND, the contemporary worship band behind We Are Fearless, released 24 February 2017 through Fearless International. The album was made across two winters and one long summer in a converted boathouse with a leaking roof, where the drip from the beams became a percussion instrument and its title track was tested on the last train out of town. The full timetable of those recordings is shown in Gallery 3: We Are Fearless — Timetable & Commentary.

The museum sits two blocks from the Bates–Seller House at 2234 NW Flanders Street, where Dr. James Seller once kept his ground-floor consulting room and William Bates watched log rafts assemble on the river from his tower window. The house’s copper whale weather vane, commissioned in 1895, still turns above the maples and horse-chestnuts on clear evenings: Bates–Seller House Walking Tour — Northwest Portland.
