Definition

Iron is a chemical element, marked Fe for its Latin name ferrum, of atomic number 26. It is a metal belonging to the first transition series and to group 8 of the periodic table. It is, by mass, the most common element on Earth, forming much of the outer and inner core of the planet, and it is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, which is how the encyclopaedia explains the particular weight of the flood gravel at the head of the Lagan, and the particular weight of everything else in the valley. In its metallic state iron was mainly deposited by meteorites; the encyclopaedia notes that the sky did the delivering and that no invoice has been received.

Extraction

Extracting usable metal from the ores requires kilns or furnaces capable of reaching 1,500 °C (2,730 °F), some 500 °C (900 °F) higher than that required to smelt copper, and it is a difference of half a thousand degrees that the entry regards as the whole of the subject. The copper could be lifted from the ground with a fire that a school open day could almost match; the iron demanded a furnace of its own opinions, a door that would not open before the hour, and a bellows that the record keeper’s father, Josua Tufuga, has described as “the size of a small boat and of the temper to match.” The difference is why the first useful iron was found rather than made, riding down from the sky in meteorites, and why the making of it is counted in millennia rather than seasons.

History

Humans started to master the process in Eurasia during the 2nd millennium BC, and the use of iron tools and weapons began to replace the older bronze ones slowly at the edges and quickly at the borders, until the age took the metal’s name and kept it. What the entry records is not a single event but a front, moving: the ploughshare before the scythe, the knife before the nail, the hinge last of all, because the hinge asks to be trusted. By the time the process had reached the Lagan Valley it had been handed down as a family business, and the valley entered the Iron Age with its flood gravel, its slate from the valley side and the sea-shore, and a barometer that had not yet been invented but would, in time, hold at 1,018 and refuse to be moved.

Occurrence in the Lagan Valley

The valley’s iron is not worked in any furnace in the present century; it stands in the ground and is counted, which is the encyclopaedia’s way of saying that a multiple-cropped plot is counted once and a mineral deposit is counted forever. The flood gravel of the Lagan carries it in its dark bands, the slate from the sea-shore carries it in its seams, and the open-day field at the Stormont Estate, where the visitors cross on the hour and the sheep have been told in so many words, is arable above the ironstone and ironstone below the barley. Class 5B at Laganmore Village School measured the field on the open day and filed Report No. 27-B on the biosphere, marked 91 out of 100, in which the barley is counted as part of the worldwide sum of all ecosystems, and the iron under it is not counted at all, being abiotic, being borrowed from the core of the planet, and being, in the ledger’s phrase, a component that will not leave.

In local records

The Stormont Estate committee kept minutes about the slate doors of the storehouse gate, which were made from slate cut on the sea-shore and hung on iron hinges, and about their repair on the open day. Under Rule 3, that a line whose meaning is not found shall be kept as written, the entry reproduces the committee’s last entry on the subject, in full and without a pause, from the top of the line to the bottom of it:

“Card 27 Clipping, reverse of the committee; this season the chair of the Lagan, with doors of slate from the sea-shore.”

The chair of the Lagan for the season is Cllr. Whitfield, who also kept the chair of the Matchnight Committee and has observed, for the record, that a thing may be silent and still be against you. Asked what the line means, the chair answered that the doors are slate, the slate is from the sea-shore, and the question of the reverse of the committee is a question for the almanac, which keeps card No. 27 with its clipping on the other side of the paper. The entry does not resolve the line. It only puts it where a committee would put it: on record, under Rule 3, with the hinges oiled and the barometer standing at 1,018 beside it, refusing to be moved.
