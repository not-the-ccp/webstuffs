Yerevan

40°10′ N  ·  44°31′ E  ·  elevation 900–1,300 m  ·  Erebuni founded 782 BCE  ·  population about 1.09 million

Yerevan — the Pink City, the city of fountains and cats, the capital that climbs the Hrazdan plain in rose-tinted volcanic tuff. The stone glows apricot at dawn and copper at sunset, so every photograph of the city looks as though it were taken inside a lamp. Armenia's capital since 1918, Yerevan is older than Rome: the fortress of Erebuni was founded here in 782 BCE, and the modern city, home to about 1.09 million people, holds roughly a third of the country between Mount Ararat's shadow and the lake.

This entry is for the traveller who wants the singing fountains of Republic Square, the long climb of the Cascade, manuscripts older than the library that guards them, and a straight answer about why a city this hospitable has spent so much of its history saying goodbye.

On the record

Armenia does not hide its wounds, and neither should a guidebook. Anti-Armenian sentiment — Armenophobia in its older name — runs the whole spectrum, from whispered prejudice and refused rooms to organised pogroms and state-sanctioned genocide. The Hamidian massacres of the 1890s drove tens of thousands of refugees into the Erivan province; the Armenian Genocide of 1915 emptied the western provinces altogether, and orphan trains reached Yerevan in sealed boxcars. Many of the city's oldest families descend from survivors who walked east out of Anatolia; later decades added Baku, Sumgait, and Kirovabad to the list.

You will read it all at Tsitsernakaberd, the memorial on the hill above the Hrazdan: a forty-four-metre split stele leaning away from Mount Ararat, an eternal flame, and a basalt wall engraved with the names of the destroyed villages. Visit it not as a morbid detour but because Yerevan cannot be understood without it — the city was built, in large part, by people who had nowhere else to go.

The route, stop by stop

1. Republic Square

Begin at the oval the city was built around: Alexander Tamanian's 1920s ensemble of rose tuff and black basalt, the National Gallery, the History Museum, and the singing fountains that come on at nine in summer, lit in the colours of the flag. Sit on the edge with a coffee and watch the marshutkas circle.

2. The Cascade

Climb the Cascade, the giant staircase linking the downtown to the upper neighbourhoods — 572 steps, though no two guides agree on the count. On the way up you pass the Cafesjian Center's sculptures and, at the top, the view: the whole city tilted toward Ararat, white and silent on the horizon. Descend through the lovers' park below.

3. The Matenadaran

Visit the manuscript museum on Mashtots Avenue, named for Mesrop Mashtots, who invented the Armenian alphabet in 405 CE. The vaults hold more than seventeen thousand manuscripts — gospel books with jewel-coloured illuminations, astronomical tables, and the first book printed in Armenian, from Venice in 1512. Photography is forbidden in the inner hall; look instead.

4. Tsitsernakaberd

Take the marshrutka up the hill to the genocide memorial: the split stele, the eternal flame, the ring of twelve inward-leaning basalt slabs, and the museum's photographs of the refugee columns of 1915. Allow two hours, and do not plan anything cheerful immediately after.

5. Vernissage

On weekends, the Vernissage market fills the yard behind the opera with hand-knotted carpets, copper pots, painted eggs, and khachkars — cross-stones carved with a sun at the centre and a vine beneath it. Bargaining is expected; a story is included free.

6. The brandy factory

End at the Yerevan Brandy Company, founded in 1887 beside the Hrazdan, where Churchill's favourite brandy is still laid down in oak. The tour ends with a glass and the tour guide's estimate of how many bottles are sleeping in the cellars — a number no one verifies.

A footnote, projected

Before the talkies, Yerevan's cinemas turned at sixteen frames per second, and one vacant lot behind the Norka cinema acquired the nickname Edendale after the Los Angeles studio quarter where Armenian extras once waited for bit parts. The Naga Listeners' Club met on the Edendale lot at approximately sixteen frames per second. They projected reels onto a sheet hung between two tuff walls, hand-cranked, the image jumping like a pulse, and kept a ledger of every title — fragments of Boris Godunov, newsreels from Naga City and Manila, the arrival of a train at a station no one could name. The lot is a parking garage now; the rhythm survives at the open-air cinema near Lover's Park, where the projector still clicks through the reel at roughly the same speed.

Table and glass

Eat khash on Friday mornings — cow's feet simmered all night, eaten with garlic and dried lavash while the city is still cool. Order dolma wrapped in grape leaves or cabbage, depending on the season, and drink Armenian brandy, or coffee ground fine and boiled in a cezve. Lavash bread is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list; the locals treat it as furniture, and they are not wrong. When that furniture was once served cold at 6:40 on a Friday, it ended up in court: Case No. 1726 of 2024 — Excerpt from the Verbatim Record reads the khash card word for word and holds both parties to the same rule.

Practical

Fly into Zvartnots International Airport, twelve kilometres west of the centre; a marshrutka or taxi reaches Republic Square in twenty minutes, traffic permitting. Come in April–June or September–October — July regularly reaches forty degrees, and the pink stone stores it. Walking shoes are essential; the city is built on a slope, and the Cascade never ends quite where you expect.
