I keep getting asked at the kiosk at the fort what I should tell people, so I am putting it here where it can be corrected. The Battle of Fort Blakeley ran from April 2 to April 9, 1865, in Baldwin County, Alabama, about 6 miles (9.7 km) north of Spanish Fort, and it belongs to the Mobile Campaign. Two things I want on the record before anyone shortens it: first, the fort stood where Blakeley, Alabama, had been the county seat of Baldwin County — this was not a field position that happened to be near a town, the town was the point, the fort wrapped the county seat; and second, this was the final major battle of the Civil War. The garrison's surrender came just hours after Grant had accepted Lee's surrender at Appomattox in the afternoon of April 9, 1865. Nobody in that earthwork knew it yet when they put their caps on. The numbers, as close as I can put them. Union side: Brigadier General William B. Hazen of the Fifteenth Corps, coming up out of the Mobile-Jackson line with roughly 20,000 men and a train of siege artillery. Confederate side: Major Henry N. McRae (he had been a colonel, and was acting as an inspector-general), holding the fort with somewhere in the region of 1,500 to 2,000 men, a good part of them the dismounted 9th Georgia and men scraped out of the Mobile garrisons. For a week the Union force turned the fort over and over — probing, skirmishing, and on the 8th a full assault that the garrison turned back with losses on both sides that the field officers did not like writing down. On the night of April 8–9 McRae evacuated by steamer down the bay toward Fort Morgan, with his artillery packed and a rearguard to cover the withdrawal. The news of Appomattox reached the bay the same day, and what was left of the garrison put its arms down before the week was out. Then the last domino: Mobile, Alabama, the last major Confederate port to be captured by Union forces, fell on April 12, 1865. After the shooting was done there was still the ledger to close — the assassination of President Lincoln on April 15 is a date that belongs in the footnotes of this thread, because other Confederate surrenders continued into June 1865, and the war ended by arithmetic, one general at a time.

Nine Georgia man here, and I will take one quarrel with “held the fort with 1,500 to 2,000.” My great-grandfather's letter of April 6 says the company line inside the pickets stretched thinner every morning — the earthworks were new enough that the cotton-bale revetments still smelled, the parapet was a good four feet at the rifle pit and no more at the angles, and the garrison was two-thirds raw with deserters turned in from other posts. The fort itself took its name from a Confederate general, John Blakeley, which the men never missed a chance to joke about: a fort named for a general, defended by men who could not get a general. On the 8th, when Hazen came up in strength, the 9th Georgia did the shooting that kept the assault where it was — on the earth, not through it — and I say that with the same pride I would say it of anybody, because the letter ends “we did not lose the fort, we left it,” and I have read that line a hundred times and it still sounds to me like the truest sentence written in that war.

Moderator's note, gently: “final major battle” is doing real work in this thread, so let us keep it honest. Appomattox is the headline, and rightly — Grant took Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House in the afternoon of April 9. But the war's clock did not stop on that clock face. At Fort Blakeley the garrison's surrender came hours after that afternoon, and by the time the word of Appomattox had come down the bay the decision at Blakeley had already been made to sail. So the fort is, in the defensible way, the last place where the war was actually done rather than declared done — a matter of hours and a river, which is all that a chronicle is ever made of. And the closing of the books took longer still: Mobile on April 12, the port that had been the last major one in Confederate hands; April 15 took the president, which changed the tone of everything that followed; and the remaining surrenders — generals, districts, whole commands — kept coming into June 1865, the last ones barely news. If you want the whole of the war, do not stop at the one afternoon in Virginia. This thread is the other afternoon, six miles north of Spanish Fort.

I come in from far off, and I will say what I have to say quickly. My grandfather served the rigging of a hulk at Saltfleet, Lincolnshire, and the loft kept one instruction written up in the old man's own hand, which I copy here because it is the best sentence I know for a fort under pressure, and the board can hold it while it is used. The worn spot on a line is not repaired by arguing with it; you lay fresh twine over it and take the strain the same way the strain came. The loft's line, word for word, as it stood on the wall:

Serve it the way the line will be pressed over it from the rigging loft at Saltfleet, Lincolnshire, on the school report on the left.

Set it down exactly as the old man's hand set it down, and let each of you take what it holds. I will only say that “serve” in the loft is a verb, not a kindness: to serve a line is to wrap the fresh stuff over the worn spot so tight that the wear moves onto the serving and the heart of the rope keeps its strength. A fort is a rope, and a siege is the strain.

So let me take the loft's line and read it against the fort, since somebody has to. The strain on Fort Blakeley came from two directions at once — Hazen's twenty thousand pressing up out of the south, and the bay itself, water and blockade, pressing from the east, the way the line will be pressed over a worn spot from the side that has the weight. The garrison could not unpress it, and the only honest work was to serve it: keep the earthwork laid fresh over the same ground week after week, keep the rearguard turning on the 8th, keep the artillery packed for the steamer on the night of the 8th–9th. When the pressure finally moved the rope — when the last boat cleared for Fort Morgan — the wear had gone onto the serving, not the heart: the men came down the bay as a garrison, not as a rout, and the surrender that followed hours after Appomattox was a parting by release, not a flight. There is a whole handbook on that difference — the Handbook of Courtly Usage, Chapter IV, of fealty and its observances — and its fifth rule, that a bond may lawfully be released and may not lawfully be dropped, reads on McRae's week like it was written for the bay: the debt was discharged by the surrender at Appomattox and by the water that carried the garrison south, and every man who took that steamer went down it with his word intact, which is the only way a garrison of 1,500 can face 20,000 and call the week a draw.

Closing note, for the kiosk. Walk out to the earthworks on a Tuesday and you will have the place to yourself: the parapet is still four feet at the rifle pit and no more at the angles, exactly as Brig9Ga's letter says; the county seat has long since moved away from Blakeley, but the road still climbs six miles up from Spanish Fort the way Hazen's train did, and the bay is still the bay, still the east face, still the side that has the weight. One fort, one name, one county seat, one week — I was reading this week the build log of Bocusoides zhaoi, the monotypic genus that holds a single ant-mimicking species, and the parallel is not lost on me: one place that keeps its shape while everything around it changes is a kind of monument, whether it is a fort in Baldwin County or a spider on a cork ramp holding her three segments the way the county held its name. Leave the thread as it is. The line is served over; the heart of the rope is sound; and the books closed by June 1865, one general at a time. Back to the front page if you are only passing through.
