Hunting & Outdoors

Hunting Party, Four Boys, Accepting Second Grown Man

Stan Marsh (age eight, blue hat) and three companions — Kyle Broflovski, Eric Cartman, and Kenny McCormick, the third of whom is always in the orange parka, identity on file at the newsstand — go up to the east ridge this week on the season’s hunt, under the supervision of Stan’s uncle Jimbo and Jimbo’s war buddy Ned, both veterans of the same long war and of one another; references: twenty years. Rifles provided. Enthusiasm negotiable. One member of the party has declared, in writing and in person, that he does not want to put a bullet in a living thing, and the party treats this as a scheduling issue, not a dealbreaker. Pickup at the culvert, 7:00 a.m. sharp, the blue trailer, the one with the dent. Firewood accepted at the door.

Lost: Scuzzlebutt, or the Boy Who Told About It

Campfire accounts by one party member (the one who brings the beef jerky and the complaints in equal measure) describe a creature in the pines: one tail, three legs, and a face you would know anywhere. The story has spooked two of the four boys and all three grown men, and the ridge has responded by doing exactly nothing, which the boys find worse. The Ledger will not confirm the existence of Scuzzlebutt, and neither will the woods. If you have seen one, do not approach, do not count its legs, and do not bring a rifle. If you have one — which the Ledger strongly doubts — write to Box 27, South Park. No pictures. The Ledger pays no postage either way.

Jimbo & Ned, Outdoors Guides, Moving Operation to the Valley

By agreement of the two, the season’s camp moves its tent poles to the valley floor and keeps the ridge for the weather. Rates by the armload of firewood; the guide’s one rule, entered in both men’s notebooks, is that nobody leaves the party, and nobody leaves the party for anything, including a squirrel. The two have guided, since the war, eleven parties, one of them themselves. A spare canvas, two stoves, and the war medal (display only) come with the booking.

Personals

“I’m Not Going to Do It” — One Boy, Age Eight, Asks the Ridge for a Way Out

The undersigned, holder of the blue hat, reports that at the sight of the first live thing of the season his rifle went quiet, and that he would like the classifieds, and the ridge, and one or the other of the grown men, to furnish a way out of the matter that does not cost an apology. No lectures. No “that’s how it is, kid.” Responses to P.O. Box 4, the blue envelope, or to the bench by the culvert between now and the first snow. The boy is, as of this printing, still in the party, still with the rifle, and still not going to do it.

Found

Found: One Desk Drawer, Lodge Office, Oak, Brass Latch

Found at the lodge office, under the counter where the season’s ledger lies: one desk drawer, oak, brass latch, last fitted to its carcass in 1974 and kept on the workbench ever since, unlatched and full of pencil stubs. The drawer is now refitted and in use. The lodge clerk, who has held the bench since the war, enters in the log, and the Ledger prints it here word for word, because a line entered in the log is a line the lodge keeps: The drawer now holds a 1974 interior — stop the register never once been refused. The clerk reads her own line as follows: the drawer now holds an interior fitted in 1974, and the register — the season’s ledger, which is what the lodge calls it — is stopped from any further entry, on the ground that it has never once been refused, and a book nobody refuses needs no new page. Claim the drawer, if it is yours, at the lodge office with two witnesses and a pencil stub. The lodge holds it until the ridge cools.

For Sale & Wanted

For Sale: One 12-Gauge, One Barrel, Near Mint, Dent on the Stock

Honest dent, honestly earned, in the direction of a tree. Sells at $40 to a buyer who can hold a target steady for a full minute, which the seller times, standing behind the buyer, out of habit. Call Ned at the lodge, the phone that rings twice, after nine, when the party is asleep or at least pretending. The second grown man of the hunting party, in a private sale, will not sell the party’s rifle. See also: Wanted, one good reason not to shoot, cash, no questions, no lectures — see the personals column, the blue envelope.

Notices & Legal

Geological Notice — The Ridge to the East

Printed at the county’s request: one volcano, on the east ridge, asleep since 1911, showing steam since Monday, ash on the birch stand since Tuesday. The party detailed in this section’s hunting column is, at the time of this printing, on the ridge, and is not aware of this notice, and the Ledger, which prints the notice anyway, is not aware of the party’s location either, and considers this the season’s main event. Hikers, hunters, and anyone who has heard a story about Scuzzlebutt should know that the mountain is doing its own thing, on its own schedule, and that the schedule is, as of this hour, short. The Ledger regrets the lateness of the notice. The mountain did not. The eruption, when it comes, will be loud; the county asks that windows be closed and that the party, when it returns, be received without comment.

Company on the Same Shelf

The Ledger Keeps Its Other Notices Here

Readers who take to the log book’s habit of entering a line word for word and leaving it unruled will find a cousin of the practice, a full shelf over, at the Leipzig bureau: the lost property notice No. 1809/47, one German composer found in the cloakroom, whose pencil line in the coat lining the clerk also prints exactly as found, and whose holding period, like the lodge’s drawer, keeps running. The Ledger’s field desk keeps a longer page in the same quiet hand — the walking tour of the Carolina parakeet, seven stops through the Wren Grove — a county that logged its last bird the same way the lodge logs its drawer: one line, in ink, and then the bell. And on the question of what happens when a big underground thing decides to stop being underground, the bench at Reactor 4, built entry by entry at scale 1:200, keeps the numbers the Ledger would otherwise have to print here, and does so in geiger-counter ticks instead of dollars per line. The full map of the shelf, including the culvert and the blue trailer, is at the front page of the Ledger.

The Rate Card

How to Place an Ad, One Dollar per Line

Bring the line, written, to the newsstand at the corner of the schoolyard fence, before six, and the press boy prices it at a dollar a line, with the last word of the last line rounded up. Geology is priced by the county, free, and appears wherever it pleases; the Ledger’s standing arrangement with the ridge is that the ridge may publish without asking. Personals are printed without the writer’s surname, and the blue envelope is the only address the Ledger requires. Nothing in this rate card applies to the mountain. The mountain has never once been refused, and the register, per the lodge log, is stopped.
