GOODMAN, E. URNER

Entry G-13 · of the dream in which a tall, soft-spoken man in a khaki necktie keeps a program for twenty years and signs his work in the margins of a handbook

The Dream, as Most Often Reported

A dreamer reports, in the standard form of the case, that he is standing at the foot of a long wooden building in the early autumn of 1931, and that at the top of the steps a man stands who is not young, who is not old, who is, in the dream's own phrase, exactly the right age to be kept on. The man's necktie is khaki, his shoulders are square, and he holds a book with both hands the way a man holds a bird: carefully, and knowing it will fly. The dreamer is told his name is Edward Urner Goodman, though the dream does not think of the name so much as hear it, the way one hears a train a county away. Goodman has, the dream says, been in this building since before the dreamer was born — born, in fact, on the 15th of May, 1891, a date the dream is unusually firm about — and he will keep the building's program until 1951, when he will set the book down and step off the steps and not be missed for one whole summer, which is the dream's longest and most frightening sentence.

Inside, the building runs on. There are boys in neckerchiefs who do not age, a kitchen that serves exactly what is on the menu, and a long room full of paper where a new handbook is being composed line by line. Goodman moves through it writing in the margins. When the dreamer asks what he is writing, Goodman answers, in the dream's best voice, that he is writing the leaders' handbook, the one the leaders will actually use, and that a handbook is a building the reader walks through, so the writer has to keep the doors open.

Of the Figures, and Their Meanings

The khaki necktie. A necktie of this colour, worn by a man who is not a scout, means the dreamer is being run by a program rather than leading one. To wear it oneself is a warning against taking office at a jamboree.

The handbook. A handbook that is always being written means work that will outlive its writer; the dreamers who see it in good condition report it was printed in runs that a press in New York could not finish in a season. A handbook with blank pages at the back is the commonest form, and is not a bad sign. It means the story is only at its first printing.

The training center. A high place, reached by a switchback trail, where the air is thin and the instructors are patient. To dream the center before it exists — as one-third of the dreamers do, placing it in a New Mexico landscape in the early 1930s, at an altitude of 8,200 feet — is to dream a future in good order.

The neckerchief. Cub, scout, or explorer, the knot is the dream's smallest reliable symbol. A loose knot means the dreamer's own business is unfiled.

The radio sign. In the third variation the dreamer reaches a broadcast booth at the far end of the jamboree field, and the sign above the booth, which no one in the dream is supposed to read and everyone does, reads word for word: ON AIR Gazeta da Orquestra · Pedro do Rosário thread Pedro do Rosário — anyone been? The dictionary records the line without emendation. Dreamers who can read the sign report a thread of conversation running beneath the music, a thread about a road and a walk and a porch where something is still being sold by the ounce; dreamers who cannot read the sign report only that the orchestra was good and that they, personally, had not been. Both reports are accepted.

The Omens

To dream Goodman at the steps: you will be asked to sign for a book you did not ask for, and you will. To dream him writing: something you have been keeping informally will be printed. To dream the necktie in the wash: a program is changing hands, and the new hands are competent, which frightens you less than it should. To dream the center on foot, in bad weather, with the trail open: you are where the training is, and you know it. To wake from the dream on the 13th of any month: no added meaning; the date is the man's, not the dreamer's, and the dictionary keeps his date of 13 March, 1980, only for the record.

The Numbers of the Case

The dream, in its consistent versions, keeps five figures and keeps them exactly: 1891, the year the man was born; 1931, the year he came in; 1951, the year he went out; 20, the years between, which the dream counts on its fingers even though it has no fingers; and 1980, the year, past the dream's horizon, when the building's staff first learns, from a paper, that the man in the khaki necktie has finished. The dreamers who have met him — in Los Angeles, in the small print of a newspaper column filed by the police for safekeeping, on a long walk along a numbered road east through the mountains — report the same detail, and the dictionary prints it once: he thanks the dreamer for keeping the handbook on the shelf, and he is right to, because a building that is no one's is only a dream until someone is on the steps.

Related sheets in this shelf: the ledger column that keeps the lost badge and the red book, Small Advertisements, Los Angeles Evening Ledger, 11 November 1931; and the walking tour of the numbered road that the radio thread was heard on, The Ninety-Four — a Walking Tour of California State Route 94. Both are filed under G, with this entry, in the same drawer, for the reason the dream gives, which is that the three of them share a date in 1931. A fourth sheet shares the bench and the radio sign, and is filed beside them: the naturalist's field notes on the department that keeps the numbered road — Caltrans, Field Notes of a Naturalist, Vol. II — where the man in the khaki necktie is transcribed without emendation, which is to say, not at all, and where one ledger line at the far end of the interval is kept word for word.

The Dictionary's Opinion

A dream of programs, in which the man in charge has served twenty years and is not yet tired, is the rarest and the steadiest of dreams. It does not promise wealth. It promises a shelf, a trail, and a book that will be reprinted. Dreamers are advised to keep the entry open until the fourth printing.
