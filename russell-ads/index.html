Small Ads & Notice Board

Entered against the name of Bertrand Arthur William Russell, 3rd Earl Russell, born 18 May 1872 at Trelleck, hereafter styled in every ad simply “Russell.”

REGISTERED READ ALOUD KEPT 97 YEARS

§0 Notice to the Column

The Office issues this column on the standing instruction of the keeper of the file: that where a name has been entered against too many offices to be served by a single page, the small ads take it. The name is that of Bertrand Russell, English mathematician and philosopher, a founder of analytic philosophy and a leading philosopher of the twentieth century, who produced pioneering work in logic, in set theory, and in the philosophy of language, and who afterwards — the Office notes this with particular pleasure — did something rarer, which was to explain all of it to the general public and be blamed for it. He was, by the count in the ledger, a controversial figure of the first rank: outspoken in his atheism, his pacifism, and his opposition to nuclear weapons; more known to the world for his books and his commentary on social and political questions than for the mathematics that made his name; the grandson of John Russell, 1st Earl Russell, a former prime minister of the United Kingdom, who sat in the same House and left the earldom in the line. The ads below are set in the order the Office received them, which is not quite the order of the years, and the years are not the order of the consequence. What the ads do not carry is carried in the rule the office shares with its neighbours on the shelf: what the register prints, it prints in full; what it cannot read, it keeps word for word and says so.

WANTEDAd. 1872

One Mathematician, Wanted by the Century

Wanted, a grandson of a prime minister, age twenty of May 1872, to be found by the twentieth century for purposes of logic. He was to be supplied, the notice added, with a family that had sat at the table of the government and had left it, a house at Trelleck in the Welsh Marches where the reading began, and a temperament unsuited to staying where the reading pointed. The century took its time. It wanted him in 1901 to lecture in logic and mathematics at Cambridge, and found him willing; it wanted him at St John's College by 1905, and found him there, in the chair of philosophy, writing with a friend in a three-volume manuscript that ran to more than 1,800 pages. Applicants need not apply on the grounds of moderation.

FOR SALEAd. 1910–13

One Volume, Three Parts, Very Heavy

For sale, Principia Mathematica, three volumes, Cambridge, 1910, 1912 and 1913, authors B. Russell and A. N. Whitehead, complete, with the famous page on which two plus two is proved, and the later pages on which the arithmetic, as the buyer is invited to find, has not quite finished. Sold at the publisher's price of one guinea per volume; the Office reminds purchasers that a library that has not carried this set has not yet been tested for flooring. No returns will be received on the ground of difficulty. A cheaper companion is in stock for ten shillings and sixpence: The Problems of Philosophy, 1912, one volume, portable, and the volume by which the name was carried into the drawing rooms of the public.

LOSTAd. 1901

One Paradox, Unclaimed

Lost, a set. It contains all the sets that do not contain themselves, and it is not to be inferred, on inspection, that this settles the question of whether it contains itself. Last seen in a lecture room at Cambridge, in the first decade of the century, before it had a name; found, by 1901, in the work of Gottlob Frege and the foundations of his arithmetic, where it is reported to have done material damage. If found at large, the Office advises the finder not to open it and to send it to the Register. A reward is not offered; the paradox has never once been returned, and the set theorists have since built fences all round the garden in which it used to sit.

IMPRISONEDAd. 1916–18

He Will Not Move

It is recorded for the column, and is kept on the same shelf as the entry for Eric Chappelow (1890–1957), Questions & Answers of the Conscience Register, where the register holds that the campaign which carried the poet out of a four-month cell was supported, among others, by the name of this ad: Russell of the Register, with W. B. Yeats and George Bernard Shaw in the same line, a philosopher, a poet and a playwright standing in the one place that the prison authorities had to weigh the cost against. The ad then continues, for the name itself refused to move. In 1918 he was arrested under the Sedition Act for a speech against the war, tried at the Central Criminal Court, and sentenced to three months’ imprisonment and a fine of £1,000; the same term was visited on Cambridge University, which in June 1918 declared its fellowship of him “vacant,” on the ground of his “avowed and persistent antagonism to the British Nation.” The register's summary of that time is deliberately short, and it had come through anyway. He served his term at Brixton, the books from the library arriving in the cell as they were allowed; the sentence was afterwards reduced, and the fine was set aside, and Cambridge, having nothing left to say, took the fellowship back in 1925 on the quiet, and said nothing about it, which the Office notes was not in the register's habit, but was in the century's.

WITNESSES WANTEDAd. 1953–64

The Demonstration, 1 June 1953

Witnesses wanted to a demonstration at Trafalgar Square on 1 June 1953, organised by the newly constituted Committee of 100 (whose names, the notice adds, had been published and whose members were accordingly exposed, and whose first act was the founding of CND and its white disc with the three lines, the symbol by which the cause would be known). At eighty-one the name of this column walked the line with them. He was arrested, and he would be arrested again, the witness is reminded, as often as the century could keep him within reach; he kept his seat in the House of Lords to the end of his strength, the House where his grandfather had commanded from below it. Witnesses describing the walks, the arrests, the nuclear cases, and the opposition, which was opposed to the weapons and not to the weather, will add to the file. The register observes, in its only meteorological line, that the shelf it keeps carries a day — 13 September 1993, Station No. 1093, Chicago — in which the printed forecast was bright and the ground truth was not, and that the two readings were kept together; the entry stands at Today, Weather Almanac Entry, Station No. 1093, and the column and the almanac observe the same rule against the fine ordinary day.

DECEASEDAd. 1970

One English Mathematician and Philosopher

Deceased at Penrhyndeuleu, Llandinam, Wales, on 2 February 1970, in the ninety-seventh year, Bertrand Russell, 3rd Earl Russell, the name of this column. The ad is kept in the Office in the plain form it was set down: a founder of analytic philosophy, a logician, a writer of the public books, an atheist without apology, a pacifist without a break in the record, the man who put the general reader inside the argument and the argument inside the reader. The funeral service was held in the Church of the Holy Spirit, Cardiff, and the Office notes, against all its own rules of brevity, that the century which wanted him in 1901 and could not finish him in 1918 is here declared finished by him, by a day, having gone out of it at ninety-seven, which is not a number the register has ever met in a cell. One volume of problems, one volume that was not. The fence remains.

§9 The Column's Ledger

Six ads, one name, kept in this order: wanted, for sale, lost, imprisoned, witnesses wanted, deceased. The Office reminds readers that the same shelf holds the register's own entry for the poet the campaign carried out of the cell, at Eric Chappelow, Questions & Answers, and that the whole shelf, with the almanac entries and the field manuals and the ledgers that share the rule, stands at the Webstuffs front page. Ads not printed here are not denied; they are kept in the margin, as all such things are kept, unfinished and word for word.
