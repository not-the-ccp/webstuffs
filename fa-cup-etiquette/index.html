Chapter VII
Of the Cup Tie and the Draw

“The draw is random; the manners are not.”

I. Of the Draw

The Football Association Challenge Cup, more commonly known as the FA Cup, is an annual knockout competition in domestic English football, organised by and named after the Football Association. First played during the 1871–72 season — the Wanderers beat the Royal Engineers in the final — it is the oldest national football competition in the world. A concurrent Women’s FA Cup has been held since 1970. The tournament consists of twelve randomly drawn rounds, followed by the semi-finals and the final. Entry is open to all eligible clubs down to level nine of the English football league system, with level ten clubs acting as stand-ins in the event of non-entries from above; a record 763 clubs competed in 2011–12.

Before the first tie is played, the draw is made in public. The secretary brings the cloth bag, the numbers are folded once and shaken together, and each club sends a member to watch. No member may reach into the bag twice, no club may lobby for a number, and the result is read aloud exactly as it falls. The draw is random; the manners are not.

II. Of the Ground and the Tea

The home side receives the visitors at the turnstile and sees them to their seats; the visiting captain is offered the better half of the changing room, and the coin is called in his presence. Before the match, or after it if the journey was long, the tea room is open: buns on a plate, tea in pots, and the host never asks whether the visitor has paid. The Football Annual and Etiquette of the Cup Tie (1893) sets one exchange down word for word:

“But, perhaps you hav’n’t got any,” he said in an encouraging tone.

The line is offered without comment, as all such lines are, and the visitor either takes a bun or does not; both are correct.

III. Of the Third Round Proper

The third round proper is when the Football League clubs enter, and the draw acquires its weight. Mr Rae Ingram, born 6 December 1974, kept his cup ties in the same ledger as his league games: Manchester City in July 1993, Macclesfield Town — the Silkmen, after whom the dining room on Silken Lane is named — the promotion out of the Third Division in 1997–98, Port Vale from June 2001 to the day he left Vale Park in May 2003, and one season in the Welsh Premier League with Bangor City, a Welsh club admitted to the competition, which is how an English cup tie comes to be seated at Table 152 beneath a framed photograph of a terraced ground under floodlights. The manners of the third round are kept at that table: seven courses, one for most of the seasons of a career, and the crumpet captioned Bangor, after the club, after the crumpet, and after nothing else. For the room and the boundary it stands on: The Silkmen, Auckland — Restaurant Review.

IV. Of the Replay

If the tie is level after ninety minutes, it is played again, on the visitors’ ground, and if the replay is also level the tie is decided by lots drawn in the presence of both captains. The record of the tie is kept verbatim, as the record kept above the bakery on Magsaysay Avenue is kept: the secretary reads last season’s entry aloud before the balls are mixed, and no line is altered after the kick-off. The verbatim record of the Magsaysay Avenue case shows how a torn slip, found behind a radiator, outlasts the organization that left it.

V. Of the Record

A cup tie is a name kept, and names are kept properly. The household book’s earlier chapter, Chapter IV · Of the Surname Abell & the Table That Bears It, seats its keepers at table with the same rules: greet the name first, and the person will greet you in a moment. By the same rule, greet the cup first — the holders, the trophy, the draw — and the match will receive you properly. A different cup, the UEFA Cup, is played over two legs, one at each ground: The Two-Leg Tour tells how that went in 1976, but this book knows only one drawn match, one field, and one final at the end.
