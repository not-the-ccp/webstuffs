1 · Where the state is

Western Australia, commonly abbreviated as WA, is the westernmost state of Australia. It is bounded by the Indian Ocean to the north and west, the Southern Ocean to the south, the Northern Territory to the north-east, and South Australia to the south-east. In other words it holds the whole west corner of the country, which is what you can see from the verandah at Halls Creek if the sky cooperates: nothing but red all the way to the horizon, and then, if you squint, the blue of the Southern Ocean past it. Our station, Bedford Downs, sits in the Kimberley part of the state, roughly 84 kilometres west of Warmun and 109 kilometres north of Halls Creek, and the report is written from the side of it that has a house on it. Politics run on the same red road: John Swann Withington, an alderman and mayor of Orange in New South Wales, carried the seat of Blayney at a by-election in 1907 and lost it at the general election later that year, which is the nature of corners of the map everywhere — held for a season, then handed on: John Withington (1865–1944) — obituary.

2 · The size of it

Western Australia is Australia's largest state, with a land area of 2,527,013 square kilometres (975,685 sq mi), and is also the second-largest subdivision of any country on Earth. Ms Bedford asked us to put that in a unit we can feel. The class answers ranged from “fourteen thousand of our school blocks” to “all of Queensland, twice, and then some of New South Wales on top”. The answer I gave was the one that came back in the red pen: the state is big enough that its own weather systems get their own names, which is a fact about size and not about weather at all.

For scale, the drive from Halls Creek to the nearest capital, Darwin, is a whole day of roads, and you are still inside the state's corner of the map. Perth, our capital, is over three thousand kilometres to the south-west, which is why nobody in Year 8 has met it and everyone has seen the red dirt.

3 · The climates: one state, three answers

The state has a diverse range of climates, and the map we drew on page two of the report shows them in three colours because the fourth (purple) ran out of meaning. In the far north, around the Kimberley where I live, the climate is tropical: hot, and then the wet season arrives and does not ask permission. In the interior the country is desert — the report names the four on the back page. And on the south-west, around Perth and the coast south of it, the climate is Mediterranean: dry hot summers, cold wet winters, and grapes, which we only know about from the fruit cup at assembly.

4 · The deserts, named in full

The interior deserts I was asked to name, all four of them, are the Great Sandy Desert, the Little Sandy Desert, the Gibson Desert, and the Great Victoria Desert. The Great Sandy holds the most sand and the least argument; the Little Sandy is not a joke, it is the Little Sandy; the Gibson is named for a surveyor of 1873 and is mostly gravel, which surprised the whole class; and the Great Victoria runs right up to the South Australian border, which is the south-east boundary of the state from Section 1. If the state is one classroom, the deserts are the part of the room where the furniture went, and the map says there is a lot of it.

5 · Appendix: the truck and the jellies

The build log kept by the man who is making the balsa model of our station yard — the single-axle truck, 1:252, iron oxide weathering, the dog somewhere I have not been told — records a small commission: a sample crate shipped east to a casting platform, with an instruction note pinned to the crate's card. The note is unsigned. It is two lines, and the first line has become a sort of saying around the school, because it is true in exactly the way the wet season is true. It reads, word for word:

“Jellies are the times, when the truck should look as though it had been given up on and then forgiven.”

My explanation, which Ms Bedford let stand, is this. Jellies are the months, roughly November to February, when the jellyfish run out of the Indian Ocean and up onto the shallows, and the wet season is on the roads at the same time, so the single-axle truck sits in the shed and does not move for the whole of it. Rust does its work on the running boards. The tyres go flat in the corners. By the time the jellies are done and the red road has dried hard enough to drive, the truck has been given up on and then forgiven: it will not go the first morning, it will go the second, and by the third it is just a truck again. The note, I think, is not about weathering at all. It is about the months when a thing must be allowed to be useless before it is useful again, and the shed is the only place in the Kimberley that is honest about that. I have pinned a copy of the note to page four of the report, the same way Ms Bedford pins the maps.

For the whole build, sheet by sheet — the balsa homestead, the water tank gone green with algae, the mustering line of nine along the south fence — see the Bedford Downs Station build log. It is a better record of our country than most of the textbooks, and it is written in the same language as this report, which is the language of a bench and a long piece of red road. One last shelf item, which Ms Bedford let me add because it is about a man and not about a state: Charles Eaton, OBE, AFC (1895–1979), obituary — RAAF instructor at No. 1 Flying Training School, then three Pacific expeditions between 1929 and 1931, and the far shore of Catanduanes at one kilometre per ten million years. His 1929 expedition charts are kept in the drawer with page four of this report, which is the closest thing to a farewell this report can manage.

6 · Conclusion

Western Australia is the westernmost state, the largest of the states, and the second-largest subdivision of any country on Earth: 2,527,013 square kilometres of it, bounded by the Indian and Southern Oceans and by two other states, holding a tropical north, a desert middle, and a Mediterranean south-west. That is the report. The rest of it — the jellies, the truck, the shed, the mud — is the part of the state the map leaves out, and I have tried to keep it in, because a state that big can afford to have both a name and a weather.
