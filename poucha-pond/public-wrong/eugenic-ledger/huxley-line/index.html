THE ISLAND’S SEASONS OF READING · TRAVEL ADDENDUM NO. TA-70-1 · ADDENDUM TO EFT 69-01

The Huxley Line

A tourist’s route through the country of the writer — Godalming, Oxford, London, Cahuenga Pass, and the desk on the harbor · six stops, one pencil, no editing

ROUTE: east to west, Godalming to the harbor · DURATION: a lifetime, or a day, if you take the ferry · SEASON: any, but July is the writer’s own · STOP 6 COUNTER: 30,117

Who this route is for

The island keeps a case, and a case needs a season, and this summer the season is a writer. The route below goes through the country of Aldous Leonard Huxley (26 July 1894 – 22 November 1963), English writer and philosopher, born into the prominent Huxley family of Godalming, Surrey, who by the end of his life had left behind nearly fifty books — novels, essays, narratives, poems, travel writing, and screenplays — and a last sentence that no guide can finish for you. The route follows his, in the order the country offers it: out of the drawing room, over the road to Oxford, through the London drawing rooms, west over the water, and down, at the end, to the one desk on the harbor where the family’s word is kept in the state’s own column. The ferry master allows the whole line a boat ride of a day, Edgartown dock, no searchlights, and reads it the way he reads the two waters — one water and the other water, and the boat goes in whichever way both allow.

The route, in six stops

Stop 1 East Hill Street, Godalming, Surrey · 26 July 1894

The writer is born into the house on East Hill Street, where the family has kept its drawing room, its library, and its habit of thinking in public since the grandfather’s time. The grandfather, Tom Huxley, the naturalist who answered Darwin’s argument with a bulldog’s steadiness, has been dead six years by the time the boy is born; the uncle, Julian, is eleven, and will grow up to carry the family’s name into the very offices Stop 6 visits; the sister Margaret and the brother Maurice keep the room loud. Admission: the street itself, free, best walked in the morning. The drawing-room window faces the road, which is all a route can ask of a beginning. The family’s second voice in the state’s word — the one the keeper’s Entry Two on the eugenic ledger files against the 1883 clipping — is Julian’s: the geneticist who took the word from the drawing room and set it down in the offices, the fairs, and the surveys, and whose name appears in the column this route ends beside. The country is honest about that; the street is still there, and so is the uncle’s share of the account.

Stop 2 Balliol College, Oxford · 1913–1917

The route takes the ferry’s one train, Godalming to Oxford, and walks up Merton Street to Balliol, where the writer reads English literature and takes the degree. It is at Balliol that he edits Oxford Poetry — the little annual the college keeps of its own year — and puts out his first short stories and poems, the early volumes the later country will spend its life answering. Opening hours: the college as the college allows; the room the writer kept is not shown, but the stair smells of it. The guide’s note for this stop is one line, and it is the only instruction: read the annual the way the college keeps it, unedited, the way this label keeps its nights. A car accident of 1917 pulls the route off the track here for two seasons of convalescence; the writer comes back blind on the left side of his life and writes, later, that he had been given a door into darkness. The line holds for him, as it holds for the island.

Stop 3 The London drawing rooms · 1919–1937

London gives the route its drawing rooms: the witty social-satirical novel for the crowd that fills them — Crome Yellow, first published in 1921, then Antic Hay, the comedy of the country’s own lost youth — and, underneath, the grimly serious one, the one the drawing room’s mirror does not show. The volume to carry through Stop 3 is Brave New World, 1932, the book in which the eggs are hatched in state bunkers, the babies are conditioned on the milk trolley, and the whole population is written out in columns the state can sign. It is the volume the later small type is about, and the route keeps it in the luggage from here on. Merry-Go-Round (1935) and After Many a Summer Dies the Swan (1939) round the stop out; the last London season is spent in rooms the writer will not keep.

Stop 4 The road west · 1937

The writer crosses the water in 1937, the year the island’s own case is still nineteen years off, and the route follows him onto the road, with the road in the book: Eyeless in America, published in 1934, already had the country’s gas stations and its dark rooms written down; the crossing is the rest of it. The guide keeps the stop short on purpose. A route that spends its whole day explaining the road gets no further than the road. What the traveler will want: the radio under the blanket, the one the writer heard in the drawing rooms all his life, carrying in, from the wire, the other sky. The island has already recorded what came in that way; this route only passes the door.

Stop 5 Cahuenga Pass, Los Angeles · 1937–1963

The house is at 1900 Hillside Avenue, at the pass, and the room is the hillside’s. The writer lives here from 1937 until his death on 22 November 1963 — twenty-six years of screenplays, of the essays, of The Doors of Perception (1954) and its companion volume, and of the last novel, Island (1962), set on the made island of Pala, where the births are attended with a care the state never managed and where the dying is taught to the whole community, so that no one in the book is done in a column. The volume is the last he signed. Best season for the pass: morning, before the traffic becomes a reading. The room is not open; the country keeps the address, and the address is the stop.

Stop 6 The desk on the harbor · Cold Spring Harbor, New York

The route ends where the ledger sits. The record office on the harbor, from 1910, kept the master of the state’s column — the names, the class, the signature — and the folder the island received in the Friday post of 17 July 1970 came out of this desk’s keeping; the keeper’s second diary entry, kept with the case, has its account of the column and of the 30,117th row that stays in the sum on purpose. The route brings the luggage down here, all of it: the 1932 volume in one hand, the last volume in the other, and the uncle’s name in the state’s column between them. And the small type on this page, set in the guide’s own hand beneath the last paragraph, kept word for word, without a second reading, the way the line in the diary is kept:

But is the same volume, the way the island with the egg is slipped into the master ledger begins.

The guide gives the line one annotation and withholds the rest, as the manual does. It is the route’s whole sentence in the form the small type allows: the volume is the same volume — the last one, Island, the book that puts the egg, this time attended, the island, this time kept, and the dying, this time taught, into the keeping of a place small enough to sign for itself — and the beginning the line names is the beginning of this route’s last movement, the one in which the island is slipped into the master ledger not to be counted but to be read: Pala set beside the column, the egg set beside the row, the family’s second voice set beside its first, so that whoever audits the two books against the state’s one finds the count, and finds, in the same page, the count’s answer. The ferry master, who does not travel but has been told the whole line, allows it a boat ride of a day and says of Stop 6, in the only sentence of his the guide may quote, that it is the same reading as the two waters: one water and the other, and the boat goes in whichever way both allow. We do not edit. That is the route’s one rule, and it is the island’s, and the liner notes for Poucha Pond, EFT 69-01, where this addendum is kept, keep it the same way.

Practical notes

  • Best season: any; July is the writer’s own, and the island’s. Walk Stop 1 in the morning light.
  • The fare: the ferry takes you to the country of the route’s last stop only; the rest is walked, or taken by the one train, or, for Stops 4 and 5, by the road itself, which is the book.
  • What to bring: a pencil, the same one that reads the tide gauge twice a day — the route is kept in pencil on purpose, and the ferry master’s is in the same pocket as ever.
  • Luggage rule: the 1932 volume and the 1962 volume travel together from Stop 3 to Stop 6. A route that carries them apart is a route the state has already paid.
  • The one rule: we do not edit. The small type is kept word for word, the 30,117th row stays in the sum, and the line is not checked against itself. It is the same wrong, entered in triplicate; the route’s line is the same reading, entered once, for whoever audits.
