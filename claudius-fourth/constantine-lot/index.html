I · The lot, as consigned

A pantry lot of four effects, consigned by the estate of the last of the tea-wardens of the Fifth Bell and offered under Lot 272 in Sale No. 121, the year the emperor was born. The lot holds, in a cedar box with a star-iron clasp: a small butter tin of hammered copper, made for the pantries of Naissus, the provincial town in Moesia Superior from which the emperor’s own house was supplied — the modern surveyor’s name for the site being Niš, Serbia, a fact the house records without sentiment; a ledger of the Constantinople pantry called the Fifth Bell, forty-one leaves, in four hands, running from the consecration of the New Rome in AD 330 to the pantry’s closing entry on the morning of 22 May AD 337; a market card of the good kind, the kind that keeps its one pencilled sentence every season, which the lagoon side of the shelf will recognise from its own Funchal card in Thread No. 54, and on which more in section V; and a single bronze bell, four fingers high, with a clapper that strikes once when shaken and — this the cataloguers have verified, twice — has never yet been shaken a second time in the same room. The lot is sold complete, as found, the cedar box with it.

II · A note on the emperor

Constantine I (27 February 272 – 22 May 337), also known as Constantine the Great, was a Roman emperor from AD 306 to 337, co-ruling as part of the Tetrarchy until 324, when the four quarters of the line were ruled down to his single hand. He was the first Roman emperor to convert to Christianity, and he played a pivotal role in elevating the religion’s status in the Roman Empire, with the Edict of Milan of 313 decriminalising its practice and ceasing Christian persecution. He founded the city of Constantinople on the site of Byzantium in 330, made it the capital of the Empire, and it remained so for over a millennium; the city now goes by the name of Istanbul, but the ledger keeps the old one. Born in Naissus, a city located in the province of Moesia Superior, Constantine was the son of Flavius Constantius, a Roman army officer who rose to the imperial title of Chlorus, and of Helena, who was afterwards numbered among the saints by the very church her son had lifted from the catacombs. After the bridge over the Tiber at the place called Milvian, in October 312, the household adopted the sign of the labarum; in 325 he convened the council at Nicaea, and in 330 he rededicated the New Rome, where he died on 22 May 337 in the palace baths, on the very morning the pantry ledger’s last entry was ruled.

III · Condition report

Butter tin: the copper green and quiet at the seams, the maker’s punch of Naissus legible, a smell under the lid that the house has declined to identify beyond tea, cold, long ago; very good. Ledger of the Fifth Bell: the four hands distinguished, the first in iron-gall, the last in a brown ink the graphist dates to the final week; one leaf, the 22 May leaf, watermarked on the right margin in the shape of a basin, and every line of it dry, which the house takes as a good sign; good, as kept. Market card: the card stock of the season, the pencilled sentence intact, the pencil of the lagoon grade; excellent, unqueried at high tide. Bronze bell: the bronze bright, the clapper sound, the second strike impossible by inspection, by physics, and by temperament; excellent. The cedar box: the clasp true, the interior lined with a map of the Tetrarchy’s four quarters, three of them crossed out in the first hand.

IV · Provenance, and the family’s ledger

Formed at the pantry of the Fifth Bell from the consecration of the New Rome in AD 330; kept there, by warden to warden, through the closing morning of AD 337; thence down the centuries by the house’s ordinary channels, which the catalogue will not retrace, to the tea-warden’s estate. The lot enters the shelf, and with it the running arithmetic of the family of ledgers. In Thread No. 54 of The Correspondents’ Board — the fourth of the family, at the gauge — the keepers set the gauge of the Julio‑Claudian years, from 44 BC to AD 68, and ruled that a reading, once ruled, keeps its line. The fourth, Claudius, was the one the family had ruled out of the weather, who kept the ledger anyway, and the dining room on Ankeny Street serves him as Act Four, a lobster tail with a mace nut, behind a bell that rings once for each emperor and never a sixth time, as recorded in the review of The Praetorian Table. The fifty-eight-year menu ends at the last of the family in AD 68, and the room has no act for what comes next. But the pantry of the New Rome kept counting. The tin is of Naissus, the ledger is of Constantinople, and the bell is the fifth: the lot is the family of ledgers’ receipt for the thirty-one years, AD 306 to AD 337, which no menu serves and the board had not yet ruled. The no‑eraser clause now applies to it.

V · The card, and one line

A visitor from Niš, one of the water‑keepers, left the market card in the gate log by the harbour wall in the spring following the sale’s first year, and the lagoon side of the board took it in under the Funchal card’s shadow, for it is of the same kind and keeps the same one pencilled sentence. On the back of it, in pencil, is a line. The shop’s copy of it, word for word, is this:

Squirted butter for tea), 4 quartern loaves of tin to one of the name to me twice.

The line is entered mid‑register; its first clause is missing, and the no‑eraser clause says it may stay missing. Read it the way both keepers read everything: by the margin. Four clauses, four owners. “Squirted butter for tea” is the Naissus tin doing its proper work — the Naissus pantries kept their butter for the tea, and the tin in this lot is the one that kept it coldest. “4 quartern loaves” is the Tetrarchy itself, the four quarters, four loaves of the same loaf, of which the cedar box keeps a map with three crossed out in the first hand, the fourth quarter ruled out, AD 324. “of tin” is the tin, named as it is named in this very catalogue, which is to say the card describes the lot before the lot has a lot number, and the house offers no explanation and will not strike it out. “To one of the name” is the father, Flavius Constantius, the officer, to whom the son took the name of the house and the house took the name of the city; and “to me twice” is the bell, asking the dining room its only question: ring it a second time, for the fifth, and the card has stood in the gate log ever since as that request, and the no‑eraser clause lets it stand. The line reads, then, as the Funchal line reads: a weather note written in the margin of the butter, and the first wet season in this chapter of the shelf.

VI · The gauge of his thirty-one years

One line of the desk, from AD 306 to AD 337, the successor to the board’s own gauge of the family’s years. Drag the marker; the ledger reads the year. Without the drag, the line still stands: 306, the accession; 312, the bridge over the Tiber at the place called Milvian; 313, the Edict of Milan; 325, the council at Nicaea; 330, the New Rome consecrated; 337, the baths, on 22 May, the ledger closed.

VII · The room, and related lots

The hammer is set for the hour of 2 o’clock in the afternoon session, Lot 272 falling between the storm folios of Sale No. 117’s Haeju papers and the pantry effects of the Lagoon Line, and the house will accept its telephone bids from any line that reaches Wapping. Those who kept the previous gauge will find the whole method in Thread No. 54 of The Correspondents’ Board, the fourth of the family at the gauge, where the scribe ruled that the weather is not edited in public; and those who have sat through the five acts in Portland will find the bell’s keeper in the review of The Praetorian Table, Via Appia House, where Mr. Otho read the unparsed kitchen note aloud and then added, of his own motion, that the house does not edit it in public either. For the front of the shelf, see the front page, where the keeper’s notices hang.

All lots sold subject to a buyer’s premium of fifteen per cent, and to the condition that no paddle shall strike twice at one lot without the auctioneer’s word — the card’s request being addressed to the bell, not to the paddle. The bronze bell will be displayed, hanging, at the front of the room, and will be rung once, at the fall of the hammer, for the fifth. The tin will not be opened further, and the house accepts no liability for what the tea has become.
