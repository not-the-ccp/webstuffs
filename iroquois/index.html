FIELD BOOK No. 12 SOUTH BANK STATION, LOUISVILLE, KENTUCKY 38°08′ N, 85°46′ W

Iroquois, Louisville

Notes on a residential ground divided by Beechmont, kept from a porch on Kenwood Drive, 14 April — 2 June

LHNRC · FIELD SERIES · XII

Entry 1 · Tuesday, 14 April · 07:10 · light frost on the maple, then gone by the kitchen door

Arrived at the south side of Louisville by way of the Parkway and down Third Street, where the oaks stand close to the kerb and the morning is still. The ground I have come to work is the neighborhood called Iroquois, a largely residential quarter laid out as a suburb after the war, most of its houses raised in the late 1940s and through the 1950s — a generation of American houses, the kind that keeps its porches out to the street and its lawns mown to a single height. The name belongs to the neighborhood only; the people it is named for have never lived here, which is as true of the name as it is of the herons.

The ground is odd in shape. Beechmont, the older and busier district, runs through the middle of it like a river running through a marsh, so that Iroquois is properly two parcels: the northwestern section, which in the old records still answers to Bryn Mawr, and the southeastern section, which answers to Kenwood. The whole holding is roughly bounded by Hazelwood Avenue to the north, the Beechmont strip through the center, Third Street at the western edge, and Kenwood Drive to the east, with Iroquois Park holding the lower right corner. I counted, on the first walk, forty-one houses along Hazelwood alone, and not one without a mailbox.

Entry 2 · Wednesday, 22 April · 18:40 · the first of the day counted at the fence of Iroquois Park

At the park fence, in the failing light, a great blue heron stood on the dry side of the old creek bed and watched me count. It does not move, which is the whole of its argument: I have seen it in the same yard, on the same stone, at the same hour, eleven mornings in a row. The residents of Kenwood Drive call it “the surveyor,” and there is reason in the nickname, because it takes the neighborhood in from top to bottom — Hazelwood to the flight path, Third Street to the Beechmont strip — without turning its head more than a man will.

Along the flight path the sound is a fact of the weather, the way the Wash keeps its tide: the airport at Louisville International lies just to the south-east, and the departures come over at the height of the chimney tops, two or three times an hour, the whole afternoon. The residents have complained of it in writing, at hearings, and in the garden, and have challenged the airport’s expansion at every opportunity, because the expansion would be more of the same — wider, lower, closer to the porches. It is an honest conflict; I record it without taking sides, the way I record the heron. A full log of how the world tracks its flying machines is kept elsewhere, at the OpenSky Network visitor’s brochure, where some two thousand volunteer receivers keep the sky under civil count; I would trade all of them for one old man’s porch.

Entry 3 · Thursday, 9 May · 21:15 · a neighbour’s phrase, set down verbatim

Mr. Alder, who keeps the end of the row on Kenwood Drive and the oldest screen door on the ground, stood with me at the window while the surveyor was at its evening round. He has lived in the neighborhood since 1957, when the brick was still warm and the Beechmont strip was a two-lane thing with a feed store. He will not sign anything against the airport; he says the noise is the price of the porches. Asked what the heron is for, he considered the question for the length of one departure, and said: “The watch is a complete, and sitting fast.” I have set it down verbatim, because it is the clearest statement of the ground I have had. The watch — the bird’s, the street’s, his — is a complete, and sitting fast: the watch is a complete, and sitting fast. It has been on duty since before the tenement complex on the corner came down, and it does not intend to stand down for the runway.

Entry 4 · Friday, 30 May · 06:50 · the old name, and its clearing

On the site of the notorious Iroquois Tenement Housing Complex, torn down years ago, there is now a hard lot where the grackles feed before the sun is up. The complex had the neighborhood’s name and not the neighborhood’s manners, and its demolition is one of the two or three things the old residents will agree on, along with the frost dates and the feed store’s closure. The grackles do not know the history; they have simply found a good table on a bad corner, and they take it in a line down Third Street, eleven deep, which is the most orderly thing in the neighborhood after the mailbox row. The opposite order, and its full cost in months of unpaid wages, is worked out at the pay table in the Carthaginian quartermaster’s chapter on the Truceless War of 241–238 BC, where a body twenty thousand strong holds three years of arrears against a city and the whole shelf of the argument comes down on one notice printed in red ochre.

For the species I have worked from this ground, the heron’s own patient method — one parcel at a time, the head moving a quarter-turn at a time — I refer the reader to my colleague’s field notes on Eli Wallach, who spent the same kind of career on the method of standing still until the scene arrives; and for what a marsh edge does to an observer who keeps it long enough, to the notes kept at Knight Island in the Napa River, where the tides keep the same honest argument with the cordgrass that Beechmont keeps with the two parcels. The whole shelf of the field book returns, as usual, to the Webstuffs front page.
