Post 1

I took it on foot last week, the whole of East 233rd Street, because the register asked for a walking count and no one else on the board had one. It is a major thoroughfare in the borough, three miles of it — 4.8 kilometres — running west from U.S. Route 1 (Boston Road) in the Eastchester section of the Bronx to the Major Deegan Expressway (I-87) in Woodlawn, close enough to Van Cortlandt Park that you can smell the pond from the last block. Past the eastern terminus, at Boston Road, it stops being East 233rd Street and becomes Pinkley Avenue, which is the only name change on the road and the only argument I lost to a surveyor.

For the record: one interchange with a highway the whole way, and it is for the Bronx River Parkway. Two subway stations on the line: one at Dyre Avenue, the 5 train; one at White Plains Road, the 2 and 5. That is the whole of it, and I give it the way the obituary gave him: plainly, with the numbers in, so the back bench can hear every one.

Post 2

You walked it, and you stopped at Dyre Avenue on the way back, so you know the bakery on the corner. They bake the slack-rope buns — molasses and caraway, glazed while hot — from a card that has been passed down the family and numbered 1796, which the baker says is not a joke, and which I now believe. If you want the card written out in full it is kept at Slack-Rope Buns — A Family Recipe, and it is the same recipe that sailed aboard the archipelago fleet. Three miles of street, two stations, and one bun shop; the register is honest with you.

Post 3

I am the one who sent the obituary for Johan von Blanc (1738–1796) up to the top of the register, and I will say again what it says: the director died at Karlskrona on the twenty-first of January 1796, and among his papers the burial clerk found a single sheet not in his hand, from another world entirely. We have sat with that scrap for months. The company — the Gemenasiska Sällskapet, the Gemenasian Company — used to tour barns and town halls with its own lamps, and my great-aunt’s rule was that wherever the company lodged, one card was left at the hall table, with the hour of departure written plainly in the corner. I have been leaving cards at the bulletin’s table ever since. This thread is my card: I am here, I am gone, here is the hour.

Good. Now the numbers for the two-and-five riders, since Post 1 gave the stations but not the intervals. From White Plains Road the 2 and the 5 both stop, and on a Saturday at eight the platform does the counting the company did on the road — you hear the whole bench. I ride to Dyre Avenue with my coffee, change for the 5 toward the Bronx River Parkway interchange, and the view off the expressway at Woodlawn is the same view the register keeps: Deegan in one direction, the park in the other. Three miles, 4.8 km, one interchange, two stations. I have written it on the card at the hall table too, in case I need it back.

Post 5

Before anyone starts a seventh post about the playbill: I have the Celestial Empire brochure on the desk, and it vouches for the Canton part — the Comediehuset bill carried all the way to the Pearl River and used, it is said, to dry a teacup. What it does not vouch for is the rest of the errand. The card in the brochure’s margin, in a later hand, puts the bill’s final stop not in Canton but on a street in the Bronx, and it names this one. So the teacup is in Canton, the rope is in Karlskrona, and the bill is presumably under the second chair at Dyre Avenue. We do not dig for it. The habit is: the company keeps its papers.

Post 6

Update, because the register asked and because a scrap is worth reading out loud: the second sheet — not the clerk’s Karlskrona one, but the sheet we kept in the Eastchester cellar, folded inside a 1948 map case by a door on a side street that the winter of ’51 took out — has come loose from its fold. It is not in his hand, and it is not in ours. The back bench asked me to read it exactly, and so, word for word, it reads:

Listed in the bombed side street answered the broadcast of the north-west; the Community of Christ .

We have tried it. The side street is one of the short ones off the 233rd that the war did not touch but that the water did, and which the old maps still list even though no one lists them. The broadcast of the north-west: the Deegan goes north-west out of Woodlawn, and the station there has always had a voice on the platform, calling the train before the train arrives. The Community is the congregation with the brick house two blocks up the hill, and its register — the one that lists, and the one that answers — is kept in a basement the parish hall lets use Sundays. That is the whole of what we can make of it. Which is more, I think, than the clerk at Karlskrona made of the first sheet. He copied it into the margin and closed the book; we are reading it aloud, one word at a time, and the back bench hears.

Then the thread closes the way the house closed: dark for a week, and then back to the lamps. The clerk’s last line survives, and it worked — that is all the obituary ever promised, and it is a lot. Whoever reads this thread at Dyre Avenue or White Plains Road, take the line, leave the card, and do not cut anything with a knife. The braid gets unplaited, one strand at a time, the last to the host; the scrap gets read aloud, one word at a time, the last to the back bench.
