The Office of Unresolved Matters

Build Log No. 3 — a working model, scale 1:24, of a city-court filing office, linden, brass, and 312 pins · K. Workshop, attic, Prague VIII, above the shop · log kept in pencil, as the rules say

1883 — 3 July — 3 June — 1924 · the gap is priced in

Entry 1 14 April 1982 — the commission

Weather: clear, wind from the Vltava side · Bench: 1.4 m, linden shavings · Material: Form KB-78

The Society met on the first Thursday and I brought the commission to the table in a bread box, which is how I carry commissions. Lot 78 of the Southern Branch sale has been on my mind since the spring catalogue: the papers of Mary Marquet, the 1931 programme, the midnight dress, the studio letter of 3 June 1927 — 3 June, the date of a death in 1924 that the catalogue never once mentions, and that is enough for me; three years is a very polite interval — and, in the condition card, the line about the brass cap of the Sallette de l’Aube gas lamp, which does not survive. The catalogue files the search for the cap as a line in a field book. I proposed that the Society build the office where such a line is filed, at scale 1:24, working, with one lamp socket left empty on purpose, because the gap is the point and a gap you have filled is no longer the gap.

Eleven members sat around the table. The vote was eleven to zero, with one abstention that counted in both directions, which is the only arithmetic the Society admits. I was assigned the build. The rules say the log is kept in pencil, that every entry is numbered, and that the log may not describe the builder. I am keeping the log in pencil. I have numbered the entries. Whether the log describes me is for the tribunal (Entry 4) to decide.

Entry 2 16 April 1982 — parts list and first refusal

Bench: sanded · Parts: as per schedule · Post: two parcels

Parts list, entered for the file. Linden, 4 boards, 19 mm, from the joiner on the river road. Brass strip, 2.3 m, for the ledger rail — 2.3, chosen with care, because I do not put random numbers in a working model. Pins, 312, for the 312 filing cards, each card to carry one line of the search: the cap, the lamp, the island, the bantam named in the intertitles of Mabel’s Married Life, the championship of a man called Kalemba, treated as set dressing and not touched. Pencil, HB, one, the rules say one. A lamp socket, brass, unthreaded, because a threaded socket is an acceptance and we are not accepting. The refusal came by post, in the same afternoon: the Building Trades Guild does not issue working permits for offices that file without resolving. I am building it anyway. The Guild, I am told, has itself never resolved this matter.

Entry 3 22 April 1982 — the drawer

Bench: drawer No. 4 in fit · Insect: one

Drawer No. 4 ran false all day, and when I opened it at last it had, in my hand, the shape of a segmented, arched back, and I held it a long time before I put it back, because the shape was the most accurate thing the whole model had produced. I have read the novella of 1915 — the man who wakes as a large insect, and the family that cannot decide about him, and the glass of milk — and I will say of it only this: it is the only blueprint I have ever owned that specifies the room, the family, and the milk, and no dimensions at all. I re-fitted the drawer. It now runs true and remains, to my private eye, the correct shape. I have not told the Society. There is no form for it. I asked at the post office and the clerk said there is no form for it, and wrote no form on the back of my request, and the writing was very good. The 1915 novella was published the year before the sale of the Sallette lamp cap was ever dreamed of, and that is the only connection I need. The model is a machine for holding connections. It does not require any to be true.

Entry 4 6 May 1982 — the tribunal

Court: kitchen, 19:00 · Charge: one · Fee: 40 crowns

The family held a tribunal over the model on the kitchen table. The charge was that the model is a filing office and that a filing office must resolve something, and that it is not lawful to build a machine whose entire output is a line about a brass cap. I answered from Entry 1. The judge — the eldest, in the large coat — found that the charge is well-founded, that the defendant is correct that no office must resolve in order to be an office, and that the matter is therefore deferred. An appeal is possible. The appeal form, I have discovered, is the back of the charge sheet, in smaller handwriting, and it is in a language I am told is mine. I filed the appeal. It was returned, unopened, stamped GEPRÜFT — examined — which is the one word on the stamp that the office cannot be proved to have meant. The fee of 40 crowns is deducted from my bench allowance, which settles it in the only currency that settles anything.

Entry 5 30 May 1982 — the inspector

Inspector: one, from the Guild · Corner: missing

An inspector from the Building Trades Guild came to the bench at 10:40, measured the model twice, and did not measure the gap, which I took to be a compliment. He measured the lamp socket, wrote it down, took a corner of the filing cabinet with his hand, as casually as one takes a corner of a tablecloth, and left a form, Form KB-78, revision 4, in the drawer. Rev. 4 differs from my rev. 3 in one place only: it provides for a second lamp socket, threaded. I keep his corner in the bread box. It is the truest piece of the build. On the door of the model I have lettered, as the law of the room requires, the sign: THE LAW IS FOR EVERYONE, with a man seated outside it in the margin, small, with his hand down, as if at rest and not as if at a loss — the reader will know the novel of 1925 by its door and its lawyer and the man who is told, at the end, not to be sad. I know it by the same. The castle of 1926 comes last, in Entry 6, and I warn the reader that it comes last because it is last, and not because of the order of the log.

Entry 6 12 June 1982 — the castle

Post: one parcel, 1.1 kg · Return: none

Lot 78 now hangs, the catalogue says, in a private room above a cinema in Marseille, where the 1931 programme is pinned at the door, as it was in 1931. I decided that the model would go to the room, because a filing office belongs in the room where the gap is priced in. I crated the model at 1.1 kg, addressed the parcel, and posted it, and the post office gave me a receipt in red ink, which in my experience is the ink of a promise. Nothing has come back. There is no form, I have now confirmed, for a model that is not delivered, and the post office will not say whether the parcel is at the room or on the way to the room, and I will add, because the log must add: the village at the foot of the castle has a door, and the door is a door, and that the novel of 1926 ends with the man who cannot get past the door, and that the parcel is, in my working model of the matter, exactly one parcel ahead of the man. I am content with this. The fables are the small ones and the truest, and the aphorisms have the jokes in them, and the jokes are on the side, and I will not copy one in here, because the log may not describe the builder, and a copied aphorism describes me better than my own hand ever has.

Entry 7 3 July 1982 — the day of the birthday

Bench: all 312 cards in · Lamp: unlit, by design

Today the log is 99 years late to a birthday — 3 July 1883, Prague, a German-language Jewish writer, and I have kept the arithmetic in the header and not in the body, because the header is the only place in the log where numbers are allowed to be about him. All 312 cards are in the cabinet, and the search is fully filed: the cap, the lamp, the island in the Napa that the field notes keep by its water, the bantam, the championship, the line that names all of them in one breath. The model is finished, which is to say it is ready to do what it does, which is to file. Under the lamp socket, unthreaded, empty, I have pinned a slip of paper on which is written the only line of the consignment that cannot be returned, in the field book’s own hand, copied in pencil by mine. The lamp will not be lit this evening or any evening. A lamp that is lit is a lamp that has accepted. The office files, and the office waits, and the waiting is the output, and anyone who has ever stood in a queue for a form that will not come knows the output better than I do, and has not said so, which is the whole of the method.

Marginalia Tucked inside the back cover of the search.

Hand: unknown · Copied: pencil, from pencil

That is the whole heading, in one breath, no comma: the marginalia are tucked inside the back cover of the search — the search being the catalogue’s search for the Sallette’s brass cap, opened in the field book and never, on the record, closed. I found the slip the way the field notes find slips: in a book I had borrowed, on a page the tide or the thumb has softened, written once before me, in a hand I do not know. It reads, and I copy it here because it was written down once before me and because I cannot explain it:

THE GAP IS NOT THE ABSENCE OF THE CAP. THE GAP IS THE CAP, AND THE CAP IS THE PRICE, AND THE PRICE IS PAID IN LINES.

Hand unknown. Filed, card 313 of 312, because the office counts the gap as a card.

The field station’s notes for that island — three feet of dry land, cordgrass, rails, and the tide that takes the whole place back each morning — are kept beside this log in the bread box, and they too are a search that files itself, page by page, with the water doing the closing (Knight Island — Field Notes). And the source of the gap itself, the whole consignment, the four pieces and the one brass silence, is filed under Lot 78, priced in, as it was sold in Salle 3 under the chandelier, the mallet light once over the gap (Lot 78 — Mary Marquet: The 1931 Programme, the Dress, and a Brass Cap That Does Not Survive). The front of the house is the catalogue cover — return to the front, and the door there is a door, and it is for everyone, and that is the last entry of the rule, and the rule is the last word of the log, and the log is pencil.
