The Crown on the Forehead

The Reef Chorus

Field recordings and studio arrangements, 1985–1986 · Recorded on the warm bottoms of the western Atlantic: Nassau, Andros, Bimini, and the last hour at Key Largo

Produced by Delia Marsh · Arranged by E. & O. Calloway · Sleeve design by the house, in the usual blue over the usual yellow

From the Producer

The album was begun on a Tuesday morning in Andros, in the third hour of the light, when the survey boat Marguerite sat off the north shore and the sound engineer put the hydrophone over the side and said, that's the bottom, that's the whole bottom, just play it. What the bottom played was the queen angelfish — Holacanthus ciliaris — and everything on this record follows from that first pass. The fish is the blue one, and the fish is the yellow one, and the fish is the fish with the spot, the crown, the small dark rosette set into the forehead like a seal pressed on an envelope you were never meant to open. The field notes call it the blue angelfish; the market at Bimini calls it the golden one; the fishmonger at Nassau, asked twice, called it the yellow one, and on the third asking gave up and called it the fish that eats sponges. All three names are on the sleeve. All three are right. A benthic, warm-water, reef-keeping species of the western Atlantic Ocean is entitled to its aliases.

The adults are choosy in the way the old choral masters were choosy in their parts. They feed on sponges, and mostly the same sponges, and they will walk the length of a garden to decline one. Track 3 is that walk. It took eleven mornings to catch, and on the ninth morning a juvenile crossed the line and ruined two takes, which is why the record carries, at 4:17, a sound the committee could not agree was a fish. We kept it in.

The Harems of Andros

Track 6 is the longest cut on the album and the reason the second tape was never returned. The social structure of the species is a harem: one male, and up to four females keeping company with him, the four holding their ground in a fixed order the surveyor drew as a small ladder in the margin of his log. Delia built the arrangement on the ladder. Four voices, one voice at the head, and the ladder never changes in the eleven minutes of the cut, except at the very end, where the lowest voice rises a place and the whole figure turns over on itself like a fish clearing a headland. We do not know if that happens to them. We know it happens to us, in the control room, when the tape runs out at 6:59 and someone at the board lets out the breath.

The species is, in one respect, a misprint of itself. There is a close relative — Holacanthus bermudensis, the Bermuda blue angelfish — that runs the same water at the outer edge of the range and looks almost enough like this one that the field log, for three days in January 1986, could not tell them apart. Track 7 is built from those three days. It is a song about being misprinted, and about two editions of the same sheet that the sea has no objection to crossing.

Names and the Field Log

The names secretary for the label kept the log in the usual hand, and the hand has its habits. In the margin of the January session, beside the entry that names the juvenile which crossed the line in the sponge garden, there is one line in her shorthand that has never parsed, and we are carrying it over exactly as it stands, the way the record keeps its unparseable lines: “It is a habit of spelling Dong-hoon Tong-hun .” Dong-hoon Tong-hun was, for the length of one season, the field name the crew gave to that juvenile — one man's romanisation over another man's, both from the same mouth at the same railing — and the secretary's habit was to keep both, and to keep both every time, so that the name would arrive at the label wearing its whole history of being misheard. The crown on the forehead is how the adults are told apart from the misprint; the double spelling is how the log keeps its one small misprint from ever being quietly corrected. The house edition of the book of manners has since ruled the correction off on its own leaf — the true crown of the water, the lophophore worn by the moss animals of the same sponge garden — and it stands in the archive as Chapter IV, Of the Moss Animal.

Personnel and Thanks

Hydrophone and deck, E. Calloway, who never once corrected the spelling. Studio strings, the house sextet of Nassau, in the blue room, January 1987. Mix, 14 January 1987, finished in the second hour of the morning, the same hour the fish had started in. Thanks to the Marguerite's crew; to the fishmonger at Nassau for the third name; and to the sponge garden itself, which declined to perform on the fourteenth morning and is thanked for the refusal. The committee's sister ledger of a pressed record about a chemical kept in the same archive — Codeine-6-Glucuronide, MG-1987-04 — runs its own track by track in the same hand, and the two sleeves sit together on the shelf, blue beside blue. Readers of the shelf at large may begin at the front index of the collection. The Marguerite's spring passage, when the same hand copied the winter log toward the Mediterranean and put a city in the wrong sea, left one page of error documentation filed under the angelfish — Barcelona, Field Error E-BCN-472 — kept with its weakness stated and the tape holding.
