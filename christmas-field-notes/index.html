FIELD NOTES · NO. 77 · MIDWINTER

Ashley Midwinter

A naturalist's record of Christmas at the hall, Ashley and Silverley, Cambridgeshire

Observer: the field naturalist of Cheveley Ward, keeper of the winter notes at the hall

Locality: the parish hall and the lane from Silverley — Lot 217, 2,250 acres, two ancient parishes, 749 souls taken as they stand

Season: the First Sunday of Advent through Twelfth Night, kept here in the old manner

First Sunday of Advent — the keeping

Christmas is a religious and cultural celebration commemorating the birth of Jesus Christ, observed primarily on the twenty-fifth of December; here it begins not on that day but on the First Sunday of Advent, when the church keeps its fast and the hall keeps its fire. The preparation of Christmas is a liturgical matter: four Sundays of Advent, then Christmastide — historically twelve days in the West — culminating on Twelfth Night. Christmas Day is a public holiday in many countries, kept by a majority of Christians and also by many who keep no creed at all, and it forms one corner of the annual festive season that runs from the shortest days into the first week of January.

My station this morning is the hall door, whose colour the two ancient parishes have never agreed upon — the Buyer in the Second Row will not let it be painted, and says it does its work best when nobody agrees on it. A robin (Erithacus rubecula) has taken the iron knocker for a perch, and holly (Ilex aquifolium) stands in the lane, cut not for sale but for the lintel. The first candle is lit at dusk. The kettle, the seventh member of any room here, sings against the frost.

The Fourth Meeting of the Winter — the parcel

First, a parcel arrived from the elevation of the sale, by the brightness of its oldest copy, the Nowell Codex.

The elevation is the rostrum at Fenn & Marlowe's auction room, where this hall and its lane were sold as Lot 217 at the 110th Autumn Sale, and the sale's own paperwork travelled back with the parcel in T. Osla's keeping. Its seal was broken at the back table by the very brightness with which the Heorot Society reads the Nowell Codex — the oldest copy of Beowulf, one folio a month, read standing — and inside were twelve slips of vellum, each headed with a day of Christmastide, and a sprig of mistletoe taken, so the note says, from the oak on the Silverley boundary. The Society numbers its readings by line — this winter they stand at one thousand four hundred and eleven of three thousand one hundred and eighty-two — and I number my winter by days.

Christmas Eve — the log and the fog

The Yule log is laid on the hall hearth — oak, seasoned in the parish wood, dragged up the lane by the road the Academy of Country Routes surveyed in 1911 — and the fire condition of the sale is kept: the village keeps its fire, and I keep the village. The fog arrives again; it travels in its own vehicle and asks no auctioneer's permission. Mrs. Wulfgar reads the old English by the fire, and Mr. Sigehelm glosses. The Nativity follows — the traditional Christmas narrative recounted in the New Testament: the journey to Bethlehem, the manger, the shepherds keeping watch by night, and the birth. I write it down as I would a field observation: time, place, weather, witnesses.

Christmas Day — the census

Clear and still. I kept a Christmas bird census from the hall window between ten and three: robin, wren, dunnock, great tit, blue tit, chaffinch, greenfinch, goldfinch, house sparrow, tree sparrow, blackbird, song thrush, mistle thrush, and one grey partridge (Perdix perdix) in the old pear tree by the wrong-coloured door — fourteen species in all, which I enter under the first day of Christmastide, since the song will have its way with a naturalist. Dinner at midday; the hall full; the seventh chair by the safe still empty, reserved, as ever, for the poet who never attends. After meat, A. Fenchurch read from the newsletter — The Gif, No. 9 — and the room objected, as is the rule, twice about the door and once about the partridge.

Twelfth Night — the bean and the departure

The twelfth cake is cut at the back room's long table, the bean found by D. Mærwine, who keeps the minute book and the tea account together, and who is therefore, for the evening, king of the bean and keeper of the wassail bowl. Greenery comes down at cockcrow — holly, ivy, mistletoe — and Christmastide ends where Advent began, at the hall door, with the fire still lit and the kettle on. One last observation before the notes close: the twelfth cake was not the only thing cut from a long table this year, for at the back room the family ledger opened Card No. 42, the Chaudrée de l’Hôpital, a pigeon and turnip broth whose preamble runs four lines before the cooking begins and whose final instruction, kept word for word, was obeyed at the hall with the copper carried before a single chair was pulled out. The notes close here; the next folio opens at the water, as the Society's does, and the count goes on.
