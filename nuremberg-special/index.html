SPECIALSERVICE1935

This is the working timetable of the special service built for the Nuremberg rally of 1935 — the Nürnberger Gesetze, the Nuremberg Laws, were read and adopted on this line, at its terminus, on a Saturday evening in the Dutzendteich, and the whole apparatus of the season, the trains first — arrived, or was meant to, by the numbers below. The times are as printed. The commentary, in the margins of the service, is set off from the board where a reader's notes are kept: the two measures of 15 September, the supplementary decree of 14 November, and the cellar under the castle that the season did not reach. Read the board straight down, and the notes when you are ready for them; the line was drawn in ink, but so was the comment.

Commentary I — Two measures, one coupling

The legislation of 15 September 1935 comprised two measures, and the timetable of the season treats them the way the railway treats coupled carriages: separately numbered, moved as one. The first, the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour, prohibited marriages and sexual relations between Jews and persons of “German or related blood”, and barred Jewish households from employing German women under the age of 45. The second, the Reich Citizenship Law, cut the word citizen out of the reach of anyone not of “German or related blood”: those so excluded were demoted to state subjects, Staatsangehörige — persons inside the state but without its full rights, in the manner of a passenger who may ride the train but may not hold a ticket. The session that adopted them was the special session of the Reichstag, held at the Dutzendteich stadium on the night of 15 September 1935 during the annual rally; the text of this board carries the evening as a running service, because a session of that kind was conducted, as it were, on the move — a train of speeches, the law itself as the final carriage, arrived and signed.

On the etiquette of the marriage that the first measure was drawn to close — the broker at table, the fee, the seat of the bride — the archive keeps The Bartered Bride — Chapter IX: Of the Marriage Broker at Table; and on what a treaty of union does when two legislatures ratify it, one by one, see Acts of Union 1707 — Frequently Asked Questions, where the 110 to 6 vote is set down with its arithmetic.

Commentary II — The 14 November supplement

Two months and a half after the evening above, the line ran an extension: the supplementary decree of 14 November 1935, which did the work a timetable does when a connection has failed — it fixed the names of the passengers. It defined a person as a Jew who had descended from three-quarters Jewish ancestors; it made a Jew of the grandchild of a Jewish parent; it caught those married to a Jew on 7 September 1935 who had a child born out of wedlock after 31 July 1936, and those with a child born out of wedlock after the same date. A person with two Jewish grandparents was to be a Mischling of the first degree, with one a Mischling of the second; the head of state held the power to strip citizenship in individual cases, and to restore it by special act. The decree entered into force the day it was signed, and the board was reprinted the same week: the extension, 14 November 1935, added in ink. What the two measures had drawn, the supplement numbered; a law that can name is a law that can list, and a list is a timetable. On 15 September 1938 the line carried its last rally. On 9 November 1938 the service was suspended; the board was left up at the platform, as the board is left up.

Commentary III — The brewer's cellar

Before the law came to the terminus, Nuremberg had already been Nuremberg for a thousand years, and the deepest record of the city is not in the statute books. Beneath the castle on the Königstuhl, and under the old town along the Pegnitz, the brewers kept their cellars — the Keller of the Nuremberg brewing houses, vaults older than the town council, cut into the hill where the water ran cold and constant. The city's beer, Nürnberger Bier, had been shipped by cart to the courts of Bavaria and Bohemia long before 1935, and the cellars that fed it did not read the Reichsgesetzblatt. A law can draw a line through the street above, through the register and the marriage book and the employment ledger — the three ledgers the first measure reached for — but the line stops at the stone. The brewers kept their vats full through the seasons of this board, and the town kept its cellar door as it had kept it since the medieval guilds set the rule that the cellar was not to be broken. The brewer's cellar, it is not to be broken. The comment filed with this board takes that line as the season's only exception clause: the laws of 15 September 1935 were promulgated to the sound of the stadium, but the city's oldest working room was a cellar, and a cellar does not echo, does not applaud, and is not broken by a reading. Whatever else the season of No. 9/35 carried through the Nuremberg arches, it did not carry into that room; the fermentation went on at its own temperature, under its own stones, on its own schedule — and the board, which knows the whole service to the minute, has no entry for it at all, which the comment keeps as the cleanest record on the line.
