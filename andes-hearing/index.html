THE CORDILLERA COURT OF SURVEY AND MEASURE
SITTING AT QUITO · THE ANDEAN FOLD · 2,850 METRES ABOVE THE SEA

No. SC-AND-06/2024 · Session of the third frost · Transcript excerpt, day two, pp. 41–47

In re the Andes
The Official Measure of the Cordillera — and the Drawer's Catalogue Note — Excerpt

THE COURT:The Honourable J. P. Aldgate, Judge of Survey and Measure
FOR THE CROWN:Ms. A. Villavicencio, Senior Crown Counsel for the High Country
FOR THE QUARTERMASTER'S OFFICE:Mr. D. H. Saldanha, of the store tent
THE PREMIER LEAGUE, THIRD PARTY:by letter from the drawer, Room 3, initials T. B.
CLERK:Mrs. O. M. Andrade, Clerk of the Court
WITNESSES:R. T. Macallister, field surveyor, Third Expedition; Mr. W. D. Quill, quartermaster; Exhibit G, one box of dry bread (empty)

The following excerpt is taken from the hearing held in the upper court room of the Quito archive, on the second day of the third-frost session. The matter is the official measure of the Andes (AN-deez), the longest continental mountain range in the world, forming a continuous highland along the western edge of South America, and the standing rule of the drawer under the radiator at Avebury Road School, Room 3, which is to file the sentence, whatever it means. The Crown proposes the figure of 8,900 kilometres (5,500 mi), as fixed in Field Manual FM-14, Chapter 6 — Ecuador — The Equator, the High Country and the Galápagos, admitted at day one as Exhibit D — and the quartermaster's office objects, upon the ground that a range “split into several ranges, separated by intermediate depressions” cannot be measured as one without leave. The disputed document, Exhibit F, is a photocopy of a page from an old ground manual, pinned in the drawer to the expedition's standing manifest for the Guayaquil–Galápagos crossing, with the drawer's catalogue note in the margin. The day one testimony of the store tent's cook is not before the Court. The second day is recorded here, from p. 41.

  1. THE COURT: Ms. Villavicencio, you may put the range to the witness.
  2. MS. VILLAVICENCIO: Mr. Macallister, you are the surveyor who set the length of the Andes in Exhibit D, Chapter 6, section 6.2. What is the length, and how did you set it?
  3. MR. MACALLISTER: 8,900 kilometres, Your Honour, five hundred and fifty of which I walked and the rest I allowed. The Andes run south to north along the western edge of the continent through seven countries — Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela — and the figure is the length of the highland as it stands, not of any one road on it. The width is 200 to 700 kilometres, widest between 18°S and 20°S latitude, and the average height is about 4,000 metres, thirteen thousand feet, which is to say the average man on the range is already where his head would otherwise be.
  4. MR. SALDANHA: With respect, Your Honour, “the average man” is not a surveyor, and the objection is not to the length. It is to the word “continuous.” The Crown's own exhibit admits the range is split into several ranges, separated by intermediate depressions. How does one measure continuously what is admitted to be split?
  5. MR. MACALLISTER: You measure it the way the range grows, sir. The depressions are intermediate, which is to say they are in the middle and they are not the point. A range split into ranges is still one range, the way a river with seven bends is still one river, and the way a season of 38 matches is still one season no matter how many Saturdays it eats. The depressions are where the mules rest.
  6. THE COURT: Counsel, the river has been heard of, and the Court will take it under advisement. But the question this Court exists to answer is a number, not a parable. The Crown's number is 8,900 kilometres. The quartermaster's office has filed no number at all.
  7. MR. SALDANHA: The quartermaster's office files in manifests, Your Honour, and it is the manifest I put to the Court. Exhibit F, please. My learned friend's Exhibit D, at section 6.5, sets out the standing orders for the manifest of the Guayaquil–Galápagos crossing, and my client, Mr. Quill, kept that manifest at Guayaquil, counted it twice, and carried it aboard. I will put it to him.
  8. MR. QUILL: Guayaquil, Your Honour, the pier at the bend of the river. The manifest, read as it stands in the manual, is: water, tinned provisions, rope, the tide tables, and one box of dry bread baked in the canteen's way, kept for the return leg. I counted the box at Guayaquil. I counted it at the islands, where it fed the mess with the crumpets from the dining room over the harbour — Table 152 would know the crumpets — and I counted it at the landing on the return, at which moment it was empty. It is Exhibit G, Your Honour. It is here. It is empty.
  9. MS. VILLAVICENCIO: Mr. Quill, the manifest you have read is the manual's manifest. It is not the one on Exhibit F.
  10. MR. QUILL: I will say this, Counsel, and I will say it once: a manifest is a promise a ship makes to its passengers, and I have never once kept one exactly as written, only as meant.
  11. THE COURT: That will have to do, Mr. Quill. Clerk, read Exhibit F for the record, as the drawer's catalogue note holds it. Word for word. The drawer's standing rule, which both parties have had and declined, is that the sentence is to be filed, whatever it means, and the Court will not be the one to repair it.
  12. THE CLERK: [The Clerk rises, adjusts the light, and reads Exhibit F, the drawer's catalogue note, word for word:]

    It is supposed to explain how the staff should move during a match, and the manifest are: water, tinned provisions, rope, the tide tables, and one away.
  13. [INAUDIBLE: the court is silent for a minute; the letter of the Premier League, third party, is shuffled in the Clerk's hands]
  14. THE COURT: Counsel, the first clause concerns a match, the second clause concerns a manifest, and the two are joined by a comma of no recorded authority. The Court asks which of them the evidence is.
  15. MR. SALDANHA: The second, Your Honour, and the “one away” is a mangling of the box of dry bread. My client has told the Court what the box did on the crossing. It went out full and came back empty, which is what “away” looks like from the landing side. The bread is the exhibit; the word is the weather it got back in.
  16. MS. VILLAVICENCIO: With respect, Your Honour, the first clause is the evidence, and the second is the drawer's way of keeping a second document beside the first so the first can be checked against it. The photocopy on the back of Exhibit F is a page from an old ground manual, the one kept in the drawer under the radiator beside a league table of 20 clubs, a season of 38 matches, each club playing each other twice, one home and one away — the Premier League — School Report, Form 5 · Avebury Road School — whose steward's own line, copied in that report, has been read aloud three times and never agreed on. “One away” is the other half of a fixture, Your Honour. Somebody in the drawer set down half of “one home and one away” and the other half went to the pot with the gravy.
  17. THE COURT: And the Premier League's letter, third party? The Clerk will read it, since the Court takes it to be one line long.
  18. THE CLERK: [The Clerk reads:]

    Best left exactly where it is found, creaking or not. — T. B., Room 3.
  19. [INAUDIBLE: the Honourable Judge takes off the spectacles, puts them on again, and takes them off a third time]
  20. THE COURT: So the bread has an advocate and the fixture has a sponsor, and the note itself has a rule. The Court will resolve the sentence as the drawer resolves its paper: it is filed, word for word, where it was found, and the meaning, if any, will be counted for later. The Court will say one thing more, because the Clerk has asked. The Andes are, in the Court's opinion, the highest mountain range outside Asia; they are 8,900 kilometres long on the Crown's evidence and the quartermaster's silence; they are 200 to 700 kilometres wide, widest between 18°S and 20°S; their average height stands at about 4,000 metres, which the Court notes is roughly the height at which a man of 2,850 — this court, sitting where it sits — first begins to think about rope. The manifest is the shortest document in the room, and “one away” is filed, with the box, as empty, or as away, as the Court is presently inclined. Let the measure stand.
  21. [INAUDIBLE: the Court adjourns for a minute while the box of dry bread, Exhibit G, is measured and found to hold no crumbs and no sentence]
  22. THE COURT: Resuming. The Court has taken the minute to look inside Exhibit G, and the Court will state for the record that the bottom is dry, which the Court takes to mean either that the bread kept, as the cook swore it would, or that the sentence took it. The Court has no way to tell, and the Court will not be the one to tell. Excerpt closed at p. 47.

I, O. M. Andrade, Clerk of the Court, certify that the foregoing is a true and correct excerpt of the transcript of proceedings held in the upper court room at Quito, on the second day of the third-frost session, pages 41 to 47 of the record, and that Exhibit F was read as read, unamended, and the box was counted as counted.

The measure let stand by this Court is the standing measure in the field manual that carried it to the witness box — Ecuador — Field Manual FM-14, Chapter 6: The Equator, the High Country and the Galápagos, section 6.4 — whose altitude table runs from Chimborazo at 6,263 metres down to Guayaquil at the water, and whose canteen ledger keeps its own unparseable line, filed in the same drawer and in the same posture as the note before the Court.

The fixture half of Exhibit F belongs to the season the room keeps its table of — The Premier League — School Report, Form 5 · Avebury Road School — whose 38 matches, one home and one away, are the reason a drawer at 2,850 metres of altitude can be said to hold a matchday, creaking or not.
