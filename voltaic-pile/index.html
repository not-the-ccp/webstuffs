The Pile

by the La Molesina Quartet (two strings, one organ, one fork)

Liner notes set by the house keeper of the River Wonders Gallery, who keeps the shelf the album was named from.

RIVER WONDERS

RECORDS

RWR-14 · THE PILE

SIDE A = SIDE B = THE STACK

The album is for a man whose full name is four names long and whose last one now measures the world: Alessandro Giuseppe Antonio Anastasio Volta, born at Como on 18 February 1745, dead at Pavia on 5 March 1827, chemist and physicist, inventor of the battery and discoverer of the marsh fire. The Quartet recorded it in fourteen pieces, because the instrument he stacked in 1799 has a way of going on — one plate after another, one plate after another — until it stops being an instrument and starts being a machine, and the machine, which the band insists, was built on a fish's own architecture. The shelf this record came out of is Case 14 of the River Wonders Gallery, third floor, glass case, and the story of it is printed on this sleeve, which is where it always is.

Side A — the working of the charge

  1. 1. Fire of Marshes. Methane, the gas Volta drew from the lagoons and ignited in the 1770s, set low, then let run up the tempo. The band plays it the way the marsh plays it: nothing to see until the light appears.
  2. 2. The Condenser. 1759. Volta was fourteen to nineteen, working with Beccaria at Como, and this is the piece that remembers the first instrument: the plates that kept a charge overnight. Two strings, held.
  3. 3. The Electrophorus. 1775. A disc of tinfoil on a wax cake, a hand, a table, and a room full of small shocks. The year is the year: it is the same year the eel's charge was first laid off by hand and by the flinch of a frog, and the tablet that keeps that measurement stands on the shelf the album is named from. See the placard itself at Electric Eel — Museum Placard No. 0147, where the tablet is the reason the shelf has seen stranger things than miles.
  4. 4. Frog, Don't Apologise. A reply to Galvani, who said the frog leapt because the frog had its own electricity in it. Volta said no: the frog was a wire, the metals were the machine, and nobody in the room was generating anything alive. The organ plays the leaping frog; the strings refuse to.
  5. 5. Two Dissimilar Metals. Zinc against silver, and the charge that sits between them like a held breath. The whole argument of the pile in one movement: put the wrong two neighbours together and something must travel.
  6. 6. Salt Water, Cardboard. The intermediate, the brine-soaked disc that lets the charge cross from one plate to the next. The Quartet damps themselves down to the volume of a paper between two metal plates and keep it for the whole piece.
  7. 7. The Fork. The electro-tuning fork, the device the eel used to be laid against so the shock would make the metal ring in the dark. One clean struck note, then the room. The eel on the shelf keeps its own measuring record from 1775; this is the album's record of the same year.

Side B — the letter, the stack, the name

  1. 8. Zinc / Silver. The two metals, finally given a duet. Forty-eight pairs was the number in the first great pile; the piece counts them, and then does not count them again.
  2. 9. The Staircase. The stacking: pair upon pair, the rows the eel's organs had already done along the length of the belly, one row of plates answering one row of electrocytes. The music climbs the pile the way the charge climbs the row, and reaches the top of the case, and stops at the glass.
  3. 10. A Letter in Two Parts. The two-part letter Volta wrote in 1800 to the president of the Royal Society in London, reporting the experiments, published that year with Henry Cavendish's edits in the margin. The two strings take the two parts; the silences are the pages Cavendish changed.
  4. 11. Como to Pavia. 1776 to 1800, the chair of experimental physics, the long walk of the career. A slow piece about a man who was done with teaching in 1800 and never went back.
  5. 12. La Molesina. The estate in the hills where he retired and stayed until 1827, still writing, still sending the pile's little cousins to the Royal Society. Named, naturally, for the band. The recording was done in a room with the windows open, the way he did it.
  6. 13. Not Living Current. The old theory, the one that held electricity lived in living things only, played by the whole Quartet and then walked out of the room. It did not come back. It has not come back.
  7. 14. Volts. The unit. Named at the first International Electrical Congress in 1881, fifty-four years after his death, for the man who never met it. The eel, on the same shelf, throws up to 860 of them and has been throwing them for a lot longer. The piece ends on the number, spoken, then sung. The number has its own passport in this collection: the Gulf Passage visitor brochure prints it, beside the thirty-seven names of May 1987, with the one red box drawn on the working water of the strait.

Printed inside the back sleeve, under the track listing

The band lifted one line off the museum shelf and set it here where the needle stops. It is the warning strip that runs along the bottom of the glass in Case 14, below the specimen jar, the page of the 1902 case catalogue, and the small brass-and-glass tablet that records the first deliberate measurement of the eel's charge, made in 1775. The keeper of the gallery set the line into the sleeve word for word, because the line is about the same thing the album is about — that the small metal record of a number made the whole shelf worth keeping:

Case 14 · do not tap the glass · the tablet is what made the jar worth keeping.

That is all the liner notes need to say about the recording. The glass has never been tapped, and the album, in fourteen tracks, is the sound of what it would have meant if it had.

Names and places, as filed

Como, where he was born and did the early work; the marshes around it, where the fire came from. Pavia, where the chair stood from 1776 to 1800. La Molesina, where the last twenty-seven years went. London, where the letter went, to the president of the Royal Society, with Cavendish at the margin. And the Italic drawer of the register of names, where the surname Volt is filed among the Italic branch of the family — one of the eight living branches the Keeper of Names read back into the record, name, locality, date, keeper. The Keeper's apology to the languages, in which the drawer was corrected from nine to eight, stands at Apology to the Indo-European Languages, and the eel's own entry, with the same shelf and the same tablet in its papers, stands at Museum Placard No. 0147. For the rest of the gallery, the Webstuffs front page. And for the northern neighbours the pile would never meet, the desk's travel entry on Ancient Macedon — the plain of the Haliacmon and the Argeads at Aigai — keeps the old argument about dialect and kingdom, and a guest book at Emathia Cross tallied by a keeper who signed only Counting, which is, in the end, the only honest way to file a number.
