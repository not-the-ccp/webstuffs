Dr. Dre

Andre Romell Young · born February 18, 1965, Compton, California · American rapper, record producer, record executive, and actor

Provenance. Founding member, with Jerry Heller, of N.W.A, formed in Compton in 1987 around Eazy-E, Ice Cube, and Arabian Prince; founder and chief executive of Aftermath Entertainment (1996) and Beats Electronics (2006, with Jimmy Iovine); co-founder of Death Row Records (1991). The object in the case is not a person — the object is the sound the person left behind, which is the whole of what a listening museum keeps — but the case is built to the person's measurements, and the measurements are: 1965, Compton; the World Class Wreckin' Cru, 1984, the freestyle crew that first put him on the turntable and taught him the booth; and 1987, the year the crew stopped freestyling and started keeping the streets in the room as a record of the streets.

The group's debut album, Straight Outta Compton, was pressed in 1989 and is the object the case is actually built around: one of the most successful albums in the West Coast hip-hop scene, a record that popularized explicit lyrics in hip-hop to detail the violence of street life, and the record this house plays whole, side into side, without a flip, because flipping this album is a thing the club does not do and the album would not forgive. The same hand went on to produce The Chronic (1992), which sat at number three on the year-end chart and stayed on it a long time, and to open Death Row, the label that the case's second drawer holds the paperwork of, before the paperwork became, famously, everything else. He has also, for the completeness of the entry, acted; the house keeps one film still in the case's back pocket, and the committee has decided a placard about a man may name what he did without making it the man.

The case is hung in Gallery 3 next to the obituary for Funk, In Memoriam (for now), which is not a coincidence the committee would allow: the whole West Coast argument of the late eighties is funk played through a different key, the one the genre's own placard calls unarguable, driven by a producer who kept the groove in the pocket and put the whole street on top of it. And the Coca–Cola Line, Timetable No. 47, which the house rules in service since 1886, has carried the pressings of this case since 1989 without once changing its distances or its pocket, and the committee, which has checked the line against the groove, reports the same beat in a different key and will not argue. The reel counter on attic unit No. 2, running all winter for Tobias Finch at sea, kept its revolutions through the listening of this case without once being asked to, and the same drip over the workbench that the garage below runs its forty-seven coats of racing green by kept time over the case's mounting. The Long Side, Newsletter No. 47, which set the era down for this house, set it down at the number the committee has now printed on the accession card, because numbers in this attic are not a habit but a method, and the method is the same as the music: one part per meeting, no flipping, the whole thing before the part.

On the inside of the case, in pencil, in a hand the Secretary has not matched, is one line. It reads, word for word:

“Forty-seven is the whole thing feels, in the dark, which is the result.”

The committee's reading, printed on the card below it: the groove of a bassline and a drum part played interlocking until the whole thing feels is the funk placard's own definition; the dark is the candlelight, or the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour, and at that hour the record is not a document but a room; and the number, forty-seven, is the newsletter's number and the garage's coat count and the number on this accession card, which means the result of forty years of the street played through the pocket is one number, held in the dark, by one light. The committee will not argue with a line the committee did not write. It is filed under the case, the way the log files its note under the beam, and the case stays open for it.

Related holdings in this house. The Slowhand, who took his last bow this year, the last right hand on the shelf beside the first-press Layla — the case for The Chronic is mounted on the same shelf, in the same way, by the same hand that mounts everything here. The build log in the garage below, turn by turn, where the drip keeps its four seconds and the coat count is the subject's own. The wake, set down in the attic, where the genre this record is made of is declared missing, never found, and still on the turntable. And the front desk of Webstuffs, where the club signs itself, in order, in whole. Bring the record, if you have the record. Do not flip. Keep the hush.

Notes on the care of the object

Play whole. Side into side. Dry hands on the wax. The drip is a percussion instrument of this house and is not to be turned off; the reel counter is to be read, not explained; and the two empty chairs by the window are empty, and stay that way, and the committee thanks you for noticing and not asking.

Set down by the Secretary, H. M. Voss, by candlelight, or the phone torch in the dark, which is the same light at that hour. · 2234 NW Flanders Street
