Restaurant Review · Larkspur Lane, three doors off the market square

Sokolivka Соколівка

Four birds. The falcon is on the wall, the other three are in the kitchen.

Sokolivka takes its name from the village — there are several, in Ukraine, all of them spelled Соколівка, all of them small enough to be mentioned in a single sentence each — and from the bird in the name, the falcon, whose Ukrainian is sokil. The dining room is on Larkspur Lane, three doors from the market square, behind a door the colour of beet juice, and the first thing you notice is that the room is small in the way a kitchen is small: every surface is working, and nothing has been moved for your comfort. The second thing is the row of painted houses above the pass, a hand-painted frieze in eight houses, ochre and sky-blue and the red of a barn door, done by the proprietor’s cousin in the village that supplies the name. I asked how many Sokolivkas there were to choose from. “Doesn’t matter,” she said, “they all keep a falcon in the roofline.”

The menu is printed on stock that smells faintly of the print shop, and it is the best-argued menu I have read all year. Oatmeal porridge with honey from the upper pasture — eleven drams of honey per bowl, no more, because the honey is from one apiary and the apiary has one rule. The beet shakshuka is built in a brick-lined pot and arrives still bubbling at the rim, with a dollop of fermented dairy that the chalkboard calls, in English, “something like but not quite a cheese.” The duck is dry-salined for nine days and carved at the table with a knife that the waiter holds out to you first, which is the right order of operations. I was seated at table five, which is the table the house keeps for people eating alone, and the lamp there is lower than the others by exactly the amount you need.

Here is the strangest fact of the evening. The register behind the bar is an old thermal unit, pre-war, that still talks to the kitchen printer in the wrong alphabet. Sometime during the second course it coughed, and the kitchen printer, asked for a course count, printed its complaint on the day’s till roll in a line that went, letter for letter, exactly this:

“A drop biscuit is not the currency sign, the broken bar, the diaeresis, the acute on the market prints a row of painted houses on the console.”

The proprietor read it twice, nodded, and wrote the line under a line of her own in the day book, which is where it has lived since, uncorrected, under a photograph of the frieze. It is a machine complaining that it cannot say what it means, in the alphabet it was built for, and I have decided it is the truest thing about the room. The houses on the frieze, after all, are the market square, and the square does the printing.

Service is brisk and unhurried at once, which I have only seen before in places where the staff share a cellar. Wine is by the glass and by the bottle, most of it from the west, nothing on the board above a price that reads like a fine. The dessert is a round cake in a cracked top, split and re-stacked, with a jam that changes with the orchard. I left at half past nine, through the beet-juice door, with the till roll line in my head and the falcon in the roofline in mine.

It is not the certified, credentialed kind of dining room — no post-nominal on the duck, no ledger behind the pass — and I think that is its one clean argument, set beside the careful work on Hall Street in our review of Designation. It is, if you prefer the family-baking way of putting it, a drop biscuit of a restaurant: dropped in one deliberate place, from a fixed height, and let to set. I go back for the porridge. Eleven drams, no more.
