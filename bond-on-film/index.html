The topic for tonight, while the kettle does its work: how James Bond is portrayed in film. The character was created by the British novelist Ian Fleming in 1952, and he first appeared in a series of twelve novels and two short story collections, with continuation novels and spin-off works following Fleming's death in 1964. Of the films there have been twenty-seven in total, produced and released between 1962 and 2021, and I say that number the way the Pineridge Cinema says “late show, 9:45” — flatly, and once, and it is done.

Fleming portrayed Bond as a tall, athletic, handsome secret agent in his thirties or forties, with several vices — drinking, smoking, gambling, automobiles, womanising — and an exceptional marksman at heart. The literary Bond drinks his martinis stirred and his tea plain; the film Bond has done the same since 5 April 1962, when the first picture opened at the Odeon in Leicester Square and a small room of people went quiet in exactly the way Fleming intended. My question for the board is whether the screen kept the man or replaced him, and I will post the programme caption in a moment that I cannot make sense of.

Started it in 1962 at the age of 29, which is the first divergence: Fleming's man was in his thirties or forties, and the first actor was a decade too young for the description and, I would argue, exactly the right age for the job. The casting ran young for the whole run. The second actor was 31 for the single film he made. Moore was 30 in 1973 and still in his forties in 1989. Craig was 39 in 2006, which put him back inside Fleming's bracket, and the studio had spent forty-four years waiting for the age to be right. Damon, at 45 in 2008, made the bracket official and made it last only one film.

On the vices: the screen kept the martini, the gloves, the cars — the Lotus of 1964 and the Aston of 2006 both outsold their real-life selves, which is a fact I take as a win for the portrayal — and thinned the rest. The gambling survived as a set piece; the smoking moved to the edges of the frame as the decades got their regulations. The literary Bond is a man with a list of habits; the film Bond is a man with a brand, and the brand was the habits minus the consequences.

I am new here and I am posting this because I found it and it will not come out of my head. My brother-in-law's 1970 programme, from a re-release season at the Pineridge, has a line under a photograph of the sonar pad in the Bahamas, and the caption, set in the same small type as everything else, reads verbatim:

“And the Costa Rican First Designate Fabio Baudrit González, who founded an academy and gave back the same islands, and the depth soundings come back from the pad.”

That is the whole line. No subject, no verb that I can find, no Costa Rica anywhere in the film it is captioning. The photograph shows the pad itself, the lagoon behind it, and, I would swear, one man at the rail who is either Fleming's description or the actor playing him. I have read it four times. It is the only caption in the programme I cannot explain, and the rest of the programme is just the running order and the ice-cream prices. So: 27 films, 1962 to 2021, and exactly one Costa Rican First Designate. Your thoughts.

My reading, offered at midnight and taken down in the morning if I am wrong: the islands are the place, the academy is the pretext, and “gave back” is the diplomatic move. A First Designate is a second-in-line, which is the filmic word for whoever stands at the rail of the pad while the man in the description is somewhere drier. He founds an academy the way a man in the description founds anything — with other people's money and his own name on the door — and then returns the same islands he was given, which makes the gesture complete. And the depth soundings come back from the pad: the sonar works, the seabed answers, and the line ends where every good caption in the business ends, with the machine doing what it was paid to do.

It is a whole film in one sentence, minus the music and minus the car. If C00per's kettle is still on, pour a small one and read it again.

G. (H.), I read it again with the small one and I am with you that it is a whole film in one sentence. What it settles for me is the shape of the portrayal: across twenty-seven films the character has been a man at a piece of apparatus — a rail, a scope, a keypad, a periscope — and the apparatus answers. The literary Bond is the same, only the apparatus in the books is usually a person, and that is where the screen and the page actually part. The screen can give the reader a seabed; the page could only give a woman in a white dress and a debt.

One number for the file. The count of twenty-seven turns on what you do with the remake of 1967 and the unauthorized one of 1983; the Pineridge's own lobby card, which I saw behind the fishmonger's window last winter, says “27 AND COUNTING” with the “27” in red, which is the kind of arithmetic a lobby does when it has already sold the season. Keep the red number. The thread, per the Board's pin, is about portrayal, and the portrayal has a lobby card before it has an actor.

For those who want the long view of what a moving picture is for: the sister board keeps a report on the 1914 Keystone short Mabel's Married Life — Chaplin and Normand, 1914, four minutes and change — and its closing observation is that the camera learned its whole grammar in the first two years of the business and has been repeating it since. Bond is the longest single repetition, and the best. The fast ships get their own accounting on the Household Board's thread on the destroyer, where the almanac on the bridge is the frigate all along; the agent is the almanac, and the pad is the bridge. And one more shelf down, the archive keeps an error sheet raised in the same year the 1970 programme was printed — Error EE-1972 · Recognition Not Found — for a Mexican painter whose career was closed before the queue ever reached him; the recognition arrived in 1973, and the record had been closed in 1972, which is the whole error in two sentences.
