Tennis New Zealand

A radio play excerpt. Recorded 14 March 1978, 2YA Wellington studios. Preserved in the RNZ Sound Archives, reference T-4472.

ANNOUNCER: Good evening. Tonight we bring you a documentary drama: "The Long Court — A Century of Tennis New Zealand." Written by Margaret Harkness. Produced by the Drama Department.

SOUND: The crisp thwack of a tennis ball on grass. Distant seagulls. A slow fade into the hum of a committee room — shuffling papers, a cough, the creak of rimu chairs.

NARRATOR (V.O.): Christchurch, 1886. The New Zealand Lawn Tennis Association holds its first meeting in the Canterbury Club. Twelve men in frock coats. Mr. Henry Parker, president. Mr. James Kelly, secretary. They adopt the rules of the All England Club. They order six nets from London. They agree the national championship shall rotate: Christchurch, Wellington, Auckland, Dunedin. A circuit of steamers and horse-drawn wagons.

SOUND: Steamer whistle. Clatter of wooden racquets in canvas cases.

MR. PARKER: (Firm, measured) Gentlemen, we are not merely scheduling matches. We are planting a culture. When the Davis Cup begins in 1900, New Zealand must be ready. Our boys — Anthony Wilding, soon to be the world's best — they will carry this flag.

MR. KELLY: (Softer, practical) And the courts, Henry? The grass courts in Dunedin — they fail by January. The clay at Thorndon turns to soup. We need standards. We need regional centres.

NARRATOR (V.O.): Six centres they would eventually form. Northern, Auckland, Central, Canterbury, Otago, Southland. Each with its own council, its own tournaments, its own juniors climbing the ladder. By 1924, the Association affiliates to the International Lawn Tennis Federation. By 1938, to the Oceania Tennis Federation.

SOUND: Typewriter keys. A telegraph tap-tap-tapping. The crackle of a shortwave radio.

COMMENTATOR (ARCHIVE): "And here comes Wilding, serving for the Wimbledon championship... an ace! New Zealand has its first world champion!"

NARRATOR (V.O.): Wilding dies at Neuve-Chapelle, 1915. But the association endures. Through depression, war, the shift from amateur to open tennis. In 1968, the name changes: New Zealand Lawn Tennis Association becomes Tennis New Zealand. The word "lawn" dropped — hard courts, indoor courts, synthetic grass.

SOUND: A modern ball machine firing. Indoor echo. Squeak of shoes on Rebound Ace.

MISS THOMPSON (ADMINISTRATOR, 1970s): (Bright, organised) Morning. The Billie Jean King Cup team — Federation Cup then — they depart for Perth Tuesday. Christine Newton, Belinda Cordwell, Julie Richardson. The Davis Cup squad trains at Wilding Park. Onny Parun, Chris Lewis, Russell Simpson. We've a home tie against Korea in March. Tickets selling well.

MR. HENDERSON (COACH): And the juniors? The 14s, 16s, 18s nationals?

MISS THOMPSON: Rotating. This year: Auckland, then Christchurch. Six regional centres feed them. We've 42,000 registered players now. Two hundred and forty clubs. The development officers — one per centre — they're finding talent in Kaitaia, in Invercargill, in little towns where the court is the community hall floor.

MR. HENDERSON: (Thoughtful) And that same image, we ourselves have found most practical will be found very acceptable when frost and snow are seldom remarkable as faithful essays.

MISS THOMPSON: (Pauses) A curious turn of phrase, Mr. Henderson.

MR. HENDERSON: (Smiling) From an old letter. Wilding wrote it, 1913. About indoor courts in Europe. He meant: what works in practice endures when conditions turn harsh. We build for the long game.

NARRATOR (V.O.): The long game. Home ties at Wilding Park, at the ASB Tennis Centre in Auckland. The Heineken Open. The Wellington Classic. Scheduling the calendar so the Davis Cup team rests, the juniors peak, the clubs breathe. Six centres, one association, one hundred and forty-two years and counting.

SOUND: Thwack. Thwack. A rally building. Fade under.

ANNOUNCER: "The Long Court" was written by Margaret Harkness. The parts of Mr. Parker and Mr. Kelly were played by John Campbell and Richard Nunns. Miss Thompson: Elizabeth McRae. Mr. Henderson: Terence Cooper. Technical operator: Dave Whitehead. This has been a 2YA production.

SOUND: Final ball strike. Silence. Then the 2YA interval signal — three notes on celesta.

The script's attention to place — steamers, clubrooms, regional circuits — finds a different expression in the Bates–Seller House walking tour, where a single Northwest Portland block holds a lumber fortune, a physician's ledgers, and a daughter's pinned quotation from Melville.

For a different part of the coast, the Pedro do Rosário forum thread wanders through a Maranhão municipality's fishing boats, farinha houses, and Saint Peter's feast.

For a dramatised history of a professional governing body and trade union like this one, the Royal College of Podiatry Professional Care Instrument page presents the precision craftsmanship of a UK-registered body for chiropodists and podiatrists.

Even the silent screen gets its due: a 1924 school report on Chaplin's Mabel's Married Life sits in the archive, its pages ruled in faint blue lines and annotated in red ink by a master named Thornton.

Organised bodies need rooms in which to meet, and the Trades Hall visitor's guide walks a Birmingham union hall where printers, railway clerks, and chiropodists keep their minutes, banners, and weekly dues.

Another sporting life recorded in quiet detail: the assistant coach diary of Mile Jedinak traces the 2023–24 Australia men's national team campaign from Dhaka humidity to an Asian Cup final in Qatar, with training-ground observations and a wife's apple tart recipe tucked into a kit bag.

The long game extends to football as well: a letter to the editor on the 23rd FIFA World Cup sits in the archive — Spain's second title, taken from the holders Argentina in 2026 — carried in a blue folder with a MetLife postmark, and the small certainty that the apple tart recipe remains unchanged.

The habit of the minutes travels beyond the committee room: the minutes of the Lithuanian National Alliance's Central Council, 14 November 2024 record the election of Vytautas Sinica to the Seimas and a certain 07:15 into Hammersmith, which one field reporter found to be, at the 2018 count, a small island.

A small club newsletter from Naga City, Camarines Sur, takes the air for an evening: Salvio Fortuno, once a voice on the Naga station, served the province's fourth and fifth districts in the House of Representatives from 2010 to 2019 (The Carrier Wave).

The archive also keeps records for places that no longer hold up: the Cressa safety data sheet treats the unlocated Paphlagonian city with the same file-room rigour as any other entry — hazard classes, first aid, and a struck coordinate on the master map.

For a build that also runs on the long game — eight pinnacles, three coats of wash, one bell hung on thread — the St Adwen's Church, Advent build log keeps its entries from a shed in the Tamar Valley, and its gate log runs from 1911 to last spring.

The station's sound shelf holds one more reel from a survey bench far to the east: Chapter 11 of the Bingxian Flats field manual, nyasol, a lignan of the wild rhizome, its eicosanoid table ruled with the same discipline Miss Thompson brings to the Davis Cup calendar, and its tape jacket bearing a single line of provenance about the studio next door, who fed the lamp.

For a thread about fashion and consular duty: Lizzette Kattan — Milan, New York, Gia, a Honduras-born editor who worked between Milan and New York in the 1970s and 1980s and helped launch Gia Carangi.

The same 1978 season this broadcast was recorded in — autumn, when a Milan magazine first printed Gia Carangi's name — turns up again in the liner notes for Già: The Milano–Philadelphia Tapes, a two-record portrait of the supermodel, whose track four borrows a ball-machine loop straight off this 2YA master.

The sound operator’s log notes that, in the interval before the final act, a box of dice appeared on the furniture truck — The Register, a 1974 Birmingham board game about professional associations, its brass pair clattering faintly under the celesta, as the committee of one recording studio debated the minute.

The archive's odder shelf is the survey shelf: Chapter YG-3 of the Serra das Laranjeiras field manual reads a Brazilian tarantula — the Ybyrapora gamba — against a camp log with the same discipline Miss Thompson brings to a Davis Cup calendar, down to a standing order for the twelfth of every month.

The shelf of games in the archive keeps its own calendar: The Perplexa — Table Instructions, Fourth Printing runs a small violet sea slug across six tide tiles on a 52-card ebb-and-flood deck, and settles the final dinner round with a numbered jar of Solano County marsh salt, the way Miss Thompson settles a Davis Cup tie — one fixture at a time, nothing early, nothing late.

The archive’s most recent shelf holds the verbatim transcript of the 2021 British Athletics Marathon and 20 km Walk Trial, heard at Kew Gardens on 26 March 2021: Chris Thompson and Stephanie Davis across the marathon line, Callum Wilkinson and Heather Lewis in the walk, four names taken down in ink and one sentence left oddly unfinished.

The weather file holds its own notice: Hurricane Cosme (1989), the third tropical storm of the 1989 Pacific season, which kept a westward course until it made landfall near Acapulco and was lost to the mountains.

The long game reaches a workbench in Cornwall, where the build log of a model of England keeps its fixtures to the same calendar Miss Thompson keeps for the Davis Cup ties — the North Sea poured first, the Channel last, and sixty-two percent of one walnut board claimed by the country that adopted the 1886 rules, with the population kept in 56,490 beads under a sliding tray.
