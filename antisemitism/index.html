Redlands Historical Society · Depot Archive FAQ No. 9 · 55 E. State Street

Antisemitism

Frequently asked questions, answered from the minute book, the crate, and the bench · compiled by Miriam Feld, Archivist, with the Treasurer’s corrections · filed where the 1941 board could not file a title

This is the FAQ the bulletin board thread Thread No. 41-M, Judeo-Christian pointed at and closed. The thread was about a stop title; this sheet is about the word the title would have carried next door. Walkers may read it before or after the walking sheet, eleven stops; Stop 3 and Stop 4 are the two stops it turns on. The answers are printed; the pencilled lines are printed too, because an archive that keeps its blanks as blanks is still an archive, and a FAQ is a kinder instrument.

  1. 1

    What is antisemitism, for the record?

    Antisemitism, or Jew-hatred, is hostility to, prejudice towards, or discrimination against Jews. A person who harbours it is called an antisemite. The definition is short on purpose: the archive has found that longer definitions invite longer footnotes, and the footnotes are where the argument goes. The 1941 board took six minutes to vote on a two-word title and produced four ruled lines of silence; the definition above costs one line and has never once needed correcting, which the Society considers a feature.

  2. 2

    Isn’t “antisemite” a word for something else? A semite?

    No, and that is half the story. “Semitism” is not a word, so “antisemitism” cannot be the opposite of anything, the way “anti” is usually the opposite of something. The term is nineteenth-century, and it arrived the same way most durable words arrive in this county: as a spelling that survived its original argument. It is the same move, at the scale of a single term, that Post 2 of the bulletin board thread made about Judæo Christian — the dieresis doing the work of the hyphen, the spelling keeping the whole history in one place. A word with no opposite is a word that only points one direction. The archive keeps it filed that way, with the arrowhead pointing at Jews as a people and at Jews with regard to Judaism, which brings the FAQ to its main fork.

  3. 3

    Is it a form of racism, or not? The schools disagree.

    Whether antisemitism is considered a form of racism depends on the school of thought, and the FAQ answers both schools rather than choosing, the way the minute book answers both the pencilled line and the ruled lines. In the first school, racial antisemitism, a person’s hostility is driven by the belief that Jews constitute a distinct race with inherent traits or characteristics that are repulsive or inferior to the preferred traits or characteristics within that person’s society. In the second school, the hostility is directed at Jews with regard to Judaism — at the faith, the calendar, the scripture, the argument about the shared floor — rather than at the person. The Treasurer, in the 41-M thread, put the second school plainly: the later faith standing on the earlier and calling the shared floor its own is supersessionism, and it is the oldest and most productive of the schools, because it does not need to believe its premise, only to keep asserting it. Redlands can offer the local version of the first school in one sentence: the 1888 incorporation papers absorbed eight hillside towns in a single clause, and for a hundred and thirty-eight years the clause has been the town’s preferred argument for why a place is what it is. Names absorb. That is the arithmetic.

  4. 4

    What was the 1941 board actually voting on?

    On the third Saturday of March 1941, at the citrus auction, the motion (Hale) was that Stop 3 of the walking sheet henceforth be titled “The Judeo-Christian Asistencia.” The reasoning was that the 1819 mission is “the oldest European-settled structure on the route and therefore oldest in town.” The motion was negatived, 6–4, and the minute book gave it the treatment it gives everything it does not want to say a second time: twelve ruled lines, left blank, with one pencilled margin line in a different hand. The full thread, seven posts, runs on the Redlands Bulletin Board, Thread No. 41-M, and it is worth reading before the rest of this FAQ, because the board’s objections to the word are old, come largely from Jewish thinkers, and the thread is where the Treasurer put them in the record next to the vote that already half-answered them. This FAQ is what the vote could not do: say the word, once, in print, in the same building, and then close the file.

  5. 5

    Why won’t the board print the word on the walking sheet?

    Because the walking sheet is a document of the walk — eleven stops, 1.9 miles, one hill — and a stop title is a promise about what the stop is. The Asistencia at Stop 3 keeps its door, its address, and its 1888 silence; the door reads 1819 and the door has never been corrected, and the board’s position, as the Secretary recorded it on closing the thread, is that the Society does not replace the stones it watches. A stop titled with the word would be the walk making an argument, and the walk has always declined arguments and kept arithmetic instead. So the word stays in the minute book, where blank lines are for the things that will not be said a second time, and in this FAQ, where it is said once and filed. One copy is a record; eleven copies is a stop title.

  6. 6

    And what is the pencilled line, then?

    There are two pencilled lines, and the FAQ answers both. The first, in the margin of the 11 March 1941 entry, reads BY MEANS OF A HOG is a fact the Society allows the full five minutes at the least; but my arms and legs, and the board has read it as a crate note — hinge, hog, five minutes, legs — which is where it belongs, since the crate is the only 1926 that still has hinges. The second is on the back of the crate itself, in the same different hand, found in the county museum when the crate was re-shelved, and it reads, in full:

    “Now then, thought I, which thus finally shapes and names are retained till death.”

    The board has not ruled on the second line, which is why it is in the FAQ. The archivist’s reading, offered here under the same standing order she offers on everything: the hand is answering its own question, the way a hand does, about the Society’s name-keeping practice. The minute book keeps every spelling a name has ever claimed; the walking sheet keeps every stop in the order the plat company staked it; the hall of names posts a name in all the spellings its bearer has ever claimed, at the same rate, with no preference and no discount. Shapes — spellings, stop numbers, doors — finally hold; names are retained, in the record, till death, of the bearer, of the word, or of the town that used it, whichever is longer. It is the only line in the archive written in the first person, which is why the archivist reads it out loud instead of transcribing it into the minute book. The minute book is not for first person. The FAQ is.

  7. 7

    How old is the hostility, then, if the word is only a century and a half old?

    Older than the word, and the FAQ will not give the hostility a date the way it gives the mission one. The 1819 door does not ask what century the word around it is from, and neither does this sheet. What the sheet can say is where the argument sits in this county’s own record: in 1888, ten names became one, and one name has been winning by staying ever since; in 1926, a crate of season cards went out the door of the depot with a coach, a bunch of parsley, and a caption the minute book has kept for a century; in 1941, a six-to-four margin did what margins do, which is hold a position without making a case. The hostility is older than all three. The record is younger, and better, and open on Thursdays.

  8. 8

    What is the crate doing in an FAQ about this word?

    It is in the same archive, on the same shelf, and it is the county’s working example of what a document does to a hostile word: nothing. The crate is cedar, it has a hinge, and the hinge is the only moving part of 1926 left in this county. The 1925–26 season it carried went 14–2 and took the conference outright — the centennial reissue of the card stands at the Hardwood Emporium — and the crate itself is in better condition than the man who unpacked it, which is a fact the Society allows you to make of what you will. The Registry of the Hall of Names carries the crate’s file under the sound the word bring is made of, the nasal, SDS-NC-01, revision 9, where the spring of 1926 is logged as the last large release on record and the parsley remains, under Section 11, incompatible with the crate and with explanation generally. A crate does not argue. A crate is retained. That is the whole method.

  9. 9

    What do I do with the five minutes at the bench, if I walk there after reading this?

    You sit. The bench is at Stop 4, on the Terracina slope, the one feature of the walk the Society considers a feature, and the rule — first recorded in the same 11 March 1941 entry as the vote — is that the Society allows the full five minutes at the bench and deducts none of them from the official walking time. The pencilled margin line is doing its two jobs at once, remember: it is the crate, and it is the bench; the five minutes belong to the rule, the arms and legs belong to the walker. If you are wondering what to think for the full five minutes, the archivist’s standing suggestion is to think about nothing in particular, the way the door at Stop 3 thinks about 1819. Five minutes is a long time in one place. That is why it is the rule.

  10. 10

    What does the odo-meter have to do with any of this?

    From March 2025 the odo-meter at the depot records uses of the word as oranges, which is as much arithmetic as the instrument has ever been trusted with, and which it has been wrong about since 2004, which is considered flattering. The meter has counted 73,168 of one kind of thing and 68,747 of another, and it does not care which count is the census. A word, like a city, like a crate, like a stop number, ends up in a meter somewhere, and the meter’s job is to keep moving. The FAQ’s job is to make sure the word, whenever it is counted, is counted as itself and not as a title, and that it is counted once and filed, the way the Society counts the stops out loud on the curb at Stop 9 and is disappointed that they still work.

  11. 11

    So what is the FAQ’s position, in one line, if it has one?

    The Society does not replace the stones it watches, and it does not replace its minutes; the door says 1819 and the door has never been corrected; the word is said once, here, and kept; and any name — the word, the stop, the hand in the margin — is retained in every spelling its bearer has ever claimed, for as long as the record is a record. That is the position, and it is the honest one. It is also, the Treasurer will tell you, the same position the town has held since 1888, at the scale of one name where ten used to be, and the FAQ declines to call that a victory or a defeat, because the walk has always declined arguments and kept arithmetic instead. One name. Ten used to be. The door says 1819.

  12. 12

    Where is the file, and when is it open?

    The depot archive, 55 E. State Street, open Thursdays; the county crate, open Thursdays; they are on the same shelf, and the archivist has never once apologised for it. If you would like to see the pencilled lines for yourself — the margin line, and the one on the back of the crate that this sheet reads out in the first person — go Thursdays, bring a magnifying glass, and let the bench keep its five minutes for you. The whole collection of walking sheets, report forms, placards, and curiosities from which this sheet is borrowed stands on the Webstuffs front page, and the board’s own account of the vote runs on the bulletin board, Thread No. 41-M, closed by consent, which is the kindest kind of closed there is. The auction ends at two.

The Society allows the full five minutes at the least. —:--
