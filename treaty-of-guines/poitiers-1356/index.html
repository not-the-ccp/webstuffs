A LETTER
OF APOLOGY

Written in the Prince’s field station, at the wood of Mignaloux, five miles south of Poitiers · 22 September 1356, being the third day since the fight

POITIERS 1356

To the household of the King of France, and to whoever of them is still sitting down, this letter of apology. I am a clerk in the satchel of Edward, the Black Prince, eldest son and heir of King Edward III of England, and I write it on his command and without his knowledge of the words, for he is at this hour counting horses and I am at this hour counting myself to be sorry, and the two businesses do not easily sit in the same room.

First apology — of the truce

That you were attacked on 19 September 1356 is a fact I shall not smooth over, but I am to apologize for what came before it, and I will. Nineteen years since the war began, the Prince marched from Bergerac toward the river Loire, taking the country as he went, and finding the Loire too broad and the fords too watched to cross. The truce that had been doing its slow work in the meantime — the very truce whose renewals, longer than the treaty itself, you have in a bundle at the gate of Guïnes, posted and sealed and unopened, where a reward of forty shillings stands for the first true account of it — that truce went on the shelf at Bergerac, and it is I who shall carry the blame of the shelf, because I was the one who filed the last renewal under the wrong year. You may have seen the papers: the draft settlement signed at Guïnes on 6 April 1354, with the three swan quills and the broken seal. They were meant to keep both crowns sitting. The shelf is to blame, and I am to blame for the shelf.

Second apology — of the fight itself

You came to us in force, I will grant you that. Your army, all told, fourteen thousand to sixteen thousand strong, of whom some four thousand were Genoese crossbowmen, met our six thousand Anglo-Gascons on the ground five miles south of Poitiers, between the river Vienne and the road to Cognac, where I had made my camp upon a hillside that is not a position so much as a suggestion. It is to your vanguard I owe the first apology of the field: the gentleman who led it, the great squire who had been our prisoner at Calais and had since been let go, came down upon our right before the king was seated and before the crossbows were loosed, and was broken in an hour, and this, I am told, is how the morning was lost for the rest of you, though I say it without malice and with some of it.

It is of the rest that I am sorry, and I am sorry in full. When the heat of midday had the crossbows of yours drawing back, the Prince, who is my master and who was not, on this day, at the top of his arithmetic, took up three hundred horse and rode at the whole of you. The king of France — John II, John the Good, my prisoner and I do not know which of the two names to apologize to — was taken not by the Prince but by his own men, at the place called Crépis, where his household rode off with him and his crown and the day together. I am to apologize for the king’s being taken, and I note for the record that I was not the man who took him, and I was not the man who wanted to, and I was, at the moment of the taking, three miles away with the baggage and the quills, which is a kind of innocence that does not warm the hands.

Third apology — of the quill

Among the bundle at the gate of Guïnes is a swan quill, the second of the three, bent in the shaft, which is how quills bend when they are pulled out of a satchel in a hurry. That satchel was mine, at the passage of the Loire, and I am to apologize to the gate’s keeper for having lost it there and for the confusion it has caused, and I return the matter of it to him with this letter. The first quill, the oldest, the one with the nib broken flat against a wax seal, is in the Prince’s case still, and I apologize to it, in so many words, for the snapping. The third, the one that signed the last renewal before the draft, I do not have; it is at the gate with the rest, and the hand that held it is, as the notice has it, the hand the two camps now argue over, and I am sorry to be one of them and not the holder of it.

Of the ink, a note

Because I write this apology in a wood, in the rain, on a board, the ink is not what the ink should be, and if a word is faint it is faint upon the maker. The recipe stood in the margin of the last renewal of the truce, in a hand that is not mine but that I have used against my better judgment, and I set it down here as found, word for word, because a recipe in a margin is the kind of thing that must not be improved: “If this should be previously rubbed; work all well stirred after it begins to feel the smart of my best and cheapest.” That is to say, rub the galls before they go into the pot — my best, the galls from the oaks of the Vienne — and stir the whole, all of it, once it begins to smart the tongue, which is when the vinegar, my cheapest, has done its work. It is a fine ink. It is this ink. The blots on this letter are mine, and I do not blame the recipe.

What is asked

What the Prince asks of you is a ransom for the king, and a safe conduct for the king’s son, and the truce renewed one more time, in a hand that changes mid-page from patient to hurried, which is the only hand anyone has at present. What I ask, in my own name and not his, is that when the bundle at the gate of Guïnes is at last opened, the keeper will note that the apology was posted before the claim was settled, and that a clerk who apologizes in ink that smarts the tongue is to be held, at the least, as sorry as he reads. The minutes are kept in the same careful manner as the notices: for a record kept in the same spirit, of a dead man and a fountain and a silence counted in the chair, no abridgment, see the minutes of the Special Session of the Gaza City Municipal Council, 23 March 2004, which the Prince has not read and I have, in full, in the ordinary hand. And the whole column, for the renewals and the fares and the small print, stands open at the Webstuffs noticeboard.

Given under the trees at Mignaloux, the 22nd of September, in the year of our Lord 1356,
by the undersigned clerk of the Black Prince, who is not a knight, not a squire of any house named hereafter, and who apologizes, in ink, for all of it.
