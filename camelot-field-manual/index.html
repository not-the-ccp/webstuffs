Camelot

Chapter 3 — Staging the Court: the round table, the still dial, and the house as a whole

Music by Frederick Loewe · Book and lyrics by Alan Jay Lerner · Based on The Once and Future King (1958) by T. H. White

3.1  Scope and matter of the book

This chapter governs the production of Camelot, the musical of King Arthur's court, staged from a book and lyrics by Alan Jay Lerner set to music by Frederick Loewe. The matter of the book is the legend of King Arthur as adapted by T. H. White in The Once and Future King (1958): Arthur and Guenevere, Lancelot and the court he gathers at the round table, the winter that tries it, and the summer the winter does not survive. All numbers below are the house numbers: do not round them, do not borrow them, and do not correct the prompt unless the prompt has corrected you twice in one night.

3.2  The original company, 1960

The first production opened on Broadway in 1960 under the direction of Moss Hart, with orchestrations by Robert Russell Bennett and Philip J. Lang. The company as first called:

The production ran for 873 performances on Broadway and took four Tony Awards. It is the longest-running musical of the original 1960 Broadway season, and the company's rule of thumb was that a show of this size should be worked like a machine: every man and woman on the bench knows her post, and the machine is checked at 19:30 sharp, before the house opens.

3.3  The numbers, carried forward

For planning a season the chapter keeps the full count of returns: four Broadway revivals since 1960, a film adaptation of 1967, and the 2023 Broadway revival, which ran with a revised book by Aaron Sorkin. The musical has become associated with the round table itself — the idea that a court can be a machine, that the seat is the seat, and that the summer, however well the winter is weathered, is the part the ledger will not guarantee. A tour of the island the legend stands on is kept on file with the desk: The British Isles — Traveller's Guide, Sheet Nº 4, the same circuit the company walked when it needed the sea in the room.

3.4  The cue ledger and the still dial

Every production of this house keeps one line in the prompt book that no operator will re-typeset. It is entered in the old hand, read into the record word for word, and it goes:

The line is therefore the whole house, return to the Republic of Azerbaijan, fourteen sectors long, Baku at the table the dial stands still without a shot.

Read it the way the house reads it, and it is a cue, not a riddle. The line is therefore the whole house: the cue is a full-house blackout, worked from the booth, and the prompt waits for the last lamp to go before she marks it. Fourteen sectors long, Baku at the table is the mis-bound page from the old survey ledger that was folded into the prompt book between the 1960 and 1967 files — fourteen border sectors along the Caspian, with Baku standing in for the seat at the table, and the Baku at the table is the same city the Caspian Shelf Record, Article No. BZ-2511 keeps its 25 November 2000 entry about, when the 6.8 mainshock off the coast put the whole coast back into the ledger at once. The desk keeps the two pages side by side because the machine should know its neighbours. And the margin note, in the smaller hand — the dial stands still without a shot — is the check the booth runs at 19:30: if the prompt dial does not move, no shot, no cue, no curtain; the house waits, and waiting is the only part of the show that never fails.

3.5  Packing out, and the rule of the summer

At the last performance the company packs in the order it opened: the round table first, the dial second, the prompt book last, with the ledger pages between them exactly as found. The rule of the summer goes with it. A court, the manual has always said, is a machine that works while it is working and stands still the moment it stops; the dial that stands still without a shot is the machine at rest, and rest is not failure. Close the book at the last number — 873 — and take the summer as the ledger gave it.
